# IELTS Preparation Course — the plan for an eight-book series
## Academic and General Training in one book, built on the TOEFL series' machinery

*Written against the delivered TOEFL iBT 2026 series: eight volumes, sixty
units, 1,085,425 body words, 3,752 figures, nine gates. This plan says what
clones, what changes, and what has to be written before any content is.*

---

## 1. The decision that shapes everything: one book for both modules

Academic and General Training are not two tests. They are one test with two
substitutions:

| | Academic | General Training | Shared? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listening | 4 parts, 40 items | identical | **100%** |
| Speaking | 3 parts, 11–14 min | identical | **100%** |
| Reading **question types** | the same eleven | the same eleven | **100%** |
| Reading **texts** | 3 long passages | 5 shorter texts in 3 sections, the last one long | differs |
| Writing Task 2 | 250-word essay | same task, plainer prompts | ~90% |
| Writing Task 1 | describe data, a process or a map | write a letter | **differs entirely** |

So roughly **85% of the course is common**, and the only part that genuinely
forks is Writing Task 1. A combined book is not a compromise: it is the
architecture the test already has.

The better news is that the five-cycle unit this series already runs maps onto
that fork without being redesigned. Cycles 1 to 3 take an everyday sub-topic
and cycles 4 and 5 take an academic field — which is exactly the General
Training / Academic seam:

* **Cycles 1–3** — notices, advertisements, timetables, workplace documents,
  the everyday register. This is General Training Reading Sections 1 and 2,
  and it is where the **letter** (GT Task 1) is taught.
* **Cycles 4–5** — the long passage, where cycle 4 states the claim a field
  rests on and cycle 5 is the objection to it. This is Academic Reading, GT
  Reading Section 3 (which is also a long text), and where **data description**
  (Academic Task 1) and Task 2 are taught.

Every student does all five cycles. A General Training candidate still needs
the long-passage skill, because GT Section 3 is a long text and carries the
hardest questions on that paper. An Academic candidate still needs the
everyday register, because Listening Parts 1 and 2 are entirely in it. Only
Writing Task 1 is marked **A** or **GT** and done by one of the two.

## 2. The ladder: eight books, half a band each

IELTS reports 0–9 in half bands. The overall band is the mean of the four
section bands rounded to the nearest half (a .25 rounds up to .5, a .75 up to
the next whole band). That gives a cleaner ladder than the TOEFL 1–6 scale
did, and the eight books fall out of it naturally.

| Book | CEFR | IELTS target | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1 | A1–A2 | **below 4.0** | not test-ready, and says so on its own cover |
| Foundation 2 | A2–B1 | **4.0–4.5** | the bridge into the graded course |
| Volume 1 | B1 | **5.0** | first sitting |
| Volume 2 | B1+ | **5.5** | the commonest score, and the commonest ceiling |
| Volume 3 | B2 | **6.0** | most undergraduate entry |
| Volume 4 | B2 | **6.5** | most postgraduate entry — **the first money book** |
| Volume 5 | B2+/C1 | **7.0** | professional registration, PR points — **the second** |
| Volume 6 | C1 | **7.5–8.0** | the top of what preparation can move |

Volumes 4 and 5 are the two books most candidates will actually buy, because
6.5 and 7.0 are the thresholds written into university and immigration
requirements. They should be written first (section 8).

**The 6.5 problem is this series' Volume 6 problem again.** The overall band is
an average, so a candidate at 7.5 reading and 5.5 writing scores 6.5, and
writing is almost always the one holding them down. Every graded volume gets
the band-arithmetic page that Volume 6 of the TOEFL series carries, from
Volume 3 on rather than at the end — because at IELTS the arithmetic bites a
band and a half lower.

## 3. The technique, and how the books prove it

The course teaches one reading method and teaches it as a method, not a tip.

### 3.1 The three moves

1. **Orient.** Title, any subheadings, the first paragraph. Thirty seconds.
   You are buying a map, not meaning.
2. **Load the questions.** Read *all* the questions for that passage before
   reading the body. Predict what each answer will **look like** — a date, a
   proper name, a number with a unit, a noun phrase of two or three words.
3. **The paragraph loop.** Read one paragraph properly. Then go back over the
   questions still open and close any that paragraph answered, scanning back
   into it if you need the exact words. Then the next paragraph.

### 3.2 Why it beats skim-then-scan — four mechanisms, each one teachable

The course never asserts the method is better. It gives the reason, and then
builds an exercise that makes the reason visible.

1. **Question order matches text order.** Most IELTS reading types — True/False/
   Not Given, Yes/No/Not Given, sentence completion, summary completion,
   multiple choice — are set in passage order. A single forward pass therefore
   meets the answers in the order you need them. Skim-then-scan throws that
   free ordering away and then pays to rediscover it.
2. **One careful pass instead of a dozen bad ones.** Traditional practice is
   one gist pass that retains little, then one hunting pass per question —
   thirteen or fourteen passes over a 900-word text. The loop is one real
   reading plus short look-backs.
3. **Scanning for keywords fails on paraphrase, and IELTS paraphrases on
   purpose.** The answer sentence rarely contains the question's words; that
   is the test's main instrument of difficulty. Reading a paragraph for
   meaning catches a paraphrase. Hunting for a word cannot.
4. **Not Given is a claim about absence, and you cannot scan for an absence.**
   Failing to find something is not the same as establishing it is not there.
   Only having read the paragraph can you say the text does not commit. This
   is the single commonest lost mark on the paper, and it is unreachable by
   scanning in principle, not just in practice.

### 3.3 Where the method needs adapting — stated, not hidden

A method taught without its limits gets abandoned the first time it fails.
Three types need a declared variant, and each gets its own lesson:

* **Matching Headings** is not in passage order and cannot be predicted. Run
  the loop, but close headings *after* each paragraph while the gist is fresh,
  and accept that two will stay open until the end.
* **Matching Information** ("which paragraph contains…") is not in passage
  order either, and is the one type where questions must stay live across the
  whole loop. Expect to close most of them late; that is the type working as
  designed, not the method failing.
* **True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given**: predict the *form* of the
  answer, never the *content*. Predicting content manufactures the confirmation
  bias that turns Not Given into True. This carve-out is important enough to
  be a named lesson, not a footnote.

### 3.4 The four exercise families that prove it

| Family | What the student does | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| **Two routes, one passage** | Two matched passages, a stopwatch, the old way on one and the loop on the other. Both times and both scores recorded in the book. | The payback is real and the student measured it |
| **The keyword trap** | Questions whose keywords sit in a paragraph that does *not* hold the answer, while the answer is paraphrased elsewhere | Scanning lands on the trap; reading does not |
| **Where did you look?** | Mark the paragraph each answer came from; the key gives the right one | Exposes answers taken from the wrong place that happened to be right |
| **The re-read counter** | Tally every return to the text; the tally goes in the unit review | The count falls across a volume. The proof is the student's own data |

**One rule on the keyword traps, and it is not negotiable.** Every trap must be
one the real test actually sets, and the answer key must name the pattern it
mirrors. A rigged demonstration teaches a student to distrust the book, and
they are right to. The real paper supplies more than enough honest traps.

### 3.5 The method has to work on screen

The core format is unchanged for 2026, but delivery is moving to computer,
with paper being withdrawn at different times in different markets. That
matters here specifically: on screen there are no margins to annotate, the
Listening transfer window drops from ten minutes to a two-minute check, and
the tools available are a highlighter and a notes function. So the loop is
taught in its on-screen form from Volume 1 — highlight the answer sentence,
never the question's keywords — with the paper variant given as the fallback.
**Before any content is written, the current format and timings must be
confirmed against ielts.org for the target market**, because the secondary
sources disagree on the phase-out and some claim content changes that no
official source states.

## 4. The unit, cycle by cycle

Sixteen parts, five cycles a skill, the same architecture the TOEFL series
ships — with the task names swapped for IELTS ones and one strand added.

| Cycle | Reading | Listening | Writing | Speaking |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | GT Section 1 text: notice, advert, timetable | Part 1: social conversation, 2 speakers | **GT Task 1**: a letter | Part 1: interview |
| 2 | GT Section 1–2 text: workplace document | Part 2: social monologue | **GT Task 1**: a second register | Part 1 + Part 2 cue card |
| 3 | GT Section 2 text: staff handbook, training | Part 3: academic discussion, up to 4 speakers | Task 2 under 250 words | Part 2: the long turn |
| 4 | **Academic passage / GT Section 3** — the claim the field rests on | Part 4: academic lecture | **Academic Task 1**: data, process or map | Part 3: discussion |
| 5 | **Academic passage** — the objection to cycle 4 | Part 4: a second lecture | **Task 2** at full length | Part 3: the harder turn |

Added strand, in every unit: **Active Reading**, one lesson on one move of the
method or one of its carve-outs, plus the unit's exercise from the four
families in 3.4.

Passage lengths, which are *not* the TOEFL ones — Academic Reading runs
longer than TOEFL's, and the whole paper is 40 questions in 60 minutes with no
separate transfer time:

| | Foundation 1 | Foundation 2 | V1–V2 | V3–V4 | V5–V6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GT-style texts (cycles 1–3) | 90–140 | 140–200 | 200–280 | 280–360 | 340–420 |
| Academic passage (cycles 4–5) | 180–240 | 240–320 | 420–560 | 620–780 | 800–950 |
| Listening script, Part 4 | 180–240 | 240–300 | 300–400 | 420–520 | 520–620 |

Volume 6's academic passages reach 950 words because the real paper's third
passage does, and reading speed is built above the distance you race.

## 5. Question types: the coverage that has to be gated

The eleven Reading types and the Listening types are the syllabus. Each one is
**taught in a named lesson before it is ever tested**, and a gate enforces it.

**Reading** — multiple choice · True/False/Not Given · Yes/No/Not Given ·
matching information · matching headings · matching features · matching
sentence endings · sentence completion · summary, note, table and flow-chart
completion · diagram labelling · short answer.

**Listening** — multiple choice · matching · plan, map and diagram labelling ·
form, note, table and flow-chart completion · sentence completion · short
answer.

**Writing Task 2** — opinion · discuss both views · problem and solution ·
advantages and disadvantages · two-part question.
**Academic Task 1** — line graph · bar chart · pie chart · table · process ·
map · multiple charts.
**GT Task 1** — formal · semi-formal · informal letter.

Spread across the ladder: Foundation 1 and 2 meet the gentler types only
(completion, short answer, matching); Volume 1 adds True/False/Not Given;
matching headings and matching information — the two that break the method's
default shape — arrive in Volume 2 with their carve-out lessons; Yes/No/Not
Given, the writer's-view type, waits for Volume 3 where stance vocabulary is
taught.

## 6. What clones, what is new

**Clones unchanged** — the expensive, proven part:

* `art.py`, `artbase.py`, `docxw.py` and `dejavu_metrics.json`: the exact font
  metrics and the text-fitting layer, which is what makes gate 6 possible.
* The gate machinery itself: gates 1, 2, 3, 6, 8 and 9 are structural and
  measure CEFR, not TOEFL.
* **The gate 9 density windows transfer verbatim.** They are anchored to CEFR
  levels, and the hardest calibration in the whole project is therefore
  already done. A B2 IELTS passage and a B2 TOEFL passage are the same prose.
* The five-cycle unit, the no-repeat vocabulary ledger, the seam measurement,
  the figure families for notices, emails, posters, boards and discussion
  panels.

**New, and to be written before any unit:**

* `checktest.py` for the IELTS shape: 40 Reading items across 3 passages (both
  Academic and GT variants), 40 Listening across 4 parts, 2 Writing tasks, a
  3-part Speaking test.
* **A question-type coverage gate** — the IELTS analogue of the handoff gate.
  It fails a volume if any type is tested before the lesson that teaches it,
  or if any type falls below its minimum count.
* **A technique gate** — every unit carries one Active Reading lesson and one
  exercise from the four families; every volume carries at least one timed
  two-routes comparison.
* IELTS frames: the cue card, the letter plan, the graph/process/map figures,
  the 0–9 band descriptor tables, the answer-sheet layout.
* Band descriptors for all four skills on the public IELTS criteria (Task
  Achievement, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and
  Accuracy; Fluency, Pronunciation). Unlike TOEFL 2026, IELTS **publishes** its
  band descriptors, so these can follow the official criteria rather than being
  the course's own — which is a real advantage and should be said on the page.
* A raw-score-to-band conversion for Reading and Listening. Approximate
  conversions are widely published and stable (roughly 30/40 for band 7 in
  Academic Reading, 23/40 for band 6, with GT Reading needing more marks for
  the same band). The page must say these are approximate and vary by paper.

## 7. The gates, restated for IELTS

| Gate | What it proves |
|---|---|
| 1 structural | every unit has every block, at the right item counts |
| 2 vocabulary | head words taught once across all eight books, untaught words capped per block |
| 3 containment | no volume teaches a structure a later volume owns |
| 4 handoff | every graded structure has a named prerequisite in the Foundation books |
| 5 test shape | 40 + 40 + 2 + 3 parts, in both modules |
| 6 no clipped text | every fitted run inside its box, measured |
| 7 readiness | a Foundation 2 leaver meets ≤ 12 unknown word types in a Volume 1 passage |
| 8 zero omissions | every passage, script, model and explanation present in the .docx |
| 9 density | the four profile numbers inside each volume's CEFR window |
| **10 type coverage** | **no question type tested before it is taught; minimum counts met** |
| **11 technique** | **every unit carries its Active Reading lesson and exercise** |

Gates 10 and 11 are written **before** the content, for the same reason gate 9
was: gate 9 failed Volumes 3 and 4 on the day it was written, and that failure
is what drove the work that fixed them.

## 8. Sequence, and an honest word on scale

The TOEFL series is eight books, 1.09 million body words and 3,752 figures.
The IELTS clone is the same order of work or slightly more, because the
academic passages are longer. It is not a weekend's adaptation, and anyone
planning it as one will produce the thing this project spent its last month
fixing: a book whose cover claims a level its prose does not have.

The order that keeps the risk at the front:

1. **The machinery and the gates.** Shared layer extracted, the IELTS test
   shape written, gates 10 and 11 written. All nine existing gates re-run on
   the TOEFL series afterwards to prove the extraction broke nothing — they
   exist precisely to make that safe.
2. **One complete vertical slice: Volume 4, Unit 1.** The hardest common case —
   band 6.5, both modules, five cycles, the technique strand, both Writing
   Task 1 forks. Build it, run every gate, print it, read it. Decide the shape
   is right while one unit is cheap to rewrite.
3. **Volume 4, then Volume 5.** The two books the market actually buys.
4. **Volumes 3 and 6**, outward from there.
5. **Volumes 1 and 2.**
6. **Foundation 1 and 2**, written last and written against measurement, the
   way Foundation 2 was: scan the finished Volume 1 for every word a Foundation
   leaver will not know, and teach exactly those.

Writing the Foundation books last is not a scheduling convenience. It is the
only way they can be built from a measured gap rather than a guess, and that
measurement is what made gate 7 pass at a mean of 1.5 unknown types.

## 9. Open questions worth settling before step 1

1. **Market and format.** Which country's candidates, and are they sitting on
   computer or paper? This changes the technique chapter and the timings
   printed in every test.
2. **One book or two covers?** This plan assumes one book per volume carrying
   both modules. The alternative — one shared book plus two thin module
   supplements for Writing Task 1 and the reading texts — is cheaper to print
   and worse to study from. I would hold the single book.
3. **Band descriptors.** IELTS publishes its criteria. Using them verbatim is
   defensible and better than inventing; it also means the writing models must
   be built to score against them, which is a constraint on authoring, not a
   freebie.
4. **Volume 6's ceiling.** 7.5–8.0 is the top of what preparation moves.
   Above that the limit is the candidate's English, not their technique, and
   the book should say so as plainly as Foundation 1 says it does not prepare
   you to sit the test.
