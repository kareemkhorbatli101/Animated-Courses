# IELTS Preparation Course — the plan for an eight-book series
## Academic and General Training in one book, built on the TOEFL series' machinery

*Written against the delivered TOEFL iBT 2026 series: eight volumes, sixty
units, 1,085,425 body words, 3,752 figures, nine gates. This plan says what
clones, what changes, and what has to be written before any content is.*

---

## 1. The decision that shapes everything: one book for both modules

Academic and General Training are not two tests. They are one test with two
substitutions:

| | Academic | General Training | Shared? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listening | 4 parts, 40 items | identical | **100%** |
| Speaking | 3 parts, 11–14 min | identical | **100%** |
| Reading **question types** | the same eleven | the same eleven | **100%** |
| Reading **texts** | 3 long passages | 5 shorter texts in 3 sections, the last one long | differs |
| Writing Task 2 | 250-word essay | same task, plainer prompts | ~90% |
| Writing Task 1 | describe data, a process or a map | write a letter | **differs entirely** |

So roughly **85% of the course is common**, and the only part that genuinely
forks is Writing Task 1. A combined book is not a compromise: it is the
architecture the test already has.

The better news is that the five-cycle unit this series already runs maps onto
that fork without being redesigned. Cycles 1 to 3 take an everyday sub-topic
and cycles 4 and 5 take an academic field — which is exactly the General
Training / Academic seam:

* **Cycles 1–3** — notices, advertisements, timetables, workplace documents,
  the everyday register. This is General Training Reading Sections 1 and 2,
  and it is where the **letter** (GT Task 1) is taught.
* **Cycles 4–5** — the long passage, where cycle 4 states the claim a field
  rests on and cycle 5 is the objection to it. This is Academic Reading, GT
  Reading Section 3 (which is also a long text), and where **data description**
  (Academic Task 1) and Task 2 are taught.

Every student does all five cycles. A General Training candidate still needs
the long-passage skill, because GT Section 3 is a long text and carries the
hardest questions on that paper. An Academic candidate still needs the
everyday register, because Listening Parts 1 and 2 are entirely in it. Only
Writing Task 1 is marked **A** or **GT** and done by one of the two.

## 2. The ladder: eight books, half a band each

IELTS reports 0–9 in half bands. The overall band is the mean of the four
section bands rounded to the nearest half (a .25 rounds up to .5, a .75 up to
the next whole band). That gives a cleaner ladder than the TOEFL 1–6 scale
did, and the eight books fall out of it naturally.

| Book | CEFR | IELTS target | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1 | A1–A2 | **below 4.0** | not test-ready, and says so on its own cover |
| Foundation 2 | A2–B1 | **4.0–4.5** | the bridge into the graded course |
| Volume 1 | B1 | **5.0** | first sitting |
| Volume 2 | B1+ | **5.5** | the commonest score, and the commonest ceiling |
| Volume 3 | B2 | **6.0** | most undergraduate entry |
| Volume 4 | B2 | **6.5** | most postgraduate entry — **the first money book** |
| Volume 5 | B2+/C1 | **7.0** | professional registration, PR points — **the second** |
| Volume 6 | C1 | **7.5–8.0** | the top of what preparation can move |

Volumes 4 and 5 are the two books most candidates will actually buy, because
6.5 and 7.0 are the thresholds written into university and immigration
requirements. They should be written first (section 8).

**The 6.5 problem is this series' Volume 6 problem again.** The overall band is
an average, so a candidate at 7.5 reading and 5.5 writing scores 6.5, and
writing is almost always the one holding them down. Every graded volume gets
the band-arithmetic page that Volume 6 of the TOEFL series carries, from
Volume 3 on rather than at the end — because at IELTS the arithmetic bites a
band and a half lower.

## 3. The technique, and how the books prove it

The course teaches one reading method and teaches it as a method, not a tip.

### 3.1 The three moves

1. **Orient.** Title, any subheadings, the first paragraph. Thirty seconds.
   You are buying a map, not meaning.
2. **Load the questions.** Read *all* the questions for that passage before
   reading the body. Predict what each answer will **look like** — a date, a
   proper name, a number with a unit, a noun phrase of two or three words —
   and mark the words in each *question* that carry it.
3. **The paragraph loop.** Read one paragraph properly. Then go back over the
   questions still open and close any that paragraph answered, looking back
   into it if you need the exact words. Then the next paragraph.

Three mechanics sit inside move 3 and are taught as part of it, not as tips:

* **Bump into an answer, underline it, keep reading.** Do not stop mid-
  paragraph to answer. Mark it and close it when the paragraph ends. The
  paragraph is the unit of comprehension; breaking it to answer costs the
  thread.
* **A long paragraph is two paragraphs.** The loop works because one paragraph
  fits in working memory. When it does not — 150 words and up — split it at
  its own turn and run the loop twice. This is a judgement the books teach
  explicitly, because an unsplit long paragraph is where the method fails.
* **The marks on a question are a memory aid, not a search key.** This is the
  distinction the standard advice gets backwards. Marked words let you
  re-read a question in two seconds instead of ten when you check it after
  each paragraph. They are *not* strings to hunt for in the text. Teaching
  highlighting as a search key is what produces the keyword trap in 3.4.

### 3.2 Why it beats skim-then-scan — four mechanisms, each one teachable

The course never asserts the method is better. It gives the reason, and then
builds an exercise that makes the reason visible.

1. **Question order matches text order.** Most IELTS reading types — True/False/
   Not Given, Yes/No/Not Given, sentence completion, summary completion,
   multiple choice — are set in passage order. A single forward pass therefore
   meets the answers in the order you need them. Skim-then-scan throws that
   free ordering away and then pays to rediscover it.
2. **One careful pass instead of a dozen bad ones.** Traditional practice is
   one gist pass that retains little, then one hunting pass per question —
   thirteen or fourteen passes over a 900-word text. The loop is one real
   reading plus short look-backs.
3. **Scanning for keywords fails on paraphrase, and IELTS paraphrases on
   purpose.** The answer sentence rarely contains the question's words; that
   is the test's main instrument of difficulty. Reading a paragraph for
   meaning catches a paraphrase. Hunting for a word cannot.
4. **Not Given is a claim about absence, and you cannot scan for an absence.**
   Failing to find something is not the same as establishing it is not there.
   Only having read the paragraph can you say the text does not commit. This
   is the single commonest lost mark on the paper, and it is unreachable by
   scanning in principle, not just in practice.

### 3.3 One loop, four variants — the type-specific lessons

A method taught without its variants gets abandoned the first time it fails.
The three moves never change. What changes is what move 2 predicts and what
move 3 does at the end of a paragraph, and each variant is a named lesson.

**Matching Headings — two parts, one at each end of the loop.**

*Part 1, inside move 2.* Read each heading and predict **what a paragraph
under it would have to contain**. Not which paragraph it is — you have not read
them. You are loading expectations so that a paragraph arrives recognised
instead of cold. It is nearly free, because the headings are being read
anyway.

*Part 2, inside move 3.* At the end of each paragraph, **name it yourself
before you look at the list**: summarise what it said, find the main idea, put
a heading on it. Only then go to the options and match.

Part 2 carries the weight, and the mechanism is anchoring. The failure mode of
Matching Headings is capture by an option that repeats a word from the
paragraph, and **you cannot be captured by an option you have not read yet**.
Having committed to your own gist you are comparing two summaries, which is a
judgement a student can make, instead of scanning options for familiarity,
which is a judgement they cannot.

**The risk the lesson must name.** Part 1 primes you with the examiner's
wording and Part 2 depends on being independent of it. A student who does Part
1 too hard will "generate" a heading in option iv's exact words and think they
have summarised when they have remembered. So the rule on the page: *your
heading comes out in your own words; if it comes out in the paper's words,
that is a signal to check, not an answer.* Say it again differently, then
match. Without that line the two parts quietly cancel each other.

**Matching Features — the quoted-claim types** (which researcher said X,
which study found Y). In move 2, read the claims and **understand them**.
Ignore the names entirely; do not go looking for them. In move 3, when a
paragraph has somebody asserting something, just understand what was
asserted. Then when you check the open questions and recognise an idea, go
back for the name and write it down.

The reason is that the test paraphrases the **claim** and prints the **name**
unchanged. Names are the easy half and they wait for you; the claim is the half
being tested.

It is tempting to answer that scanning for capitalised words is at least fast.
It is, and the speed buys nothing, because **a search key that matches
everything has no discriminating power**. Every name on the list is in the
passage by construction, so finding one narrows nothing — you must visit all of
them anyway. The scan returns a pointer, not an answer, and at that pointer the
whole job is still waiting: understand the claim, then compare it against every
remaining statement rather than one. Two further costs follow. You are reading
the claim **in a fragment**, stripped of what frames it — whether it is being
reported, endorsed, or set up to be knocked down, which is precisely what these
tasks and Yes/No/Not Given are built on. And claims attribute **by anaphora**:
a researcher is named once and then carries three sentences of "she went on to
show", "his data suggested". Scanning finds the anchor and misses the claims
hanging off it. Only reading tracks the pronouns.

So the rule the books teach is narrower and more useful than "scanning is bad":
**scanning earns its keep only when the key is rare in the text and unique to
one question** — a year, a unit of measurement, an unusual technical term in a
completion question. A name list in a matching task fails both halves of that
test.

**Matching Information** ("which paragraph contains…") is not in passage
order and cannot be predicted at all. These questions stay live across the
whole loop and most close late. That is the type working as designed, not the
method failing, and the book says so in advance so that a student does not
read their own progress as failure.

**True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given.** Predict the *form* of the
answer, never the *content*. Predicting content manufactures the confirmation
bias that turns Not Given into True, which is the commonest lost mark on the
paper.

**Everything else** — completion, short answer, multiple choice, matching
sentence endings — runs the plain loop, in passage order, and is where the
method pays most.

### 3.4 The four exercise families that prove it

| Family | What the student does | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| **Two routes, one passage** | Two matched passages, a stopwatch, the old way on one and the loop on the other. Both times and both scores recorded in the book. | The payback is real and the student measured it |
| **The keyword trap** | Questions whose keywords sit in a paragraph that does *not* hold the answer, while the answer is paraphrased elsewhere | Scanning lands on the trap; reading does not |
| **Where did you look?** | Mark the paragraph each answer came from; the key gives the right one | Exposes answers taken from the wrong place that happened to be right |
| **The re-read counter** | Tally every return to the text; the tally goes in the unit review | The count falls across a volume. The proof is the student's own data |

**One rule on the keyword traps, and it is not negotiable.** Every trap must be
one the real test actually sets, and the answer key must name the pattern it
mirrors. A rigged demonstration teaches a student to distrust the book, and
they are right to. The real paper supplies more than enough honest traps.

### 3.5 The arithmetic, which is the strongest claim the course can make

The method states its own cost. Academic Reading is three passages, about
2,150 to 2,750 words in all, 40 questions, 60 minutes, no transfer time.

The fixed cost of the method is **7.5 minutes**: thirty seconds orienting each
passage, two minutes loading each passage's questions. Everything else is one
pass through the text plus look-backs.

| Reading speed | Reading the text | Left for 40 questions | Per question |
|---|---|---|---|
| 80 wpm | 27–34 min | 18–26 min | 27–38 s |
| **100 wpm** | 21–28 min | **25–31 min** | **38–46 s** |
| 120 wpm | 18–23 min | 30–35 min | 44–52 s |
| 150 wpm | 14–18 min | 34–38 min | 51–57 s |
| 200 wpm | 11–14 min | 39–42 min | 58–63 s |

Three things follow, and all three are load-bearing for the series.

1. **100 words a minute is enough.** On the longest paper it still leaves 38
   seconds a question *after* the paragraph holding the answer has been read.
   The method does not need a fast reader.
2. **It survives at 80.** Which means it can be taught in Foundation 2, at the
   bottom of the ladder, rather than being a technique students graduate into.
   The loop is the constant across all eight books; speed is the variable.
3. **Speed buys decision time, not finishing time.** At 200 wpm the candidate
   is not finishing early, they are spending a full minute a question on Not
   Given decisions and matching information. That is the right thing to tell a
   Volume 6 student, and the wrong thing to let them discover for themselves.

**On the comparison with the standard advice, be precise and be fair.** The
honest claim is not that skim-then-scan is slower on average — that depends on
parameters nobody has measured. It is that **the loop's cost is bounded and
computable while the hunt's is neither**. Reading the text once has a known
price. A hunt that fails has no price, because nothing says when to stop, and
a failed hunt is followed by another. The fat tail is what strands a candidate
on passage three, and it cannot be planned around because it cannot be
costed.

So the course's claim is: *this is the method that can tell you what it costs
before you sit down.* That is provable, it is on the page in the table above,
and it does not require any assertion about why the usual advice is taught the
way it is. A course that says "the standard method has no stated reading speed
and therefore no time budget you can plan with" is making a claim it can
defend. One that speculates about the reasons is picking a fight it does not
need and cannot win.

### 3.6 Reading speed as a syllabus, not a by-product

Reading speed in a second language is mostly not a reading skill. It is
lexical access — how fast a word is recognised — plus parsing that has become
automatic, minus the regressions that unknown words force. For a B1 reader the
dominant term by far is **vocabulary**: one unknown word in a sentence costs a
regression, and regressions are where the minutes go.

The series already owns the instrument for this. **Gate 7 measures unknown
word types per passage**, and gate 2 caps untaught words per block at twelve.
Those gates were built to make the books readable; they are also, unchanged,
the mechanism that makes speed-building possible. Keep unknown types low and
speed rises on its own.

On top of that, four things a book can actually do:

* **Timed passage in every unit.** One fixed-position passage, words counted
  and printed, the student records seconds and converts. The number goes in
  the unit review next to the re-read counter.
* **Repeated reading.** The same passage read three times, each timed. Within-
  session gains are large and part of it transfers. It is the best-evidenced
  fluency exercise there is and costs one page.
* **Chunking.** The early volumes print some passages pre-broken into sense
  groups, then stop. Word-by-word reading is the habit being broken.
* **A page on what to read between units.** Extensive reading drives L2
  reading speed more than any exercise. A book cannot contain it but it can
  prescribe it, with a list pitched at each volume's level.

**The ladder**, measured on each volume's own academic prose:

| Book | Target | Band it serves |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1 | 60–80 wpm | — |
| Foundation 2 | 80–100 | 4.0–4.5 |
| Volume 1 | 100–120 | 5.0 |
| Volume 2 | 110–130 | 5.5 |
| Volume 3 | 130–150 | 6.0 |
| Volume 4 | 150–170 | 6.5 |
| Volume 5 | 170–190 | 7.0 |
| Volume 6 | **190–210** | 7.5–8.0 |

**The subtlety that has to be printed next to the chart.** Speed is measured on
that volume's own prose, and gate 9 makes each volume's prose denser than the
last. A student holding 130 wpm from Volume 3 into Volume 4 has got faster,
because the words got harder. Without that sentence on the page, a flat line
reads as a plateau and the student quits the exercise that was working. Every
speed figure in the books is printed with the density of the text it was
measured on.

### 3.7 Pacing: the number students are usually given is the wrong number

The standard figure is twenty minutes a passage, about thirteen questions, so
"roughly ninety seconds a question". That is a **gross** figure and it
misleads, because it silently includes reading time not yet spent. A student
pacing against ninety seconds spends it on question 1 and finds out at
question 9.

This method can state the **net** figure, which is the one a student can
actually pace against. Fixed cost a passage is about 2.5 minutes — thirty
seconds orienting, about nine seconds a question to read and mark thirteen.
What is left after the reading is done:

| Reading speed | Reading 880 words | Left for 13 questions | **Net per question** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 80 wpm | 11.0 min | 6.5 min | **30 s** |
| 100 wpm | 8.8 | 8.8 | **40 s** |
| 140 wpm | 6.3 | 11.3 | **52 s** |
| 180 wpm | 4.9 | 12.7 | **58 s** |
| 200 wpm | 4.4 | 13.1 | **61 s** |

**Teach 40 to 60 seconds a question, and say the word *net*.**

**Two clocks, and the exam clock never squeezes.** A Volume 1 student at about
110 wpm attempting a real full-length passage needs roughly twenty-five
minutes once realistic decision time is allowed. The exam gives twenty. That
ten-minute gap is the course, and it closes because the passage grows and the
re-reads fall, not because anybody is told to hurry. So:

* **Teaching exercises derive their allowance, never pick it.** Allowance =
  orient + (questions x 9 s) + words / that volume's target wpm + questions x
  decision time. It lands near 7 minutes in Foundation 1 and 13 in Volume 6,
  and every exercise prints the word count and the speed it assumed. That is
  gate 12 doing its work.
* **Test sections always use the real twenty minutes a passage**, from Volume
  3 on, once passages are near full length.

By Volume 6 a student finishes a full exam passage in about thirteen derived
minutes against twenty allowed. The seven-minute cushion is where Not Given
decisions get made.

### 3.8 Passage length and paragraph count

The design rule to defend hardest: **hold paragraph length constant and grow
the paragraph count.**

The loop works because one paragraph fits in working memory. Grow the
paragraphs and the method breaks; grow the count and stamina builds without
touching it. Real IELTS paragraphs run about 100 to 130 words, seven to nine of
them — which is already the right size for the loop, so the target is the right
shape to aim at.

| Book | Paragraphs | Each | Passage | Questions | Derived allowance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1 | 3 | ~70 | 210 | 5 | 7 min |
| Foundation 2 | 4 | ~75 | 300 | 6 | 8 min |
| Volume 1 | 5 | ~95 | 480 | 8 | 10 min |
| Volume 2 | 5 | ~108 | 540 | 9 | 11 min |
| Volume 3 | 6 | ~115 | 700 | 10 | 12 min |
| Volume 4 | 7 | ~110 | 770 | 11 | 12.5 min |
| Volume 5 | 8 | ~108 | 860 | 13 | 13.5 min |
| Volume 6 | 8 | ~115 | 920 | 13 | 13 min |

Volume 6 sits a little above the exam's typical passage so that the real thing
feels short — the principle the TOEFL series used for its own reading lengths.

Two consequences. **Paragraph count decides which question types can be set**:
matching headings and matching information need lettered paragraphs and enough
of them. A five-paragraph Volume 1 passage supports a five-heading task with
seven or eight options, which is a legitimate scaled version; matching
information does not work below six paragraphs and should not be forced in
earlier. And **splitting a long paragraph becomes training rather than
routine** — if the course's own passages hold 100 to 130 words, students meet
the 150-word paragraph as the exception the real paper occasionally throws,
which is exactly when the move is needed.

### 3.9 The method has to work on screen

The core format is unchanged for 2026, but delivery is moving to computer,
with paper being withdrawn at different times in different markets. That
matters here specifically: on screen there are no margins to annotate, the
Listening transfer window drops from ten minutes to a two-minute check, and
the tools available are a highlighter and a notes function. So the loop is
taught in its on-screen form from Volume 1 — highlight the answer sentence,
never the question's keywords — with the paper variant given as the fallback.
**Before any content is written, the current format and timings must be
confirmed against ielts.org for the target market**, because the secondary
sources disagree on the phase-out and some claim content changes that no
official source states.

## 4. The same loop in Listening — and why Listening teaches it first

The method is not a reading method that happens to be useful elsewhere. It is
the same three moves, and in Listening the test **forces** them:

| | Reading | Listening |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Orient | title, subheadings, intro | the section's context line, the form, the map, who is speaking |
| 2. Load and predict | read all the questions; predict the **form** of each answer | read that section's questions; predict form **and grammar** |
| 3. The loop | one paragraph, then close what it answered | one chunk of audio, then the next — closing as you go |

**Listening is the purer case, so teach it first.** You cannot scan audio. The
questions are always in passage order, the recording plays once, and there is
no going back. A student therefore has no choice but to run the loop, and when
it works they have an intuition they already trust. Reading is then introduced
as *the same problem with the text standing still, which only makes it easier*
— and that reframing is worth more than any argument about skimming.

This inverts the usual teaching order and it is deliberate. It also means the
method is established in Foundation 2, where Listening is already being taught,
before a student has formed the scan-and-hunt habit in Reading that has to be
broken later.

### 4.1 Prediction is richer in Listening

In Reading you predict the form of the answer. In Listening you predict more,
because the answer has to fit a gap in a sentence you can already see:

* **Word class and grammar** — a noun or a verb, singular or plural, with or
  without an article, past or present.
* **Category** — a number, a date, a time, a price, a name that will be spelt
  out, a place.
* **Range** — a price in a form about a gym is £20 to £60 a month, not £4,000.
  Hearing "forty" when the gap wants a price makes "£40" the safe reading.
* **The word limit** — "no more than two words" is itself a prediction, and it
  tells you the answer is a short noun phrase, not a clause.

### 4.2 The four Listening-only moves

1. **Look ahead.** While you write the answer to question 5, your eyes are on
   question 6. The recording does not wait. This is the single most trainable
   listening habit and it needs its own exercise family.
2. **Guess from context.** The answer word may be one a student does not know.
   What carries it is the frame around it — the sentence it sits in, the
   preposition before it, the examples after it. Guessing from context is a
   taught skill with its own exercises, not a hope.
3. **Recover from a miss.** The fatal error is chasing a lost answer and losing
   the next three. The drill is explicit: mark it, drop it, catch the next
   question's signpost, come back at the end. Every practice section should
   include at least one answer that is genuinely easy to miss, so the recovery
   is rehearsed and not just described.
4. **Hear the correction.** "It's on Tuesday — sorry, no, Thursday." "The
   deposit is fifty pounds, which includes the key." The test's favourite
   instrument is the self-correction, the qualification and the distractor that
   answers a question nobody asked. These are patterns, they are enumerable,
   and they are taught as patterns.

Signposting is the connective tissue for all four: *right, moving on to* ·
*before I forget* · *the main thing to remember* · *actually* · *having said
that*. A student who hears the signpost knows a change is coming and is already
looking at the next question.

### 4.3 Listening grows too

The same principle as reading: the work grows, the clock does not squeeze.

| Book | Part 1 | Part 4 monologue | Speakers in Part 3 | Items a section |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1 | 120–160 words | 150–200 | 2 | 5 |
| Foundation 2 | 160–220 | 200–260 | 2 | 6 |
| Volume 1 | 220–300 | 260–340 | 2–3 | 8 |
| Volume 2 | 280–360 | 320–400 | 3 | 9 |
| Volume 3 | 340–420 | 400–480 | 3 | 10 |
| Volume 4 | 380–460 | 460–540 | 3–4 | 10 |
| Volume 5 | 420–500 | 520–600 | 4 | 10 |
| Volume 6 | 450–540 | 560–650 | 4 | 10 |

Two things grow besides length: **the number of speakers** (Part 3 reaches
four, and telling four voices apart is its own skill), and **the density of
distractors**. A Foundation recording says the answer once, clearly. A Volume 6
recording says a wrong answer first, corrects it, and qualifies the right one.

## 5. Main idea and supporting detail — the spine that runs through all four skills

This is the strongest cross-skill thread in the design and it should be named
on the page, because students do not notice it by themselves.

**The same single judgement is tested in all four sections:**

* **Reading** — Matching Headings *is* "what is the main idea of this
  paragraph". So is the summary-completion task, and so is every "the writer's
  main point in paragraph C is…" multiple choice.
* **Listening** — note and table completion is a main-idea-versus-detail
  judgement made in real time: the recording gives both and the gap wants one
  of them.
* **Writing** — an outline is main ideas with their supporting details. Task 2
  body paragraphs are a topic sentence plus support; Task 1 is an Overall
  statement plus topic sentences plus figures.
* **Speaking** — Part 2 is one minute to build a main-idea outline. Part 3 is
  an answer that has a point and support for it rather than a list.

**Matching Headings is outlining in reverse.** Writing an outline produces
exactly what that task asks a student to recognise. Saying this out loud, and
building the exercises so that a student does both directions on the same text
in the same unit, is most of what makes the thread real rather than asserted.

### 5.1 Choosing main ideas: the six tests

Students are told to "brainstorm" and then left to it. The judgement that
actually matters is which of the brainstormed ideas become body paragraphs, and
it has criteria:

1. **Distinct** — no two overlap. If two can be merged, they were one idea.
2. **Roughly equal weight** — none is a sub-point of another. They sit at the
   same level of generality.
3. **Each answers the question**, not the topic. The commonest essay failure is
   three good paragraphs about the subject that never answer what was asked.
4. **Together they cover enough** that no obvious branch is missing.
5. **Each is developable** — you have three or four sentences of real support
   for it. An idea you cannot support is not a body paragraph, however good it
   sounds.
6. **Ordered on purpose** — strongest last, or chronological, or whatever the
   chosen structure requires.

### 5.2 The outlining exercise families

Each appears in every unit from Foundation 2 on, at that volume's level:

| Exercise | What the student does |
|---|---|
| **Fill the outline** | Given a thesis and eight to twelve sentences, sort them into main ideas and the details that sit under them |
| **Spot the intruder** | One sentence belongs under no main idea |
| **Spot the overlap** | Two proposed main ideas are the same idea in different words — merge them |
| **Spot the unequal** | One proposed main idea is a detail of another — demote it |
| **Outline to essay** | Write from a given outline, so the plan is visibly doing work |
| **Essay to outline** | Reverse-engineer a model's plan. This is the reading skill wearing writing clothes |
| **Passage to outline** | Outline a reading passage from the unit — the single exercise that makes the thread explicit |
| **The one-minute outline** | Speaking Part 2 cue card, against a clock |

The last three are the ones that make the series different from a book that
teaches four skills in four silos.

## 6. Writing

### 6.1 Task 2: eleven structures, chosen by intent rather than by wording

The structures are organised by **what the writer wants to do**, not by what
the question says. This matters more than it looks: a student who has memorised
"if the question says *discuss both views*, use structure X" is defeated by a
question phrased unusually, and produces an essay that answers the template
instead of the prompt. A student who asks *what do I want to do here* can place
any prompt.

| | The writer wants to | Shape | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | present one point of view | intro states the opinion (not optional) · BP1–3 reasons | summary, perhaps with a twist |
| 2 | present two views, one of them theirs | BP1 the opposing view · BP2 their own | summary |
| 3 | present two opposed, even extreme, views and then choose, moderate or reconcile | BP1 first position · BP2 second | **detailed — it has to prove the position** |
| 4 | give advantages and disadvantages of one choice and take a position **immediately** | BP1 advantages · BP2 "However, I believe the disadvantages are greater" | summary |
| 5 | give advantages and disadvantages and say which are greater **afterwards** | BP1 advantages · BP2 disadvantages | **the position, and why** — or "we can have the advantages without the disadvantages if…" |
| 6 | weigh **two** choices and then pick | BP1 choice 1 · BP2 choice 2 | the choice and **why**: one outright, one conditionally, or both under different conditions |
| 7 | analyse a problem | BP1 causes · BP2 effects · BP3 solutions and why they work | summary with a twist |
| 8 | present a situation and how to deal with it | BP per action, each with its reason | summary |
| 9 | state a position, raise an objection and rebut it | BP1 support · BP2 counterargument and rebuttal | summary |
| 10 | answer more than one related question | BP per question | summary |
| 11 | agree in some circumstances and disagree in others | BP1 when they agree · BP2 when they disagree | summary |

Three things in this taxonomy are worth teaching as explicit lessons because
most books miss them:

* **4 against 5 is a real choice, not a rule.** The same prompt takes either.
  Structure 4 commits in body paragraph 2 and reads as confident; structure 5
  defers to the conclusion and reads as weighed. Teaching both and letting the
  student choose is teaching rhetoric rather than a template.
* **The conditional move — "both, under different circumstances"** — appears
  in 3, 6 and 11 and is the high-band move. It is what separates an essay that
  takes a position from an essay that takes a *considered* position.
* **The conclusion is not one thing.** In most structures it is a summary,
  optionally with a twist — a hope, a wish, a piece of advice, a warning, a
  prediction. In 3, 5 and 6 it carries the argument and must be longer, because
  the position has been deferred to it. The twist taxonomy is five concrete
  endings a student can practise, which beats "restate the thesis".

### 6.2 Task 1 Academic: ten principles

These come from the attached technique and are the spine of the Task 1 strand.
They are stated as principles with a reason, and each gets a lesson:

1. **It is an essay, so give it an essay's structure**: an Overall (thesis)
   statement in the introduction, topic sentences, and a **declared basis** for
   dividing the body paragraphs.
2. **Cover everything, but summarise.** No element may be ignored; simple
   behaviour over a stretch may be compressed into one sentence, and often a
   whole line over a whole period can be.
3. **Keep comparisons balanced.** If you said a thing was first at the start,
   you may not go silent about its position later — above all when it changes.
   Balance usually costs three or four words.
4. **Write for a reader who cannot see the graph**, and the essay has to be
   useful to them.
5. **No bare numbers.** Every figure carries a description — high, low,
   relatively low, the value it started or ended at. The test of this is sharp:
   **delete every number and the essay should still read.**
6. **Do not state conclusions; steer the reader to them.** Show that DVD sales
   fell as Blu-ray appeared and rose, and the reader concludes the substitution
   themselves and feels clever. The report you are writing sits *before* the
   Conclusions and Recommendations section of a real study, which is the fact
   almost no candidate knows.
7. **Never jump from the biggest value to the smallest.** Go in order, group as
   you go, and compare how near or far each is from its neighbour. The second
   country by visitors is not less interesting than the last.
8. **A conclusion is not required.** Its substance belongs in the Overall
   statement so the reader knows from the first sentence what the data says.
   Use the space at the end instead for the comparison that would not fit the
   body paragraphs.
9. **Cohesion downward.** Every body paragraph supports the Overall statement;
   everything in a body paragraph supports its topic sentence. That is what
   makes it an essay and not a heap of bullet points.
10. **Where generic advice conflicts with this, follow this.** The technique's
    own evidence is the side-by-side: the official model answer and the
    improved one on the same graph.

Three of these convert directly into exercises that no other course has:

* **The number-deletion test** (principle 5). Hand the student a model with
  every figure removed and ask whether it still describes the graph. Then hand
  them their own essay with the figures removed.
* **The blind-reader test** (principle 4). One student reads the essay aloud;
  the other, who has not seen the visual, **sketches it**. Compare with the
  original. Nothing else exposes an under-described graph so fast.
* **The side-by-side** (principle 10). A weaker published model and a stronger
  one on the same visual, with the differences annotated, exactly as the
  attached file does for the Newport crime graph.

### 6.3 Task 1: strategic organisation, which is the part that is actually hard

Selecting and grouping is where Task 1 marks are won, and it has a rule.
**Count the data dimensions first.**

**One-dimensional visuals** — a single set of items with one measure each: a
pie chart, a single-series bar chart, a process diagram (one dimension, time),
a sequence of maps. The three moves are **order, group, compare**:

* Order the items — by size, or by sequence where the dimension is time.
* Group those that sit close together or behave alike.
* Compare neighbour to neighbour, so the reader gets the shape of the
  distribution, and never jump from top to bottom (principle 7).

*Worked example — a pie chart of household spending over six categories.*
"Housing dominates at 35%. Food and transport follow, close to one another at
around 20% each. The remaining three — clothing, leisure and other — are all
below 10% and within two points of each other." One dimension, ordered,
grouped, compared.

**Two-dimensional visuals** — the data varies along two axes at once: three
crimes across nine years; five age groups in two years; five arrest reasons by
gender. Here the move is: **choose one dimension to structure the body
paragraphs, and run the one-dimensional moves along the other dimension inside
each paragraph.**

The structuring dimension is chosen by three tests:

1. **Which dimension carries the story?** Where is there a turn, a reversal, a
   change of rank?
2. **Which gives two or three paragraphs rather than five?**
3. **Does each paragraph become a single coherent claim?**

If the chosen dimension has many values — years, usually — grouping is not
optional, and the group boundary goes **where the story turns**.

**Hybrid** — where a second visual has a different shape from the first, give
it its own treatment and join the two explicitly. A two-dimensional line graph
handled by series, then a one-dimensional bar chart handled by order and group,
joined with "On the other hand".

### 6.4 The organisation decisions, worked from the attached models

Each of these is a lesson, because each shows the rule choosing differently:

| Visual | Dimensions | Structured by | Why, and what happens inside |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newport crime, 3 crimes × 9 years | 2D | **crime** (3 BPs) | Only three items and no two share a pattern, so no grouping needed on that axis. Inside each paragraph, move along time. |
| *the same graph, the other way* | 2D | **time** (2 BPs) | Ten years is too many to list, so grouping is **compulsory** — and the boundary goes at mid-2006, where burglary and car theft swap rank. The story picks the cut. |
| Donations, 5 age groups × 2 years | 2D | **year** (2 BPs) | Five age-group paragraphs would be absurd; the story is "same shape, different peak", which is a comparison between years. Inside each, walk the age groups in order. |
| Arrests, 5 reasons × 2 genders | 2D | **gender** (2 BPs) | Inside each, order the reasons and **group**: "the remaining three — drink driving, breach of order and theft — came close together, averaging about 13%". |
| French and Mandarin, 2 languages × 20 years | 2D | **time** (2 BPs) | Only two series, yet time wins: the relationship itself changes — opposed patterns to 2005, near-identical stability after. Structuring by language would bury the turn. |
| Bee colonies + honey, 2 charts × 5 years | 2D | **time** (2 BPs) | The two series move oppositely for two decades and together for two. Again the reversal picks the cut. |
| Education level × 3 years, Bulgaria | 2D | **education level** (3 BPs) | Three by three, so either works — level wins because each level has one clean trend shape, making each paragraph a single claim. |
| Nutrients × 2 groups, plus a table | 2D + table | **nutrient, grouped by shared behaviour** | Fat alone; vegetables and fruits together because both reverse; cereals and fish together because both are equal. Then "Turning to the table". |
| UK visits (line) + top countries (bar) | 2D **+ 1D** | **hybrid** | Line graph by series, two paragraphs; then the bar chart by order and group, joined with "On the other hand". |
| Headland erosion | 1D (sequence) | **stage** | Order is given; group into phases where a process is long. |
| Stokeford, 1930 and 2010 | 1D (time) | **map** | One paragraph a map, and inside each, move through space in a fixed order so the reader can follow. |

The lesson a student takes from the table is that the rule is not "always do it
by category". **The story picks the dimension**, and the clearest sign of a turn
— a swap in rank, a reversal of direction, a change in the relationship between
two series — is where the body paragraphs should divide.

### 6.5 Task 1 General Training: the letter

The letter forks from the Academic task completely and is taught in cycles 1 to
3, where the everyday register already lives. Three registers — formal,
semi-formal, informal — and the choice is driven by who the reader is, which is
the only thing the prompt reliably tells you. The outlining spine applies
unchanged: the three bullet points in the prompt **are** the main ideas, and the
commonest failure is treating them as topics to mention rather than as
paragraphs to develop.

## 7. Speaking

### 7.1 The two-to-five rule

Every answer has a shape, and the shape is an outline said out loud:

| Sentences | Pattern | |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | **answer + support** | the floor — an answer with nothing behind it is not an answer |
| 3 | **answer + support + support** | |
| 4 | **answer + support + its follow-up + support** | |
| 5 | **answer + support + follow-up + support + follow-up** | the ceiling for one Part 1 or Part 3 turn |

The mapping to section 5 is exact, and the books should draw it on the page:

| Speaking | Outline | Writing |
|---|---|---|
| the answer | the main idea | the topic sentence |
| a support | a supporting detail | the evidence or reason |
| a follow-up | the development | the explanation that earns the detail |

So a student who can outline can speak, and a student who can speak in this
shape is drafting a body paragraph aloud. **This is the same lesson arriving
for the third time**, and saying so is what stops it being three lessons.

**It is a model, not a constraint, and the books must say that in those
words.** Two is a floor rather than a target; five is a shape, not a quota. An
answer that runs to six sentences because the sixth is doing work is a better
answer, and an answer padded to five with a sentence that says nothing is a
worse one. The rule exists to stop a candidate answering in three words and to
stop them rambling, and both failures are commoner than anything in between.

**The ladder.** The shape grows with the volume and with the part of the test:

| Book | Part 1 | Part 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1–2 | 2 | 2 |
| Volumes 1–2 | 2–3 | 3 |
| Volumes 3–4 | 3 | 3–4 |
| Volumes 5–6 | 3 | 4–5 |

Part 1 stays short on purpose. A candidate who gives a five-sentence answer to
"Do you work or study?" has misread the room, and the examiner has eleven more
questions to get through.

### 7.2 Part 2: the card is an outline that has already been written for you

The bullets on the cue card are the main ideas. Run the two-to-five rule on
each one and the long turn builds itself. Three rules, in order of how often
they are broken:

1. **Depth beats breadth.** A long list is boring, it is simple, and it will
   not get you far. Two points developed outscore five points mentioned, every
   time, because development is what the criteria reward and a list is what a
   nervous candidate produces.
2. **Take the bullets in order**, unless there is a real reason not to. The
   order is usually the natural order of the story, and a candidate who jumps
   around spends fluency on navigation.
3. **Give them roughly equal weight** — as far as that makes sense. One bullet
   starved and one bloated reads as a candidate who ran out of material and
   padded, which is exactly what it is.

**The arithmetic says this is calibrated, not arbitrary.** Four bullets at four
sentences each, at an ordinary spoken 15 words a sentence, is 240 words — which
is 1.5 minutes at 160 words a minute and 2.0 at 120. The long turn asks for one
to two minutes. Across the plausible range (three to five sentences a bullet,
120 to 160 wpm) the rule produces 1.0 to 2.8 minutes, so a student running it
lands inside the window without watching a clock. That is worth printing,
because a candidate who trusts the shape stops counting and starts talking.

### 7.3 Expressions and structures, never templates

The course teaches **functional expressions** — ways to concede, to qualify, to
compare, to speculate, to correct yourself, to buy a moment honestly — and
**general structures**, which are shapes. It does not teach sentences to insert
regardless of what was asked.

This is not only a matter of taste. A memorised opening is recognised, and it
does not demonstrate the ability the criteria are written to measure; worse,
the template usually answers a question slightly different from the one asked,
which costs the response criterion before the language is even considered. The
practical test the books use: **if you could say this sentence in answer to a
different question, it is a template, and it is costing you.**

The stance strand already in the architecture does this work. It teaches
*arguably*, *on balance*, *it depends what you mean by*, *I would go further
than that* — expressions that commit to a degree, which is precisely the
flexibility the criteria reward and the thing a template cannot fake.

## 8. What the test measures, and what that makes this course

### 8.1 Every lesson names the criterion it moves

IELTS publishes its criteria. Writing is marked on Task Response, Coherence and
Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Speaking is
marked on Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and
Accuracy, and Pronunciation. Reading and Listening have no criteria at all —
they are raw-marked and converted.

**That asymmetry is a design instruction, not a footnote.** In Reading and
Listening, technique converts straight into marks: a better method finds more
answers. In Writing and Speaking there is no such line, and a technique only
pays if it moves one of four named criteria. So every lesson in the productive
strands **states the criterion it serves**, and the gate checks that it does.

| Technique | Criterion it moves |
|---|---|
| Choosing distinct, equally weighted main ideas | Coherence and Cohesion |
| The two-to-five rule | Fluency and Coherence; Task Response |
| Outlining before writing | Task Response and Coherence and Cohesion |
| Task 1: every number carries a description | Task Achievement |
| Task 1: a declared basis for dividing the body paragraphs | Coherence and Cohesion |
| Task 1: complete coverage, summarised | Task Achievement |
| Stance and hedging expressions | Lexical Resource |
| The conditional move ("both, under different circumstances") | Task Response |
| Refusing templates | Lexical Resource; Task Response |

A student who knows which of four numbers a technique moves can spend their
last fortnight on the one that is holding their band down. A student who has
been given a bag of tips cannot.

### 8.2 Why there are two modules, and what follows from it

If IELTS were a test of English in the abstract there would be one paper. There
are two because the thing being measured is **language in a domain** — academic
study on one side, work and daily life in an English-speaking country on the
other — and the conventions of a domain are part of what is being tested, not
packaging around it.

That is why General Training is bound up with workplaces, notices, handbooks
and letters, and why Academic is bound up with argument, evidence and the
shapes a research literature uses. A candidate who knows the grammar and has
never met the conventions will still miss: they will not know that a notice
buries its condition in the last line, or that an academic passage states a
claim and then qualifies it, or that a letter to a landlord is not a letter to
a friend.

**So this course cannot be a grammar and vocabulary course with test practice
attached.** It has to teach the discourse of both domains — which is what the
five-cycle unit is already for. Cycles 1 to 3 are the everyday and workplace
domain; cycles 4 and 5 are the academic one. The architecture was already
making this argument; the books should now say it out loud, in the front
matter, because a student who understands why the two modules exist stops
treating the other module's cycles as padding.

### 8.3 Skills and technique, not hacks

The line between them is not attitude, it is durability, and there is a test
for it:

> **A hack stops working if the test changes slightly. A skill is something
> you would keep doing the day after the exam.**

Outlining survives. Reading one paragraph at a time survives. Knowing that a
number without a description is worth less than a number with one survives —
it is just good writing. "Open every conclusion with *In conclusion*" does not
survive, and was never worth anything.

Every lesson in this series should pass that test, and where something is a
genuine exam artefact — the word limit on a completion answer, the two-minute
check at the end of computer-delivered Listening — the book says so plainly and
calls it what it is. A course that mixes the two and labels neither teaches
students to distrust both.

### 8.4 Receptive before productive, inside every cycle

**If you cannot read and understand, you will not be able to speak or write.**
Ideas come from having read; structure comes from having seen structure;
vocabulary is recognised long before it is produced; and the main-idea judgement
is learned by recognising it before anyone can perform it.

This fixes the order inside a cycle: **read, listen, then speak, then write**,
on the same sub-topic, with the productive tasks drawing on the ideas and the
language of the receptive ones. The TOEFL series already works this way — the
email you write in cycle 2 answers the announcement you heard in cycle 2 — and
for IELTS it has a sharper consequence:

**Cycles 4 and 5 are a writing lesson disguised as a reading lesson.** Cycle 4
reads the claim a field rests on; cycle 5 reads the objection to that claim.
That pair *is* essay structure 2, 3 and 9 from the Task 2 taxonomy, met as a
reader before it is required of a writer. By the time the student plans the
essay in cycle 5 they have just read two models of the exact move they are
being asked to make. The plan should make that explicit in the unit, with a
line of text that says so, because students do not notice it by themselves and
it is the best thing the architecture does.

## 9. The unit, cycle by cycle

Sixteen parts, five cycles a skill, the same architecture the TOEFL series
ships — with the task names swapped for IELTS ones and one strand added.

| Cycle | Reading | Listening | Writing | Speaking |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | GT Section 1 text: notice, advert, timetable | Part 1: social conversation, 2 speakers | **GT Task 1**: a letter | Part 1: interview |
| 2 | GT Section 1–2 text: workplace document | Part 2: social monologue | **GT Task 1**: a second register | Part 1 + Part 2 cue card |
| 3 | GT Section 2 text: staff handbook, training | Part 3: academic discussion, up to 4 speakers | Task 2 under 250 words | Part 2: the long turn |
| 4 | **Academic passage / GT Section 3** — the claim the field rests on | Part 4: academic lecture | **Academic Task 1**: data, process or map | Part 3: discussion |
| 5 | **Academic passage** — the objection to cycle 4 | Part 4: a second lecture | **Task 2** at full length | Part 3: the harder turn |

The order inside a cycle is fixed and it is not arbitrary: **read, listen,
speak, write**, all on the one sub-topic, because nobody produces a pattern
they have not first processed (8.4). Cycles 4 and 5 carry the heaviest version
of this — the claim and the objection are read before the same move is asked
for in the Task 2 essay — and the unit says so on the page.

Two strands are added to every unit:

* **Active Reading / Active Listening** — one lesson on a move of the method,
  one of its four reading variants or one of the four Listening-only moves
  (4.2); the unit's exercise from the four families in 3.4; and the timed
  passage whose words-per-minute goes into the unit review beside the re-read
  counter.
* **Outlining** — one exercise from the eight families in 5.2, at that
  volume's level, with at least one unit a volume running the
  passage-to-outline exercise that makes the cross-skill thread explicit.
* **The answer shape** — the two-to-five rule (7.1) practised in Speaking and
  then shown to be the same object as the outline and the body paragraph. Each
  productive lesson names the criterion it moves (8.1).

Passage lengths, which are *not* the TOEFL ones — Academic Reading runs
longer than TOEFL's, and the whole paper is 40 questions in 60 minutes with no
separate transfer time:

| | Foundation 1 | Foundation 2 | V1–V2 | V3–V4 | V5–V6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GT-style texts (cycles 1–3) | 90–140 | 140–200 | 200–280 | 280–360 | 340–420 |
| Academic passage (cycles 4–5) | 180–240 | 240–320 | 420–560 | 620–780 | 800–950 |
| Listening script, Part 4 | 180–240 | 240–300 | 300–400 | 420–520 | 520–620 |

Volume 6's academic passages reach 950 words because the real paper's third
passage does, and reading speed is built above the distance you race.

## 10. Question types: the coverage that has to be gated

The eleven Reading types and the Listening types are the syllabus. Each one is
**taught in a named lesson before it is ever tested**, and a gate enforces it.

**Reading** — multiple choice · True/False/Not Given · Yes/No/Not Given ·
matching information · matching headings · matching features · matching
sentence endings · sentence completion · summary, note, table and flow-chart
completion · diagram labelling · short answer.

**Listening** — multiple choice · matching · plan, map and diagram labelling ·
form, note, table and flow-chart completion · sentence completion · short
answer.

**Writing Task 2** — the eleven structures of 6.1, which cut the same ground
as the usual prompt-type list (opinion · discuss both views · problem and
solution · advantages and disadvantages · two-part question) but are indexed by
intent rather than by wording.
**Academic Task 1** — line graph · bar chart · pie chart · table · process ·
map · multiple charts.
**GT Task 1** — formal · semi-formal · informal letter.

Spread across the ladder: Foundation 1 and 2 meet the gentler types only
(completion, short answer, matching); Volume 1 adds True/False/Not Given;
matching headings and matching information — the two that break the method's
default shape — arrive in Volume 2 with their carve-out lessons; Yes/No/Not
Given, the writer's-view type, waits for Volume 3 where stance vocabulary is
taught.

## 13. Sequence, and an honest word on scale

The TOEFL series is eight books, 1.09 million body words and 3,752 figures.
The IELTS clone is the same order of work or slightly more, because the
academic passages are longer. It is not a weekend's adaptation, and anyone
planning it as one will produce the thing this project spent its last month
fixing: a book whose cover claims a level its prose does not have.

The order that keeps the risk at the front:

1. **The machinery and the gates.** Shared layer extracted, the IELTS test
   shape written, gates 10 and 11 written. All nine existing gates re-run on
   the TOEFL series afterwards to prove the extraction broke nothing — they
   exist precisely to make that safe.
2. **One complete vertical slice: Volume 4, Unit 1.** The hardest common case —
   band 6.5, both modules, five cycles, the technique strand with its timed
   passage, both Writing Task 1 forks. Build it, run every gate, print it,
   read it. Decide the shape is right while one unit is cheap to rewrite.
   Then do one more thing before scaling: **sit the unit's reading yourself
   at 150 wpm with a stopwatch** and confirm the arithmetic in 3.5 closes on
   real pages rather than on a spreadsheet.
3. **Volume 4, then Volume 5.** The two books the market actually buys.
4. **Volumes 3 and 6**, outward from there.
5. **Volumes 1 and 2.**
6. **Foundation 1 and 2**, written last and written against measurement, the
   way Foundation 2 was: scan the finished Volume 1 for every word a Foundation
   leaver will not know, and teach exactly those.

Writing the Foundation books last is not a scheduling convenience. It is the
only way they can be built from a measured gap rather than a guess, and that
measurement is what made gate 7 pass at a mean of 1.5 unknown types.

## 14. Open questions worth settling before step 1

1. **Market and format.** Which country's candidates, and are they sitting on
   computer or paper? This changes the technique chapter and the timings
   printed in every test.
2. **One book or two covers?** This plan assumes one book per volume carrying
   both modules. The alternative — one shared book plus two thin module
   supplements for Writing Task 1 and the reading texts — is cheaper to print
   and worse to study from. I would hold the single book.
3. **Band descriptors.** IELTS publishes its criteria. Using them verbatim is
   defensible and better than inventing; it also means the writing models must
   be built to score against them, which is a constraint on authoring, not a
   freebie.
4. **Volume 6's ceiling.** 7.5–8.0 is the top of what preparation moves.
   Above that the limit is the candidate's English, not their technique, and
   the book should say so as plainly as Foundation 1 says it does not prepare
   you to sit the test.
5. **How novel is the method, and does it matter?** Question-first reading,
   self-generated headings before matching, and paragraph-by-paragraph
   processing each exist separately in other people's practice. What is not
   out there is the three of them integrated into one bounded loop with its
   variants taught as part of it and **its cost stated in minutes**. That is
   the defensible claim and it is the better one commercially, because it can
   be demonstrated on a page. A novelty claim invites someone to find a prior
   art; an arithmetic claim invites them to check the arithmetic, which holds.
6. **Collect the data the course keeps asking students for.** Every unit makes
   a student record a wpm figure and a re-read count. If even a few hundred of
   those come back, this becomes the only IELTS course with a measured
   learning curve behind its central claim. Worth designing the page so the
   numbers are easy to send back.
