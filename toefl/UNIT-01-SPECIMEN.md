# Unit 1 · Learning Together — worked specimen

The Phase 3 pilot, specified exercise by exercise. Every item below is newly
authored. Theme and lesson slots are the source book's; content is not.

**Unit theme:** Learning Together → campus orientation and study groups.
**Language-use context:** Academic Navigational (ETS's own category — the
register of registrars, advisors, syllabi and campus notices; the most
distinctively TOEFL of the three contexts, which is why it is the pilot).
**Cast introduced here** (recurring across all twelve units): Professor Nadia
Haddad (lecturer, British English), Mei Lin (first-year student, US English),
Dan Okafor (peer advisor, US English), Priya Raman (student, Australian
English), Tomás Ferreira (student, US English).

---

## Lesson 1 · Vocabulary → Complete the Words

**Interactive scenario.** The screen opens on a numbered illustrated scene: the
first day in a seminar room — a syllabus on the desk, a noticeboard, a sign-up
sheet, a clock, a lecturer at a whiteboard. Twelve numbered hotspots. Tapping a
number speaks the word and shows it; this is the picture-dictionary half of the
source lesson, unchanged in function.

Then the word goes to work. Each item is a sentence with one word partially
spelled, letter boxes for the rest. Typing a letter fills a box; a wrong letter
turns that box only, never the whole word, so the learner can see which part of
the word they do not have. `Check Answers` and `Try Again` as everywhere.

The ladder is deliberate: items 1–3 test spelling of a known word, 4–6 test
choosing the right word from the sentence's meaning, 7–8 test *word form* —
ETS's "Apply basic spelling and word form knowledge to complete predictable
gaps."

| # | Item | Answer | Band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The s y _ _ a b _ s lists every assignment and its due date. | syllabus | A2 |
| 2 | Our study group meets in the l i b r _ _ y on Thursdays. | library | A2 |
| 3 | Bring your student I D to o r i _ _ t a t i o n on Monday. | orientation | B1 |
| 4 | You cannot take the advanced course without the p r _ _ _ _ u i s i t e. | prerequisite | B1 |
| 5 | The essay d _ _ _ l i n e moved from the 3rd to the 10th. | deadline | B1 |
| 6 | Each seminar is worth three c r _ _ i t s toward your degree. | credits | B2 |
| 7 | Students who attend regularly show steady i m p r _ _ _ m _ n t. | improvement | C1 |
| 8 | Group work is only p r o d _ _ t _ v e when everyone prepares. | productive | C1 |

Items 7 and 8 are the point of the lesson: *improve → improvement* and
*produce → productive* are morphological, not lexical, and that is what the
B1–C1+ band on this task actually tests.

## Lesson 2 · Real-life writing → Write an Email + Build a Sentence

### The augmentation, in place

**Interactive scenario.** A scenario card, then a working email chrome — To,
From, Subject, body — with the scenario pinned beside it so it stays visible,
which is what the spec requires of its stimuli.

> **Scenario.** Your Introduction to Psychology seminar meets Tuesdays at 2:00.
> You have been offered a work shift at the same time. Write to Professor
> Haddad. Ask whether you may move to the Thursday section, explain why, and
> ask what you need to do.
>
> **To:** n.haddad@university.edu · **Audience:** your professor ·
> **Purpose:** request and ask for next steps

**The mechanic that teaches, not just tests: the register dial.** Above the
body sits a two-position switch — *To Professor Haddad* / *To your classmate
Mei*. Flipping it rewrites the model answer, sentence for sentence, into the
other register:

| Formal (to the professor) | Informal (to a classmate) |
|---|---|
| I am writing to ask whether it would be possible to move to the Thursday section. | I wanted to ask if I could switch to Thursdays. |
| I have been offered a shift that unfortunately overlaps with the Tuesday seminar. | I got offered a shift that clashes with Tuesday. |
| I would be grateful if you could let me know what I need to do. | Let me know what I should do? |

The learner writes their own, then flips the dial and sees the same content at
the other register. Register stops being an adjective and becomes a thing you
can watch move. Then the 5-point rubric — the task's real maximum — with each
band stated in terms the learner can apply to their own draft: did you address
all three parts of the prompt, is the register right for the named audience, is
it organised, is the grammar accurate, is it the right length.

### Build a Sentence (4) — the sentences the email needs

Word tiles, drag or click or keyboard, into order. Taken from the email's own
register so the two halves of the lesson are one lesson.

| # | Tiles → target | Structure | Band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I / am / writing / to / ask / about / the / Thursday / section | simple, infinitive of purpose | A2 |
| 2 | The / shift / overlaps / with / the / seminar, / so / I / cannot / attend | compound, *so* | B1 |
| 3 | Although / I / would / prefer / Tuesdays, / I / am / able / to / attend / on / Thursdays | complex, concessive | B2 |
| 4 | I / would / be / grateful / if / you / could / let / me / know / whether / there / is / space | complex, conditional + embedded *whether* | C1 |

Distractor tiles are always present — never an exact tile count, so the last
tile is never a free placement. (The rule the CMA build settled on, carried
over.)

## Lesson 3 · Grammar → Build a Sentence + Complete the Words

The source's L3 at this level teaches *simple, compound and complex sentences*
and, per CCRS L.2.B (c.), *commas in greetings and closings of letters*. Both
are kept.

**Interactive scenario.** A grammar chart — a real boxed paradigm, printed
typography, not a bulleted list — showing the three sentence types with their
joining words colour-coded: coordinators (*and, but, so, or*) in one colour,
subordinators (*although, because, if, when, whether*) in another. The chart
stays on screen while the exercises run beneath it.

Then the **comma rule, taught visually**: an email opening and closing with the
commas as draggable chips. Drop a comma after *Dear Professor Haddad* and after
*Kind regards* and the letter settles; put one in the wrong place and the
document shows you where the convention is broken. The same mechanic as the CMA
statements — getting it right produces the document.

Six Build a Sentence items laddering simple → compound → complex → relative
clause → complex nominal phrase, following the spec's described progression for
this task ("compound verbs, dependent clauses, relative clauses, and complex
nominal phrases"). Two Complete the Words items on the joining words
themselves, so the grammar vocabulary is spelled as well as placed.

## Lesson 4 · Everyday conversation → Listen and Choose a Response + Listen to a Conversation + Listen and Repeat

Every audio item shows the speaker — the spec requires a *"visual
representation of the speaker(s)"* — so the cast portrait appears with each.
Audio plays **once**, as on the real test, with that stated plainly before the
first item rather than sprung on the learner.

### Listen and Choose a Response (6)

One short utterance, no longer than six stressed syllables as the spec
requires, then three printed replies. A1 → B2.

| # | You hear | Pick the reply | Band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Is this seat taken?" | **No, go ahead.** / Yes, I'll take it. / It's over there. | A1 |
| 2 | "Where's the seminar room?" | **Second floor, on the left.** / At two o'clock. / Yes, it is. | A1 |
| 3 | "Did you get the syllabus?" | **Not yet — is it online?** / I'll get there soon. / It's on Thursday. | A2 |
| 4 | "Shall we meet before the test?" | **Sure — Wednesday work for you?** / I met her already. / It was a good test. | B1 |
| 5 | "I'd rather not present first." | **That's fine, I'll go first.** / Neither would I present. / I'd rather present it. | B2 |
| 6 | "I'd have signed up earlier if I'd known." | **You still might get in.** / I signed up too. / I didn't know either. | B2 |

Item 6 is the top of this task's band and shows what it costs: the reply only
works if the learner heard a counterfactual and understood that the speaker did
*not* sign up early. The two distractors are each plausible to someone who
missed exactly that.

### Listen to a Conversation (4 items on one dialogue)

Mei Lin at the advising desk with Dan Okafor, 90 words, two speakers, A2–C1 —
inside the spec's 35–100-word intermediate band. She wants to join a study
group that is already full; he offers a waiting list and a second group that
meets at an awkward hour. Items: the gist, one detail she states, Dan's
*purpose* in mentioning the second group, and one inference about what she will
probably do — the four skills the spec lists for this task, one item each.

### Listen and Repeat (4)

Hear, then say it back. The ladder is the spec's own: "short, simple sentences
containing one independent clause … to longer utterances featuring compound
verbs, dependent clauses, relative clauses, and complex nominal phrases."

1. The group meets on Thursday. *(one independent clause)*
2. We read the chapter and compared our notes. *(compound verb)*
3. If the room is full, we move to the library. *(dependent clause)*
4. The students who prepared beforehand found the discussion far more useful.
   *(relative clause + complex nominal phrase)*

See §6.1 of the plan on recording: by default the learner hears it, says it,
then reveals the model with its transcript and self-rates against the 5-point
scale.

## Lesson 5 · Reading → Read in Daily Life + Read an Academic Passage + Listen to an Announcement + Listen to an Academic Talk

The busiest lesson, and the one that carries the unit's data visual — CASAS
6.8.1, *interpret graphs and charts*, is on Lesson 5 in all twelve units of the
source. Every reading lesson in this book has a chart, because every reading
lesson in that one did.

### Read in Daily Life (4)

Two authored documents, both staying on screen while the items run beside them,
as the spec requires.

**Document A — a campus noticeboard notice** (41 words, inside the 15–50 band):
study-group sign-up, room, time, what to bring, who to contact. Two items: one
locating a detail, one on the notice's purpose.

**Document B — a sign-up form**, half completed, with a deadline in a footnote.
Two items: one scanning for a detail, one inference about who may still sign up.
Rendered as a real form — ruled fields, a tick box, small print — on the
document renderer that the CMA statements already use.

### Read an Academic Passage (4)

A 185-word passage on **the spacing effect** — why study sessions spread over
days beat the same total hours crammed into one. Thematically it is *Learning
Together*; academically it is cognitive psychology, which is where B1–C2
academic reading actually lives. Authored to Flesch–Kincaid 11–13, inside the
spec's high band (10.5–20.0).

**The chart beside it:** two retention curves over fourteen days, massed
practice against spaced practice, same total study time. The fourth item cannot
be answered from the prose alone — it needs the chart — which is what CCRS
R.7.C ("interpret information presented visually … and explain how the
information contributes to an understanding of the text") asks of this slot.

Items: main idea · a stated detail · an inference that is implied but not
stated · a pronoun referent (*"they"* in the third paragraph, where the nearest
noun is the wrong antecedent). Those four are the skills the spec names for
this task.

### Listen to an Announcement (3) — augmentation

Academic Navigational, 70 words, the register the source book never uses: a
library PA announcement that the orientation workshop has moved room and that
the sign-up deadline has been extended, with one instruction for people who
already registered. Items: what changed · what registered students must do ·
the announcer's purpose in the last sentence.

### Listen to an Academic Talk (4) — the largest augmentation

Professor Haddad, 240 words, inside the spec's 250-word ceiling, on **the
testing effect** — that retrieving information is a better way to learn it than
re-reading it. Built on a cause-and-effect rhetorical pattern, which the spec
names as something these talks carry and the items test.

**The notepad.** A pad opens beside the player. The learner may type while the
audio runs; the notes stay visible during the items but the audio does not
return. This mirrors the real test and teaches the skill the test silently
assumes — that you take notes on a single pass.

Items: the main claim · a supporting detail · an inference about what the
professor would say of a student who only re-reads · the meaning of *retrieval*
as she uses it, which the talk defines in passing rather than explicitly.

## Lesson 6 · Teamwork & Language Review → Write for an Academic Discussion + Take an Interview + mixed review

### Write for an Academic Discussion (1) — augmentation

**Interactive scenario.** A threaded discussion board. The professor's post at
the top, two classmates' posts beneath it, the composer at the bottom. All
three stay visible while the learner writes.

> **Professor Haddad.** Some universities now require all first-year students to
> join a study group; others leave it optional. Which policy better supports
> learning, and why?
>
> **Priya Raman.** It should be required. The students who would benefit most
> from a group are exactly the ones who never sign up for one, usually because
> they are the least confident.
>
> **Tomás Ferreira.** I'd keep it optional. Every compulsory group I've been in
> had two people doing the work and four along for the ride. A group only works
> if the people in it chose to be there.

The two posts are authored to disagree *on a real axis* — access versus
motivation — so there is something to take a side on. The rubric's top band
requires engaging with a classmate **by name** and adding something neither of
them said, not restating the question. That is the difference between a 3 and a
5 on this task, and it is the single most teachable thing about it.

### Take an Interview (2) — augmentation

A question delivered by audio with the interviewer on screen, a countdown, one
pass.

1. *Describe a time you learned something together with other people. What made
   it work?* (narration, 45 s)
2. *Some students prefer to study alone. Why do you think that is?* (opinion
   and explanation, 45 s)

The two prompts are the spec's own progression — "brief factual statements to
more elaborate expressions of opinion, explanation, prediction, and narration."

### Mixed review (6)

Interleaved across all six lessons, re-shuffled each visit, and weighted toward
the traps this learner actually missed — the mechanic already built for the CMA
review page. Item 6 is always a repeat of whichever trap they got wrong most
recently, in a new surface form.
