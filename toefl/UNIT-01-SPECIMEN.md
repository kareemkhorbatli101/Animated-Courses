# Unit 1 · How We Learn — worked specimen

The Phase 3 pilot. The architecture, strand for strand, is the book's Unit 1
(pp. 4–19); the subject is cognitive psychology — memory, retrieval and study
method — per `UNIT-MAP.md`. Every item is newly authored.

**Why this topic for this grammar.** Unit 1 teaches present and past verb forms
in contrast. *How We Learn* is where that contrast is the subject: what you *do*
when you study, against what a study *found* when it tested it.

## What the book's Unit 1 is, and what carries over

| Strand | Step Forward 3, Unit 1 |
|---|---|
| Themes | Study skills and the U.S. educational system · Express uncertainty → **memory and study method · express uncertainty** |
| Vocabulary | study skills · types of schools in the U.S. · the prefix *dis-* → **study method and memory · the prefix *dis-*** |
| Academic language | accurately, challenge, revise, research, schedule |
| Grammar | review present and past verb forms · *yes/no* questions and answers · adjectives and adverbs to describe skills |
| Writing strategy | focusing on the main idea in different paragraphs |
| Reading strategy | reading with a purpose · reading complex charts and tables multiple times |
| Pronunciation | *sh*, *ch* and *j* |
| Data visual | a learning pyramid — active versus passive recall after two weeks |
| L2 writing task | a journal entry about the first day of class |
| L5 reading | a newspaper advice column, with line numbers and a footnote → **kept as a form, new subject** |
| AT WORK | showing that you are listening → **ON CAMPUS: showing that you are following a lecture** |
| Teamwork / Problem Solving | a labelled classroom scene · interview classmates, make a pie chart · a new student feels alone → write her a letter |

**Skeleton kept, subject replaced.** Grammar, morphology, pronunciation, the
data-visual form and all three lesson templates carry over unchanged. What goes
is the life-skills content: the U.S. educational system and the public library
give way to how memory actually works, which is a real TOEFL reading and lecture
subject and happens to be the one thing every test-taker is doing anyway.

**The per-unit writing strategy becomes the unit's writing focus.** Unit 1's is
*focusing on the main idea in different paragraphs*, which is exactly what
separates a 3 from a 5 on Write for an Academic Discussion. The book's email
strand starts in Unit 2, so the TOEFL book's email teaching ladder starts there
too — both productive tasks appear in every unit, because the test has both
every time, but the taught focus follows the book's strand.

**Cast** (recurring across all twelve units): Professor Nadia Haddad
(lecturer, British), Mei Lin (first year, US), Dan Okafor (peer advisor, US),
Priya Raman (student, Australian), Tomás Ferreira (student, US).

## Lesson 1 · VOCABULARY → Complete the Words (8)

**Scene.** The book's numbered illustrated scene, kept in form: nine actions in
one full-colour drawing with callouts 1–9. Tap a number, hear it, see it. The
set is re-cut from classroom habits to the things memory research actually
distinguishes: *re-read · highlight · test yourself · space your sessions ·
summarise from memory · explain it aloud · interleave two subjects · take a
break · check what you got wrong.*

The first two are the ones that feel productive and are not — which is the
unit's whole argument, planted in its vocabulary before the reading makes it.

Then the letterbox items. A wrong letter turns **that box only**.

| # | Item | Answer | Band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I always t a _ e n _ t e s in the lecture. | take notes | A2 |
| 2 | Close the book and t _ s t yourself. | test | A2 |
| 3 | Spread your study over a s c h _ d _ l e of several days. | schedule | B1 |
| 4 | Reading it again is not the same as trying to r e c _ l l it. | recall | B1 |
| 5 | You will need to do r e s _ _ r c h for this essay. | research | B1 |
| 6 | Read your draft again and r _ v _ s e it. | revise | B2 |
| 7 | She answered a c c _ r _ t _ l y every time. | accurately | C1 |
| 8 | Working alone was the biggest c h _ l l _ n g e. | challenge | C1 |

Items 5–8 are the book's own **Academic language** list for Unit 1 — *research,
revise, accurately, challenge, schedule*. The book already marks them as the
academic register; the TOEFL task is where they pay.

**Word study — the prefix *dis*-.** The book's affix for Unit 1, kept, and it is
a gift: *disagree · disprove · disconfirm · disregard* is the vocabulary of
research, so the academic re-cut costs nothing. The book circles the right word;
the TOEFL shape completes it: *The second experiment failed to
d i s p r _ _ e the claim.* The affix ladder
runs across all twelve units — *dis-, -ful, -tion, inter-, un-, -al, re-,
-er/-or, -ment* — and it is the backbone of this task's B1–C1+ band.

## Lesson 2 · WRITING → Write for an Academic Discussion (1) + Build a Sentence (4)

The book's Unit 1 writing is a reflective journal entry about the first day of
class, taught through *focusing on the main idea in different paragraphs*: a
challenge, how it was overcome, what was learned. That three-move structure is
the Academic Discussion answer.

**The lesson keeps the book's five steps exactly:** read the model → underline
the answers in it → plan and take notes → write on a template → editing
checklist → peer comment → revise.

> **Professor Haddad.** Some students say they learn more in a group than alone.
> Others say a group slows them down. Which is closer to your experience, and
> why?
>
> **Priya Raman.** A group makes me explain things, and explaining is when I
> find out what I don't actually know.
>
> **Tomás Ferreira.** Every group I've been in had two people working and four
> watching. I get more done in an hour alone than in three with other people.

**WRITER'S NOTE** (the book's own sidebar, kept): *Give each paragraph one main
idea. Here, paragraph 1 states your position, paragraph 2 gives the reason it is
true for you, paragraph 3 answers the strongest objection.*

**Editing checklist**, in the book's four-tick form: ☐ I stated my position ·
☐ I gave a reason from my own experience · ☐ I answered Priya or Tomás **by
name** · ☐ I wrote 100 words or more.

That third tick is the difference between a 3 and a 5 on this task.

**Build a Sentence (4)**, drawn from the register the discussion needs:

| # | Target | Structure | Band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | I usually study better on my own. | simple | A2 |
| 2 | I take notes in class, but I revise them at home. | compound | B1 |
| 3 | Although Tomás makes a fair point, my experience has been different. | complex, concessive | B2 |
| 4 | What I find most useful is having to explain an idea to someone else. | cleft + complex nominal | C1 |

Always more tiles than places.

## Lesson 3 · GRAMMAR → Build a Sentence (6) + Complete the Words (2)

The book's grammar, unchanged: present and past verb forms, *yes/no* questions
and answers, adjectives and adverbs describing skills.

**The book's page sequence is kept whole** because every step of it already
feeds Build a Sentence: paired realia (a paragraph beside a handwritten notebook
page) → paradigm charts → the **Language connection** cloze that makes the
learner state the rule in their own words → circle-and-match → **edit the
sentence and rewrite it** → a labelled scene to ask and answer about.

The error-correction step is the one worth protecting. *"Yan memorize words
every day"* → the learner rewrites it. That is sentence reconstruction, which is
Build a Sentence's construct, in a form that forces the learner to notice the
error first.

Six items laddering simple → compound → *yes/no* question → complex → relative
clause → complex nominal phrase, plus two Complete the Words on *accurately* and
*carefully* (the book's adverb focus).

## Lesson 4 · EVERYDAY CONVERSATION → Listen and Choose a Response (6) + Listen to a Conversation (4) + Listen and Repeat (4)

The book's Unit 1 conversation is a student with a career counsellor, and its
functional theme is **expressing uncertainty**. Re-aimed: Mei Lin with Dan
Okafor, the peer advisor, about which courses to take — Academic Navigational,
same function.

Every audio item shows its speaker. Audio plays **once**, stated up front.

### Listen and Choose a Response (6)

One utterance, no longer than six stressed syllables, then three printed replies.

| # | You hear | Pick the reply | Band |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Is this the advising office?" | **Yes, go right in.** / Yes, I advised him. / It's good advice. | A1 |
| 2 | "When's the deadline?" | **Friday at five.** / In the office. / Yes, it is. | A1 |
| 3 | "Have you chosen your courses?" | **Not yet — I'm still deciding.** / I chose a quiet place. / They're good courses. | A2 |
| 4 | "Shall I put you on the waiting list?" | **Yes, please do.** / I waited a long time. / It's a short list. | B1 |
| 5 | "I'm not quite sure that's the right one." | **We can look at the others.** / I'm sure it is right. / I'm not quite ready. | B2 |
| 6 | "I'd have applied sooner if I'd known." | **There may still be places.** / I applied as well. / I didn't know either. | B2 |

Item 5 is built on the book's own uncertainty phrases — *I'm just not sure · I'm
not quite sure · It's hard to say · I can't tell you for sure.* Item 6 is the
top of the band: the reply only works if the learner heard a counterfactual and
understood the speaker did **not** apply early.

**IN OTHER WORDS…** (the book's blue sidebar, kept) lists the seven hedges, each
with audio.

### Listen to a Conversation (4 on one dialogue)

Mei and Dan at the advising desk, 90 words, two speakers — inside the spec's
35–100-word band. Items: gist · a stated detail · Dan's *purpose* in mentioning
a second option · an inference about what Mei will do. The four skills the spec
names, one item each.

### Pronunciation → Listen and Repeat (4)

The book's Unit 1 sounds are *sh*, *ch* and *j*, drawn from the conversation
itself. Kept, and the sound-sorting chart with them. Then the repeat ladder:

1. I'm not sure. *(one clause, /ʃ/)*
2. I checked the schedule and chose a different class. *(compound verb, /tʃ/ + /ʃ/)*
3. If you change your major, you should see an advisor. *(dependent clause, /dʒ/)*
4. The students who chose the Thursday session finished the project much sooner.
   *(relative clause + complex nominal phrase)*

See §6.1 of the plan on recording: by default, hear it, say it, reveal the model
with its transcript, self-rate on the 5-point scale.

## Lesson 5 · READING → Read in Daily Life (4) + Read an Academic Passage (4)

The book's five-step reading sequence is kept whole: glossed definitions →
preview and predict from the title and picture → read → **READER'S NOTE** →
T/F/NI citing the line → word study → the data visual.

### Read in Daily Life (4)

A **campus advice column**, set as the real thing: masthead, columnist's
photograph, two columns, line numbers every five lines, and a numbered footnote
glossing one idiom — because the items then ask the learner to cite the line.
Three letters and three replies on finding study space, borrowing equipment and
getting help with an assignment. 48 words per exchange, inside the 15–50 band.

Items: a detail · the columnist's purpose · one T/F/**NI** **with the line
number** · the meaning of the footnoted idiom from context.

The NI option is the book's, not the test's, and it is better: a learner who can
tell *false* from *not stated* will not be caught by a TOEFL distractor built on
information the passage never gave.

### Read an Academic Passage (4)

A 185-word passage on **the testing effect** — that retrieving an idea fixes it
better than re-reading it. Authored to Flesch–Kincaid 11–13, inside the spec's
high band. Thematically it is the book's own Unit 1 subject; academically it is
cognitive psychology, which is where B1–C2 academic reading lives.

**The data visual.** The book's Unit 1 chart is a learning pyramid of recall
after two weeks, with a side note explaining how to read it and five gap-fill
questions. Kept in function, redrawn as a chart that will bear the weight: two
retention curves over fourteen days, re-reading against self-testing, same total
hours. The book's **READER'S NOTE** goes with it unchanged — *read a complex
chart three or more times: first for the general idea, then for details, then
for the difficult vocabulary.*

The fourth item cannot be answered from the prose alone. Across the twelve
units the chart type follows the book's own sequence — graph, pie, bar, flow and
organisational, scatter, table, infographic — so the learner meets every form the
test can show them.

## ACADEMIC (1 page) → Listen to an Announcement (3) + Listen to an Academic Talk (4)

The book's seventh block is **AT WORK**, on showing that you are listening. In
other units it is **ACADEMIC**. In the TOEFL book it is always ACADEMIC, and it
is where the two missing listening genres live.

**Listen to an Announcement (3).** 70 words, Academic Navigational: the library
orientation has moved room and the sign-up deadline is extended, with one
instruction for those already registered. Items: what changed · what registered
students must do · the announcer's purpose in the last sentence.

**Listen to an Academic Talk (4).** Professor Haddad, 240 words, inside the
250-word ceiling, on the testing effect — built on the cause-and-effect pattern
the spec names. A **notepad** opens beside the player; the learner may type while
it runs, the notes stay visible for the items, the audio does not return. That
mirrors the real test and teaches the skill it silently assumes.

The book's active-listening chart — *lean forward · nod slowly · make eye
contact · take notes* — is kept as the page's teaching frame, because taking
notes on a single pass is exactly what this page is now for.

## TEAMWORK & LANGUAGE REVIEW → Take an Interview (2) + mixed review (6)

The book's page: a labelled classroom scene to write conversations for, an
interview of three classmates, and a pie chart of the class's answers. Then
PROBLEM SOLVING — a new student feels alone; write her a letter.

**Take an Interview (2).** The book's interview questions become the timed,
single-pass, recorded form, with the interviewer on screen and a countdown.

1. *Describe a time you learned something with other people. What made it work?*
   (narration, 45 s)
2. *Some students prefer to study alone. Why do you think that is?*
   (opinion and explanation, 45 s)

The spec's own progression — brief factual statements through to opinion,
explanation, prediction and narration.

**Mixed review (6).** Interleaved across the unit, re-shuffled each visit,
weighted toward the traps this learner missed. Item 6 always re-asks their most
recent error in a new surface form.

**PROBLEM SOLVING → Write an Email (1).** The book's slot, kept: read a short
scenario about someone with a problem, then write to them. Mei has missed the
sign-up deadline for the study-skills workshop. Write to Dan Okafor, say what
happened, ask whether a place is left, and ask what to do next. Audience and
purpose are given as explicit cues, the way the TOEFL prompt gives them. The
book's mail-client chrome, **NEED HELP?** frames and four-point editing
checklist all come straight from its Unit 2 lesson — which is where the email
teaching ladder properly begins.
