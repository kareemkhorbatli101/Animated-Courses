# The twelve units

**What is borrowed:** the seven-block unit architecture, the three invariant
lesson templates, the grammar syllabus *in the book's own order*, the morphology
ladder, the pronunciation ladder, the one-data-visual-per-unit rule and the form
it takes each time, the exercise taxonomy, and the density.

**What is replaced:** the topics. Step Forward 3 teaches adult life skills —
laundromats, job applications, car repair. This book teaches what the 2026 TOEFL
actually examines.

**The rule that makes it non-arbitrary:** each academic topic was chosen because
*the unit's grammar lives there naturally*. Comparatives belong with species
variation; conditionals belong with economics; the passive belongs with method.
The grammar is not bolted onto a topic — it is the topic's native register. That
is the whole argument for keeping the book's grammar order while throwing its
subject matter away.

## The map

| # | Grammar (kept, in the book's order) | New topic | Why the grammar lives there |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | present and past verb forms · *yes/no* questions · adjectives and adverbs | **How We Learn** — cognitive psychology, memory, study method | What you *do* now against what a study *found* then: the present/past contrast is the subject |
| **2** | *be going to* and *will* · plans, promises, predictions · *would rather* | **Climate and Prediction** — earth science, models, uncertainty | Prediction is the future tense's native ground, and hedged prediction is the register |
| **3** | comparatives · superlatives | **Adaptation and Diversity** — evolution, variation, selection | Comparing populations *is* the method |
| **4** | present perfect · *for* and *since* · contractions | **Migration and the Human Past** — archaeology, early settlement | "People have lived in this valley for 9,000 years" |
| **5** | present perfect questions · *ever, already, yet* · past vs present perfect | **Exploring Space** — astronomy, missions, instruments | What has already been done, what has never been attempted |
| **6** | separable and inseparable phrasal verbs · possessive pronouns | **Food, Energy and the Body** — nutrition, metabolism | Enzymes *break down*, cells *take in*, the liver *stores it up*: process language is phrasal |
| **7** | real conditionals · questions with conditionals · future time clauses | **Markets and Choices** — economics, incentives, trade-offs | Economics is written in conditionals: *if prices rise, demand falls* |
| **8** | *used to* · *used to* in questions · present perfect continuous | **Disease and Public Health** — epidemiology, prevention | "People used to die of it; researchers have been tracking it since" |
| **9** | time clauses, describing and comparing · *and…too, and…not either, but* | **Cities and Movement** — urban geography, transport, infrastructure | Sequencing a journey and comparing two systems |
| **10** | gerunds as subjects · gerunds vs present continuous · infinitives and gerunds | **Rules, Rights and Ethics** — law, philosophy, civic life | *Lying is wrong. Protesting is protected.* The gerund subject is the sentence of ethics |
| **11** | present passive · active and passive · the passive with *by* · *be able to* | **How Science Works** — method, evidence, peer review | The passive is the voice of the methods section |
| **12** | verbs + infinitives · verbs + gerunds or infinitives | **Art, Audience and Culture** — humanities, media, performance | *Audiences enjoy watching · artists choose to work · critics tend to argue* |

Twelve units, twelve academic fields, spanning the four domains the TOEFL draws
on — natural sciences, social sciences, humanities and business. The grammar
sequence is untouched, unit for unit.

## The three contexts live inside every unit

ETS examines three language-use contexts, so the unit's academic topic is its
spine, not its whole body. Each block carries the context the matching task type
is actually set in:

| Block | Context | What that means in Unit 3, *Adaptation and Diversity* |
|---|---|---|
| L1 Vocabulary | Academic | the vocabulary of variation and selection |
| L2 Writing | Academic Navigational | an email to a lab demonstrator about a missed session |
| L3 Grammar | Academic | comparing two finch populations |
| L4 Everyday Conversation | **Social Interpersonal** | two students arranging when to do the write-up |
| L5 Read in Daily Life | **Social Interpersonal** | a museum gallery notice and a specimen label |
| L5 Academic Passage | Academic | 185 words on beak depth after a drought |
| ON CAMPUS | Academic Navigational | asking a demonstrator for the data sheet |
| Teamwork | Academic | a discussion board on whether zoos conserve or confine |

So the everyday, A1–B2 end of the test still gets taught — Listen and Choose a
Response and Read in Daily Life are Social Interpersonal by specification — but
it is student life around an academic subject, not life skills instead of one.

## AT WORK becomes ON CAMPUS

The template is kept exactly — gist · tick what is mentioned · listen again for a
deeper point · a *Do this / Say this* function chart · partner application. Only
the register moves, from workplace to campus, because that is where Academic
Navigational sits. The four functions confirmed from the book are re-aimed
rather than replaced:

| # | Book's function | Becomes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | show that you are listening | show that you are following a lecture |
| 4 | show a willingness to learn | ask for help without losing face |
| 5 | give a helpful suggestion | give a helpful suggestion in a group project |
| 11 | praise someone | acknowledge a classmate's contribution |

The remaining eight are lifted the same way from their own AT WORK pages when
each unit is authored.

This block is also where the two new listening genres live — **Listen to an
Announcement** and **Listen to an Academic Talk** — because its A/B/C trio
already clusters three items on one audio exactly as the test does.

## The morphology ladder, re-exemplified

The affix per unit is the book's. Only the examples change, from everyday words
to the academic vocabulary the same affix generates:

| # | Affix | Academic examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *dis-* | disagree, disprove, disconfirm, disregard |
| 2 | *-ful* | harmful, powerful, meaningful, doubtful |
| 3 | *-tion* | adaptation, variation, selection, population |
| 4 | *inter-* | interact, interbreed, intercontinental, interpret |
| 5 | *un-* | unmanned, unstable, unexplored, unlikely |
| 6 | (phrasal verbs instead — the book sets no affix here) | break down, take in, give off, build up |
| 7 | *-al* | marginal, seasonal, capital, financial |
| 8 | *-re* | recover, recurrence, resistant, reinfect |
| 9 | *-er / -or* | commuter, conductor, indicator, regulator |
| 10 | *-ment* | judgment, enforcement, argument, entitlement |
| 11 | *-ment* | measurement, experiment, assessment, instrument |
| 12 | word stress in nouns and verbs | ˈrecord / reˈcord · ˈcontrast / conˈtrast · ˈconduct / conˈduct |

Unit 1's *dis-* is a gift: *disagree, disprove, disconfirm* is the vocabulary of
research, and it is where the book already puts it.

## The pronunciation ladder, unchanged

*sh, ch, j* (1) · *j* and *ch* (2) · *v, b, f* (3) · two pronunciations of *th*
(4) · *I'm, I'd, I'll* (5) · linking in phrasal verbs (6) · linking words (7) ·
*z* and *s* in *use (to)* (8) · the schwa (9) · stressed words in sentences (10) ·
rising and falling intonation (11) · homophones (12).

Kept exactly. Three of these — the schwa, sentence stress and noun/verb word
stress — are the highest-value items in the book for TOEFL Speaking, and the
book already sequences them late, where they belong.

## The data visual, form by form

The form changes every unit, following the book's own sequence, because the test
can show the learner any of them:

| # | Form | Subject |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | line graph | retention over 14 days: re-reading against self-testing |
| 2 | pie | sources of global greenhouse emissions |
| 3 | bar | mean beak depth across finch populations before and after a drought |
| 4 | flow chart + organisational chart | a migration route; an excavation team's structure |
| 5 | scatter | planet mass against orbital distance |
| 6 | table | energy density and composition of the macronutrients |
| 7 | **pie with a key** | where public spending goes — the key is the taught skill |
| 8 | paired pies | leading causes of death, 1900 against today |
| 9 | comparison table | four transport modes by cost, time, capacity and emissions |
| 10 | line graph | offences reported against offences prosecuted, over time |
| 11 | bubble infographic | research funding by field, area encoding magnitude |
| 12 | pie | audience share by medium |

Every one carries a **source line** and its own **READER'S NOTE** strategy, as the
book's do. Two departures from the originals, both deliberate: the book's pies
are drawn in 3-D, which distorts the very areas the items ask the learner to
compare, so ours are flat; and the bubble chart keeps its figures printed inside
the circles, because area alone is not readable to the precision the items
demand.

## What this costs

Nothing structural. The grammar ladder, the affix ladder, the pronunciation
ladder, the chart sequence and all three lesson templates survive intact — which
is the whole claim of one-for-one correspondence. Every word of content is new,
which it had to be anyway.

What it buys is that a learner finishing Unit 12 has read, heard, written and
spoken about twelve academic fields at B1–C2, instead of twelve areas of
American adult life at A2–B1.
