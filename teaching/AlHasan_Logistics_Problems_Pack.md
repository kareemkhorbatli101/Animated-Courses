**Level:** CEFR B1 — the register of Student Book 2. It sits on Unit 5 (*Shipping In and Clearing Customs*), Unit 6 (*Goods In: Quality, Quantity and Stock*) and Unit 9 (*When It Goes Wrong*), and can be taught after any of them.

## Text 1 — Twenty-five sacks that were not there

**Before you read:** Your delivery note says 1,200 sacks. You count 1,175. What do you do *before* the driver leaves?

Altintash buys barley feed from a supplier in Turkey, and the feed arrives in sacks on wooden pallets. Last Tuesday a lorry came to the Homs store with an order of 1,200 sacks: forty-eight pallets, twenty-five sacks on each pallet.

Ms. Dana was not at the store that morning. The storeman, Walid, was alone, and the driver was in a hurry. "I have another delivery at two o'clock," the driver said. "Please sign here." Walid walked around the lorry, counted the pallets quickly and signed the delivery note.

In the afternoon the team moved the sacks inside and counted them properly. There were forty-seven pallets, not forty-eight. The store had received 1,175 sacks, so twenty-five sacks were missing. At $18 a sack, that is $450.

The problem was not the money. The problem was the signature. Walid had signed a clean delivery note, and a clean delivery note says one thing: *everything arrived, and it arrived in good condition*. The supplier's office in Mersin answered the email the next day. "Our packing list says forty-eight pallets," they wrote, "and your man signed for forty-eight pallets."

Rami called Ms. Dana. "From today," he said, "nobody signs until the count is finished. If the driver cannot wait, he writes *not counted* on the note and we count after he goes. If anything is missing, we write the number on the note, we take photographs, and we send the claim the same day. A signature is not a formality. It is evidence."

**Comprehension**

1. How many sacks did Altintash order, and how many arrived?
2. Why did Walid sign the delivery note so quickly?
3. What does a clean delivery note tell the supplier?
4. Why was the supplier able to refuse the claim?
5. What two things does Rami now want the storeman to do if a driver cannot wait?
6. In your own words, what is the lesson of this text in one sentence?

**Words in the text.** Find the word or phrase that means:
a) a list of everything inside the shipment · b) a request for money because something went wrong · c) something that proves what happened · d) in a hurry, with no time.

**Talk about it.** The driver was not the thief and the supplier may not be either. Who do you think took the pallet — and how could Altintash find out?

## Text 2 — Rain on the road from Lattakia

**Before you read:** Goods arrive damaged. Three different companies touched them. Who pays?

In March, Chemco bought one hundred tonnes of steel coil from Westgate Metals — forty coils, two and a half tonnes each. The coils travelled by sea to Lattakia and then by road to the depot outside Damascus.

The ship arrived on a Thursday. The weather was bad all weekend, and the coils waited in the open yard at the port until Monday morning. When the lorries finally reached the depot, Ms. Dana climbed onto the first one and did not like what she saw. The plastic wrapping was torn on several coils, and underneath the wrapping the outer layers of steel were wet and orange with rust.

She stopped the unloading and called Rami. Then she did four things, in this order. She photographed every damaged coil, with the coil number visible in the picture. She wrote on the delivery note: *6 coils water-damaged on outer wraps — see photographs — goods accepted subject to inspection*. She asked the driver to sign her copy as well. And she called an independent surveyor, who came the next morning and wrote a report.

Six coils were damaged: fifteen tonnes of steel, worth $720 a tonne, so $10,800 of stock. The inner layers were probably clean, but no customer accepts a coil with rust on it without a discount.

Who pays? That is the difficult question. The wrapping may have been poor when it left the mill, which is the supplier's problem. The coils may have been left in the rain at the port, which is the carrier's problem. Or the damage may be covered by the cargo insurance, which is the insurer's problem. Without the photographs, the note on the delivery document and the surveyor's report, Chemco could prove nothing and would carry the loss itself.

**Comprehension**

1. How did the coils travel from Westgate Metals to the depot?
2. What did Ms. Dana see when she climbed onto the lorry?
3. Write down the four actions she took, in order.
4. Why is *goods accepted subject to inspection* better than simply signing the note?
5. Name the three parties who might be responsible for the damage.
6. What exactly would Chemco lose if Ms. Dana had done nothing?

**Words in the text.** Find the word that means:
a) the brown-orange damage that water does to steel · b) an independent person who examines goods and writes a report · c) the company that moves the goods · d) a reduction in the price.

**Talk about it.** Ms. Dana accepted the goods instead of refusing them. Was that right? What would happen to the depot if she refused every damaged delivery?

## Text 3 — Four free days, and then the clock starts

**Before you read:** A container sits at the port for eleven days. Why might that be expensive, even if nobody damages anything?

Maham was building a water tank at a site near Hama, and the valves for it came from Italy in three containers. The shipping line gave Al-Hasan four *free days*: four days to take the containers away from the port after the ship was unloaded. After four days, the line charges demurrage for the container, and the port charges storage for the space.

The containers were discharged at Lattakia on the 3rd. They left the port on the 14th. That is eleven days, so seven days were chargeable.

The arithmetic is simple and unpleasant. Demurrage was $85 a container a day: 7 × 85 = $595 for each container, and 3 × 595 = $1,785 for all three. Storage was another $40 a container a day: 7 × 40 = $280 each, so $840 for the three. Together, 1,785 + 840 = $2,625 — for goods that nobody touched and nobody damaged.

Why did the containers wait? Mr. Samir explained it to Mr. Tarek afterwards, and the explanation had three parts. The bank released the original documents two days late, so Komosh could not present them at customs on the first day. Then a public holiday closed the customs office. Then, when the office opened again, there were no low-loaders available in Lattakia, because the same holiday had stopped every other importer as well.

"And the site?" asked Mr. Tarek. Eng. Bilal had kept thirty men waiting for four days. That cost more than the demurrage.

"So what do we change?" said Mr. Tarek.

Mr. Samir had an answer ready. "We ask the line for seven free days in the contract, not four. We ask the bank to release documents against our undertaking, so we are not waiting on the post. And we book the transport before the ship arrives, not after."

**Comprehension**

1. What are *free days*, and how many did Al-Hasan have?
2. How many chargeable days were there, and how is that number calculated?
3. Show the arithmetic for the total cost of $2,625.
4. Give the three reasons the containers waited at the port.
5. Mr. Tarek says the real cost was larger than $2,625. Why?
6. What three changes does Mr. Samir propose?

**Words in the text.** Find the word or phrase that means:
a) money the shipping line charges when you keep its container too long · b) taken off the ship · c) a vehicle for carrying very heavy loads · d) a promise in writing.

**Talk about it.** Which of Mr. Samir's three changes would you make first, and why? Which one will the other company probably refuse?

## Text 4 — A piece of paper worth nine days

**Before you read:** Everything in the container is correct. One document is missing. What happens to the goods?

NorthBridge Industrial Trading shipped two packing machines to Bashak in September. The machines were correct, the invoice was correct, and the bill of lading was correct. The certificate of origin was not.

A certificate of origin says which country the goods were made in. Syrian customs needs it, because the duty on a machine from one country is not the same as the duty on a machine from another. Ms. Chen's office had issued the certificate, but it had not been stamped by the chamber of commerce, and without that stamp customs would not accept it.

So the machines stayed in the port. Storage ran at $60 a day. Mr. Samir had three options and he put all three to Rami in one email.

The first was to wait for a corrected certificate. NorthBridge could have it stamped and send it by courier: three days, plus $95. The second was to clear the goods under a bond — Al-Hasan would pay a guarantee to customs, take the machines away, and get the money back when the document arrived. That was faster but it tied up cash and it needed Lina's signature. The third was to pay the higher rate of duty, which customs applies when origin cannot be proved, and argue about it later. That was the fastest and the most expensive, and nobody liked it.

Rami chose the courier, and the machines were released on the ninth day: nine days of storage at $60 is $540, plus the $95 courier charge. He also wrote one line into the purchase order template that evening: *no shipment leaves the supplier until a scanned copy of every stamped document has been received and checked in Damascus*.

**Comprehension**

1. What was correct about the shipment, and what was wrong?
2. Why does Syrian customs need a certificate of origin?
3. What exactly was missing from the certificate?
4. Describe the three options Mr. Samir gave Rami.
5. How much did the delay cost in total, and how is it calculated?
6. What change did Rami make so that this does not happen again?

**Words in the text.** Find the word or phrase that means:
a) a tax paid on goods entering a country · b) money left with customs as a guarantee · c) a fast delivery service for documents · d) given permission to leave the port.

**Talk about it.** Option two was faster but used company cash. If you were Lina, what would you need to know before you signed for the bond?

## Text 5 — The right steel, the wrong grade

**Before you read:** Goods arrive on time, undamaged, in the right quantity — and they are still the wrong goods. How is that possible?

Maham ordered eighty tonnes of steel plate for the beams of a new workshop: grade S275, which is specified by the engineer because of the load the beams must carry. Westgate Metals confirmed the order, shipped on time and delivered to the site at Hama without a mark on the plate.

Eng. Bilal read the mill certificate, which is the document that states what the steel actually is. It said S235.

S235 is good steel. It is simply not as strong as S275, and the beams were designed for S275. Eng. Bilal could not use it, and he said so before a single plate was cut: once steel is cut, nobody takes it back.

Mr. Delgado's answer came the same evening and it was honest. The mill had run out of S275 and had substituted S235 "of equivalent commercial value". Nobody at Westgate had asked Al-Hasan first, and that was the whole problem — not the steel, but the silence.

Three solutions were discussed. Westgate could take the plate back, which meant return freight, a long delay to the site, and a second customs process. Westgate could offer a credit: the price difference was $40 a tonne, so 80 × 40 = $3,200. Or Maham could keep the plate and use it where S235 is enough — floor plates, platforms, stairs, guarding — and order S275 again for the beams.

Mr. Tarek chose the third, with the credit, and added one sentence to the contract with Westgate: *no substitution of grade, size or origin without written approval from Damascus, whatever the commercial value*.

**Comprehension**

1. What was right about this delivery? Give three things.
2. What document told Eng. Bilal there was a problem, and what did it say?
3. Why did Eng. Bilal stop the work before any plate was cut?
4. According to Mr. Delgado, why did the mill send S235?
5. What does the text say was "the whole problem"? Explain it in your own words.
6. Which solution was chosen, and what else did Mr. Tarek do?

**Words in the text.** Find the word or phrase that means:
a) the quality or strength class of a material · b) put one thing in the place of another · c) money given back or taken off a future invoice · d) the paper from the factory that states what the material is.

**Talk about it.** Westgate did not lie — they shipped steel of "equivalent commercial value". Was it still wrong? What should a supplier do when it cannot supply exactly what was ordered?

## Text 6 — The number on the seal

**Before you read:** What is a container seal, and why does its number matter more than the lock itself?

When a container is loaded and closed, a numbered seal is fixed to the door. The number goes onto the bill of lading. If the number on the door when the container arrives is the same as the number on the document, nobody has opened it on the way. If the numbers are different, somebody has.

In February a container of lubricant oil arrived at the Chemco depot from Lattakia: eighty drums, two hundred litres each. The bill of lading said seal KM-4471. The seal on the door said KM-4530.

Ms. Dana did not open the container. She photographed the seal and the door, she called Mr. Samir, and she called Rami. Only when the three of them agreed, with the driver standing beside her and a second witness from the office, did she cut the seal and open the doors.

Seventy-seven drums. Three were missing, at $340 a drum: 3 × 340 = $1,020.

What followed was slow. A police report was made at the depot the same afternoon. The carrier's office said the container had been opened by customs at the border for an inspection, and that customs had fitted the new seal — which may well be true, and which the carrier had to prove, because the inspection was not written anywhere on the documents. The insurance claim was submitted with the photographs, the police report and the signed statement of the second witness.

"Three drums is a small loss," Rami told the team afterwards. "The method is not small. Photograph the seal before anyone touches the door. Never open a container with a wrong seal number alone. And write the seal number on the goods-received note every single time, even when it matches — especially when it matches."

**Comprehension**

1. What is the purpose of a container seal number?
2. What did the bill of lading say, and what was on the door?
3. Why did Ms. Dana not open the container immediately?
4. Who did she want present when the doors were opened?
5. What explanation did the carrier give, and why could it not simply be accepted?
6. Why does Rami want the seal number written down even when it is correct?

**Words in the text.** Find the word or phrase that means:
a) a person who sees something happen and can describe it · b) an official examination of goods · c) sent in, for a decision · d) a container for liquids, holding 200 litres.

**Talk about it.** Rami says "three drums is a small loss, the method is not small". What does he mean? Give an example of a bigger loss that the same method would prevent.

## Conversation 1 — A driver with no papers

*Ms. Dana (Quality, the depot) and a driver, at the depot gate, 9.15 a.m.*

**Driver:** Good morning. Fifteen pallets of cement for Al-Hasan.

**Ms. Dana:** Good morning. Can I see the delivery note, please?

**Driver:** I don't have it. The office gave me the load and told me to come here.

**Ms. Dana:** No delivery note at all? Not even a loading slip?

**Driver:** Nothing. Only the address on my phone.

**Ms. Dana:** Then I can't book these in. I don't know who the supplier is, what the order number is, or how many pallets should be on your lorry.

**Driver:** There are fifteen. You can count them.

**Ms. Dana:** I can count fifteen pallets. I can't count what I was *supposed* to receive. That's a different number and it's the one that matters.

**Driver:** Please. I've been driving since five o'clock.

**Ms. Dana:** I understand, and I'm not sending you away. Here's what we'll do. Pull over to bay four. I'll call Chemac and ask them to email the delivery note and the order number now. If it comes in twenty minutes, we unload and you sign my copy.

**Driver:** And if it doesn't come?

**Ms. Dana:** Then you wait, or you take the cement back. I'm sorry. I can't sign for goods I can't identify.

**Driver:** All right. Bay four.

**Ms. Dana:** Thank you. The kettle's in the office — go and sit down while I call.

**Comprehension**

1. What is the driver delivering, and how much of it?
2. What is missing, and what does the driver have instead?
3. Ms. Dana says she can count fifteen pallets but not something else. What?
4. What solution does she offer, and what are the two possible outcomes?
5. Find one thing Ms. Dana says or does that keeps the conversation polite.

**Useful phrases.** *Can I see … , please?* · *I can't book these in.* · *Here's what we'll do.* · *I'm sorry, I can't sign for …*

**Role-play.** A supplier's driver arrives with a delivery note for twelve pallets and twelve pallets on the lorry — but the order number on the note belongs to a different company in the Group. Student A is the driver, Student B is on the gate.

## Conversation 2 — Four o'clock on a Thursday

*Fadi (Admin, head office) and Mr. Samir (Komosh), by telephone.*

**Fadi:** Samir, the lorry from the port is still on the road. The driver says he'll reach the depot at about half past four.

**Mr. Samir:** On a Thursday? The depot closes at five.

**Fadi:** I know. That's thirty minutes to unload twenty-two pallets.

**Mr. Samir:** It can't be done safely in thirty minutes, and I'm not asking the men to do it. What's in it?

**Fadi:** Fuel additives for Chemco. Nothing fragile, nothing cold.

**Mr. Samir:** Then it can wait until Sunday morning. The question is where the lorry waits.

**Fadi:** The driver won't like that.

**Mr. Samir:** No, he won't, and he'll be right — it's not his fault that the ship was late. Let me give you three choices. One: he parks inside our yard tonight, with the load sealed, and we unload first thing Sunday. Two: he goes back to the transport company's yard in Harasta and returns Sunday, and somebody pays for that trip. Three: we open the depot for two hours on Friday morning with three men, and we pay the overtime.

**Fadi:** Which do you want?

**Mr. Samir:** The first, if the yard has room — it's the cheapest and the load doesn't move twice. But Dana decides, not me. It's her yard and her security.

**Fadi:** I'll call her now. And Samir — why did nobody tell us this morning that the ship was late?

**Mr. Samir:** That's the better question. I'll find out.

**Comprehension**

1. What time will the lorry arrive, and why is that a problem?
2. Why does Mr. Samir refuse to unload in thirty minutes?
3. What are the three choices he gives Fadi?
4. Which does he prefer, and for what two reasons?
5. Mr. Samir says Fadi's last question is "the better question". Why?

**Useful phrases.** *It can't be done safely in …* · *Let me give you three choices.* · *It's the cheapest and the load doesn't move twice.* · *That's the better question.*

**Role-play.** A refrigerated lorry — *nothing cold* is no longer true — arrives at 4.30 p.m. Student A wants it unloaded tonight; Student B must decide and explain.

## Conversation 3 — Too heavy for the forklift

*Ms. Dana and Walid (storeman), in the yard.*

**Walid:** Ms. Dana, come and look at this crate.

**Ms. Dana:** That's the packing machine from NorthBridge. What's wrong?

**Walid:** It's on the plate. Four thousand two hundred kilos.

**Ms. Dana:** And our forklift lifts three thousand.

**Walid:** Exactly. I'm not touching it.

**Ms. Dana:** Good. Don't. Who booked this delivery?

**Walid:** The office, last week. The order says "machine, one crate". It doesn't say the weight.

**Ms. Dana:** It should. Is the lorry a flatbed or a curtain-sider?

**Walid:** Flatbed. So a crane can reach it from the side — if we can get a crane.

**Ms. Dana:** Maham has a mobile crane at the Hama site. Call Eng. Bilal and ask when it's free. If it's free tomorrow, the lorry waits in the yard overnight and we pay the driver's standing time. If it isn't, we hire one in Damascus.

**Walid:** And today?

**Ms. Dana:** Today nothing moves. Put cones round the lorry so nobody tries to be helpful.

**Walid:** Right. And next time?

**Ms. Dana:** Next time the purchase order carries the weight of every single item, and goods-in sees it before the lorry leaves the supplier. If a crate weighs more than three tonnes, we arrange the lifting *before* it arrives, not while a driver is sitting in his cab watching us.

**Comprehension**

1. How heavy is the crate, and what can the forklift lift?
2. Why does Ms. Dana say "good" when Walid refuses to move it?
3. What information was missing from the order?
4. What are the two ways of getting a crane, and which is tried first?
5. What rule does Ms. Dana make for the future?

**Useful phrases.** *I'm not touching it.* · *Who booked this delivery?* · *Today nothing moves.* · *Next time the purchase order carries …*

**Role-play.** Student A is the driver, who has another job at two o'clock and wants to leave the crate on the ground. Student B explains why that is not possible.

## Conversation 4 — Labels nobody can read

*Ms. Maya (Alten IL, retail) and Rami (Bashak, imports), by telephone.*

**Ms. Maya:** Rami, I have a problem in the Mezzeh shop. The cleaning liquid you imported — the labels are in Turkish.

**Rami:** All of it?

**Ms. Maya:** Six hundred bottles. Turkish on the front, Turkish on the back. No Arabic anywhere.

**Rami:** The product is correct, though?

**Ms. Maya:** The product is perfect. I can't sell it. The law says the safety information has to be in Arabic, and even if it didn't, a customer in Mezzeh is not going to buy a bottle of chemicals she can't read.

**Rami:** Fair. What are our options?

**Ms. Maya:** Three. The supplier sends Arabic labels and we stick them on — that's six hundred bottles by hand, maybe two days with four people. Or we print them here, which is faster but then the label is ours, and so is the responsibility if it's wrong. Or we send the whole lot back.

**Rami:** Sending it back costs more than the goods are worth.

**Ms. Maya:** I thought you'd say that.

**Rami:** Let's do the first. I'll ask Ms. Chen for the Arabic artwork today and for printed labels by courier. We don't pay for them and we don't pay the labour either — their specification sheet promised "labelled for the Syrian market".

**Ms. Maya:** It says that?

**Rami:** In writing. It's the only reason this conversation is short.

**Comprehension**

1. What is wrong with the six hundred bottles?
2. Give the two reasons Ms. Maya cannot sell them.
3. What are her three options, and what is the risk in the second one?
4. Why does Rami refuse the third option?
5. Why does Rami say the conversation is short?

**Useful phrases.** *The product is perfect. I can't sell it.* · *What are our options?* · *I thought you'd say that.* · *It's the only reason this conversation is short.*

**Role-play.** Student A is Ms. Chen, who says the labels were correct when the goods left Turkey and the cost is Al-Hasan's. Student B is Rami, with the specification sheet in front of him.

## Conversation 5 — The right goods, the wrong town

*Huda (Accountant) takes a call from a customer, Mr. Faris.*

**Huda:** Good morning, Al-Hasan, Huda speaking.

**Mr. Faris:** Good morning. I'm calling about order 4471. Forty bags of waterproofing powder. They were delivered yesterday.

**Huda:** Yes, I can see the order. Is there a problem?

**Mr. Faris:** They were delivered to Homs. My site is in Hama.

**Huda:** I'm very sorry. Let me look … yes, I see it. The delivery address on the order is our Homs branch, not your site. That's our mistake, not yours.

**Mr. Faris:** It doesn't matter whose mistake it is. I have men waiting and no powder.

**Huda:** You're right. Then let's deal with today first and the paperwork afterwards. Two things. Our Homs store has the forty bags, and there's a van going from Homs to Hama at eleven. If I put them on it, they're with you by two o'clock. Is two o'clock in time?

**Mr. Faris:** Two is better than nothing.

**Huda:** Second: is there anything you need in the next three hours that you don't have? If there is, tell me now and I'll find it in Hama.

**Mr. Faris:** No. The powder is the last thing.

**Huda:** Then I'll call the Homs store now and call you back within fifteen minutes to confirm the van. And there'll be no delivery charge on this order.

**Mr. Faris:** Thank you. That's a reasonable answer.

**Huda:** Thank you for being patient. It shouldn't have happened.

**Comprehension**

1. What did Mr. Faris order, and what went wrong?
2. Whose mistake was it, and how does Huda handle that?
3. What does Huda mean by "let's deal with today first and the paperwork afterwards"?
4. What three things does she offer him?
5. Find the sentence where she checks for a problem the customer has not mentioned yet.

**Useful phrases.** *That's our mistake, not yours.* · *Let's deal with today first.* · *Is two o'clock in time?* · *It shouldn't have happened.*

**Role-play.** The same call, but the customer needs the powder at ten o'clock and the van leaves at eleven. Student A is the customer; Student B must offer something real.

## Conversation 6 — Who pays for the wet cement?

*Rami (Bashak) and Mr. Samir (Komosh), in the office.*

**Rami:** Samir, the claim came back. The insurer won't pay for the two damaged pallets.

**Mr. Samir:** On what grounds?

**Rami:** They say the damage happened after discharge, and our policy covers the goods to the port, not from it.

**Mr. Samir:** That sounds right, unfortunately. What were the terms on the order?

**Rami:** CIF Lattakia.

**Mr. Samir:** Then there's your answer. Under CIF the seller arranges the freight and the insurance as far as the port of discharge. Once the goods are discharged, the risk is ours.

**Rami:** And the pallets got wet in the port yard, three days after discharge.

**Mr. Samir:** Then it's ours. Not the seller's, not the carrier's — unless we can show the carrier was told to keep them under cover and didn't.

**Rami:** Can we show that?

**Mr. Samir:** I doubt it. I'll check the storage instruction, but I don't think there was one.

**Rami:** So we carry it. How much?

**Mr. Samir:** About four hundred dollars of cement. The lesson is worth more than that.

**Rami:** Go on.

**Mr. Samir:** Two things. First, buy the extra cover — an inland transit policy from the port to the depot costs very little and we don't have one. Second, stop leaving goods in the port yard for three days. Every day there is a day our insurance doesn't cover and the weather does what it likes.

**Rami:** Write me both, with the numbers, and I'll take it to Mr. Tarek on Sunday.

**Comprehension**

1. Why did the insurer refuse the claim?
2. What does CIF mean for where the seller's responsibility ends?
3. At what moment did the risk pass to Al-Hasan?
4. What is the one situation in which the carrier would be responsible?
5. What two changes does Mr. Samir propose, and which one costs very little?

**Useful phrases.** *On what grounds?* · *Then there's your answer.* · *So we carry it.* · *The lesson is worth more than that.*

**Role-play.** Student A thinks the supplier should pay anyway, "because they chose the port". Student B explains the terms. Use *under CIF …*, *once the goods are discharged …*, *unless we can show …*

# Answer Key

### Text 1
1. Ordered 1,200; 1,175 arrived (47 pallets × 25), so 25 sacks short. 2. The driver was in a hurry and had another delivery at two o'clock, and Walid was alone. 3. That everything arrived, and in good condition. 4. Because their packing list said 48 pallets and Al-Hasan's own man had signed for 48 — the signature was the evidence against them. 5. Write *not counted* on the note, and count after the driver leaves (then photograph, record the shortage and claim the same day). 6. Accept: *never sign for goods you have not counted — a signature is evidence, not a formality.*
**Words:** a) packing list · b) claim · c) evidence · d) in a hurry.

### Text 2
1. By sea from Westgate Metals to Lattakia, then by road to the depot near Damascus. 2. Torn plastic wrapping and wet, rusted outer layers on several coils. 3. Photographed every damaged coil with its number visible; wrote the damage on the delivery note; had the driver sign her copy; called an independent surveyor. 4. Because it records that the goods were received but not yet checked, so Chemco keeps the right to claim after a full inspection. 5. The supplier (poor wrapping), the carrier (left in the rain), the insurer (cargo insurance). 6. $10,800 — 6 coils × 2.5 tonnes × $720.
