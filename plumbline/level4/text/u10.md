# Unit 10 · A True Figure, Misleading
%%SUB A book from 1954 about charts in which every number is correct and the picture is still wrong%%
%%GOALS

A line rises steeply across the page. The numbers printed on it go from 102 to 106. Nobody lied: the
figures are the real ones, the chart was drawn accurately, and the impression it gives is false. A
small book from 1954 made this famous, and it is still the commonest trick on a published chart.

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: how a correct chart gives a false impression**
> **Source: Darrell Huff, *How to Lie with Statistics*, W. W. Norton, **1954**. Printed here in our
> own words.**
>
> The book is a short, plainly written popular book about the ways statistics and charts mislead. It
> has sold in enormous numbers and is where most people who know the phrase *truncated axis* learned
> it.
>
> Its best-known chapter names what it calls the **gee-whiz graph**: a chart whose vertical axis does
> not start at zero, so that a small change fills the page.
>
> | The device | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | the axis does not start at zero | a four percent rise looks like a doubling |
> | the vertical scale is stretched | the same, by a different route |
> | pictograms scaled by height | doubling the height quadruples the area |
> | the uneven horizontal interval | an irregular series looks like a trend |
>
> Every figure on such a chart can be correct. The chart is not a lie about the numbers; it is a lie
> about the **impression**, and the reader supplies the lie without being asked to.
>
> For a writer, two things follow. First, **read the axis before the line**: find where the vertical
> scale starts, and look at the first and last printed figures before choosing a verb. A line that
> fills the page may be a rise of four percent, and *rose sharply* would then be wrong. Second,
> **hedge what you cannot establish**: the figure *appears to show* an increase; the chart *suggests*
> a relationship. A chart does not prove a cause and your answer should not say it does.
>
> **A POPULAR POLEMIC, WITH NO DATA OF ITS OWN**
> The book is **not research**. It presents **no original data**, reports **no experiment**, and makes
> its case with **invented and anecdotal examples**, some of them unsourced. It is an entertainment
> and an argument, and the author does not claim otherwise.
>
> Its advice is also **not all sound by modern standards**: statisticians have argued that the strong
> form of *always start at zero* is wrong for some data, since a scale from zero can hide a change
> that genuinely matters, and that the honest answer is to **label the axis clearly** rather than
> obey a single rule.
>
> There is a further point readers should know. Published accounts of the author's later career record
> that he was **engaged by the tobacco industry** in the 1960s and prepared a manuscript applying the
> same arguments against research on smoking, which was never published. The 1954 book's arguments
> stand or fall on their own; who later found them useful is a separate fact, and a reason to notice
> that the technique of doubting a figure is available to anybody.
>
> And it says nothing about **examinations**. In an examination the figure is given to you and you
> cannot redraw it. What you can do is read the axis and choose the verb the numbers support.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year, with what it is and is not
> stated plainly.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Read the axis before you choose the verb.** A line that climbs from the bottom left to the top
> right of the page is not necessarily a sharp rise; it is a sharp rise only if the numbers say so.
> Look at the first printed figure, look at the last, work out roughly what the change is as a
> proportion, and then choose: *rose slightly*, *rose steadily*, *rose sharply*. Candidates lose marks
> for *rose dramatically* on a four percent change more often than for any other single word.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Truncated axes are everywhere, and not always dishonestly. Financial charts almost never start at
> zero, because a share price that moves two percent in a day is news and a chart from zero would show
> nothing. The convention differs by field: a scientific journal will insist on a marked break, a
> newspaper business page will not mark it at all. **Ask your group: where have you seen a chart whose
> axis did not start at zero? Was it trying to mislead you?**
