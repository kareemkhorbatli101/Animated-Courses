# Unit 7 · A Process Has Stages
%%SUB A standard from 1985 that fixed the shapes of a flow chart, and the English a process needs%%
%%GOALS
Describe a process in stages, in order
Use the passive without naming who acts
Say what a flow chart symbol means
Sequence with *once*, *after which*, *at this stage*
Say what a superseded standard is still good for
%%

No figures at all. A diagram of boxes and arrows, and you have to say what happens, in what order,
and to what. The shapes are not an accident: somebody standardized them in 1985, and knowing what a
diamond means is the difference between describing a process and guessing at one.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard number, its title and the year, a four-row table of symbols and meanings, a boxed paragraph headed withdrawn, and still in use, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: where the shapes on a process diagram come from**
> **Source: ISO 5807, *Information processing — Documentation symbols and conventions for data,
> program and system flowcharts, program network charts and system resources charts*, first edition
> **1985**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Flow charts had been drawn for decades before anybody agreed on the shapes. The 1985 standard is
> the version that settled it internationally: a published set of symbols, each with a defined
> meaning, so that a chart drawn in one country could be read in another.
>
> | The symbol | What the standard assigns to it |
> |---|---|
> | rectangle | a **process**: an operation is performed |
> | diamond | a **decision**: the flow divides according to a condition |
> | parallelogram | **data**: input or output |
> | rounded box | a **terminator**: the start or the end |
>
> The standard also fixes the conventions around the symbols: that flow runs **top to bottom and left
> to right** unless an arrowhead says otherwise, that a **connector** is a small circle joining a line
> that continues elsewhere, and that each symbol carries a short text inside it rather than a number
> explained in a key.
>
> For a reader, two of these matter more than the rest. The **diamond** tells you a process is not a
> straight line: at that point it does one thing or another. And an arrow that **returns to an earlier
> symbol** tells you the process **repeats** — which is a fact about the process, not a detail of the
> drawing.
>
> **WITHDRAWN, AND STILL IN USE**
> The standard has since been **withdrawn**: it is no longer a current international standard, and
> modeling notations published later are what formal process documentation now uses. Quoting it as
> current would be wrong.
>
> What it does establish is **where the shapes came from** and that their meanings were **agreed**
> rather than invented by whoever drew your diagram. Most process diagrams in general use follow
> these conventions loosely, by inheritance; many do not follow them at all, and a diagram in an
> examination booklet is under **no obligation** to.
>
> So read the shapes as a **strong hint** and the labels as the authority. If a diagram uses a diamond
> with one arrow out of it, the diamond is decorative and the label is what you describe.
>
> And the standard says nothing whatever about **English**. It assigns meanings to shapes. The passive
> voice, the sequencing words and the question of whether to name an agent are this unit's business,
> not the standard's.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its edition and its year, and with its current
> status stated.*

### 1 · The passive, in a process

| | |
|---|---|
| The material **is collected** and **sorted**. | present passive, two verbs |

### 2 · Sequence

| | |
|---|---|
| **First**, the material is collected. | the start |
| **Once** it has been sorted, it is washed. | one stage finishing |
| **After which**, it is inspected. | joining to what came before |

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **A process task has no numbers, so it has no numbers to hide behind.** Everything depends on the
> order, the passive, and whether you noticed the loop. Count the stages before you write. Find every
> diamond. Then write the overview as a count and a shape — *the process has eight stages, two of
> which involve a decision, and one stage is repeated until the item passes* — and work through in
> order from there.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Flow charts travel. Because the shapes were standardized, a chart drawn in one language can be read
> by somebody who speaks none of it: the rectangle, the diamond and the arrow do the work. That is the
> whole purpose of a symbol standard, and it is why so much technical documentation is drawn rather
> than written. **Ask your group: where have you seen a diagram you could read without knowing the
> language it was labeled in? What made it readable?**
