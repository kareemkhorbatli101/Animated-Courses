# Unit 3 · Making a Number Visible
%%SUB A report from 1858, a diagram drawn to change a policy, and what encoding a quantity as area costs%%
%%GOALS
Describe a proportion accurately
Say what a part-whole figure shows and what it hides
Use fractions, percentages and *accounted for*
Make the verb agree with a proportion
Say why a diagram drawn to persuade can still be sound
%%

In 1858 a report on the army's dead divided them by cause and drew them as areas, so that the
largest cause could not be argued with. The diagram worked. It also encodes quantity in the way the
eye reads least accurately, which is Unit 4.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the report and the year, a description of the diagram, a three-row table of the categories, a boxed paragraph headed drawn to persuade, and read by the eye least accurately, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: a diagram that changed a policy, and what it cost to draw it that way**
> **Source: Florence Nightingale, *Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency and Hospital
> Administration of the British Army*, London, **1858**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The report was written after the Crimean War and ran to several hundred pages of tables. Inside it
> are diagrams that are now among the most reproduced in the history of statistics.
>
> Each diagram is a circle divided into **twelve segments**, one for each month. Each segment is
> divided into bands showing the deaths in that month by **cause**: deaths from **preventable
> disease**, deaths from **wounds**, and deaths from **other causes**. The size of each band is
> **proportional to the figure**, so the shape of the year is visible at once.
>
> What the shape shows is the argument of the whole report. Deaths from **preventable disease** vastly
> outnumbered deaths from wounds. The war's dead were, in the main, not killed by the enemy.
>
> The figures had been published before, in tables. The tables had changed nothing. The author's
> judgment was that a picture would be looked at by people who would not read a column of numbers,
> and the judgment was correct: the report is part of the history of army medical reform.
>
> **DRAWN TO PERSUADE, AND READ BY THE EYE LEAST ACCURATELY**
> This is a **document with a case to make**, by somebody who intended to change a policy and did. It
> is not a neutral presentation of data and does not pretend to be. That does not make it dishonest;
> it makes it an argument, and an argument has to be judged on whether its figures support it.
>
> The design has been **criticized ever since**, on a specific ground that matters for this book. The
> quantity is encoded as **area**. Unit 4 of this book is about a set of experiments showing that the
> eye judges **length** and **position** far more accurately than it judges **area** — so two segments
> of similar size are hard to compare, and a reader can easily mistake a doubling of the radius for a
> doubling of the quantity when it is in fact a quadrupling of the area. The diagram is excellent at
> showing **one** overwhelming comparison and poor at showing **small** ones.
>
> The underlying data has limits of its own. The **classification of causes of death** in army records
> of that period was unreliable: the categories were applied by different hands under conditions that
> made careful recording impossible, and a death recorded under one cause might have been recorded
> under another elsewhere. The argument survives this, because the difference it rests on is very
> large. A smaller difference would not have survived it.
>
> And it is a **historical document about one war**, not a general method. Nobody should draw a
> polar-area diagram today because this one worked.
>
> *Our summary of a published report. Reproductions of the diagram are freely available: look at one,
> and then look at Unit 4.*

### 2 · Agreement with a proportion

| | |
|---|---|
| **A quarter of the deaths were** from wounds. | *deaths* is plural → *were* |
| **A quarter of the total was** from wounds. | *total* is singular → *was* |
| **Seventy percent of the cases were** preventable. | plural |
| **Seventy percent of the figure was** preventable. | singular |
| **The remainder consisted of** other causes. | *remainder* is singular |

**2A  Choose the verb and say the sentence aloud.**

1 About a third of the segments ( is / are ) larger than the rest.
2 Sixteen percent of the total ( was / were ) from wounds.
3 Half of the cases ( was / were ) recorded in two months.
4 The remainder ( consist / consists ) of other causes.
5 A quarter of the figure ( is / are ) unaccounted for.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL4-03-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students write *a quarter of the deaths was* and *seventy percent of
the cases was*, because they make the verb agree with the fraction rather than with the noun after
*of*. **Say your partner four proportions, two with a plural noun and two with a singular one, and let
them choose the verb.**

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Give the largest and the smallest, and then compare two.** A part-whole figure needs three things:
> which share is biggest, which is smallest, and one comparison between two of them with figures

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Figures that are drawn to persuade are everywhere: in advertising, in annual reports, in government
> publications, in campaigns anybody would support. A chart made to change a policy is not dishonest
> for being made to change a policy, and a chart made by a neutral body is not honest for being
> neutral. What settles it is whether the figures carry the claim. **Ask your group: when did you last
> see a chart designed to persuade you? Did the figures support what it was asking you to believe?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG qd03-f16 | A desk in the afternoon. A reproduction of the 1858 diagram lies open beside a stacked bar of the same three categories, a sheet of five shares drawn four different ways, a page of estimates with errors marked, and a cup. A clock shows three.%%

**The class estimated two small shares from four different figures.** From the stacked bar their
estimates were within two points of the real figure. From the circle of areas they were out by up to
nine, and four students put the two shares in the wrong order.

The 1858 diagram makes one comparison impossible to miss and another almost impossible to make.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 The diagram succeeded completely at what it was for. Does the criticism matter?
2 Four students reversed two shares. Would that have mattered in 1858?
3 If you had to make the 1858 argument today, what would you draw?
4 Is a figure that is excellent for one comparison and poor for another a good figure?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 4 is the experiments the criticism rests
on, and they are the most useful thing in this book for choosing what to say about a chart.
