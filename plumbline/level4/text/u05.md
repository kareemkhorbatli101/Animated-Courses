# Unit 5 · Four Data Sets, One Average
%%SUB A paper from 1973 with four sets of numbers that have the same statistics and nothing else in common%%

Four sets of eleven pairs of numbers. The same mean, the same variance, the same correlation, the
same line through them. Drawn, they look nothing like each other. Somebody built them in 1973 to
make one point, and the point is the reason this task exists.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a table of the shared statistics, a four-row table of the shapes, a boxed paragraph headed constructed on purpose, and a short argument rather than a study, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what a single number cannot tell you**
> **Source: F. J. Anscombe, *Graphs in Statistical Analysis*, The American Statistician, **1973**.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper is short and its argument is one sentence: **compute and also graph**. What makes it famous
> is the demonstration.
>
> The author gives **four data sets**, each of **eleven pairs** of numbers. He arranged them so that
> the usual summary figures come out the same for all four.
>
> | The statistic | The value, for all four sets |
> |---|---|
> | mean of x | 9 |
> | mean of y | 7.5 |
> | variance of x | 11 |
> | correlation between x and y | about 0.82 |
> | the line fitted through the points | y = 3 + 0.5x |
>
> Anybody working from that table alone would report four similar relationships. Drawn, the four sets
> look nothing like each other.
>
> The **first** is a loose, roughly straight cloud. The **second** is a smooth **curve**, which no
> straight line should ever have been fitted to. The **third** is a tight straight line with **one
> point a long way above it** — remove that point and the relationship is almost perfect. The
> **fourth** has every point at the same value of x except **one**, which sits far to the right and is
> single-handedly producing the whole correlation.
>
> The lesson is the one this book keeps returning to. A summary figure answers one narrow question. It
> does not tell you the **shape**, and in these four cases the shape is the entire story.
>
> **CONSTRUCTED ON PURPOSE, AND A SHORT ARGUMENT RATHER THAN A STUDY**
> The four data sets are **not observations**. They were **built** to have matching statistics and
> different shapes, which is exactly what makes the demonstration work and exactly what it cannot be
> used for: nobody should claim that real data behaves like this, or that a correlation of 0.82 is
> generally untrustworthy.
>
> It is a **short paper in a teaching journal**, an argument and a demonstration, not an empirical
> study. It reports no experiment and measures nobody.
>
> The moral was **not new in 1973**, and the author does not say it is. Statisticians had been saying
> *look at your data* for a long time; what the paper supplied was a demonstration short enough and
> sharp enough to be remembered, which is a different kind of contribution.
>
> And it is about **statistical analysis**, not about examinations. There is nothing in it about
> describing a chart in a writing task; what it gives you is a reason why that task is worth setting
> at all.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. The four plots are everywhere: look at them, then look away and
> try to describe them from the table.*

### 2 · Concession

| | |
|---|---|
| **Although** the averages are identical, the shapes differ. | *although* + a clause |
| **Despite** the identical averages, the shapes differ. | *despite* + a noun phrase |
| **In spite of** having the same mean, the sets differ. | *in spite of* + noun or *-ing* |
| The averages are identical. **Nevertheless**, the shapes differ. | a new sentence |
| The averages are identical, **yet** the shapes differ. | plainer |

**2A  Rewrite each sentence with the word in brackets.**

1 The statistics match. The plots do not. (although)
2 They have the same correlation. They have different shapes. (despite)
3 The line fits all four sets. Only one of them is straight. (in spite of)
4 The figures are identical. The stories are not. (nevertheless)
5 The mean is 7.5 in every case. The spread is quite different. (yet)

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL4-05-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students write *despite the averages are identical* and *although the
identical averages*. **Despite** and **in spite of** take a noun; **although** takes a clause. **Say
your partner four concessions and let them put the right word in.**

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **If you are given a table, the average is the least interesting figure in it.** The row of totals
> or averages at the foot is the easiest thing to write about and the thing every other candidate will
> write about. The marks are in the rows: the highest, the lowest, the one that moves against the
> others, the one that barely changes. Write the average once, in the overview, and then leave it
> alone.
