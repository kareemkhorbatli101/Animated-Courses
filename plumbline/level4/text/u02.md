%%SUB An atlas from 1786, the picture that replaced a table, and the claims its own data could not support%%

Before 1786, numbers over time lived in tables. One man drew them as a line, argued that a picture
could be understood faster than a column of figures, and was right. He also wrote, underneath his own
graphs, conclusions the graphs could not carry.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the atlas, the place and the year with the third edition date beneath, a description of two plates, a short note on the bar chart, a boxed paragraph headed an innovator and a promoter, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: where the line graph came from**
> **Source: William Playfair, *The Commercial and Political Atlas*, London, **1786**; the enlarged
> third edition of **1801** is the one usually reproduced. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Before this book, figures that changed over time were printed as **tables**. If you wanted to know
> whether imports had overtaken exports, you compared two columns of numbers with your finger.
>
> The atlas did something new. It plotted the figures as **lines** on a pair of axes — time along the
> bottom, value up the side — so that the shape of a century of trade could be taken in at a glance,
> and the **gap** between two lines became a visible area rather than a column of subtractions. These
> are generally regarded as the first statistical **line graphs** in print.
>
> The author's argument for doing it is the argument every chart has rested on since: a picture of
> data can be **grasped faster** than the same data in a table, and it can be remembered.
>
> The atlas also contains a **bar chart** — and the reason it does is worth knowing. For Scotland's
> trade he had figures for **one year only**, with no series over time. With no time axis to plot
> against, he drew a bar for each trading partner instead. The bar chart exists because of **missing
> data**, not because its inventor thought bars were better than lines.
>
> **AN INNOVATOR AND A PROMOTER**
> The man who invented these graphs is also a warning, and the warning is the reason this unit is the
> second in the book rather than a footnote.
>
> He was a **promoter** as well as an engineer: a writer of pamphlets with a case to make about trade
> and national strength. Under and around his own plates he drew **political conclusions his data
> could not establish** — about which policies had caused what, and about the condition of the nation.
> The lines show what the figures did. The commentary says why, and the why is not in the lines.
>
> The **provenance** of some of his figures is uncertain. He worked from the published accounts of his
> period, those accounts are known to be incomplete, and in places he appears to have **estimated** or
> **smoothed** to produce a continuous line where the record had holes in it. A continuous line is a
> claim that there was a continuous series.
>
> And the word **first** should be handled carefully. Historians of graphics have found earlier
> plottings of quantities against time, and attributions of priority are argued about. What is not in
> doubt is that this atlas made statistical graphics a public method.
>
> *Our summary of a published atlas. Reproductions of the plates are freely available: look at one.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Statistical graphics spread across the world from a handful of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century
> books, and the conventions they fixed — time along the bottom, value up the side, more is higher —
> are now so familiar that they look like nature. They are not: they are decisions, made by particular
> people for particular arguments, and a reader who grew up with a different convention reads a chart
> more slowly. **Ask your group: in your first language, do charts read left to right? Have you ever
> met a chart you could not read because of its layout?**
