# Unit 8 · A Map Is a Selection
%%SUB A book from 1991 that starts by saying every map is a lie, and then explains why it has to be%%
%%GOALS
Describe where something is, and what moved
Describe change between two maps of one place
Use prepositions of place accurately
Say what a map has left out
Say why a selection is not the same as a distortion
%%

Two maps of the same town, twenty years apart. Nothing to measure, no line going up, no bars to
compare. What you have to say is what is where, what has appeared, what has gone, and what moved —
and the first problem is that both maps have already decided what you are allowed to see.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, three short definitions in bold, a four-row table of what a map leaves out, a boxed paragraph headed a book about making maps, not describing them, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what a map has already decided for you**
> **Source: Mark Monmonier, *How to Lie with Maps*, University of Chicago Press, **1991**. Printed
> here in our own words.**
>
> The book opens with an argument that sounds like a provocation and is not one: **every map must
> distort reality**, and a map that did not would be useless.
>
> The reasoning is simple. A map is **flat** and smaller than the thing it shows, so it has to
> **scale**, **generalize** and **select**. It cannot show every building, every tree, every pipe. Its
> maker decides what goes on it, and everything else is left off.
>
> | What every map does | What follows from it |
> |---|---|
> | reduces the scale | small things vanish |
> | generalizes shapes | a winding road becomes a smooth line |
> | selects features | what is not selected is invisible |
> | chooses symbols | symbol size is a decision, not a measurement |
>
> The book's warning is that these decisions are **invisible to the reader**. Nothing on a map says
> *the following four kinds of thing have been omitted*. The selection looks like the world.
>
> For a writer describing two maps, one point follows directly. **What is on the map is all you have,
> and it is not everything that was there.** The farmland that disappears between the first map and the
> second has not necessarily been destroyed; it may simply no longer be what the second map was drawn
> to show. Describe what the maps show. Do not explain what you cannot see.
>
> **A BOOK ABOUT MAKING MAPS, NOT DESCRIBING THEM**
> The book is addressed to **map users and map makers**, and most of it is about how maps mislead in
> advertising, in politics, in planning disputes and in defense. It is an **argument illustrated with
> examples**, not an experimental study: it does not test readers or measure how often anybody is
> misled.
>
> It says nothing about **examinations**, nothing about the two-map task, and nothing about English.
>
> And its strongest claim needs reading carefully. **To select is not to deceive.** The book does not
> say map makers are liars; it says that selection is unavoidable and therefore that a map can be
> honest and still mislead. Borrowing the title's word and dropping the argument gets it backwards.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The point about what a two-map task lets you say is ours, not the
> book's.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **A two-map task asks three questions and most candidates answer one.** They list what is new. What
> has gone is the second question and what has stayed is the third, and the third is the one almost
> nobody writes, because an unchanged feature feels like nothing to say. It is not nothing: it is a
> fact about the place, and in a description of change it is the only thing that gives the change a
> size.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Maps are political. A boundary drawn in one place and not another, a name in one language and not
