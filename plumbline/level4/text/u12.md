Eleven units of figures, charts, processes, maps and axes, and one small thing left over: how to
write a quantity on the page. There is a standard for it, published in 2022, and the surprise is how
much of it is about writing rather than about measuring.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard number, its title, its edition and the year, a five-row table of rules and examples, a boxed paragraph headed a standard about measurement, which turns out to be about writing, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: the rules for writing a number with a unit**
> **Source: ISO 80000-1, *Quantities and units — Part 1: General*, second edition, **2022** (first
> edition 2009). Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The standard's subject is **quantities and units**: what a quantity is, how the international system
> of units is built, and how quantities are written. Most of it is for scientists and engineers. A few
> pages are for anybody who writes a number down.
>
> | The rule | Written correctly |
> |---|---|
> | a **space** separates the numerical value from the unit symbol | 25 kg, 40 km/h, 15 % |
> | unit **symbols are not abbreviations**: no plural, no period | 25 kg (not *25 kgs*, not *25 kg.*) |
> | the **percent sign is the number 0.01**, so it is a unit-like symbol with a space | 15 % |
> | a **range** is written so it cannot be read as a subtraction | from 10 % to 20 % |
> | the **decimal sign** is a point or a comma, depending on the language | 1.5 or 1,5 |
>
> Behind the rules is one idea, and it is the useful one. A **quantity** is a numerical value
> **multiplied by** a unit. *25 kg* means twenty-five times one kilogram. That is why the space is
> there: it is a multiplication sign you are not printing.
>
> The same idea explains the percent sign. If **%** means the number **0.01**, then *15 %* is fifteen
> hundredths, and *an increase of 15 %* and *an increase of 15 percentage points* are different
> quantities, because the first is a proportion of something and the second is a difference between two
> proportions.
>
> For a writer, the practical list is short. Put a **space** before a unit. Do not pluralize a
> **symbol**. Write a **range** with *from* and *to*. And keep a **distinction** between a percent and
> a percentage point, because confusing them turns a small change into a large one and an examiner can
> see it happen.
>
> **A STANDARD ABOUT MEASUREMENT, WHICH TURNS OUT TO BE ABOUT WRITING**
> The standard is a **technical document** addressed to measurement science. It is **not a style guide
> for general prose**, it is **not free to obtain**, and in practice most publishers, newspapers and
> house styles **do not follow it**: *25kg* with no space and *15%* with no space are normal in
> published English and nobody is penalized for them.
>
> So it is a **convention**, not a rule of English. We teach it because it is **unambiguous**, because
> it is consistent with how quantities actually work, and because one of its four points — the
> difference between a percent and a percentage point — is **not a convention at all**, but a
> difference in meaning.
>
> And it says nothing about **examinations**, nothing about describing a figure, and nothing about how
> to choose a verb. There is no standard for any of that, which is what the other eleven units of this
> book were for.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its part number, its edition and both of its dates,
> with its status in general writing stated.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Units travel badly. A distance in miles or kilometers, a weight in pounds or kilograms, a temperature
> in Fahrenheit or Celsius, paper in A4 or letter: the figure in front of you may be in a unit you
> never use. The international system exists to make these comparable, and the 2022 standard is part of
