## How to use this book

This is the fourth book of six. It is about **one task**: Writing Task 1, in which you are given
information — a chart, a table, a diagram, a map — and asked to describe it in at least a hundred and
fifty words in about twenty minutes. Every unit is built on one real published document, named with
its edition and its date.

**Twelve units**, sixteen pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | One real published document, with its date and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · In the Test** | Clear English, a dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `PL4-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**It is built on the history and the science of graphics, not on phrase lists.** Most preparation
material for this task gives you a list of verbs — *rise, increase, climb, soar* — and a template.
This book gives you the 1786 atlas in which the line graph and the bar chart were invented, the 1858
diagram that made an army's mortality visible, the 1973 paper with four data sets that have identical
statistics and different shapes, the 1984 experiments on which kinds of comparison the eye does
accurately, and a 1954 book on how to mislead with a true figure.

**The task is a description, and a description can be false.** Everything in this book turns on one
distinction: what the chart **shows**, and what you have **concluded**. A conclusion the data does not
support is the commonest fault in a band-6 answer, and it is not a vocabulary problem.

**Twelve real sources, and twelve limits.** A 1786 atlas whose author also made claims his own data
did not support. A design argument that offers no experiment. Experiments on elementary perceptual
tasks in a laboratory. A standard for flowchart symbols that has been superseded in practice. A
principle of official statistics that governments sign and break.

**Nothing here is a past paper.** Every chart, table, map, diagram and prompt in this book was drawn
for it, and every figure in them is invented unless the text says where it came from.

**Money has no symbol in this series.** Every figure is written without a currency sign, because the
unit is whatever currency the chart is in — and a chart that does not say is a chart with a hole in
it.
