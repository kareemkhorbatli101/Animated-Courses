%%SUB Experiments from 1984, an ordered list of what a reader can judge, and what that means for what you say%%

Somebody asked a question nobody had asked experimentally: when a reader takes a quantity off a
graph, which kinds of comparison do they get right? The answer is an ordered list, and it tells you
which sentences in your answer a reader can check.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a seven-row ordered table of the perceptual tasks, a short description of the two experiments, a boxed paragraph headed a guide to design, not a law, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: which comparisons a reader can actually make**
> **Source: W. S. Cleveland and R. McGill, *Graphical Perception: Theory, Experimentation, and
> Application to the Development of Graphical Methods*, Journal of the American Statistical
> Association, **1984**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> When you read a quantity off a graph, you are doing one of a small number of jobs with your eyes.
> You might be comparing two **positions** on the same scale, or two **lengths**, or two **angles**, or
> two **areas**. The paper calls these **elementary perceptual tasks**, and asks which of them people
> do accurately.
>
> From theory and from experiment, the authors propose an **ordering**, most accurate first:
>
> | Rank | The task |
> |---|---|
> | 1 | position along a **common scale** |
> | 2 | position along **identical, non-aligned** scales |
> | 3 | **length** |
> | 4 | **direction** and **angle** |
> | 5 | **area** |
> | 6 | **volume** and curvature |
> | 7 | **color saturation** and **hue** |
>
> They then ran experiments. In one, participants compared positions and lengths; in another, angles
> and areas. Judgments of **position** were substantially more accurate than judgments of
> **length**, and judgments based on **position** were more accurate than those based on **angle** —
> which is the experimental basis of the familiar advice that a bar chart is easier to read than a pie
> chart of the same figures.
>
> **Why this matters in a writing task.** You are given a figure and asked to report its key features.
> The ordering tells you which features a reader can **verify** at a glance and which they cannot. A
> sentence comparing two bars on the same baseline rests on the most reliable judgment there is. A
> sentence comparing two similar-sized slices of a pie, or two circles sized by area, rests on the
> least reliable — so if you make such a comparison, **give the figures**, because the picture will not
> settle it.
>
> **A GUIDE TO DESIGN, NOT A LAW**
> The experiments used **specific stimuli** and a **specific group of participants**, doing
> **elementary** tasks under laboratory conditions. They are not a measurement of how anybody reads a
> real chart with a real question in mind.
>
> The ordering is built partly from **theory** and partly from a **small number of experiments**, and
> the authors present it as a **guide to designing graphs**, not as a law of perception. Later work has
> complicated parts of it: the ranking of particular tasks has been revisited, and the position of
> some encodings depends on the judgment being asked for.
>
> And it is about **accuracy of extraction only**. It says nothing about whether a figure is memorable,
> whether it is persuasive, or whether it is the right figure for the argument. The 1858 diagram in
> Unit 3 scores badly on this ordering and changed a policy.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. Read the ordering if nothing else; it is half a page.*

### 1 · Multiples and differences

| | |
|---|---|
| **twice as many as** · **three times as many as** | a multiple |
| **three times higher than** | a multiple, with an adjective |
| **ten points higher than** | a difference in the units |
| **ten percentage points higher than** | a difference, when the units are percentages |
| **an increase of ten percent** | a multiple of the original figure |

**Careful.** If a figure goes from 20 percent to 30 percent, it has risen by **ten percentage
points** and by **fifty percent**. Both are true and they are different sentences.

**1A  Describe each change in two ways. Then read both aloud with a partner.**

2 From 400 to 1,200.
3 From 15 to 12.
4 From 60 to 90.
5 From 2.5 million to 5 million.

### 2 · Contrast

| | |
|---|---|
| The first city grew, **whereas** the second fell. | a clause on each side |
| The first city grew, **while** the second fell. | the same; also means *at the same time* |
| The first city grew. **By contrast**, the second fell. | starts a new sentence |
| The first city grew, **but** the second fell. | plainer, and fine |
| **Unlike** the first city, the second fell. | *unlike* + a noun |

**2A  Join each pair with a different contrast word. Then read all five aloud.**

2 Housing accounted for a third. Clothing accounted for seven percent.

4 The town gained a railway station. It lost its market.
5 Two of the lines moved together. The third moved against them.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL4-04-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students write *whereas* at the start of a sentence with nothing on
the other side of it, and *by contrast* in the middle of a clause. **Say your partner four contrasts
and let them put each connector in the right position.**

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Some conventions in graphics are near-universal and some are not. More-is-higher, time-along-the-
> bottom and a shaded map for regional data travel almost everywhere; but three-dimensional charts,
> which the 1984 ordering places near the bottom, are popular in some publishing cultures and almost
> absent from others, and the same is true of thermometer-style and human-figure charts. A reader's
> accuracy depends partly on what they have practiced reading. **Ask your group: what kinds of chart
> appear most in newspapers in your country? Are any of them near the bottom of the ordering?**
