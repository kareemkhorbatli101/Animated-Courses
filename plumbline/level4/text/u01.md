Four criteria, applied to one answer, carrying equal weight. They are published, they are short, and
almost nobody reads them — which is why so many answers are strong in three of the four and have
never met the first.

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: the four criteria for Task 1**
> **Source: the public band descriptors for Academic Writing Task 1, published by the test owners.
> Downloaded 1 August. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Your answer is given a figure on **four criteria**, and the four carry **equal weight**.
>
> **Task Achievement** asks whether you have done the task you were given: described the information,
> selected and reported the **key features**, made comparisons where they are relevant, and written at
> least the stated minimum of **150 words**. An answer that is **under length** is penalized under
> this criterion. So is an answer that adds material the figure does not contain.
>
> **Coherence and Cohesion** asks whether the answer is organized: whether there is a progression,
> whether paragraphing helps, and whether the links between sentences work without being mechanical.
>
> **Lexical Resource** asks about the range and the accuracy of your vocabulary — whether you have
> enough words for the job and whether you use them correctly.
>
> **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** asks about the variety of your structures and how many errors
> there are.
>
> The descriptors run from band 0 to band 9 and describe, for each criterion at each band, the
> **features** a response at that level tends to show. They are descriptions of performance, not a
> list of points to be collected.
>
> **THE PUBLIC VERSION, AND WHAT IS NOT PUBLISHED**
> These are the **public** versions. That word is on the documents and it matters. Fuller descriptors
> are used in examiner training and in marking, and they are **not published**. This book has not seen
> them, does not know what is in them, and does not pretend otherwise. Anybody who tells you what the
> unpublished versions say is guessing.
>
> What the public descriptors do **not** give you is a method. They do not say how many paragraphs to
> write, where the overview goes, how many figures to quote, which verbs to use, or what a good
> answer looks like. They describe the features of a performance; turning that into a way of writing
> on a Tuesday is somebody else's job, and most of this book.
>
> They are also **revised from time to time**. The version you read is the version you read; quoting a
> descriptor without a date is quoting nothing.
>
> *Our summary of a published document. Read it yourself — it is two pages and it is free.*

### 1 · Writing an overview

An overview says what the whole figure shows. It contains no explanation and no opinion.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Overall, output fell over the period.** | one movement, the whole figure |
| **Overall, the two groups moved in opposite directions.** | a relation, not a cause |
| **The most striking feature is the gap after 1994.** | one named feature |
| ✗ *Overall, the chart shows some interesting changes.* | says nothing |
| ✗ *Overall, the fall was caused by the recession.* | not in the figure |

**1A  Write an overview sentence for each description. Then read it aloud.**

1 Three lines, all rising, one much faster than the others.
2 Four bars, one far taller than the rest.
3 Two lines crossing in the middle of the period.
4 A table in which every figure falls except one.
5 A process of seven stages, three of them repeated.

**1B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL4-01-08%%

**1C  Describe a figure to your partner in words only. They write the overview.**

### 2 · Saying what a figure shows, without saying why

| Describing | Explaining |
|---|---|
| The figure **fell** by a third. | ✗ The figure fell **because of** the crisis. |
| Output **remained** stable **until** 1998. | ✗ Output remained stable **thanks to** good management. |
| The two lines **crossed** in 2004. | ✗ The two lines crossed **as a result of** the new policy. |
| The third group was **the smallest throughout**. | ✗ The third group was **unfairly** treated. |
| Sales **recovered** to their earlier level. | ✗ Sales **fortunately** recovered. |

**2A  Rewrite each explaining sentence as a describing sentence.**

1 Numbers fell sharply in 1998 because of the new charges.
2 The figure rose steadily thanks to improved training.
3 Unfortunately, the third city never recovered.
4 The two lines crossed as a result of the change in policy.
5 Output was high, which shows the factory was well run.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL4-01-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students write fluent, confident sentences that the figure cannot
support, and lose marks under the first criterion while gaining them under the other three. **Say your
partner four explaining sentences and let them give you the describing version.**

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Describing data without explaining it is an unusual thing to ask. In most academic writing, and in
> most school essays anywhere in the world, a figure is introduced in order to make a point: you
> describe the fall and then you say what it means. This task asks for the first half and marks you
> down for the second, which is strange and is not a mistake in the task — it is a deliberately
> narrow instruction. **Ask your group: in your own subject, would you ever describe a chart without
> interpreting it? Who would ask for that, and why?**
