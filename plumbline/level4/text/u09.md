# Unit 9 · Two Effective Digits
%%SUB A paper from 1977 that says most of the digits in a published table are doing no work at all%%
%%GOALS
Round a figure and say you have rounded it
Approximate without losing accuracy
Say how much of a number a reader can use
Use *approximately*, *just over*, *around*, *nearly*
Say why a rule about tables is not a rule about everything
%%

A figure in a table reads 48,273,916. You have 150 words and twenty minutes. Nobody is going to copy
that number, and if you do copy it, nobody will remember it. One paper from 1977 worked out how much
of a number a reader can actually use, and the answer is smaller than you think.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a four-row table of the paper's advice, a worked example of one figure rounded twice, a boxed paragraph headed about tables, and about 1977, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: how many digits a reader can use**
> **Source: A. S. C. Ehrenberg, *Rudiments of numeracy*, Journal of the Royal Statistical Society,
> Series A, **1977**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper begins from an observation about published tables: they are **full of digits nobody
> reads**. A figure given to eight digits is read as its first two and the rest is noise.
>
> Its central recommendation is **two effective digits**: round each figure so that two digits are
> doing the varying, and put the magnitude in the column heading rather than in every cell.
>
> | The advice | Why |
> |---|---|
> | round to **two effective digits** | more digits are not read |
> | put the **unit at the top** | *millions* once, not in every cell |
> | **order rows by size** | the pattern becomes visible |
> | give a **row or column average** | each figure gets something to be compared with |
>
> A worked example. The figure **48,273,916** at two effective digits is **48** in a column headed
> *millions*. The figure **1,048** beside **1,052** is a harder case: the varying digits are the third
> and fourth, so rounding to *1.0* and *1.1* thousand throws the difference away. **Effective** digits
> are the ones that vary, not the first two on the page.
>
> The paper's argument is that **tables are for reading**, and that a table which cannot be read at a
> glance has failed at the only job it had, however accurate its contents.
>
> For a writer, the use is immediate. A figure you quote in an answer should be **short enough to be
> read once and held**: *approximately 48 million*, *just over 1,000*, *around a third*. Quoting
> 48,273,916 spends six words and gains the reader nothing.
>
> **ABOUT TABLES, AND ABOUT 1977**
> The paper is about **presenting data in tables**, written for a statistical readership nearly fifty
> years ago. It is a **paper of recommendations**, supported by examples and by the author's long
> experience of commercial data; it is **not a controlled experiment** on readers, and it does not
> measure how many digits people retain.
>
> The two-digit recommendation has been **disputed**, and the paper itself says it is a rule of thumb
> rather than a law. It is **wrong for some purposes**: financial accounts, engineering tolerances,
> scientific constants and anything legally binding need every digit they have. A rule for making a
> table legible is not a rule about numbers in general.
>
> And it says nothing about **examinations**. The reason this unit exists is that rounding sensibly is
> the fastest way to get more comparisons into 150 words without becoming inaccurate — which is this
> book's point, not the paper's.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its journal and its year, with the limits of its
> advice stated.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Rounding buys you words.** *Approximately 48 million* is three words; the full figure is six and
> the reader gets nothing extra. Across an answer that is the difference between four comparisons and
> seven. But round **honestly**: say *approximately* or *just over*, keep the rounding consistent down
> a column, and never round two figures to the same number when the task is asking you to compare
> them.

> How numbers are written differs from place to place. A comma marks the thousands in some countries
