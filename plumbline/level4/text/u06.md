# Unit 6 · Ink That Carries No Data
%%SUB A book from 1983 that asks, of every mark on a chart, whether it is telling you anything%%
%%GOALS
Say which parts of a figure carry data and which do not
Pack several figures into one sentence
Say what a decorated chart is hiding
Use *of which*, *with*, and the participle to compress
Say why a design argument is not an experiment
%%

A chart has grid lines, a shaded background, a three-dimensional bar, a frame, a legend, a pattern of
diagonal stripes. One book from 1983 asked how much of that is data. The answer changes what you
look at, and it changes what you write.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qd06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, three short defined terms in bold, a worked ratio, a boxed paragraph headed a design argument, not an experiment, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: how much of a figure is actually data**
> **Source: Edward R. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, Graphics Press,
> **1983**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book takes one question and asks it of every mark on a figure: **is this mark telling the reader
> a number?**
>
> It gives three terms that have gone everywhere since.
>
> **Data ink** is the ink that cannot be removed without losing a number. The bars, the plotted
> points, the figures printed on them.
>
> The ratio the book proposes is **data ink divided by all the ink on the figure**. It argues this
> proportion should be pushed as high as it will go: remove the frame, thin or delete the grid, drop
> the shading, label the thing itself instead of building a legend.
>
> **Chart junk** is the rest: heavy grids, shaded backgrounds, patterns of diagonal stripes,
> three-dimensional effects on flat data, decorative frames. A three-dimensional bar carries exactly
> one number, the same as a flat bar, using several times the ink.
>
> The **lie factor** is a second ratio: the size of the effect **as drawn** divided by the size of the
> effect **in the data**. If a figure doubles in height to show a rise of twenty percent, the drawing
> is overstating the change, and the ratio says by how much.
>
> For a writer, the practical use of the first idea is this: when you look at a figure, **look at the
> data ink and ignore the rest**. The decoration is not information and it will not earn you a mark.
> And the lie factor is a warning about axes, which is the subject of Unit 10.
>
> **A DESIGN ARGUMENT, NOT AN EXPERIMENT**
> The book is an **argument about design**, made with historical examples and the author's own
> redrawings. It is **not an experimental study**: it does not test readers, measure reading times or
> count errors, and the ratio is **proposed**, not derived from data about how people read.
>
> The author is making an **aesthetic and ethical case**, and says so. Parts of it have been
> **disputed** — later work has argued that some decoration helps readers remember a figure, and that
> stripping a chart to the bone can make it harder to use. The strongest form of the rule, *remove all
> non-data ink*, is not a finding; it is a position.
>
> It is also about **making** figures, and you are not making them. You are **reading** one and
> writing about it, which is why only the first half of the idea is in this unit.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The ratio is the book's; the judgment about what to ignore in an
> examination figure is ours.*

### 1 · Of which, with, and the participle

| | |
|---|---|
| 480 households, **of which** 120 had no car. | a part of a total |
| 480 households, 120 **of which** had no car. | the same, figure first |

4 Transport rose. Clothing fell. (while)
5 Three cities grew. The largest doubled. (having)

|---|---|

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Packing too much in produces sentences nobody can follow: *Transport,
which was the largest category throughout the period which the chart covers, having risen from 71,
which it stood at in the first year, to 86…* **Three facts in a sentence is plenty. Say two of your
sentences aloud with a partner and ask whether they could follow them.**
