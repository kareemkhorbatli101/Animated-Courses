# Plumbline · sources

**Academic English for IELTS · International Edition.** Six books, seventy-one units carrying a
source and one review unit without one; seventy-one separate source bodies. Nothing in this series
reuses a figure or a source from Wayfinder English, from the Clearline business series, from Foreign
Service English, from Keystone or from Throughline.

## Nothing in this series is official

**This is an independent teaching draft.** It is **not published, endorsed or approved by the British
Council, by IDP: IELTS Australia, by Cambridge University Press & Assessment, or by any other
examination body**, and it carries no official status. IELTS is the trademark of its owners and is
named in these books only to say which examination they prepare for.

**No question, passage, recording or prompt from any past examination is reproduced anywhere in the
series.** Every practice item in every book was written for it. Where the series describes what the
examination asks, it describes a published document — the test format, the public band descriptors,
the published scale, the notice to candidates — names it, dates the day it was read, and prints what
that document says it does not cover.

**Nothing in these books is advice about anybody's admission, registration, visa or employment.** The
band a reader needs is the band the organization asking for it says it needs, on the day they apply.
Every book says so, in the back matter, in those words.

**And nothing in these books is a prediction.** A practice score is what a person did on one set of
questions on one morning. The series says this in Book 1 Unit 2, repeats it whenever a figure
appears, and comes back to it in the last unit of Book 6.

## The rule this series is built on

Every reading text in every book quotes something real: a published document from the test owners, a
framework published by an intergovernmental body, a journal paper, a book, a word list. Three rules
apply without exception.

1. **The text names its document, its edition or its date, and its publisher or author.** Not "the
   examiners want" but "the public band descriptors for Writing Task 2, as published, read on this
   date". Not "research shows" but "a review published in *Psychological Science in the Public
   Interest* in 2013, which rated ten techniques and said what *low* meant". An edition and a date
   are part of a citation, and a student who cannot name one has not finished the sentence.
2. **The text prints what the document does not cover.** In this series that is the point. A test
   format gives the shape of the papers and not their contents. A band scale publishes an indicative
   raw-score guide and says it is indicative. A framework written to compare courses states that it
   is not prescriptive and is not a test. A table of means describes a self-selected crowd and
   cannot predict one person. A 1885 experiment had one subject and used nonsense on purpose. A 1990
   paper is a contested hypothesis and says so. Every reading text ends with that line, and every
   unit's honesty note repeats it.
3. **Every person, class, center, university, employer, figure and conversation is invented.**
   Meridian Language Center does not exist. Neither do its teachers, its students, its clients or
   the universities and hospitals that appear in the dialogues, and **the band each invented
   organization asks for is invented too**. Where a unit needs a failure — a memorized answer, a
   missed group of answers, a notebook full of corrections that changed nothing — that failure is
   written from the shape of the document being taught, not from any real candidate at any real
   center.

**The series is provider-neutral.** No preparation course, publisher, app, teacher-training scheme or
software product is recommended anywhere in the six books. Where sample material is needed, the books
say to use the test owners' own published sample material and nothing else.

**Money has no symbol anywhere in this series.** A fee is written as a number and the unit is
whatever currency the reader pays in. A number without a symbol is readable in every country; a
number with the wrong symbol is readable in none.

and the organizations that ask for bands actually talk. The CEFR equivalence printed beside each book
is the mapping published by the test owners, and it is indicative.

## Eleven admissions, one per unit

- **Unit 1.** The published format is a description of a shape. Every student who has ever been
  surprised by a question type was surprised by something the document never claimed to tell them.
- **Unit 2.** The number printed largest on the result form is the one that is least use to anybody
  who needs a band in **each** paper. The large print is for the people who want one number.
- **Unit 3.** A framework written to help compare courses across a continent is used daily as a
  gate. The Council of Europe has said more than once that it is not for that.
- **Unit 4.** Everybody in the published tables chose to be there. A mean of about two million
  strangers is a planning figure, and it has been quoted at individual students as a verdict for
  years.
- **Unit 5.** Most wrong answers in a Listening paper are not vocabulary. They are the question the
  candidate stayed with after the recording had left it, and almost no lesson in the world diagnoses
  that.
- **Unit 6.** The rule students break most often is the watch, and nobody who breaks it means to.
  The rule students most want explained — what counts as a memorized answer — is the one the notice
  does not define.
- **Unit 7.** Four thousand extra word families buy the last three per cent of coverage. Coverage of
  96 per cent still loses the question if the missing word is the one the question turns on.
- **Unit 8.** Somebody chose two thousand words by hand, using judgement as well as counting, and
  much of the world learned those two thousand first. It was a good list. It was still a choice.
- **Unit 9.** The three most popular study techniques in the world came out at the bottom of the
  review, and the technique that feels like failing came out at the top.
- **Unit 10.** One man, one metronome and a list of nonsense, and every review schedule since has
  been drawn on his curve. The schedules are not in his book.
- **Unit 11.** Six corrections, six notebook entries, no change — and then one lecture, and the
  thing stopped. One student in one term is not evidence, and it is still the most useful thing in
  the unit.

# Book 2 · Plumbline 1 — *The Mark You Lost*

Target band 5.0 · CEFR B1+ · figure prefix `qb` · audio prefix `PL2` · 196 figures · 195 tracks

One paper: Listening. Twelve units, twelve sources, and only two of them about the examination. The
other ten are about how a stream of speech is cut into words, what happens to small words when
nobody is being careful, what a listener has decided by the third sound, how long a name survives
between hearing it and writing it, what a lecture's own sentences give you free, and how wide the
number at the end is.

| Unit | Title | Source, as cited in the text | What the text prints as the document's own limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Four Parts, Forty Questions | The **guide for teachers** published by the test owners, read 11 February | It names the question types that **may** appear — not which will, how many of each, or in what order; it says nothing about the content of any recording; it is revised, so the edition is part of the citation; and it is written to explain the paper's purpose, not how to answer quickly |
| **2** | The Stream | **Richard Cauldwell**, *Phonology for Listening: Teaching the Stream of Speech*, Speech in Action, **2013** | A pedagogic account for teachers, with its own terminology, built from close listening to recordings; the three-way division (greenhouse, garden, jungle) is a **teaching device**, not an experimental finding, and the author does not claim otherwise; it reports no trial, gives no timetable and promises no gain in any score |
| **3** | The Words That Disappear | **Gillian Brown**, *Listening to Spoken English*, Longman, **1977**; second edition **1990** | A description based mainly on one variety — educated southern British English of the period — written before large spoken corpora; the second edition revised it rather than replacing it; **no rule in it is absolute**, since how much is elided depends on the speaker, the speed and the style; and it describes the language rather than teaching it |
| **4** | Slips of the Ear | **Z. S. Bond**, *Slips of the Ear: Errors in the Perception of Casual Conversation*, Academic Press, **1999** | Every slip in it is one somebody **noticed**, so it can describe what misperceptions look like and cannot say how often they happen — the author is explicit about this; it is English casual conversation of one period, largely by native-speaking listeners; it does not claim to describe what learners mishear; and being observational it establishes no cause |
| **5** | The Brain Guesses Early | **W. D. Marslen-Wilson and A. Welsh**, *Processing interactions and lexical access during word recognition in continuous speech*, **Cognitive Psychology, 1978** | A laboratory model from shadowing and mispronunciation-detection tasks with a small number of native-speaking participants; **the first author revised the role of context in work published in 1987**; it says nothing about learners, nothing about whether recognition can be trained, and nothing about any examination |
| **6** | Holding It While You Write | **A. D. Baddeley and G. Hitch**, *Working Memory*, in *The Psychology of Learning and Motivation*, vol. 8, **1974** | A model, revised continuously since, with a fourth component — the episodic buffer — added by the first author in **2000**, so four parts cannot be cited to the 1974 paper; the evidence is digit span and reasoning under load, not writing answers; capacity figures vary with material and measurement, so *two seconds* is an order of magnitude; and it is a model of a system, not a technique |
| **7** | The Lecture Has a Shape | **C. A. Chaudron and J. C. Richards**, *The effect of discourse markers on the comprehension of lectures*, **Applied Linguistics, 1986** | One study, one institution, one group of learners, on lectures prepared for the experiment; the measure was a comprehension and recall test rather than a band; **the effects were real but modest**; and it cannot say what any particular lecturer will do — some never signpost at all |
| **8** | Where the Stress Falls | **A. Cutler and D. M. Carter**, *The predominance of strong initial syllables in the English vocabulary*, **Computer Speech and Language, 1987** | It establishes a **count** of a vocabulary at the time of counting; that listeners **use** the pattern is an **inference**, supported by other work and not demonstrated here about any individual; the strategy has a known failure in words like *result*, *agree* and *allow*; and the paper says nothing about learners and contains no exercise |
| **9** | Accents You Have Not Heard | **R. C. Major, S. F. Fitzmaurice, F. Bunta and C. Balasubramanian**, *The effects of nonnative accents on listening comprehension: implications for ESL assessment*, **TESOL Quarterly, 2002** | Particular accents, particular listener groups, particular materials; the effects were **small** and **not consistent** across groups; **the authors explicitly ask readers not to generalize** beyond the varieties tested; and it is not a study of this examination and makes no claim about any band. The unit keeps it strictly apart from the test owners' own published statement that a range of accents is used |
| **10** | Being Taught to Listen | **L. Vandergrift and M. H. Tafaghodtari**, *Teaching L2 learners how to listen does make a difference: an empirical study*, **Language Learning, 2010** | One quasi-experimental study, one term, one institution, one language pair, using **intact classes** rather than individuals assigned at random; the outcome was a listening test, not a band; the method cannot be separated from the teacher who delivered it; and *the less skilled gained most* is a subgroup result awaiting replication |
| **11** | The Score You Cannot Feel | **L. J. Cronbach**, *Coefficient alpha and the internal structure of tests*, **Psychometrika, 1951** | The coefficient is a property of a **set of items answered by a group**, not of a test; it is **not** a measure of whether a test measures one thing; it rises with the number of items whatever their quality; **the author wrote critically about its use in work published after his death in 2004**; and the paper contains no reliability figure for any examination |
| **12** | Forty Marks, One Band | The published **indicative raw-score guidance** for Listening, with a review of the eleven above | It is described as **indicative**; the number of correct answers that reaches a band is worked out **for each version**, so it is not the table used on your paper; it says nothing about the width round your own score; and it cannot be run backwards — a practice score of 23 is 23 answers on one morning, not a band |

## Twelve admissions, one per unit

- **Unit 1.** Every student who was surprised by a question type was surprised by something the guide
  never claimed to tell them. And almost every student practices the part they are already best at.
- **Unit 2.** Four thousand words, and four words out of nine from four seconds of real speech. The
  words were never the problem, and nobody finds out until the jungle arrives in Part 3.
- **Unit 3.** The commonest words in English are the ones learners cannot hear, because they were
  learned in their full form and are met flattened.
- **Unit 4.** A mishearing is real English with the right rhythm, which is why it is believed, and
  why the only available check is whether the answer fits the printed words round the gap.
- **Unit 5.** Eleven of nineteen blank gaps were answerable from the printed words alone, with no
  recording at all. The student who found that out called it cheating.
- **Unit 6.** Three blanks in a row is one problem and two casualties, and the repair is to write
  fewer words — which feels careless to everybody who was ever taught to answer in full sentences.
- **Unit 7.** The small joining words are the ones courses teach. The sentences that announce the
  shape are the ones that helped, and they arrive in the first forty seconds, free.
- **Unit 8.** The strategy that finds most word beginnings in English is the strategy that produced
  *sult* on an answer line. It worked perfectly and gave the wrong answer.
- **Unit 9.** Two students, one recording, opposite rankings. With two people it is about two people,
  and a rumor about accents is almost always larger than the evidence behind it.
- **Unit 10.** Thirty-eight practice papers bought two marks. One slow cycle found that three of four
  errors came from the wording of the questions, which no score would ever have said.
- **Unit 11.** Everybody believes their highest score, including the people who know better. A
  number has a width, and no result slip in the world prints it.
- **Unit 12.** Forty marks become one band through a table published as a guide. Count marks.

# Book 3 · Plumbline 2 — *Finding, Not Reading*

Target band 5.5 · CEFR B2 · figure prefix `qc` · audio prefix `PL3` · 196 figures · 195 tracks

One paper: Academic Reading. Three passages, forty questions, sixty minutes, and no extra time at the
end. The book is built on the arithmetic that follows from those four numbers, and on eleven pieces of
published research about what reading is and what a reading test can measure.

| Unit | Title | Source, as cited in the text | What the text prints as the document's own limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Ninety Seconds a Question | The published **description of the Academic Reading test**, read 2 May | It describes a design and not your paper: not the topics, not how many questions of each type, not their order, not which passage carries the detailed argument; and it is silent on how to divide the hour, because a method is somebody else's job |
| **2** | What a Reading Test Measures | **J. Charles Alderson**, *Assessing Reading*, Cambridge University Press, **2000** | A **review of a field** rather than a study, written for test developers, reaching few firm conclusions **on purpose**; it reports that some items are answerable without the text, that background knowledge affects performance, that the question type changes the score, and that named reading skills are hard to separate in candidate data; it is of its period; and it does **not** hold that reading tests are invalid — a measure can be imperfect, well made and the best available at once |
| **3** | Where the Eye Actually Goes | **Keith Rayner**, *Eye movements in reading and information processing: 20 years of research*, **Psychological Bulletin, 1998** | A review of **laboratory eye-tracking**, mostly with skilled **first-language** readers under controlled conditions; it is about the mechanics of the eye, not comprehension; it says nothing about second-language reading; and it contains **no training** — what it gives is a floor that honest advice about reading faster has to respect |
| **4** | One Unknown Word in Fifty | **M. Hsueh-Chao Hu and I. S. P. Nation**, *Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension*, **Reading in a Foreign Language, 2000** | **One study, one fiction text**, one group of learners, two comprehension measures; the unknown words were **invented**, a clean method with a known cost; the thresholds are **proportions of readers, not switches**; the 98 per cent figure is offered as **probable**; and it says nothing about academic prose or about examinations |
| **5** | Five Hundred and Seventy Families | **Averil Coxhead**, *A New Academic Word List*, **TESOL Quarterly, 2000** | It **deliberately excludes the most frequent 2,000 words**, so on its own it is one word in ten of a page the reader cannot read; built on a corpus of one period; **coverage varies by discipline**; counting families assumes derivational competence; it is a list and not a syllabus, ordered by frequency rather than usefulness; and later work has proposed alternatives |
| **6** | This, Such, The Latter | **M. A. K. Halliday and R. Hasan**, *Cohesion in English*, Longman, **1976** | A **description of a language** written for linguists, not a reading method; **cohesion is not coherence**, and a student taught that linkers make writing coherent has been taught something the source does not say; the categories overlap and coding is interpretive; and it is of its period and has been extended since, including by one of its own authors |
| **7** | Looking for the Wrong Card | **P. C. Wason**, *Reasoning about a rule*, **Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1968** | An **abstract** laboratory task with a small group; **performance changes dramatically with everyday content**, so it does not show that people cannot reason; the interpretation has been argued about for fifty years; and it has nothing to do with reading tests — **the connection drawn in the unit is the book's own teaching analogy, stated as such** |
| **8** | What a Paragraph Is About | **W. Kintsch and T. A. van Dijk**, *Toward a model of text comprehension and production*, **Psychological Review, 1978** | A **formal model** with evidence from recall and summarization; **both authors rebuilt it**, in a book-length reworking in 1983 and in the first author's different architecture in 1988, so the 1978 paper is a stage rather than a position; the macro-rules are a modeling claim, not a demonstrated procedure; and the one-line-first technique comes from the arithmetic, not from the model |
| **9** | Words a Minute | **William Grabe**, *Reading in a Second Language: Moving from Theory to Practice*, Cambridge University Press, **2009** | A **synthesis** in which the author separates research findings from his own implications for teaching; the rate figures are **approximate** and a group figure says nothing about an individual; the evidence on extensive reading is **uneven in quality**, as the author notes; and it offers no technique that works in a fortnight and says so |
| **10** | How Sure the Writer Is | **Ken Hyland**, *Metadiscourse: Exploring Interaction in Writing*, Continuum, **2005** | A framework built from corpora of academic writing whose categories are **functional**, so the same word does different work in different places and **any count of hedges is a count of somebody's readings**; conventions vary by discipline and by writing culture; and it is about writing, not a method for answering questions |
| **11** | A Formula That Counts Syllables | **Rudolf Flesch**, *A new readability yardstick*, **Journal of Applied Psychology, 1948** | It counts **two surface features** and is blind to word frequency, cohesion, argument and the reader's topic familiarity; **chopping sentences in half raises the score and lowers the clarity**; validated on general prose of its period, not academic text and not second-language readers. **The unit also states that this series scores its own reading texts with such a formula, and calls it a floor rather than a judge** |
| **12** | Six Qualities, No Formula | **L. F. Bachman and A. S. Palmer**, *Language Testing in Practice*, Oxford University Press, **1996**, with a review of the eleven above | The six qualities **cannot be evaluated separately** and **trade off**, and the authors give **no formula** for weighting them: usefulness must be argued for a particular test, used with particular people, for a particular purpose; it is for test developers rather than candidates; and it is of its period, before the field's move towards treating validity as an argument to be built and evaluated |

## Twelve admissions, one per unit

- **Unit 1.** Ninety per cent accuracy and twenty-six marks, because eleven questions were never
  answered. The hour pays for marks on a sheet, not for care.
- **Unit 2.** Two of four questions answered with the passage covered. A right answer does not prove
  that anybody understood anything.
- **Unit 3.** The clear part of a reader's vision is about fifteen letters wide, and speed-reading
  courses have been selling the opposite for fifty years.
- **Unit 4.** Six unknown words in a hundred is below the density at which most readers in one study
  could retell a text — and four minutes went on a word no question asked about.
- **Unit 5.** Two hundred and twenty academic word families bought three marks, and the sentences
  that still stopped the student contained no academic words at all.
- **Unit 6.** Nineteen marks across one class, traced to five words everybody learned in their first
  year. Seven of them to one *this*.
- **Unit 7.** Thirty-one *falses* written where there were nineteen, and in every wrong one there was
  no sentence to point at.
- **Unit 8.** Reading eight headings against six paragraphs is forty-eight comparisons. Writing one
  line first is eight.
- **Unit 9.** One hundred and forty words a minute means twenty minutes of a sixty-minute paper spent
  reading, so the eleven blanks were arithmetic rather than carelessness.
- **Unit 10.** Fourteen marks from five words known since school, four of them hedges.
- **Unit 11.** The same paragraph scored 18, 44 and 66 with an identical syllable count. The entire
  difference was punctuation.
- **Unit 12.** Accuracy fell from ninety per cent to eighty-four and the score rose from twenty-six
  to thirty-three. Something real was traded for something that counts.

## Figures, transcripts and invented figures

196 figures, prefix `qf`, generated from `plumbline/_build/spec_qf1.py` to `spec_qf4.py`. No figure in
this book appears in any other book of this series or in any of its five sister series.

**Every question, card, transcript, model answer, rating sheet, admissions page and realia document
in the book was written for it.** No question, prompt, recording or transcript from any past
examination is reproduced anywhere. The repair transcripts in Unit 6, the run-length transcript in
Unit 5, the rating matrix in Unit 9, the error tally in Unit 10 and the threshold pages in Unit 11 are
all the book's own.

**Every figure attributed to "our own class" is invented** — the repeated-phrase count in Unit 1, the
four-form survey in Unit 2, the collocation errors in Unit 3, the ranked word list in Unit 4, the run
lengths in Unit 5, the repair tally in Unit 6, the three task measures in Unit 7, the stress survey in
Unit 8, the five rated speakers in Unit 9, the twelve errors in Unit 10, the five score uses in Unit
11 and the five summary sheets in Unit 12. Each says on its face that it is an invented class's own
count, with eleven students, and several units stop to say why eleven students cannot establish
anything.

**Two tables are this book's own reading of a document rather than anything the document says.** The
matrix of core and non-core pronunciation features against what classrooms actually correct, in Unit
8, and the table of the test's features against purposes and constraints, in Unit 12, are both ours.
Each says so, and Unit 12 invites the reader to say which row we have got wrong.

**Unit 2 says that Book 3 of this series taught a method its own source argues against.** That is
true: Book 3 Unit 5 was built on the 2000 academic word list, and this unit's source is in part a
critique of it, of its exclusion of the commonest two thousand words and of its word-family basis.
The reading text states that the earlier list was worth teaching, because it is the list most
published material still uses and because its own author stated its limits — and that a reader of
this series is entitled to be told that part of their course rests on a position the field has moved
past.

**Unit 12 turns the discipline on the whole series.** It states that the examination is a compromise
arrived at under administrative constraint — fourteen minutes because forty was not affordable — that
these six books exist because that examination exists, and that if it had been designed differently
the units would be different units. What it offers in the series' defense is what the series has done
throughout: every unit built on a real published document, named with its date, with its own authors'
stated limits printed beside it, and most of those documents not about examinations at all.
