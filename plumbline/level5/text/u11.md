Somebody wrote 6.0 on your essay. A second reader, equally trained, might have written 6.5, and the
research on this is not a rumor: it is a standard topic in a textbook on how writing is assessed.
What follows from it is not comfort. It is a reason to read the comment and not the number.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qe11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a five-row table of the facets, a two-column comparison of holistic and analytic scales, a boxed paragraph headed an overview for teachers, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what is going on at the other end of your essay**
> **Source: Sara Cushing Weigle, *Assessing Writing*, Cambridge University Press, 2002. Printed here
> in our own words.**
>
> The book is an overview of how writing is assessed, written for teachers and test developers. Its
> organizing idea is that a score is produced by several things at once, only one of which is the
> candidate.
>
> | The facet | What varies |
> |---|---|
> | the **writer** | what the candidate can do on the day |
> | the **task** | how difficult this prompt is relative to others |
> | the **script** | what was actually produced |
> | the **rater** | severity, internal consistency, and how the criteria are weighted |
> | the **scale** | what the descriptors ask, and how they are interpreted in practice |
>
> The chapters on raters report a body of research with three consistent findings. Raters differ in
> **severity**: some are systematically harsher than others, by amounts large enough to matter. They
> differ in **consistency**: a rater may be reliable with themselves and still out of line with the
> group. And they differ in how they **weight criteria**: two readers may agree about an essay's
> qualities and still produce different totals because one cares more about organization and the other
> about accuracy.
>
> The field's response is procedural rather than hopeful. **Training and standardization** bring
> raters closer together; **multiple ratings** of the same script reduce the effect of one reader;
> **statistical adjustment** can estimate and correct for a known rater's severity. These measures
> reduce variation; the book does not claim they remove it.
>
> It also compares two kinds of scale. **Holistic** scoring gives one overall figure, and is fast and
> closer to how a real reader reacts. **Analytic** scoring gives a figure per criterion, carries more
> information and is generally more reliable, but takes longer and may not match anybody's overall
> impression. The examination you are preparing for uses four criteria, which is an analytic scheme,
> and the four are combined.
>
> For a candidate, two consequences follow, and they are not the consolation they look like.
>
> **A single practice band is a weak estimate.** One reader, one script, one afternoon. If your
> practice figures have moved between 5.5 and 6.5, that is not necessarily progress or its absence; it
> is partly the spread you would expect from the measurement itself.
>
> **The comment is better evidence than the figure.** Two readers who disagree about the band usually
> agree about what is wrong with the essay, and the thing they agree on is the thing to work on.
>
> **AN OVERVIEW FOR TEACHERS**
> This is a **textbook**: a synthesis of a field, not a study. It reports other people's research and
> is explicit about disagreements within it.
>
> The rater research it summarizes was conducted on **particular tests, with particular raters, in
> particular settings**. How much any of it describes the examination you are sitting is not something
> the book says, and not something this note knows.
>
> It is also of its **period**. Assessment practice, statistical methods and the role of automated
> scoring have all moved since, and the book was written before most of that movement.
>
> And it cannot tell you anything about **your** examiner. It describes a distribution; you will meet
> one person. Nothing in it licenses the belief that a disappointing band was the marker's fault, and
> nothing in it licenses the belief that a good one was luck.
>
> *Our summary of a published textbook, named with its publisher and its year. The advice about
> reading the comment is ours.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Read the comment, not the number.** A practice band is one reader's placement of one script, and
> half a band of movement between two practice essays may be the measurement rather than you. The
> comment is different: it names something that was on the page. If two readers name the same thing,
> that is the strongest evidence you will get about what to work on, and it is worth more than any
> figure either of them wrote.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> How much a single number is trusted differs greatly between education systems. In some, one
