# Unit 4 · The Sentence After the First One
%%SUB A paper from 1965 about what the second sentence of a paragraph is actually doing%%
%%GOALS
Say what level a sentence is on
Tell a coordinate sequence from a subordinate one
Write a paragraph that goes down rather than along
Use subordination to develop rather than to add
Say what a rhetoric built from professional prose cannot tell you
%%

A topic sentence, and then four sentences. The question nobody asks is what the four are doing: are
they four more of the same, or is each one going further into the one before? A paper from 1965 drew
both shapes, and one of them is what *extended and supported* means.

### 2 · Six more words, and the two sequences

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qe04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, two paragraphs printed with levels numbered in the margin, a short note on what the author built the scheme from, a boxed paragraph headed built from professional prose, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what the sentences after a topic sentence are doing**
> **Source: Francis Christensen, *A Generative Rhetoric of the Paragraph*, College Composition and
> Communication, 1965. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper's starting point is a complaint: students are taught what a paragraph should be like —
> unified, coherent, adequately developed — and not how to make one. The author calls his alternative
> a **generative** rhetoric, meaning one that tells a writer what to do next.
>
> Its central idea is **levels of generality**. The most general sentence in a paragraph is at level
> 1. A sentence that comments on it is at level 2. A sentence that comments on *that* is at level 3.
> The structure of a paragraph can be written out as a column of numbers.
>
> Two basic shapes follow.
>
> | The shape | What the sentences do |
> |---|---|
> | a **coordinate** sequence | several sentences at the **same** level, each commenting on the level above: 1, 2, 2, 2 |
> | a **subordinate** sequence | each sentence commenting on the one before it: 1, 2, 3, 4 |
>
> Most real paragraphs are **mixed**: 1, 2, 3, 3, 2, 3. The pattern is not a rule to obey but a
> description of what is happening, and the author's claim is that a writer who can see it can choose
> it.
>
> The practical consequence for an essay is direct. A paragraph that runs 1, 2, 2, 2 is a **list of
> instances**; a paragraph that runs 1, 2, 3, 4 has **gone into** its claim. The band descriptors ask
> for ideas that are *extended and supported*, and a drop to level 3 is what extension looks like on
> the page. Candidates who feel their paragraphs are thin are almost always writing level 2 four times
> over.
>
> **BUILT FROM PROFESSIONAL PROSE**
> The scheme was built by **analyzing published writing** — essays and articles by professional
> writers of the author's own period and language. It is **not derived from student writing**, and the
> paper offers **no evidence** that teaching it improves anybody's essays. There is no experiment, no
> comparison group and no measurement of any kind.
>
> The claim that paragraphs have a topic sentence has also been **contradicted since**. Studies that
> went looking for topic sentences in published expository prose found them in a **minority** of
> paragraphs, which leaves the level-1 sentence looking more like a teaching convenience than a
> description of how professionals write.
>
> It is also a rhetoric of **American expository prose of the 1960s**, and conventions of paragraphing
> differ by language, period and discipline. Nothing in it is about second-language writers, and
> nothing in it is about examinations.
>
> And the numbering is **interpretive**: two readers marking the same paragraph will sometimes disagree
> about whether a sentence is a second level 2 or a first level 3.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its journal and its year. The link to the band
> descriptors is ours.*

### 2 · Subordination that develops

| | |
|---|---|
| The road filled **because** cheaper journeys multiply. | a reason, one level down |
| The road filled, **which** the forecast had not allowed for. | a comment on the whole clause |
| The forecast, **having assumed fixed demand**, was wrong from the start. | a participle carrying the reason |
| **What the forecast assumed** was that demand was fixed. | a cleft, putting the assumption first |
| The road filled in eight years, **a third of the period forecast**. | an appositive, adding the measure |

**2A  Rewrite each pair as one sentence using the structure in brackets.**

1 The road filled. Journeys multiplied. (because)
2 The road filled. The forecast did not allow for it. (which)
3 The forecast assumed fixed demand. It was wrong. (participle)
4 The forecast assumed something. Demand was fixed. (cleft)
5 The road filled in eight years. Twenty were forecast. (appositive)

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL5-04-09%%

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Two body paragraphs that reach level 3 beat four that stay at level 2.** The test of a paragraph is
> not how many sentences it has; it is whether the fourth sentence is about the third. Write your
> claim, then one instance, then what the instance shows, then what that depends on. If you can read
> your paragraph and no sentence is about the one before it, you have written a list.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Paragraphing is a convention and it is learned. The shape this examination rewards — one claim, then
> down into it — is characteristic of English academic prose, and the 1965 paper itself was describing
> American essays of one decade. Other written traditions build a paragraph by circling a subject, or
> by placing the main point at the end, and a writer trained in one of those is not writing badly.
> **Ask your group: in your first language, where in a paragraph does the main point go?**
