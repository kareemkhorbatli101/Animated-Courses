# Unit 5 · The Questions It Has to Survive
%%SUB A textbook whose point is that most bad arguments are good arguments used badly%%
%%GOALS
Name the common patterns of plausible argument
Ask the questions that test each pattern
Say why an appeal to an expert is not a fallacy
Use the language of challenge: *does it follow that*
Say what a textbook can and cannot settle
%%

You have been told that appealing to an authority is a fallacy. It is not. Citing an expert is one of
the most reasonable things a writer can do; what matters is whether it survives five particular
questions. A textbook on informal logic turns the whole subject round, and it is the most useful
turning in this book.

%%FIG qe05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher, the edition and the year with the first edition date beneath, a five-row table of patterns, a boxed list of the five questions for one pattern, a boxed paragraph headed a textbook, not a study, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: how to attack an argument, including your own**
> **Source: Douglas Walton, *Informal Logic: A Pragmatic Approach*, second edition, Cambridge
> University Press, 2008 (first edition 1989). Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book's first move is to reject a way of teaching that most of us were given. The traditional
> list of **fallacies** treats certain kinds of argument as errors: appeal to authority, appeal to
> popularity, argument from consequences, the slippery slope. The book's position is that most of
> these are **reasonable patterns of argument** that can be used well or badly, and that calling them
> fallacies by name teaches students to dismiss good reasoning.
>
> In their place it offers **argumentation schemes**: recurring patterns of **plausible** argument,
> each of which comes with a set of **critical questions**. The argument is reasonable if it survives
> its questions, and faulty if it does not.
>
> | The pattern | What it asserts |
> |---|---|
> | from **expert opinion** | an expert says it, so it is probably so |
> | from **example** | this case holds, so the generalization probably holds |
> | from **analogy** | these cases are alike, so what holds of one holds of the other |
> | from **consequences** | the action would have these effects, so take it or avoid it |
> | the **slippery slope** | this step leads by stages to an outcome nobody wants |
>
> The questions for the first pattern are the clearest example, and they are the five this unit is
> built on.
>
> > **Is the source actually an expert in the relevant field?**
> > **Is the assertion inside that field?**
> > **Is the source reliable — is there a reason to doubt them?**
> > **Do other experts in the field agree?**
> > **Is the assertion based on evidence that could be stated?**
>
> An appeal that answers all five is good reasoning. An appeal that fails the second — a physicist on
> monetary policy — is the fallacy, and the fallacy is in the use, not in the shape.
>
> The second idea worth taking is **defeasibility**. These arguments do not prove; they create a
> **presumption** and **shift the burden of proof** onto whoever disagrees. That is exactly what a
> Task 2 essay does: you are not proving anything in 250 words, you are making your reader the one
> who has to answer.
>
> **A TEXTBOOK, NOT A STUDY**
> This is a **textbook**: a synthesis of a field and a set of proposals, not an empirical
> investigation. It reports **no experiment** and measures nobody's reasoning.
>
> The list of schemes is **not closed or agreed**. Different authors in the field give different lists
> of different lengths, and the author's own list changed between editions and across his other books.
> Anybody who tells you there is a fixed number of argument patterns is reporting one book's
> arrangement.
>
> Whether a move is a fallacy also depends on the **dialogue** it occurs in: on what the two sides
> were trying to do and what had already been conceded. That makes the judgment **contextual**, which
> is honest and also means there is no checklist that settles it.
>
> And it is a work of **logic and argumentation theory**, not of language teaching. Nothing in it is
> about examinations, about second-language writers, or about English.
>
> *Our summary of a published textbook, named with its publisher, its edition and both of its dates.
> The five questions are the book's; the use made of them on your own paragraph is ours.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **The prompt is an argument, and you may attack it.** Many Task 2 statements are arguments from
> consequences — *this would lead to that, so it should be done.* An essay that accepts the statement's
> reasoning and merely agrees or disagrees with its conclusion has left the best material on the
> table. Ask the statement its own strongest question, answer it, and you have a paragraph nobody else
> in the room will write.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Which authorities count differs between places and traditions. In some, a scholar's reputation

> may not share your sense of who is authoritative. **Ask your group: in your country, who would you
