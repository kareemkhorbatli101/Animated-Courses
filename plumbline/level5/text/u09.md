# Unit 9 · Telling, and Transforming
%%SUB A book from 1987 about two completely different things people do when they write%%
%%GOALS
Tell knowledge-telling from knowledge-transforming
Plan in two spaces rather than one
Say what a plan should contain that an essay should not
Use the language of planning
Say what a think-aloud study can and cannot show
%%

Two writers sit down with the same prompt. One writes down what they know about the subject in the
order it arrives. The other asks what their reader will not accept and rewrites their own opinion
because of the answer. A book published in 1987 modeled both, and most of what you have been taught
about planning is advice for the first one.

%%FIG qe09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the book, the publisher and the year, two diagrams of the models side by side, a two-row table comparing what each writer does, a boxed paragraph headed models, and think-aloud evidence, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: two different things people do when they write**
> **Source: Carl Bereiter and Marlene Scardamalia, *The Psychology of Written Composition*, Lawrence
> Erlbaum, 1987. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book proposes two models of how a text gets written.
>
> The first is **knowledge-telling**. The writer takes the topic and the kind of text as **cues**,
> uses them to retrieve something from memory, writes it down, and uses what has just been written as
> the cue for the next item. The process is efficient and it has a natural stopping point: you stop
> when nothing more comes. The resulting text is organized the way the writer's memory was organized.
>
> The second is **knowledge-transforming**. The writer works in **two problem spaces** at once.
>
> | The space | The problems in it |
> |---|---|
> | the **content** space | what do I actually believe about this, and is it consistent? |
> | the **rhetorical** space | what will this reader not accept, and what must the text do to them? |
>
> The crucial claim is that problems are **translated between** the spaces. A rhetorical problem — *my
> reader will not accept this* — becomes a content problem — *do I actually believe it, and under what
> conditions?* — and the answer changes the text and sometimes the opinion. On this model, writing is
> not a way of reporting thought; it is a way of having it.
>
> The two are presented as the ends of a developmental difference: inexperienced writers mostly tell,
> experienced ones can transform. And **both are competent processes**. The authors are explicit that
> knowledge-telling is not a deficiency but an efficient strategy that fits many tasks.
>
> For a Task 2 essay the consequence is about the six minutes before you write. A plan that lists
> your four paragraphs is a knowledge-telling plan: it is the essay in note form, and it has done no
> work. A plan that contains **the objection you cannot answer** has done work, because it has put a
> problem into the plan that the essay will have to solve.
>
> **MODELS, AND THINK-ALOUD EVIDENCE**
> The two accounts are **models**, not observed types of person. Real writers do both, in the same
> session, and the book says so: the distinction is a way of describing processes, not a way of sorting
> writers into two groups.
>
> The evidence is largely **think-aloud protocols** — writers saying out loud what they are thinking —
> together with experiments, mostly with **children and school students** in one country. Think-aloud
> has a known problem the authors discuss: asking somebody to narrate their thinking **changes the
> task**, and what cannot be put into words does not appear in the record.
>
> The research is also about writers in their **first language**, in school settings, with no clock
> running. A forty-minute examination with a stranger marking it is a different activity, and nothing
> in the book is about it.
>
> And the developmental claim is **not a measurement of anybody's band**: no part of this book assigns
> a score, predicts one, or says what a marker rewards.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year. The advice about what
> belongs in a six-minute plan is ours.*

### 2 · Translating between the spaces

| | |
|---|---|
| My reader will not accept this, **which means I must show** that the cost is one-off. | rhetorical → content |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Where a writer is expected to work out their position is a cultural matter. Some educational
> traditions ask students to arrive with a view and defend it; others treat writing as the place where
> a view is formed, and a first draft that changes direction is a sign of thought rather than of
> confusion. The 1987 research was conducted with school students in one country, and its
> developmental story is of that setting. **Ask your group: were you taught to decide before writing,
> or while writing?**
