# Unit 6 · What Would Change Your Mind
%%SUB A study from 1991 that asked ordinary people to argue, and found most of them could not say what would count against them%%
%%GOALS
Tell evidence from an illustration of your own theory
Generate a counter-argument against yourself
Refute it without abandoning your position
Say what would change your mind
Say what an interview study can and cannot establish
%%

Ask somebody what causes a problem and they will tell you. Ask them what makes them think so and
most will tell you the same thing again, in the form of a story. One study, published in 1991, put
that to a few hundred people, and the thing it found missing is the thing a band-7 essay has.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qe06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a description of the design with the three topics named, a three-row table of what was asked for and what came back, a boxed paragraph headed an interview study, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: the question most people cannot answer about their own opinion**
> **Source: Deanna Kuhn, *The Skills of Argument*, Cambridge University Press, 1991. Printed here in
> our own words.**
>
> The study interviewed about a hundred and sixty people of several ages and two levels of education,
> and asked each of them about three social questions: why prisoners return to crime, why children
> fail at school, and why people are unemployed.
>
> Each person was asked for their theory, then for the **evidence** for it, then for a
> **counter-argument** against it, then for a **rebuttal** of that counter-argument, and finally what
> would show their theory to be **wrong**.
>
> Nearly everybody had a theory and could state it fluently.
>
> | What was asked for | What usually came back |
> |---|---|
> | **evidence** | a **scenario**: a story describing how the cause would produce the effect |
> | a **counter-argument** | often nothing; where offered, a weak version easy to dismiss |
> | a **rebuttal** | sometimes, and usually stronger than the counter-argument it answered |
> | what would **falsify** the theory | very often nothing at all |
>
> The central distinction the study draws is between **evidence** and what it calls
> **pseudo-evidence**: an account of how the cause would operate, which is a restatement of the theory
> in narrative form rather than anything independent of it. *Prisoners go back to crime because they
> cannot get work — look, a man comes out, nobody will hire him, he steals.* The story is consistent
> with the theory and would be equally consistent with its being false.
>
> The study also found that the ability to produce counter-arguments and falsifying conditions was
> **only weakly related to education**, and that one group performed clearly better: people trained in
> philosophy.
>
> For a writer the consequence is exact. The band descriptors ask for ideas that are **extended and
> supported**. A paragraph that tells the story of how your cause would work has not supported
> anything; it has restated your claim at greater length. And the two sentences that most reliably
> raise a Task Response figure are the counter-argument, stated at **full strength**, and your answer
> to it.
>
> **AN INTERVIEW STUDY**
> This is an **interview study** of **three topics** in **one country** at **one period**. Nothing in
> it establishes how people reason about other subjects, in other places, or now.
>
> What counted as evidence rather than pseudo-evidence was **coded by the researcher's criteria**, and
> those criteria are themselves arguable: somebody could reasonably hold that a well-chosen scenario
> is a kind of evidence. A finding that rests on a coding decision is only as strong as the decision.
>
> The format matters too. People were asked, in conversation, to produce arguments **on the spot**. A
> person who cannot generate a counter-argument in an interview may well produce one given a week, a
> pen and a reason to care — and an essay is written with a pen.
>
> The education finding is **correlational**. Philosophy students arguing better does not show that
> studying philosophy makes you argue better; people who already argue that way may be the ones who
> study it.
>
> And it is a study of reasoning, not of English. Nothing in it is about writing, about examinations,
> or about second-language writers.
>
> *Our summary of a published study, named with its publisher and its year. The link to the band
> descriptors is ours.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Saying what would change your mind is not neutral behavior. In some settings it reads as
> intellectual honesty and in others as weakness, and in a few it is dangerous. The 1991 study was
> conducted in one country, and its finding that most people produce no falsifying condition is a

> subjects. **Ask your group: where you live, does admitting you might be wrong make you more
