# Unit 2 · Claim, Grounds, Warrant
%%SUB A book from 1958 that took arguments apart into pieces, one of which almost nobody writes down%%
%%GOALS

Justify a claim with *since*, *because* and *given that*

You write a claim and you write a reason. Between them sits a third thing: the principle that makes
the reason count as a reason at all. One book from 1958 gave it a name, and the reason your band-6
essay feels thin is usually that the third thing is missing.

%%FIG qe02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a six-row table of the parts of an argument, a worked example laid out as the diagram, a boxed paragraph headed the author called it a sketch, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: the three parts of a reason, and the one nobody writes**
> **Source: Stephen Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*, Cambridge University Press, 1958. Printed here in
> our own words.**
>
> The book's argument is that formal logic is the wrong model for how people actually argue. Real
> arguments are not syllogisms; they are more like what happens in a court, where a claim is made,
> evidence is offered, and the connection between them can itself be questioned.
>
> It proposes a layout with six parts.
>
> | The part | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | **claim** | the assertion being defended |
> | **grounds** (data) | the facts offered for it |
> | **warrant** | the general principle that licenses the move from grounds to claim |
> | **backing** | what supports the warrant if the warrant itself is questioned |
> | **qualifier** | the strength claimed: *probably*, *presumably*, *in most cases* |
> | **rebuttal** | the circumstances in which the claim would not hold |
>
> The useful idea is the **warrant**. *Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject* is
> not two statements but three: the two written, and the unwritten rule about who counts as a British
> subject. The warrant is usually left out because both sides accept it, and that is fine — until one
> side does not.
>
> The second useful idea is that warrants are **field-dependent**. What counts as a licensing
> principle in law is not what counts in medicine or in engineering. There is no single standard of
> proof for all arguments, and the book argues that philosophy's attempt to find one was a mistake.
>
> For a writer, the practical use is narrow and exact. When your paragraph feels thin, the missing
> sentence is usually the warrant: not another example, not a stronger adverb, but the principle that
> makes your example count. And when a reader might reasonably reject that principle, **state it and
> back it**.
>
> **THE AUTHOR CALLED IT A SKETCH**
> Toulmin presents the layout as an **analytical tool**, not a procedure for producing arguments, and
> describes his own account as a sketch rather than a finished theory.
>
> The categories have been **argued about ever since**. Where grounds end and warrants begin is often
> unclear in a real passage, and two analysts routinely divide the same argument differently. That
> instability is not a detail: a scheme whose boundaries cannot be agreed cannot be applied
> mechanically.
>
> The book is also **not about writing**, and still less about examinations. It is a work of
> philosophy addressed to philosophers, and the author's target was a view of logic, not a learner's
> paragraph. Nothing in it says an essay should have one warrant per paragraph, and nothing in it
> describes English.
>
> And it reports **no study**: no experiment, no corpus, no measurement of whether arguments laid out
> this way persuade anybody.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year. The use made of the warrant
> in an essay paragraph is ours.*

### 1 · Justifying a claim

| | |
|---|---|

### 2 · Stating a warrant

| | |
|---|---|

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **When a paragraph feels thin, the missing sentence is a principle, not an example.** Candidates who
> know their paragraph is weak reach for another instance, and two instances with no warrant are still
> one unjustified step. Write the sentence that begins *This matters because, in general,* — and if
> the general claim it makes is one a reader could reject, that is the paragraph you should be

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Arguing by stating your principle is a convention, not a universal. In many traditions an argument
> is carried by a well-chosen case, a precedent or a proverb, and spelling out the general rule reads
> as laboring the obvious. The 1958 book was written inside one philosophical tradition and its own
> author insisted that standards of proof differ between fields — which is a reason to notice that the
> essay this examination asks for has a convention too. **Ask your group: in your language, is a good
