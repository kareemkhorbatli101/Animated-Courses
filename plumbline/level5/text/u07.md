# Unit 7 · Why Writing Is Not Speech
%%SUB A corpus study from 1988 that counted what written English does and spoken English does not%%
%%GOALS

You know the difference between how you talk and how you write, and you cannot say what it consists
of. A study published in 1988 counted sixty-seven features across two corpora and produced an answer
that is more useful than *be formal* and more honest than *avoid contractions*.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qe07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a description of the two corpora, a two-column table of features at each end of one dimension, a line with several genres placed along it, a boxed paragraph headed a count, not a rule, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what written English does that spoken English does not**
> **Source: Douglas Biber, *Variation Across Speech and Writing*, Cambridge University Press, 1988.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The study counted **sixty-seven** grammatical and lexical features across a large number of texts
> drawn from two corpora: one of written British English and one of spoken English, both collected in
> the decades before it was published.
>
> It then looked for features that **occur together**. Where a group of features reliably turns up in
> the same texts, that group is treated as a **dimension** along which texts vary, and every genre can
> be given a position on it.
>
> The first and largest dimension sets two bundles of features against each other.
>
> | Features that cluster at one end | Features that cluster at the other |
> |---|---|
> | personal pronouns, contractions, present tense, questions, hedges, general verbs | nouns, long noun phrases, prepositional phrases, attributive adjectives, longer words, greater variety of vocabulary |
>
> The author's name for it is **involved versus informational production**, and the interpretation
> offered is about **time**: a speaker composing in real time reaches for pronouns and general verbs,
> while a writer with time to revise packs information into noun phrases.
>
> The finding that matters most is **not** that speech and writing differ. It is that they are **not
> two categories**. Genres spread out along the dimension, and some **written** genres sit closer to
> conversation than to academic prose — personal letters among them — while some spoken genres, such
> as prepared speeches, sit well toward the informational end.
>
> For a writer the practical consequence is specific. *Be more formal* names nothing you can do.
> **Use fewer pronouns, fewer general verbs and more noun phrases** names something you can do in a
> sentence, and it is what moves a text along the dimension the examination's own prose sits on.
>
> But the same finding warns against the obvious overcorrection. The informational end is not simply
> better: a text at the extreme is dense, abstract and slow, and a nominalized sentence hides who did
> what. Two or three nominalizations in a paragraph will raise your register; ten will cost you
> clarity, and clarity is in two of the four criteria.
>
> **A COUNT, NOT A RULE**
> This is a **frequency study**. It reports how often features occur in texts somebody collected. It
> is **not a prescription**, and nothing in it says a good essay should have any particular count of
> anything.
>
> The dimensions are the output of a **statistical procedure** applied to those counts, and their
> **names are the author's interpretation**. Another analyst could group the same features
> differently, and the labels — *involved*, *informational* — are summaries of a pattern, not
> discovered objects.
>
> The corpora are also of their **period** and of **two national varieties**, collected before the
> existence of most of the writing anybody now does. Where a text message or a social media post would
> fall is not something this study can say.
>
> And it is a study of **published and recorded English by first-language users**. It says nothing
> about learner writing, nothing about examinations, and nothing about what will raise a band.
>
> *Our summary of a published study, named with its publisher and its year. The advice about
> nominalization is ours.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Two or three nominalizations in a paragraph, and no more.** Enough to put your prose at the right
> end of the line; few enough that your sentences still have somebody doing something in them. The
> cheapest single improvement is to cut *I think*, *you can see* and *a lot of* and let the noun
> phrases carry the weight — and the cheapest single mistake is to nominalize until the marker cannot
> tell who acted.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Academic registers differ between languages and between disciplines. Some written traditions prize
> long, densely subordinated sentences as a mark of seriousness; others treat the same sentences as
> bad manners toward the reader. English academic prose has moved toward shorter sentences and plainer
> verbs over the last century, and the 1988 study describes one period of one language. **Ask your
> group: in your first language, does academic writing use long sentences or short ones?**
