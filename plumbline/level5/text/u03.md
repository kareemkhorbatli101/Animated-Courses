# Unit 3 · What You Can Leave Unsaid
%%SUB A lecture published in 1975 about everything a reader adds to your sentence without being asked%%
%%GOALS
Say what a reader infers from what you did not write

You write *some politicians are honest*. Your reader concludes that some are not. You never wrote

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qe03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the lecture, the volume, the editors and the year with the delivery date beneath, a four-row table of the maxims, two worked examples, a boxed paragraph headed a lecture, not a study, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what a reader works out from what you did not write**
> **Source: H. P. Grice, *Logic and Conversation*, published in *Syntax and Semantics 3: Speech Acts*,
> edited by P. Cole and J. L. Morgan, 1975. The material was delivered as lectures in 1967. Printed
> here in our own words.**
>
> The lecture begins from a puzzle. Conversation is full of meaning that is not in the words. If I say
> *the garage on the corner sells petrol* when you have told me your car has run out, I have not said
> that the garage is open, and you will nevertheless walk there.
>
> The explanation offered is that talk is a **cooperative activity**. Participants assume the other
> person is trying to be understood, and that assumption licenses a great deal of inference. The
> author sets out four **maxims** that cooperative speakers are assumed to be following.
>
> | The maxim | What it asks |
> |---|---|
> | **Quantity** | make your contribution as informative as required, and **no more** |
> | **Quality** | do not say what you believe false, or what you lack adequate evidence for |
> | **Relation** | be relevant |
> | **Manner** | be perspicuous: avoid obscurity and ambiguity, be brief and orderly |
>
> The useful idea is **implicature**: the meaning a hearer derives from the assumption that you were
> obeying the maxims. *Some politicians are honest* implies that not all are, because a cooperative
> speaker who believed all were would have said **all**. The implication is not in the sentence; it is
> produced by the sentence plus the assumption of cooperation.
>
> A second idea is **flouting**. A maxim can be broken so obviously that the breach itself carries
> meaning. A reference letter that says only *the candidate's handwriting is excellent* obeys Quality
> and visibly breaks Quantity, and every reader draws the conclusion.
>
> For a writer, two consequences follow, and they are not small.
>
> **Your hedges commit you.** If you write *some benefits are real*, you have conceded that some are
> not. If that is not your position, you have given away a paragraph for a word.
>
> **A concession stated is stronger than a concession implied.** Writing *admittedly the cost is high,
> but* controls the inference. Leaving it out does not prevent the reader making it; it only prevents
> you answering it.
>
> **A LECTURE, NOT A STUDY**
> This is a **published lecture**: an argument with invented examples, not an experiment. It reports
> no data, measures no hearers, and offers no evidence beyond the author's own intuitions about what
> people would conclude.
>
> The maxims have **not been agreed since**. Later work in the same field reduced them to a single
> principle of relevance; others have multiplied them; others again have argued that they are
> culturally specific rather than general features of cooperation. Nobody treats the list of four as
> settled.
>
> It is also about **conversation**, not writing, and the author says so. An essay has no turn-taking,
> no immediate feedback and no shared situation, and how far the maxims transfer to a written text
> addressed to a stranger is a question the lecture does not raise.
>
> And it says nothing whatever about examinations, about English teaching, or about how to write a
> paragraph.
>
> *Our summary of a published lecture, named with its volume, its editors, its publication year and
> the year it was delivered. The application to an essay's hedges is ours.*

### 2 · Choosing the word that controls the inference

| | |
|---|---|
| **Most** of the benefits are real. | most, not some |
| The benefits are **real**, though unevenly distributed. | no quantifier at all |
| The policy has succeeded, **and shows no sign of reversing**. | blocks the short-term reading |
| The data is **sufficient for this purpose**. | blocks *no better than adequate* |
| The scheme is **both lawful and sensible**. | blocks *legal but unwise* |

**2A  Rewrite each sentence so that the unwanted inference does not arise.**

1 Some of the benefits are real. (you think most are)
2 The policy has had short-term success. (you think it will last)
3 The evidence is adequate. (you think it is strong)
4 The scheme is legal. (you think it is also wise)
5 Many economists accept this. (you think nearly all do)

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL5-03-09%%

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Read the prompt's quantifiers before you read anything else.** *Some people believe*, *many
> argue*, *it is often claimed*: these are part of the question, and an essay that treats *some* as

> *most*, you have conceded half your position in one word, and a marker reading for a clear position

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> How much you may leave unsaid differs by language and by setting. Some written traditions expect the
> reader to draw the conclusion and find spelling it out insulting; others expect the writer to state
> everything and treat implication as evasive. Part of the 1975 lecture's own afterlife has been the
> argument about whether its maxims are universal or local. **Ask your group: in your first language,
