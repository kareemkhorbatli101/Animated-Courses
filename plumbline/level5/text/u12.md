### 2 · Read

%%FIG qe12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a two-row table of the two construct failures, a short list of what the paper says does and does not produce good washback, a boxed paragraph headed a conceptual paper, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what a test does to the teaching that prepares for it**
> **Source: Samuel Messick, *Validity and washback in language testing*, Language Testing, 1996.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **Washback** is the effect a test has on teaching and learning. Everybody in a preparation center
> knows the phenomenon; the paper's contribution is an argument about where it belongs.
>
> Its central claim is that washback is **not a separate property of a test**. It is part of
> **validity** — specifically of what the author calls the consequential aspect of construct validity.
> A test's social consequences, including what it does to classrooms, are evidence about whether the
> test measures what it claims to.
>
> The argument runs through two familiar failures.
>
> | The failure | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | **construct under-representation** | the test is too narrow: it leaves out parts of the ability it claims to measure |
> | **construct-irrelevant variance** | the test is contaminated: something other than the ability affects the scores |
>
> If a test under-represents its construct, preparation will concentrate on the narrow part that is
> tested, and the rest of the ability will be neglected. If a test contains irrelevant variance,
> preparation will concentrate on the irrelevant thing, because the irrelevant thing moves scores.
> **In both cases the bad teaching is a rational response to the test.**
>
> The consequence the author draws is uncomfortable for teachers and examiners alike.
> **Exhortation does not work.** Telling teachers to teach the language rather than the test does not
> produce good washback, because the teaching was a reasonable reaction to what the test rewards. What
> produces good washback is a test whose demands and the ability's demands **coincide** — which means
> minimizing both failures, and preferring tasks that resemble real use of the language.
>
> For this book, the argument has a direct application, and it is not flattering.
>
> **A preparation book is washback.** This one exists because the examination exists. Its twelve units
> are shaped by what the examination rewards, and if the examination rewarded something else they
> would be different units.
>
> The test this book is for asks you to take a position and defend it in forty minutes. That is a
> narrow slice of writing: no research, no sources, no revision, no reader you know. On the paper's own
> terms that is construct under-representation, and the honest thing to say about this book is that it
> teaches you to do well at the slice.
>
> What keeps it defensible is that the slice is not fake. Stating a position, warranting it, conceding
> the strongest objection and saying what would change your mind are real skills, useful outside any
> examination, and the eleven units of this book are built on published work about them rather than on
> templates. But a book that taught you four memorized paragraph shapes would also raise your band,
> and it would be pure irrelevant variance.
>
> **A CONCEPTUAL PAPER**
> This is a **theoretical argument**, not a study. It reports **no data**, measures washback nowhere,
> and tests none of its claims empirically.
>
> The concept of washback has been **argued about ever since**: how far it extends, whether it is
> mostly produced by the test or by how the test is used, and whether it can be shown that a test
> caused a teaching change at all, given how many other things change at the same time.
>
> The author's wider framework for validity — the one washback is folded into — has also been
> **debated at length** and modified by others, and later writers have reorganized the whole account
> of validity as an argument to be built rather than a set of aspects.
>
> And it is a paper for **test developers and researchers**. It gives candidates no advice, says
> nothing about how to write an essay, and names no bands.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its journal and its year. The application to this book
> is ours, and we think it is the honest one.*

> **Essay (extract)**
> Public examinations should be judged partly by what they do to the teaching that prepares for them.
> The argument for judging a test only by whether it measures accurately is that a measuring instrument
> is not responsible for what people do with it; **what is less often noticed is that preparation
> behavior is not a separate matter from accuracy, because a test that can be prepared for by
> memorizing shapes is, by that very fact, measuring something other than the ability it names.** The
> principle here is general: **an instrument whose readings can be moved without moving the thing
> measured is measuring something else.** It might be objected that no test can be immune to
> preparation, which is true and does not help the opposite side, since the question is one of degree
> and some tests are far more easily gamed than others. **I would abandon this position if it could be
> shown that the most easily prepared-for tests predicted later performance as well as the least.**
