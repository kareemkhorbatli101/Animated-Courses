# Unit 8 · Three Moves in an Introduction
%%SUB A framework from 1990 for the first paragraph of a research article, and the one move your essay is missing%%
%%GOALS
Name the three moves of an academic introduction
Establish a gap rather than announce a topic
Use *while*, *however*, *what is less often noticed*
Say why the framework does not fit your essay, and what still does
Say what a genre analysis describes
%%

Every introduction you have written begins by saying what the subject is. A framework published in
1990 showed that academic introductions do three things, and the second one is the one nobody
teaches: they say what is **missing**. That move is worth more to your essay than the framework it
came from.

%%FIG qe08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a three-row table of the moves with example wordings, a funnel diagram, a boxed paragraph headed a description of a genre you are not writing, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what the first paragraph of an academic article is doing**
> **Source: John M. Swales, *Genre Analysis: English in Academic and Research Settings*, Cambridge
> University Press, 1990. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book analyzes academic writing as a set of **genres**: kinds of text whose shape is produced by
> what a community of readers and writers uses them for. Its best-known result is a model of the
> introduction to a research article, built by examining how published introductions are actually
> organized.
>
> The model has three **moves**.
>
> | The move | What it does | How it is worded |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1 · establishing a territory** | claims that the area matters; reviews what is known | *X has attracted considerable attention* · *It is generally accepted that* |
> | **2 · establishing a niche** | identifies a gap, a contradiction or a neglected question | *However, little attention has been paid to* · *The evidence remains inconclusive* |
> | **3 · occupying the niche** | announces what this article will do | *This paper reports* · *The present study examines* |
>
> The shape is a **funnel**: wide, then narrow, then a step through. The author's point is that move
> 2 is what makes the article necessary. Without it, an introduction announces a subject; with it, it
> announces a reason.
>
> For a Task 2 essay the framework **does not apply**, and it is important to say so plainly. You are
> not reporting research, you have no literature to review, and you have nothing to cite. The three
> moves describe a genre you are not writing, and the author would say so first.
>
> What transfers is **one move**. An introduction that goes *the subject matters* → *this is what the
> usual argument gets wrong* → *my position* gives a reader a reason that an introduction without the
> middle sentence does not. The wording changes — *little attention has been paid* is wrong for an
> essay — but *what is less often noticed is* is exactly right, and it is one sentence.
>
> **A DESCRIPTION OF A GENRE YOU ARE NOT WRITING**
> The model was built by **analyzing published research articles**, in particular disciplines, at a
> particular time. It is **descriptive**: it reports what those introductions do, not what any text
> ought to do.
>
> The author **revised it himself**. A later book of his reworked the second move substantially, with
> different steps and a different account of how the niche is established, so the 1990 version is a
> stage in his thinking rather than a settled result. Quoting it as the final form of the model would
> be wrong.
>
> Conventions also **differ by discipline and by language**. Introductions in some fields establish no
> gap at all, and academic traditions in other languages organize an opening quite differently.
>
> And the concept of a **discourse community**, which the book leans on, has been argued about ever
> since: who exactly belongs to one, and how it is bounded, are not questions the book settles.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year. The claim that one move
> transfers to a Task 2 essay is ours, not the book's.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Saying what other writers have missed is a convention of English-language research writing, and it
> is not universal. In some academic cultures an opening that criticizes predecessors reads as
> arrogant, and the expected move is to place yourself respectfully within a tradition. The 1990 model
> describes introductions in particular disciplines in one language, and the author's own later work
> went on to compare conventions across languages. **Ask your group: in your academic tradition, may a
> new writer say what the established ones got wrong?**
