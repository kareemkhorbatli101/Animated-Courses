# Unit 10 · Planning, Writing, Reviewing
%%SUB A paper from 1981 that found writing is not three stages but three processes, interrupting each other%%
%%GOALS
Say what the three processes are and why they are not stages
Review a sentence while writing the next one
Redraft in words rather than by crossing out
Set a goal and change it when the text changes
Say what a think-aloud theory rests on
%%

You were taught to plan, then write, then check. A paper published in 1981 listened to writers
composing and found nothing of the kind: all three were happening at once, interrupting each other,
and the difference between a weak writer and a strong one was not the order but what they
interrupted.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qe10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a diagram of three processes with arrows in both directions, a four-row table of the paper's main claims, a boxed paragraph headed built from protocols, and criticized since, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Writing class**
> **Subject: what is actually happening in the forty minutes**
> **Source: Linda Flower and John R. Hayes, *A Cognitive Process Theory of Writing*, College
> Composition and Communication, 1981. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper was written against the way composition was then taught: as a sequence of stages —
> pre-writing, writing, re-writing — through which a text was supposed to pass once.
>
> The authors recorded writers **thinking aloud** while composing and built a model from what they
> heard. Its three processes are familiar; the claim about them is not.
>
> | The claim | What it means |
> |---|---|
> | writing is a set of **distinctive thinking processes** | planning, translating and reviewing are different kinds of work |
> | the processes are **hierarchical and embedded** | any one of them can happen inside any other: you plan in the middle of a sentence |
> | composing is **goal-directed** | the writer is working toward a growing network of goals, not filling a shape |
> | writers **create and change** their own goals | a high-level goal generates sub-goals, and what you learn while writing can change the high-level goal |
>
> The three processes are coordinated by what the paper calls a **monitor**: the part of the writer
> that decides, moment by moment, which process to be in. The model also separates the **task
> environment** — the assignment, and the text produced so far — from the writer's **long-term
> memory**.
>
> The difference the authors report between more and less experienced writers is not the order of the
> stages. It is how richly the writer **represents the rhetorical problem**. Less experienced writers
> treat the task as a topic to be covered; more experienced ones build a problem that includes the
> reader, the purpose and their own position, and keep working on it while they write.
>
> For your forty minutes, three consequences follow.
>
> **The text so far is part of the task.** What you have written changes what you can write next, so
> re-reading your last sentence before starting the next one is not wasted time.
>
> **Some interruptions are worth paying for and some are not.** Stopping to ask whether the reader
> will accept the paragraph is planning, and it earns its minute. Stopping to find a better adjective
> is reviewing at the wrong level, and in a timed essay it costs more than it returns.
>
> **A goal may change.** If your position narrows as you write, say so in the conclusion rather than
> defending the sentence you wrote in minute eight.
>
> **BUILT FROM PROTOCOLS, AND CRITICIZED SINCE**
> The model rests on **think-aloud protocols** from a **small number of writers**. Protocols record
> only what gets said: a process the writer cannot verbalize leaves no trace, and being asked to
> narrate changes the task.
>
> It is a **theory of process**, not a measure of quality. Nothing in it says which essays are good,
> and nothing in it predicts a score.
>
> It has also been **argued against at length**. The best-known criticism is that it treats writing as
> something happening inside one head, and leaves out the social situation — who is being written for,
> under what institutional power, in what community's conventions. A later generation of researchers
> rebuilt the field around exactly that objection, and the authors themselves went on to revise and
> extend the model.
>
> And it concerns **untimed composing in a first language**. A forty-minute examination in a second
> language is not what was studied.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its journal and its year. The three consequences for a
> timed essay are ours.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Re-read your last sentence before you write the next one, and review nothing else until the
> paragraph ends.** Those two habits cost about four seconds each and they prevent the two commonest
> timed-essay failures: a paragraph that forgets what it was arguing, and ten minutes lost to
> hunting for adjectives. Keep two minutes at the end for the only review that pays: checking that
> your conclusion says what your body paragraphs actually showed.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Whether a text may be seen in an unfinished state is cultural. In some traditions a writer shows
> drafts, invites comment and expects to redraft; in others a text is shown only when it is finished,
> and visible correction is a loss of face. A handwritten examination forces the first mode on
> everybody: your crossings-out are part of what is read, and correcting forward in words is the
> civilized way to do it. **Ask your group: in your education, did anybody ever see your drafts?**
