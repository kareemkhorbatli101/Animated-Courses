# Unit 10 · How Sure the Writer Is
%%SUB A framework from 2005, the words that carry a writer's commitment, and why coding them is interpretive%%
%%GOALS
Say what hedges, boosters and attitude markers do
Find the writer's view in a passage, and its strength
Decide a yes-no-not-given question from the writer's commitment
Use modals and verbs of likelihood accurately
Say why a functional category is interpretive rather than countable

Some questions do not ask what the passage says. They ask what the **writer thinks**, and how sure
they are of it. A framework published in 2005 names the words that carry that, and is honest about
how slippery they are to count.

%%FIG qc10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a two-part table of interactive and interactional resources, a three-row table of hedges boosters and attitude markers, a boxed paragraph headed functional categories are interpretive, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: the words that tell you what the writer will stand behind**
> **Source: Ken Hyland, *Metadiscourse: Exploring Interaction in Writing*, Continuum, **2005**.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> An academic text does two things at once. It makes claims about the world, and it manages the
> reader: organizing the text, signalling how sure the writer is, and inviting the reader to agree.
> The second of those is what this book calls **metadiscourse**.
>
> It divides metadiscourse into two broad groups. **Interactive** resources help the reader through
> the text: transitions (*however*, *therefore*), frame markers (*first*, *finally*, *to conclude*),
> references to other parts of the same text, citations, and definitions given in passing.
> **Interactional** resources position the writer and the reader: **hedges**, **boosters**, **attitude
> markers**, self-mentions (*I*, *we*) and direct engagement with the reader (*note that*,
> *consider*).
>
> Three of those matter in a reading paper.
>
> A **hedge** withholds full commitment: *may*, *might*, *possibly*, *appear to*, *suggest*, *tend
> to*, *it would seem*. The claim is made, and the writer has not promised it.
>
> A **booster** does the opposite: *clearly*, *obviously*, *demonstrate*, *must*, *undoubtedly*. The
> writer is standing fully behind the claim, and sometimes is standing there because the evidence is
> thinner than they would like.
>
> An **attitude marker** tells you how the writer feels: *unfortunately*, *surprisingly*,
> *importantly*. It is not about the strength of the claim but about its value.
>
> Now the connection to the paper. Some question types ask whether a statement agrees with **what the
> writer thinks**. A statement that removes a hedge is not the writer's view. *The decline may have
> been caused by the tax* does not support *the decline was caused by the tax*. One word, and the
> verdict changes.
>
> **FUNCTIONAL CATEGORIES ARE INTERPRETIVE**
> This is a **framework** built from corpora of academic writing, developed over a series of studies
> by the author and others.
>
> The categories are **functional**, which means they are defined by what a word is doing rather than
> by what it is. The same word does different work in different places: *clearly* can boost a claim or
> can simply mean *in a way that is easy to see*. Coding a text for metadiscourse therefore involves
> **judgement**, and analysts disagree. Any count of hedges in a text is a count of somebody's
> readings.
>
> The conventions are not universal. How much hedging is appropriate **varies by discipline** —
> physics and history do not hedge alike — and varies between **writing cultures**, so a writer
> trained in one tradition can sound evasive or arrogant in another without intending either.
>
> And the book is about **writing**. It describes what writers do; it does not tell a reader how to
> answer a question, and Book 5 of this series uses it for the other side of the same problem.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The center's copy is on the shelf; the chapter on stance is the
> one to read.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> How much a writer hedges is partly a matter of discipline and partly of writing culture. Some
> traditions treat a plain claim as confident and admirable; others treat it as unsupported and rude.
> A writer moving between them can seem evasive in one direction and arrogant in the other, without
> changing a single opinion. The framework in this unit describes the resources and does not rank the
> traditions. **Ask your group: in academic writing in your language, is a strong flat claim normal?
> What would a cautious writer sound like?**
