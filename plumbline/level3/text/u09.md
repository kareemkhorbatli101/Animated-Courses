Everything in the last eight units runs at a speed, and the speed is the thing nobody measures. A
book published in 2009 gathers what is known about reading rate in a second language, and its honest
answer about how to raise it is slow, dull and the only one supported.

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: how fast a reader reads, and how a rate changes**
> **Source: William Grabe, *Reading in a Second Language: Moving from Theory to Practice*, Cambridge
> University Press, **2009**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Reading has a **rate**, and the rate is the reason the hour in Unit 1 is so short.
>
> A fluent adult reading their **first** language manages, on ordinary prose, somewhere around **250
> to 300 words a minute**. A **second-language** reader is typically much slower — often well under
> **150**. Academic prose slows everybody, and slows the slower reader more.
>
> The reason is not mysterious. Fluent reading depends on **automatic word recognition**: the word is
> there before you have done anything about it, and attention is free for the sentence. When
> recognition is not automatic, part of every second goes on identifying words, and that is the
> **bottleneck**. Comprehension strategies do not remove it: you cannot strategize your way past a
> word you are still working out.
>
> What raises a rate? The book reviews the evidence and gives two routes, both of them unfashionable.
>
> **Extensive reading** — large quantities of material that is **easy for you**, read for meaning
> rather than studied. Easy matters: material at your level makes recognition automatic through sheer
> repetition, and difficult material does not, because you spend the time decoding.
>
> **Timed repeated reading** — reading the same short passage several times against a clock, so that
> the familiar words become fast.
>
> Both require **volume** and both work **slowly**. The book does not offer a technique that produces
> a faster reader in a fortnight, and it is explicit that it has none.
>
> **A SYNTHESIS THAT MARKS ITS OWN IMPLICATIONS**
> This is a **synthesis**: a gathering of a large research literature into one account, written for
> teachers. The author is careful to separate what the research **found** from what he thinks
> **follows** for teaching, and he labels the second kind as implications. That distinction is worth
> copying.
>
> The rate figures are **approximate**. They vary with the text, with the purpose of the reading, and
> with how the measurement was done; a figure for a group says nothing about any individual, including
> you.
>
> The evidence on **extensive reading** is, as the author notes, uneven in quality: many studies have
> design limitations, and the claim that it raises rate rests on an accumulation rather than on one
> decisive trial.
>
> And it is a book about **reading**, not about examinations. It says nothing about dividing an hour
> or about any score.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The center's copy is on the shelf; the chapters on fluency are the
> ones to read.*

### 1 · Participle clauses

A participle clause compresses one clause into a phrase. Academic prose is full of them and they are
the commonest reason a long sentence is hard.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Left outside**, the samples deteriorated. | past participle: passive meaning |
| **Examining the records**, he found three errors. | *-ing*: active, same time |
| **Having failed twice**, the method was abandoned. | perfect: earlier |
| The scheme, **approved in 1961**, ran for nine years. | past participle inside a noun phrase |
| **Published in haste**, the figures were soon disputed. | past participle, front position |

**1A  Compress each pair into one sentence with a participle. Then read your sentences aloud with a partner.**

1 The records were stored in a damp cellar. They were illegible by 1950.
2 She examined four sites. She found pottery at one.
3 The committee had met twice. It reported in March.
4 The method was introduced in 1908. It survived until the war.
5 He was writing in 1912. He predicted the opposite.

**1B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL3-09-08%%

**1C  Say four compressed sentences to a partner. They expand each one into two.**

### 2 · Expanding a participle to understand it

Reading is the opposite operation. When a sentence starts with a participle, find the subject it
belongs to.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Left outside, the samples deteriorated.** | who was left outside? *the samples* |
| **Having failed twice, the method was abandoned.** | what failed? *the method* |
| **Examining the records, he found three errors.** | who examined? *he* |
| ✗ *Left outside, we found the samples had deteriorated.* | a dangling participle: *we* were not left outside |
| **Approved in 1961, the scheme ran for nine years.** | what was approved? *the scheme* |

**2A  Say who or what the participle belongs to. Then read the sentence aloud.**

1 Having been revised twice, the figures are now accepted.
2 Writing in the 1880s, the author could not have known this.
3 Stored in sealed boxes, the letters survived.
4 Asked to explain, the committee declined.
5 Measured against the earlier survey, the result is modest.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL3-09-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students attach the participle to the nearest noun instead of the
subject of the main clause, and answer a question about the wrong thing. **Say your partner four
participle sentences and let them name the subject before you finish.**

### 📝 ON THE DAY

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Reading a large quantity of easy material is, in many study cultures, not studying at all. Study
> means difficulty: the hardest text, the dictionary open, a page an hour. The research in this unit
