# Unit 11 · A Formula That Counts Syllables
%%SUB A readability yardstick from 1948, what it measures, and what this series does with it anyway%%
%%GOALS
Say what a readability formula counts
Say what it cannot count
Break a long sentence into two without losing the relation
Join two sentences and say what that does to the score
Say why this book uses a formula it has just criticized
%%

Two numbers — how long the sentences are, and how many syllables the words have — produce a score
that says how hard a text is. The formula is from 1948, it is still used everywhere, and the list of
things it cannot see is longer than the list of things it counts.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qc11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a two-row table of the variables, a three-row table of one paragraph in three versions with a score each, a boxed paragraph headed what a formula cannot see, a short note headed and this series uses one anyway, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: how a machine decides a page is hard**
> **Source: Rudolf Flesch, *A new readability yardstick*, Journal of Applied Psychology, **1948**.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper offers a way of scoring how hard a piece of English prose is to read, from two numbers:
> the **average length of its sentences**, in words, and the **average number of syllables** per
> hundred words. Those two figures go into a formula and produce a score, higher meaning easier.
>
> Flesch also proposed a second score for how **interesting** a text is, built from the proportion of
> personal words and personal sentences — a reminder that the whole exercise was aimed at ordinary
> prose written for general readers, not at academic writing.
>
> The formula is useful, cheap and still in use after seventy-five years. It is also, by design,
> **blind**.
>
> **WHAT A FORMULA CANNOT SEE**
> It counts **surface features**. It does not know whether the words are **frequent or rare**: a
> hundred words of one syllable each can be a hundred words you have never met. It does not know
> whether the sentences are **connected**, so a page of unrelated short sentences scores as easy. It
> cannot see **cohesion**, so the *this*, *such* and *the latter* of Unit 6 are invisible to it. It
> cannot see an **argument**, so a passage whose logic is impossible to follow scores exactly as its
> syllables dictate.
>
> And it cannot see the **reader**. Topic familiarity, which Unit 2 showed changes a score, is nowhere
> in the formula.
>
> The consequence is a trick anybody can play. Take a hard paragraph and **chop every sentence in
> half**. The average sentence length falls, the score rises, and the text becomes harder to read,
> because the relations between the clauses have been thrown away and the reader now has to guess
> them. A formula can be satisfied by making a text worse.
>
> The validation was against a set of passages and criteria **of its period**, for English prose aimed
> at general readers. It was not built for academic text, and it was not built for readers of English
> as a second language — for whom vocabulary coverage, not sentence length, is the thing Unit 4
> showed to matter most.
>
> **AND THIS SERIES USES ONE ANYWAY**
> Every reading text in every book of this series is scored with a readability formula before the book
> is built, and a text outside a set range is rewritten. This unit is the honest note on that
> practice. A formula is a **floor, not a judge**: it catches a text that has drifted far from its
> level, and it cannot tell anybody whether a text is any good. Everything that makes the texts in
> this series readable — one idea at a time, a named source, a short honest paragraph at the end — is
> invisible to the number that approves them.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. It is short, and the formula is on one page.*

### 1 · Breaking a sentence and keeping the relation

| One sentence | Two sentences |
|---|---|
| **Although** it failed twice, it was revived. | It failed twice. **Even so**, it was revived. |
| The figures are incomplete, **so** no estimate is possible. | The figures are incomplete. No estimate is **therefore** possible. |
| **Because** the records burned, the date is unknown. | The records burned. The date is unknown **as a result**. |
| **While** the sample was small, the result held. | The sample was small. The result held **nevertheless**. |
| It was approved, **but** nothing was built. | It was approved. Nothing, **however**, was built. |

### 2 · Joining two sentences, and what it costs the score

| Two sentences | One sentence |
|---|---|
| The sample was small. The result held. | **Although** the sample was small, the result held. |
| The records burned. The date is unknown. | **Because** the records burned, the date is unknown. |
| It was approved. Nothing was built. | It was approved, **though** nothing was built. |
| Three sites were dug. One produced pottery. | Of the three sites dug, **only one** produced pottery. |
| The figures are incomplete. They are the only source. | **Incomplete though they are**, the figures are the only source. |

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **A hard passage is usually hard for a reason the formula cannot see.** When a passage feels
> impossible, it is almost never the sentence length: it is the unknown words of Unit 4, the reference
> chains of Unit 6, or an argument you have not yet found. Naming which of those it is takes five
> seconds and tells you what to do. *This passage is hard* tells you nothing and costs you minutes.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Readability formulas are embedded in software, in government writing guidelines and in school
> systems all over the world, and in most of those places the number is treated as a verdict rather
> than as a smoke alarm. Writers learn to satisfy the number, which can be done by making prose
> choppier, and the guidance that was meant to make documents clearer sometimes makes them less so.
> **Ask your group: does any organization in your country publish a readability rule? What do writers
