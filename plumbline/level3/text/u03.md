%%SUB Twenty years of eye-tracking summarized in 1998, and why it does not support speed reading%%

Your eyes do not move smoothly along a line. They jump, stop, jump, stop, and sometimes go back. In
1998 somebody summarized twenty years of measuring exactly that — and the measurements rule out
several things you have probably been told about reading faster.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qc03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a four-row table of measurements, a diagram of one line of text with the stops marked and a clear window drawn round one of them, a boxed paragraph headed mechanics, not a method, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: what the eye actually does, measured**
> **Source: Keith Rayner, *Eye movements in reading and information processing: 20 years of
> research*, Psychological Bulletin, **1998**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Reading feels smooth. It is not. The eye makes a series of short stops, called **fixations**,
> separated by fast jumps, called **saccades**. Nothing useful is taken in during a jump. All the
> reading happens at the stops.
>
> Twenty years of measurement give approximate figures. A fixation lasts around a quarter of a
> second. A saccade moves the eye about eight or nine letter spaces. And the part of the line that is
> clear enough to use at any one stop — the **perceptual span** — is **small and asymmetric**: a few
> letters to the left of the fixation, and around fifteen to the right, for a reader of English.
>
> About one jump in eight goes **backwards** to something already read. These **regressions** are
> normal and happen in skilled readers reading easy text.
>
> Fixation time is not fixed. A **rare** word holds the eye longer than a frequent one. A word the
> sentence has already made **predictable** is often **skipped** — and short function words are
> skipped a great deal of the time, which is one reason you cannot report them afterwards.
>
> Two consequences follow for anybody preparing for a reading paper.
>
> **The useful field is about fifteen letters wide.** Any technique that asks you to take in a whole
> line, or a block of five words, at one stop is asking for something the measurements say the visual
> system does not do. Reading faster is not a matter of widening the span.
>
> **Hard words cost time at the point of fixation.** A passage full of unfamiliar vocabulary is slow
> for a mechanical reason, not only a comprehension one, and no strategy removes that. Unit 4 is
> about how much unfamiliar vocabulary a reader can carry.
>
> **MECHANICS, NOT A METHOD**
> This is a **review of laboratory research**. Participants read in controlled conditions, often with
> the head supported, and were mostly **skilled readers of their first language** reading sentences
> and short texts rather than sitting an examination.
>
> It is about the **mechanics of the eye**, not about understanding. It does not say how to choose
> what to read closely, how to divide an hour, or how a second-language reader differs.
>
> It contains **no training**. Knowing where your eyes go does not move them, and the paper does not
> claim it does. What it gives you is a floor: a set of limits any honest advice about reading faster
> has to respect.
>
> *Our summary of a published review. Read it if your center has access; it is long and the figures
> are the part to keep.*

### 1 · Finding the main clause

Every academic sentence has one main clause: a subject and a verb that could stand alone. Everything
else is attached.

| | |
|---|---|
| Although the first survey found nothing, **later work confirmed it.** | after a concession |
| The method, which had been abandoned, **was revived.** | round a relative clause |
| Having examined the records, **the commission reported in favor.** | after a participle |
| **The figures remain the only source**, incomplete though they are. | before a concession |
| In the years that followed, **the practice spread.** | after a time phrase |

**1A  Underline the main clause and say it aloud on its own.**

1 Despite the cost, which had risen twice, the scheme went ahead.
2 Writing in 1912, the engineer predicted the opposite.
3 The samples, taken from four separate sites, showed no difference.
4 Because the records were destroyed, the figure cannot be checked.
5 What the survey did establish, after two further trials, was the rate.

**1B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL3-03-08%%

**1C  Read your partner four long sentences from any academic page. They say the main clause only.**

### 2 · Putting the stripped parts back

A stripped sentence is faster and poorer. Each removed part did a job.

| The part | What it adds |
|---|---|
| **Although / While / Despite** | a concession: something that argues the other way |
| **which / who** (non-defining) | extra information, removable |
| **that** (defining) | which one; not removable |
| **Having …ed / …ing** | an earlier or simultaneous action |
| **in 1948 / after two trials** | when, and how many |

**2A  Say what each stripped part was doing. Then read the full sentence aloud.**

1 *Although the sample was small*, the result was clear.
2 The letter, *which was never sent*, survives in a private collection.
3 The documents *that survive* are all from one year.
4 *Having failed twice*, the committee changed the method.
5 The population doubled *between 1890 and 1911*.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL3-03-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students read a long sentence, find a word from the question inside a
concession clause, and answer from the half of the sentence that argues the other way. **Say your
partner four sentences with concessions and let them tell you which half the writer believes.**

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Read the main clauses first, and come back for the rest.** In a long academic sentence the answer
> to a question is usually in the main clause, and the concessions, relative clauses and participles
> are where the distractors live. If a question word appears inside an *although* clause, stop and
> work out which half of the sentence the writer actually believes before you answer. That one habit
> is worth more than any technique for moving your eyes faster.
