# Unit 6 · This, Such, The Latter
%%SUB A description from 1976 of what holds a text together, and the distinction it insists on%%
%%GOALS

A text is not a row of sentences. Something holds it together, and most of that something is a
handful of very small words: *this*, *these*, *such*, *so*, *one*, *the latter*. A book published in
1976 catalogued them, and insisted on a distinction that most courses collapse.

%%FIG qc06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the book, the publisher and the year, a five-row table of the kinds of tie, two short constructed examples — one cohesive and senseless, one coherent with no ties — a boxed paragraph headed a description, and a distinction, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: the small words that carry you across a text**
> **Source: M. A. K. Halliday and R. Hasan, *Cohesion in English*, Longman, **1976**. Printed here
> in our own words.**
>
> What makes a sequence of sentences a **text** rather than a list of sentences? This book's answer is
> **cohesion**: a set of resources in the language that tie one part of a text to another. It
> catalogues them in five groups.
>
> **Reference** points at something else: *it*, *they*, *this*, *these*, *such*, *the former*, *the
> latter*. Most reference in academic prose is **anaphoric** — it points **back**. And here is the
> fact that costs marks: a demonstrative like *this* can point back not at a noun but at **a whole
> stretch of text** — a clause, a sentence, a paragraph, an argument.
>
> **Substitution** puts a short item in place of a longer one: *the smaller **one***, *they did
> **so***. **Ellipsis** leaves something out which the reader supplies: *Two were tested and three
> were not*. **Conjunction** marks the relation between two parts: *however*, *therefore*,
> *nevertheless*. And **lexical cohesion** holds a text together by repeating a word, or by using a
> related one — *the sample*, *the specimen*, *the material* — so that a reader follows one thing
> through a paragraph under three names.
>
> **A DESCRIPTION, AND A DISTINCTION**
> The book is a **description of a language**, written for linguists. It is an account of what
> English makes available, not a method for reading and not a set of instructions for writing.
>
> Its central distinction is the one most courses collapse. **Cohesion is not coherence.** Cohesion
> is the presence of ties. Coherence is whether the text makes sense. You can write a passage stuffed
> with *however* and *therefore* and *this* that means nothing — it is cohesive and incoherent. You
> can write a short passage with almost no ties that is perfectly clear. A student taught that
> "linkers make writing coherent" has been taught something the source does not say, and will write
> Task 2 essays that are joined together and go nowhere. Book 5 of this series returns to that.
>
> The **categories overlap** and analysts disagree about where particular ties belong; coding a text
> for cohesion is interpretive work, not counting.
>
> And it is **of its period**. Published in 1976, it has been extended and revised in later work,
> including by one of its own authors, and the field has moved around it.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The center's copy is on the shelf; the chapter on reference is
> the one to read.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **When a question turns on a sentence beginning *This*, go back one sentence and read the whole of
> it.** Not the last noun — the whole sentence, and sometimes the one before. Two marks a paper live
> in that one movement of the eye, and it takes four seconds. The same goes for *such*, which points
> at a kind of thing and not at the thing, and for *the latter*, which means the second of two and is
> wrong half the time in students' answers.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Writing traditions differ in how much joining they expect on the surface. Some expect every step to
> be marked with a connector, so that a paragraph reads as a chain; others expect the reader to supply
> the relations, and surface connectors look heavy-handed. A reader trained in the second tradition
> under-reads English connectors; a writer trained in the first over-uses them. The book in this unit
> describes the resources and takes no side. **Ask your group: in your first language, how much is
> joined on the surface? What would a translator have to add or remove?**
