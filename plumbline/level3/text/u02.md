# Unit 2 · What a Reading Test Measures
%%SUB A book-length review from 2000 whose conclusion is that we know less than the test specifications suggest%%
%%GOALS
Say why reading cannot be measured directly
Say what a question type actually requires of you
Tell apart the strength of reporting verbs in a stem
Use reporting verbs accurately
Say why a review that reaches few conclusions is still worth reading
%%

Nobody can see you read. A test can only watch what you do with items, and from that it infers
something it calls reading. A book published in 2000 spends four hundred pages on how shaky that
inference is, and concludes that the confident language of test specifications is ahead of the
evidence.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qc02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a four-row table of findings, a short worked example of an item answered without the text, a boxed paragraph headed a review, not a method, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: what a reading test can and cannot measure**
> **Source: J. Charles Alderson, *Assessing Reading*, Cambridge University Press, **2000**. Printed
> here in our own words.**
>
> Reading is not observable. Nobody can watch it happen. A test can only look at what a reader
> **does with items** and infer something from that, and the thing it infers is called the
> **construct** — what the test claims to be measuring.
>
> This book reviews what is known about that inference, and much of what it reports is
> uncomfortable.
>
> **Some items can be answered without the text.** Cover the passage and a proportion of the
> questions remain answerable: from general knowledge, from the shape of the options, from the fact
> that three of four are implausible. Those items are measuring something. It is not reading this
> text.
>
> **Background knowledge affects performance.** Two readers with the same English will not score the
> same on a passage about soil chemistry if one of them is an agronomist. A test of reading in a
> second language is partly a test of what the reader already knows, and there is no clean way to
> remove that.
>
> **The question type changes the score.** The same text, tested with multiple choice and tested with
> short answers, produces different results. Part of a reading score is therefore a fact about the
> method, not about the reader.
>
> **Named skills are hard to separate in the data.** Test specifications routinely list skills:
> reading for gist, reading for detail, inferring meaning, identifying opinion. Studies that look for
> those skills as separate things in candidates' performance often cannot find them. The names may be
> useful for writing items. Whether they are separate abilities inside a reader is a different
> question, and the evidence is thin.
>
> The book's general conclusion, stated repeatedly and without drama, is that **we know less about
> reading and about reading tests than the confident language of test specifications suggests.**
>
> **A REVIEW, NOT A METHOD**
> This is a **review of a field**, not a study. It reports other people's work and weighs it, and it
> reaches **few firm conclusions on purpose**: a reader who wants a list of answers will be
> disappointed, and the disappointment is the honest result.
>
> It is written for **test developers and researchers**, not for candidates. There is no technique in
> it for sitting a paper, and nothing in it tells you how to spend an hour.
>
> It is also **of its period**. It was published in 2000, and a great deal of work on reading
> assessment has been published since; the questions it raises are live, and some of them have moved.
>
> And none of this makes the paper you will sit invalid. A measure can be imperfect, well made, and
> the best available, all at once — which is roughly the book's own position.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The center's copy is on the shelf; the chapters on the construct
> are the ones to read.*

| The stem | What it wants | Where the answer is |

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Answer every question, even badly.** Nothing is deducted for a wrong answer, some items are
> answerable without the text, and your background knowledge is sitting in the room with you whether
> the test wants it there or not. A guess costs you nothing and occasionally costs the test something.
> This is not a trick; it is the only sensible response to a measure that admits its own imprecision.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Examinations carry different kinds of authority in different places. Where a public examination is
> treated as a measurement of a person, saying that it measures imperfectly sounds like an attack on
> it, or like an excuse. Where examinations are treated as instruments, the same sentence is ordinary
> professional language and appears in the test publisher's own research. The book in this unit is
> written in the second register and is often read in the first. **Ask your group: in your country, is
> it acceptable to say that an examination measures imperfectly? Who is allowed to say it?**
