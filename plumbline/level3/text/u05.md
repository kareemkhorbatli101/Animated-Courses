# Unit 5 · Five Hundred and Seventy Families
%%SUB A list published in 2000, ten per cent of academic text, and the two thousand words it leaves out%%
%%GOALS
Say what the academic word list is and how it was built
Say what share of academic text it covers, and of fiction
Build the family of an academic word

Say why the list is useless on its own

Five hundred and seventy word families, chosen because they appear across every academic subject and
are not among the commonest two thousand words of English. Together they are about one word in ten
of an academic page — and on their own they are worth almost nothing.

### 2 · Six more words, and four selection criteria

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qc05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a four-row table of selection criteria, a two-row table of coverage figures, a boxed paragraph headed a list, not a syllabus, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: the academic word list, and the two thousand words it assumes**
> **Source: Averil Coxhead, *A New Academic Word List*, TESOL Quarterly, **2000**. Printed here in
> our own words.**
>
> The problem the list answers is the one Unit 4 left you with. An academic page is mostly common
> English, with a layer of words on top that are not technical to any one subject and yet appear in
> all of them: *analysis*, *constitute*, *derive*, *significant*, *framework*, *hence*. Nobody's
> general word list contains them in quantity, and no subject glossary does either, because they
> belong to academic writing rather than to a field.
>
> The list was built from a corpus of about **3.5 million** running words of academic text, spread
> across **four broad subject areas** — the arts, commerce, law and science — and a number of
> subjects within each. A word family got onto the list by meeting several criteria at once. It had to
> appear across **all four areas**, so that it was genuinely academic and not the vocabulary of one
> discipline. It had to be **frequent enough** in the corpus as a whole. And it had to be **outside
> the most frequent 2,000 words of English**, which the list assumes a reader already has.
>
> The result is **570 word families**, grouped into **ten sublists** in order of frequency, so that a
> learner can work through the first sublist before the tenth.
>
> The coverage figures are the reason the list matters. Those 570 families account for about **ten per
> cent** of the running words of academic text. In a corpus of **fiction** the same families account
> for a little over **one per cent**. That gap is a measurement of what academic prose is made of.
>
> **A LIST, NOT A SYLLABUS**
> The list **deliberately excludes the most frequent 2,000 words**, so it is not a vocabulary for
> reading: on its own it gives you one word in ten of a page you cannot read. It is a supplement, and
> the author presents it as one.
>
> It was built on a **corpus of one period and one selection of texts**. Coverage **varies by
> discipline**: a page of law and a page of biology are not equally well served, and a reader in one
> field will meet some of these families constantly and others never.
>
> It counts **families**, which assumes a learner who meets *constitute* can handle *constituent* and
> *constitution*. That assumption is doing some of the work in the figure of 570.
>
> It is a **list**. It is not an order of learning, not a method, not a set of definitions, and not a
> syllabus; the sublists are ordered by frequency, which is not the same as ordered by usefulness to
> you.
>
> And later work has proposed **alternatives** built from larger and newer corpora. The list is still
> widely used, and it is not the only one, and anybody who tells you it is the definitive account of
> academic vocabulary is overstating it.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. The list itself is freely available; find it and write down where
> from.*

### 1 · Word families and their stress

| | |
|---|---|

### 2 · Unpacking a nominalization

Academic writing turns verbs into nouns. Unpacking the noun back into a clause is how you understand
it.

| The nominalization | Unpacked |
|---|---|
| *the derivation of the figure from two sources* | somebody **derived** the figure from two sources |
| *a significant reduction in output* | output **fell** by a lot |
| *the integration of the two systems* | somebody **joined** the two systems |
| *the specification of the materials* | somebody **specified** which materials |
| *constitutional reform* | somebody **changed** the constitution |

**2A  Unpack each phrase into a clause with a subject and a verb. Then say both aloud.**

1 the exclusion of the first two thousand words
2 a marked variation between disciplines
3 the assumption of prior knowledge
4 the identification of three further criteria
5 the publication of an alternative list

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL3-05-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students read a nominalized sentence, cannot find a verb that means
anything, and decide the sentence is beyond them. **Say your partner four nominalizations and let them
unpack each one before you finish.**

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **A gap usually wants a form, not a word.** In summary completion and sentence completion the
> passage will contain the verb and the gap will want the noun, or the other way round. If you can
> hear *derive* in the passage and the gap follows *the*, write *derivation*. That is not a trick; it
> is what a word family is for, and it is why Unit 5 is in a book about reading rather than a book
> about vocabulary.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Word lists travel. A list published in one country in one decade can end up as the backbone of
> preparation courses on the other side of the world twenty years later, often without its own
