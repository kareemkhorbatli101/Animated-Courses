%%FIG qc12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the book, the publisher and the year, a six-row table of the qualities with the question each asks, a short worked trade-off, a boxed paragraph headed no formula, on purpose, a review list of the eleven sources, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — one page for a new Reading student**
> **Subject: what makes a test useful, and everything in Book 3**
> **Source: L. F. Bachman and A. S. Palmer, *Language Testing in Practice*, Oxford University Press,
> **1996**, with a review of the eleven sources of this book. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> This book has spent eleven units saying what a source cannot do. This is the frame for that habit.
>
> The book argues that the overriding quality of a test is its **usefulness**, and that usefulness is
> made up of six qualities which have to be considered together.
>
> **Reliability**: would the same person get a similar score on an equivalent version? **Construct
> validity**: is the test measuring what it claims to measure? **Authenticity**: does the task
> resemble language use outside the test? **Interactiveness**: does it engage the test taker's own
> language knowledge, topic knowledge and judgment? **Impact**: what does the test do to the people
> who sit it, to teaching, and to the institutions that use the scores? **Practicality**: can it be
> designed, delivered and marked with the resources available?
>
> The important claim is the next one. These six **cannot be evaluated separately**, and they **trade
> off**. Making a reading task more authentic — a real journal page, a real purpose, a real time
> pressure — may make it less reliable, because real tasks behave less consistently. Raising
> reliability by adding many short items may lower authenticity, because nobody outside a test reads
> like that. Improving practicality may cost both.
>
> And the authors give **no formula** for the balance. There is no weighting, no total, no score out
> of six. Usefulness has to be argued for **a particular test, used with particular people, for a
> particular purpose**, and the argument is the work.
>
> **THE ELEVEN, IN ONE LINE EACH**
> **The paper.** Three passages, forty questions, sixty minutes, no extra time. *The published
> description gives a design, not your paper.*
> **Measurement.** A test infers reading from items. *Some items need no text; background knowledge
> counts; the method changes the score.*
> **The eye.** Fixations, jumps, a span of about fifteen letters. *Laboratory work with
> first-language readers, and no training in it.*
> **Coverage.** About one unknown word in fifty for unassisted comprehension. *One fiction text,
> invented unknown words, a probable threshold.*
> **The academic list.** 570 families, a tenth of academic text. *It excludes the commonest two
> thousand words, and varies by discipline.*
> **Cohesion.** *This* can point back at a whole stretch of text. *A description of a language, and
> cohesion is not coherence.*
> **Verdicts.** Find the sentence that would make it false. *An abstract task whose results change
> with content, and the analogy is ours.*
> **Gist.** Delete, generalize, construct. *A model its own authors rebuilt twice.*
> **Rate.** 250 to 300 against often under 150. *Approximate figures, uneven evidence, and nothing
> about you.*
> **Stance.** Hedges, boosters, attitude markers. *Functional categories, so coding is interpretive.*
> **Readability.** Sentence length and syllables. *Blind to everything else, and this series uses one
> anyway.*
>
> **NO FORMULA, ON PURPOSE**
> The framework is written for **test developers**, not for candidates. There is nothing in it about
> answering a question, and it makes no claim about anybody's band.
>
> It is **of its period**. The field has moved since 1996, particularly towards treating validity as
> an argument to be built and evaluated rather than a quality to be possessed, and this book is one
> stage in that movement.
>
> And its central honesty is the one worth keeping: **a measure can be imperfect, carefully built, and
> the best available, all at once** — and saying all three at the same time is harder than praising it
> or attacking it.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. Read the chapter on usefulness if you read nothing else.*
