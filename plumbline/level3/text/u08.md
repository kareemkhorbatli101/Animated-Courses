Matching headings and summary completion ask one question in two shapes: **what is this paragraph
about?** A model published in 1978 says how a reader gets from a paragraph to an answer, and names
three operations that do it.

%%FIG qc08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a three-row table of the macro-rules with an example each, a worked reduction of one paragraph to one line, a boxed paragraph headed a model its own authors rebuilt twice, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: how a reader gets from a paragraph to one line**
> **Source: W. Kintsch and T. A. van Dijk, *Toward a model of text comprehension and production*,
> Psychological Review, **1978**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> A text has two levels. At the bottom is everything it actually says, sentence by sentence — the
> **microstructure**. Above that is what it is **about** — the **macrostructure**, the gist, the thing
> you could say in one line.
>
> The model's useful part is the account of how the second is produced from the first. It names
> **macro-rules**, operations that reduce detail to gist.
>
> **Deletion** drops what does not matter. A paragraph may give the dates of six trials; the gist is
> that six trials were run.
>
> **Generalization** replaces a set of specifics with one **superordinate** term. *Iron, copper and
> tin were tested* becomes *several metals were tested*.
>
> **Construction** replaces a sequence of events by the larger act they constitute. *He packed, left
> the house, boarded a train and arrived in the evening* becomes *he traveled*. Note what construction
> does: the word that ends up in your summary — *traveled* — was **not in the text at all**.
>
> The model also takes seriously the fact that a reader cannot hold a whole text in mind. Only a few
> propositions sit in a short-term **buffer** at any moment, which is why a paragraph has to be
> reduced as you go rather than at the end.
>
> For a reading paper, one consequence is practical and large. Matching headings compares a set of
> headings with a set of paragraphs. If you read a paragraph and write **one line of your own** before
> looking at the headings, you are comparing one line with eight. If you read the headings first and
> try each against each paragraph, you are making dozens of comparisons. The model does not say this —
> the arithmetic does — but it tells you what the one line is and how it is made.
>
> **A MODEL ITS OWN AUTHORS REBUILT TWICE**
> This is a **formal model** published in 1978. Its evidence came from experiments on **recall and
> summarization**: what people produce when asked to remember or summarize a text.
>
> **Both authors revised it substantially.** A book-length reworking followed in **1983**, and the
> first author published a different architecture, the construction–integration model, in **1988**.
> Anybody citing the 1978 paper as current is citing a stage rather than a position.
>
> The macro-rules are a **description of what summaries look like**. That readers carry out those
> operations, in that order, is a modeling claim and not a demonstrated procedure inside anybody's
> head.
>
> And it is about **comprehension**, not about examinations. It offers no technique, says nothing
> about matching headings, and makes no claim about any score.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. Read the three rules if nothing else; they are a quarter of a
> page.*

### 1 · Topic sentences, and paragraphs without one

Many academic paragraphs state their topic in the first sentence. Many do not.

| Where the topic sits | How to tell |
|---|---|
| **First sentence** | the rest of the paragraph gives examples or evidence for it |
| **Last sentence** | the paragraph builds and then states the point |
| **Second sentence** | the first sentence is a link back to the paragraph before |
| **Nowhere** | you have to construct one; no single sentence says it |
| **Split** | half in the first sentence, half in the last |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Summarizing is taught very differently around the world. In some traditions a good summary keeps
> the author's own words, because changing them is a kind of disrespect or a risk of error; in others
> a good summary must be in your own words, and reusing the author's is close to copying. The model in
> this unit is neutral about that and simply describes what summaries contain — including the fact
> that a summary often ends up using a word the text never used. **Ask your group: at school, were you
> taught to summarize in the author's words or your own? What happened if you did the other?**
