# Grammar reference

Every structure in this book, in unit order. The second column is the mistake it prevents, which is
the column worth reading twice.

## Starting Point · Sixty minutes

| | |
|---|---|
| **3 passages, 40 questions, 60 minutes, no extra time** | the hour includes writing the answers onto the sheet |
| **90 seconds a question, including the reading** | which is why nobody reads three passages twice |
| **One careful reader and one who finished** | the second understood less and scored more |
| **The hour ends whatever you are in the middle of** | an answer in your head at minute fifty-nine is worth nothing |

## Unit 1 · Noun phrases; the words that carry a question

| | |
|---|---|
| **the rapid *decline* of the coastal fishing industry** | the head noun is what you search for |
| ***where exactly*, *which side*, *is that before or after*** | short questions get exact answers |
| **the stem has two or three words that carry it** | searching for the whole stem finds nothing |
| **18, 19, 20, and 3 to check** | written on the paper before the hour starts |

## Unit 2 · Question stems; reporting verbs

| | |
|---|---|
| ***According to the passage*** vs ***The writer suggests*** | stated, against following from |
| ***states · claims · suggests · implies · concedes*** | five strengths, and *implies* means the words are not there |
| **some items are answerable with the text covered** | which is why you never leave a question blank |
| **background knowledge is in your score** | and so is the question type |

## Unit 3 · Finding the main clause

| | |
|---|---|
| **Although the first survey found nothing, *later work confirmed it*.** | the main clause is where the answer usually lives |
| **concessions, relative clauses, participles** | removable, and where the distractors live |
| **a fixation is a stop; a saccade is a jump** | you read at the stops |
| **a span of a few letters left and about fifteen right** | which rules out reading in blocks of five words |

## Unit 4 · Word class from position; affixes

| | |
|---|---|
| **after *a* or *the* → a noun; after *were* → an adjective or participle** | you can fill a gap without knowing the word |
| **-tion, -ment, -ness, -ity · -ise, -ify · -al, -ive, -ous · -ly** | the ending names the class |
| **about one unknown word in fifty** | the probable threshold for unassisted comprehension |
| **read the glossary; never stop for one word** | if no question needs it, it is not a question |

## Unit 5 · Word families; nominalization

| | |
|---|---|
| **analyze → analysis → analytical → analytically** | the stress moves with the suffix |
| ***the derivation of the figure*** → **somebody derived the figure** | unpack a noun back into a clause |
| **570 families, about a tenth of academic text** | and a hundredth of fiction |
| **the list excludes the commonest two thousand words** | so on its own it is one word in ten of a page you cannot read |

## Unit 6 · Reference, substitution, ellipsis

| | |
|---|---|
| ***this*** can point at a noun, a clause or a paragraph | not at the nearest noun by default |
| ***such*** points at a kind of thing; ***the latter*** is the second of two | both wrong half the time in students' answers |
| ***they did so*** · ***three were not*** | substitution and ellipsis: supply what is missing |
| **cohesion is not coherence** | joins are not sense, and Book 5 returns to this |

## Unit 7 · Negation and scope

| | |
|---|---|
| ***all* / *not all* / *some* / *none* / *few*** | each is contradicted by something different |
| **TRUE needs a sentence that says it** | in the same or other words |
| **FALSE needs a sentence that contradicts it** | point at it with a finger |
| **NOT GIVEN is the case with nothing to point at** | however wrong the statement feels |

## Unit 8 · Topic sentences; superordinates

| | |
|---|---|
| **delete · generalize · construct** | the three ways a paragraph becomes one line |
| **iron, copper, tin → metals** | headings are written with superordinate words |
| **a construction may use a word the text never used** | which is why a shared word is not a matching heading |
| **write your own line first** | eight comparisons instead of forty-eight |

## Unit 9 · Participle clauses

| | |
|---|---|
| **Left outside, the samples deteriorated.** | the participle belongs to the subject of the main clause |
| **Having failed twice, the method was abandoned.** | not to the nearest noun |
| **250 to 300 against often under 150 words a minute** | approximate, and not about you |
| **automatic word recognition is the bottleneck** | strategies do not remove it; volume of easy reading does, slowly |

## Unit 10 · Hedges and boosters

| | |
|---|---|
| ***may · might · appear to · suggest · tend to*** | the writer has not promised it |
| ***clearly · must · demonstrate · undoubtedly*** | the writer is standing fully behind it |
| ***unfortunately · surprisingly*** | attitude, not strength |
| **a statement that removes a hedge is not the writer's view** | underline the hedge before you answer |

## Unit 11 · Sentence length and subordination

| | |
|---|---|
| **break a long sentence and keep the relation** | *even so*, *therefore*, *as a result*, *nevertheless* |
| **join two sentences with a subordinator** | *although*, *because*, *while*, *though* |
| **a formula counts sentence length and syllables** | and nothing else |
| **chopping sentences raises the score and lowers the clarity** | a formula is a floor, not a judge |

## Unit 12 · Paraphrase; review

| | |
|---|---|
| **verb → noun, active → passive, positive → negative** | almost every question is a paraphrase |
| **reliability · construct validity · authenticity · interactiveness · impact · practicality** | the six qualities |
| **they trade off, and there is no weighting** | usefulness is argued for a purpose |
| **18, 19, 20, 3 — and four habits** | point · line · hedge · previous sentence |

# The British layer

This book is written in American English, and the series carries a switchable British layer: a fixed
list of words is respelled at build time. The list lives with the series rather than in this book.

**In the Reading paper, spelling is not yours to control.** The passages are printed as published,
which means a passage may use British spellings throughout and your answer sheet will be marked by
somebody who accepts both. Where a question asks you to copy a word from the passage, **copy the
passage's spelling**, not the one you prefer: a word copied correctly is right in either system, and
a word "corrected" is a word altered.

What the layer does not touch:

1. **A quoted document keeps its own spelling.** Several sources in this book are published in
   British English, and their titles and quoted sentences are printed as published.
2. **A journal name keeps its published form.** *Psychological Bulletin*, *TESOL Quarterly*,
   *Reading in a Foreign Language*, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, *Psychological Review* and
   *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* are citations.
3. **The date.** The month in letters in prose; the international format in data. In an answer, *3
   May* rather than *3/5*.
4. **Money has no symbol anywhere in this series.**
5. **A name is never respelled.** Alderson, Rayner, Hu, Nation, Coxhead, Halliday, Hasan, Wason,
   Kintsch, van Dijk, Grabe, Hyland, Flesch, Bachman and Palmer are spelled as they are published.

# Sources

Twelve source bodies carry the twelve reading texts, one per unit, and the Starting Point carries
none. Every one is a published document, paper or book, cited with its edition or its date, and every
reading text prints what the publisher or the author says the document does **not** cover. No source
appears in any other book of this series or in its five sister series, and no figure is used twice.
The series note is in `SOURCES.md`.

| Unit | Source | What the text prints |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The published **description of the Academic Reading test**, read 2 May | It describes a design and not your paper: not the topics, not how many questions of each type, not their order, and not which passage carries the detailed argument; and it is silent on how to divide the hour, because a method is somebody else's job |
| **2** | **J. Charles Alderson**, *Assessing Reading*, Cambridge University Press, **2000** | A review of a field rather than a study, written for test developers and researchers, reaching **few firm conclusions on purpose**; it reports that some items are answerable without the text, that background knowledge affects performance, that the question type changes the score, and that named reading skills are hard to separate in candidate data; it is of its period, and it does not hold that reading tests are invalid |
| **3** | **Keith Rayner**, *Eye movements in reading and information processing: 20 years of research*, **Psychological Bulletin, 1998** | A review of laboratory eye-tracking, mostly with **skilled first-language readers** reading sentences and short texts under controlled conditions; it is about the mechanics of the eye and not about comprehension strategies; it says nothing about second-language reading; and it contains **no training** |
| **4** | **M. Hsueh-Chao Hu and I. S. P. Nation**, *Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension*, **Reading in a Foreign Language, 2000** | **One study**, **one fiction text**, one group of learners, two comprehension measures; the unknown words were **invented**, which is a clean method with a known cost; the thresholds are **proportions of readers, not switches**, and the 98 per cent figure is offered as **probable**; and it says nothing about academic prose or about examinations |
| **5** | **Averil Coxhead**, *A New Academic Word List*, **TESOL Quarterly, 2000** | It **deliberately excludes the most frequent 2,000 words**, so on its own it gives one word in ten of a page the reader cannot read; it was built on a corpus of one period and selection; **coverage varies by discipline**; counting families assumes derivational competence; it is a list and not a syllabus; and later work has proposed alternatives |
| **6** | **M. A. K. Halliday and R. Hasan**, *Cohesion in English*, Longman, **1976** | A **description of a language** written for linguists, not a reading method; its central distinction is that **cohesion is not coherence**; the categories overlap and coding is interpretive; and it is of its period and has been extended since, including by one of its own authors |
| **7** | **P. C. Wason**, *Reasoning about a rule*, **Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1968** | An **abstract** laboratory task with a small number of participants; **performance changes dramatically with everyday content**, so it does not show that people cannot reason; the interpretation has been argued about for fifty years and is unsettled; and it has nothing to do with reading tests — **the connection drawn in the unit is the book's own analogy, not a research finding** |
| **8** | **W. Kintsch and T. A. van Dijk**, *Toward a model of text comprehension and production*, **Psychological Review, 1978** | A **formal model** with evidence from recall and summarization; **both authors revised it substantially**, in a book-length reworking in 1983 and in the first author's different architecture in 1988, so the 1978 paper is a stage rather than a position; the macro-rules are a modeling claim rather than a demonstrated procedure; and it offers no technique and says nothing about examinations |
| **9** | **William Grabe**, *Reading in a Second Language: Moving from Theory to Practice*, Cambridge University Press, **2009** | A **synthesis** for teachers in which the author carefully separates research findings from his own implications for teaching; the rate figures are **approximate** and vary with text, purpose and measurement, and a group figure says nothing about an individual; the evidence on extensive reading is **uneven in quality**, as the author notes; and it is about reading, not about examinations |
| **10** | **Ken Hyland**, *Metadiscourse: Exploring Interaction in Writing*, Continuum, **2005** | A framework built from corpora of academic writing; the categories are **functional**, so the same word does different work in different places and **any count of hedges is a count of somebody's readings**; conventions vary by discipline and by writing culture; and the book is about writing, not a method for answering questions |
| **11** | **Rudolf Flesch**, *A new readability yardstick*, **Journal of Applied Psychology, 1948** | It counts **two surface features** and is blind to word frequency, cohesion, argument and the reader's topic familiarity; **chopping sentences in half raises the score and lowers the clarity**; it was validated against passages and criteria of its period, for general prose, and was not built for academic text or second-language readers. **This series scores its own reading texts with such a formula, and the unit says so.** |
| **12** | **L. F. Bachman and A. S. Palmer**, *Language Testing in Practice*, Oxford University Press, **1996**, with a review of the eleven above | The six qualities **cannot be evaluated separately** and **trade off** against each other, and the authors give **no formula** for weighting them: usefulness must be argued for a particular test, used with particular people, for a particular purpose; the framework is for test developers rather than candidates; and it is of its period, before the field's move towards treating validity as an argument to be built and evaluated |

Twelve admissions, one per unit.

- **Unit 1.** Ninety per cent accuracy and twenty-six marks, because eleven questions were never
  answered. The hour pays for marks on a sheet and not for care.
- **Unit 2.** Two of four questions answered with the passage covered. A right answer does not prove
  that anybody understood anything.
- **Unit 3.** The clear part of a reader's vision is about fifteen letters wide, and speed-reading
  courses have been selling the opposite for fifty years.
- **Unit 4.** Six unknown words in a hundred is below the density at which most readers in one study
  could retell a text — and four minutes were spent on a word no question asked about.
- **Unit 5.** Two hundred and twenty academic word families bought three marks, and the sentences
  that still stopped the student contained no academic words at all.
- **Unit 6.** Nineteen marks across one class, traced to five words everybody learned in their first
  year. Seven of them to one *this*.
- **Unit 7.** Thirty-one *falses* written where there were nineteen, and in every wrong one there was
  no sentence to point at.
- **Unit 8.** Reading eight headings against six paragraphs is forty-eight comparisons. Writing one
  line first is eight.
- **Unit 9.** One hundred and forty words a minute means twenty minutes of a sixty-minute paper spent
  reading, and the eleven blanks were arithmetic rather than carelessness.
- **Unit 10.** Fourteen marks from five words known since school, four of them hedges.
- **Unit 11.** The same paragraph scored 18, 44 and 66 with an identical syllable count. The entire
  difference was punctuation.
- **Unit 12.** Accuracy fell from ninety per cent to eighty-four and the score rose from
  twenty-six to thirty-three. Something real was traded for something that counts.

# A note on everything else in this book

Every person, class, center, university and conversation in this book is invented. Meridian Language
Center does not exist, and neither do the fourteen people in the dialogues.

**Every passage, question, heading, option, statement and answer sheet in this book was written for
it.** No question, passage, heading or prompt from any past examination is reproduced anywhere.

**Every figure attributed to "our own class" is invented** — the timing figures in Unit 1, the
covered-passage count in Unit 2, the hesitation counts in Unit 3, the density results in Unit 4, the
shading counts in Unit 5, the reference-word tally in Unit 6, the verdict counts in Unit 7, the
heading-task times in Unit 8, the reading rates in Unit 9, the hedge tally in Unit 10, the three
readability scores in Unit 11 and the change survey in Unit 12. Each says on its own face that it is
an invented class's own count, with eleven students, and several of the units stop to say why eleven
students cannot establish anything.

The three readability scores in Unit 11 were produced by applying a published formula to three
versions of one invented paragraph. The formula is real; the paragraph is ours.

Nothing in this book is advice about your admission, registration or employment, and nothing in it is
a prediction. A practice score is what you did on one set of questions on one morning.

# Closing

You began this book reading three passages carefully and running out of time. You end it able to say
how fast you read, how many unknown words you can carry, what a *this* points at, what would make a
statement false, what a paragraph is about in five words, how far a writer is standing behind a
claim, and what a readability score does and does not measure — and able to say, of every one of
those, where the figure came from and what it does not cover.

Twelve sources, and one habit: before you answer, find the thing you could point at.

Five sentences to take with you, one from each part of the book.

From Unit 1: **the hour includes writing the answers.**

From Unit 4: **does this question need the word I do not know?**

From Unit 6: **what does this point at?**

From Unit 7: **what sentence would make this false?**

From Unit 10: **how sure is the writer, exactly?**
