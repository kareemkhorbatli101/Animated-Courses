Three passages, forty questions, sixty minutes, no extra time. The published description tells you
the design. The arithmetic that follows from it tells you that careful reading from the first word
is not a strategy but a decision to leave questions blank.

%%FIG qc01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the source and the date it was downloaded, a four-row table of what the description states, a short block of arithmetic, a boxed paragraph headed what a design document will not tell you, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: the shape of the Academic Reading paper, and what follows from it**
> **Source: the published description of the Academic Reading test, from the test owners' own pages.
> Downloaded 2 May. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper has **three passages** and **forty questions**, and lasts **sixty minutes**. Unlike the
> Listening paper, there is **no extra time at the end**: the hour includes finding every answer and
> writing it on the answer sheet.
>
> The passages are taken from **books, journals, magazines and newspapers**, and they are written for
> a general educated reader rather than for a language learner. The published description says that
> **at least one** of the three contains **detailed logical argument**. Texts may include diagrams,
> graphs or illustrations; where a text uses technical vocabulary, a short **glossary** may be
> provided.
>
> Now the arithmetic, which the description does not do for you. Sixty minutes across three passages
> is **twenty minutes each**, including everything. Forty questions in sixty minutes is **ninety
> seconds a question**, including the reading. A passage of around nine hundred words, read once at a
> moderate speed, takes four or five minutes — so after one reading of one passage you have about
> fifteen minutes left to answer about thirteen questions and write them down.
>
> That arithmetic has one consequence and everybody resists it. **You cannot read all three passages
> carefully and answer forty questions in an hour.** Nobody can. The students who finish are not
> reading faster; they are reading differently, and they are reading some parts of the text and not
> others.
>
> **WHAT A DESIGN DOCUMENT WILL NOT TELL YOU**
> It describes a **design**, not your paper. It does not say what the topics will be, how many
> questions of each type you will meet, in what order they will come, or which of the three passages
> will carry the detailed argument.
>
> It is also silent on everything this book is about. It does not say how to divide the hour, which
> parts of a text to read closely, or what to do when the hour is nearly over. Those are not
> omissions: a test publisher describes the test, and a method is somebody else's job.
>
> And it says nothing about you. The number of questions you can answer in an hour is a fact about
> your reading, not about the paper.
>
> *Our summary of a published description. Read the owners' own pages, and write down the date.*

### 1 · Long noun phrases

Academic English puts a great deal inside a noun phrase. The **head noun** is the one the rest hangs
on, and it is usually the word you search for.

| | |
|---|---|
| the **rapid decline** of the coastal fishing industry | head: *decline* |
| a second **set** of measurements taken in winter | head: *set* |
| the **effects** on soil quality of repeated cultivation | head: *effects* |
| an unexpected **reduction** in the number of applicants | head: *reduction* |
| three previously unpublished **letters** from the period | head: *letters* |

**1A  Underline the head noun and say the phrase aloud.**

1 the gradual replacement of wooden sleepers by concrete ones

3 the first reliable estimate of the population

5 the almost complete absence of written records

**1C  Say four long noun phrases from your own subject to a partner. They name the head noun.**

### 2 · The words that carry a question

A question stem has a few words that decide where the answer is, and several that do not.

| | |
|---|---|
| **According to the passage, what caused the *second* decline?** | *second*, *decline*, *caused* |
| **In which year was the *first* survey completed?** | *first*, *survey*, *year* |
| **The writer says the method was *abandoned* because of…** | *abandoned*, *because* |
| **Which *two* of the following are mentioned?** | *two*, and the five options |
| **What is the writer's main *purpose* in paragraph C?** | *purpose*, *paragraph C* |

**2A  Underline the two or three words that carry each question. Then read it aloud.**

1 According to the passage, which material replaced iron in the later designs?
2 In which decade did the second group of settlers arrive?
3 The writer suggests that the earlier estimates were unreliable because of what?
4 Which two of the following does the writer mention as advantages?
5 What was the main difficulty in the third stage of the project?

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL3-01-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students search for the whole stem, which does not appear in the
passage, and conclude the answer is not there. **Read your partner four stems; they say only the

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In many education systems reading a text carefully from the first word to the last is not a
> technique but a sign of respect — for the author, for the subject and for the teacher. Students
> trained that way arrive at this paper with a habit that is admirable, slow and expensive, and being
> told to skip feels like being told to be careless. It is not the same thing: the paper is a search
> task with sixty minutes attached. **Ask your group: at school, were you taught to read a text
> completely? What would your teacher have said about skipping?**
