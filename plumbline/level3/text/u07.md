# Unit 7 · Looking for the Wrong Card
%%SUB A reasoning task from 1968, what people do instead of testing a rule, and the hardest three words in the paper%%
%%GOALS
Say what people do when asked to test a rule
Say what each of the three verdicts requires of the passage
Decide what would make a statement false
Use negation and scope accurately
Say why a laboratory task whose results change with content is still useful
%%

*True, false, not given* is not a reading difficulty. It is a reasoning difficulty, and in 1968
somebody showed what people do when they are asked to test a rule: they look for the case that would
agree with them, and not for the one that could prove them wrong.

%%FIG qc07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a drawing of the four cards with the rule above them, a two-row table of what people chose and what was needed, a boxed paragraph headed an abstract task whose results change with content, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: why *not given* is a logic question**
> **Source: P. C. Wason, *Reasoning about a rule*, Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology,
> **1968**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Four cards lie face up. You can see **A**, **D**, **4** and **7**. Each card has a letter on one
> side and a number on the other. You are given a rule:
>
> **If a card has a vowel on one side, it has an even number on the other.**
>
> **Which cards must you turn over** to find out whether the rule is true?
>
> Most people choose the **A**, and many add the **4**. Both choices feel right and only one of them
> is.
>
> Turning the **A** is necessary: if there is an odd number on the back, the rule is broken. Turning
> the **4** is useless: the rule says what follows from a vowel, and says **nothing** about what is on
> the back of an even number, so whatever you find there, the rule survives. The card that matters and
> that almost nobody turns is the **7**: if there is a vowel on its back, the rule is broken.
>
> The correct answer is **A and 7** — the case that could **confirm** the rule and the case that could
> **break** it. The common answer is A and 4: the case that could confirm it, twice.
>
> This is the finding. Asked to test a rule, people look for what would **agree** with it, and are
> very slow to look for what would **break** it. That tendency is why *true, false, not given* costs
> so many marks, and why the repair is a single question you ask about every statement: **what
> sentence in this passage would make this statement false?** If you can find one, the answer is
> *false*. If you can find a sentence that says it, the answer is *true*. If the passage contains
> neither, the answer is *not given* — and *not given* is the empty card, the case where there is
> nothing to turn.
>
> **AN ABSTRACT TASK WHOSE RESULTS CHANGE WITH CONTENT**
> The task is **abstract** — letters and numbers, stripped of meaning — and it was run in a
> laboratory with a small number of participants.
>
> The most important thing to say about it is this: **performance changes dramatically when the
> content changes.** Versions of the task built from everyday rules — about drinking ages, about
> permissions, about social agreements — produce far higher rates of correct choices. That result is
> one of the best established in the literature, and it means the 1968 task does **not** show that
> people cannot reason. It shows that they reason badly about an abstract conditional presented cold.
>
> The **interpretation is still argued about**. Competing accounts have been offered for fifty years,
> and no single explanation is settled.
>
> And the task has **nothing to do with reading tests**. Nobody in it was reading a passage. The
> connection drawn in this unit is ours, and it is a teaching analogy rather than a research finding:
> what the task supplies is one good question to ask, and the question is useful whatever the
> explanation of the original result turns out to be.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. It is short, and the four cards are worth drawing for yourself.*

### 2 · Statements that look like contradictions and are not

|---|---|---|

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Before you write *false*, point at the sentence.** Physically — put your finger on it. If there is

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Education systems differ in how much they reward disagreeing with a text. Where a student is taught
> to judge a claim against what they know, the *not given* verdict feels like a refusal to think;
> where a student is taught to stay inside the text, it feels obvious. This paper asks for the second
> habit in a very narrow way — only about what this passage settles — and says nothing about whether
> the statement is true in the world. **Ask your group: at school, were you rewarded for arguing with a
> text or for reporting it accurately?**
