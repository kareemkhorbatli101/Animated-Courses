# Unit 4 · One Unknown Word in Fifty
%%SUB A study from 2000 that replaced words with nonsense, and the threshold that came out of it%%
%%GOALS

Somebody took a text and replaced one word in twenty with nonsense, then one in ten, then one in
five, and measured what readers could still do. The result is the most useful number in this book and
the one most often repeated without its conditions.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qc04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a four-row table of densities against comprehension, a short description of the nonsense-word method, a boxed paragraph headed one text, one group, and a probable threshold, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Reading class**
> **Subject: how many unknown words a reader can carry**
> **Source: M. Hsueh-Chao Hu and I. S. P. Nation, *Unknown vocabulary density and reading
> comprehension*, Reading in a Foreign Language, **2000**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Book 1 of this series gave you the idea of **coverage**: the share of the running words on a page
> that you already know. This study asks the obvious next question. **What happens to comprehension
> as coverage falls?**
>
> The method is simple and clever. The researchers took one text and **replaced** some of its words
> with invented ones, so that they could set the density of unknown words exactly. They made versions
> at 100 percent coverage, 95 percent (one unknown word in twenty), 90 percent (one in ten) and 80
> percent (one in five), and measured comprehension with questions and with a written retelling.
>
> The pattern is steep. At **full coverage** nearly all the readers reached adequate comprehension. At
> **95 percent** only a **minority** did. At **90 percent** very few did, and at **80 percent**
> almost nobody.
>
> From that shape the authors suggest that the coverage needed for **unassisted** comprehension —
> reading without a dictionary and without help — is probably around **98 percent**, which is about
> **one unknown word in fifty**.
>
> Put that next to the arithmetic of Unit 1. If you meet one unknown word in twenty on a Reading
> passage, you are at the density where most readers in this study could not retell the text. You will
> still answer some questions, because some questions do not need the whole text. You will not be
> reading in the way you think you are reading.
>
> **ONE TEXT, ONE GROUP, AND A PROBABLE THRESHOLD**
> This is **one study**. It used **one text**, which was **fiction**, with **one group of learners**
> and **one pair of comprehension measures**. Academic prose is not fiction, and the figures might not
> transfer.
>
> The unknown words were **invented**. A nonsense word announces itself; a real word you do not know
> may look familiar, may resemble a word you do know, and may mislead you rather than simply stopping
> you. Replacing words is a clean method with a known cost.
>
> The thresholds are **proportions of readers, not switches**. At 95 percent a minority understood
> enough — so 95 percent is not a wall, it is a place where most people fail and some do not. The 98
> percent figure is offered as **probable**, and the authors say so.
>
> And it is a study of **comprehension**, not of examinations. It says nothing about answering
> question types under time pressure, and nothing about anybody's band.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. It is open access, short and worth reading in full.*

### 1 · Word class from position

English tells you the class of a word by where it sits, even when you have never seen it.

| | |
|---|---|
| after *a*, *the*, *this*, a number → a **noun** | *a second **plandic*** |
| after *was*, *were*, *is* → an **adjective** or a **past participle** | *the results were **mordant*** |
| after *to* → a **verb** | *they began to **grell*** |
| before a noun → an **adjective** | *a **mordant** reply* |
| after a verb, ending *-ly* → an **adverb** | *they worked **flantly*** |

**1A  Say the class of the invented word and what told you.**

2 Three **crelts** were found at the lower site.
3 The surface had been **plandered** twice.
4 She described the method as **wholly grellish**.
5 They proceeded **mordantly** through the list.

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Read the glossary, and never stop for one word.** If a passage has a glossary at the foot, it is
> there because the examiner knew the term was specialized, and it costs ten seconds. And when an
> unknown word appears without one, the question is not *what does it mean* but *does this question
> need it*. Most of the time it does not, and the word class is enough to fill a gap.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Being told to continue past a word you do not understand is, in some educational traditions, close
> to being told to be dishonest: the point of reading is to understand, and leaving a gap is leaving
> a job half done. The study in this unit does not disagree about reading. It says something narrower:
> that below a certain coverage you will not understand the text whatever you do, and above it you
> will manage without every word. **Ask your group: at school, what happened if you admitted you did
> not know a word? Did anybody teach you to carry on?**
