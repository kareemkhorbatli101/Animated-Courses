### 2 · Read

%%FIG qa05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a two-column table of what a comprehension lesson does and what a diagnostic lesson does, a five-row list of reasons an answer is missed, a boxed paragraph headed what this book does not promise, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to teachers and students**
> **Subject: why our listening lessons stop and go back**
> **Source: John Field, *Listening in the Language Classroom*, Cambridge University Press, 2008.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Most listening lessons in the world follow the same shape. The teacher plays a recording, asks
> questions, takes the answers, says which are right and plays it again. Field calls this the
> **comprehension approach**, and his argument is short: it **tests** listening, it does not **teach**
> it. The student who got four out of ten learns that they got four out of ten.
>
> His alternative is to treat a wrong answer as something to be **diagnosed**. Listening, he argues,
> is at least two operations. **Decoding** is turning a stream of sound into words. **Meaning-building**
> is turning those words into sense, using what you already know. A student can fail at either, and
> the classroom cure is different for each one. A student who did not know the word needs vocabulary.
> A student who knew the word but did not hear it inside the stream needs work on the stream.
>
> So the first question after a wrong answer is not *what was the answer*. It is **why did you not get
> it**. Field's book is largely a set of ways of asking that question in a classroom: going back to
> the exact second, playing three words, asking what the student thought they heard.
>
> **WHAT THIS BOOK DOES NOT PROMISE**
> It is a book of argument and classroom method, drawing on research into how people process speech.
> It is **not an experiment** and it reports no trial showing that one approach produces higher test
> scores than another. Field does not claim a quick fix and says so repeatedly: decoding improves
> slowly. The book is about teaching listening, not about test technique, and it offers no advice
> about any examination. And it cannot tell you which of the five reasons applies to you on a given
> morning. Only you were there.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. Read Chapter 5 if your center has a copy.*

> **Move on.** The single most expensive habit in the Listening paper is staying with a question after

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In many classrooms around the world a listening lesson means: play, ask, correct, play again. It is
> the cheapest lesson to run, it looks like work, and it fills an hour. Field's objection in 2008 was
> that nobody in that room finds out why anything was missed, and the student with the fastest pen
> looks like the best listener. A diagnostic lesson is slower, noisier and less tidy, and it needs a
> teacher who will stop the recording after two seconds. **Ask your group: what shape did your
> listening lessons at school have? Did anybody ever ask you why you missed an answer?**
