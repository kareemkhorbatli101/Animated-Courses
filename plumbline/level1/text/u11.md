### 2 · Six more words, and two kinds of awareness

| | What it is |
|---|---|

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qa11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a two-row table separating noticing from understanding, a short extract from a dated diary showing a form met in input and then appearing in output, a boxed paragraph headed a hypothesis, and contested, a list of four limits, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to teachers and students**
> **Subject: why we ask you where you have heard it**
> **Source: R. Schmidt, *The Role of Consciousness in Second Language Learning*, Applied Linguistics,
> 1990, with the diary study he and a colleague published in 1986. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Not everything you hear becomes part of your English. The English around you is **input**. The part
> your mind actually takes in is **intake**. The 1990 paper argues that the step from one to the other
> requires **noticing**: becoming consciously aware of the form itself, at the moment you meet it.
>
> The evidence the author found most convincing was his own. He had kept a **diary**, dated, while
> learning Portuguese in Brazil, writing down the forms he noticed people using. Looking back, the
> forms that appeared in his own speech were, again and again, the ones he had **noticed first in
> somebody else's**. Things he had been taught and had not noticed in use did not appear.
>
> He separates two kinds of awareness. **Noticing** is awareness of the surface form: you register
> that the speaker said one thing and not another. **Understanding** is awareness of the rule
> underneath. The claim is about noticing. You can notice without understanding, and — this is the
> painful part — you can understand a rule perfectly, write it in a notebook six times, and never once
> notice it in anything you hear.
>
> This is why a correction can change nothing. A correction gives you the rule. If you have never
> registered the form in real use, there is nothing for the rule to attach to, and next week you will
> produce what you have always produced.
>
> **A HYPOTHESIS, AND CONTESTED**
> This is a **hypothesis** and an argument, not an experiment with a result. It has been argued about
> ever since, and several researchers hold that some learning happens without any awareness at all.
> The paper is a position in a live disagreement, and it is honest about being one.
>
> **Noticing is very hard to measure.** You cannot see it. You can ask somebody — which is
> self-report — or you can wait and watch their output, which is slow and indirect. The diary evidence
> is **one learner, himself, reporting on himself**, which is the weakest kind of evidence and the
> author says so.
>
> The paper makes **no claim about any examination**, offers no classroom method, and gives no
> estimate of how many times you must notice something. The author's own later writing softened the
> strongest version of the claim.
>
> Four limits: a hypothesis · contested · one self-reporting learner · no method.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. Read it if your center has access; the diary is the memorable
> part.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Nothing you notice on the day will help you.** This unit explains something slow: why a term of
> corrections can leave your spoken English exactly as it was. The practical form of it is a habit,
> not a technique — when somebody corrects you, your next question is *where would I hear that*, and
> then you go and hear it. Students who do that have shorter error records by the end of a course.
> That sentence is about this center, not about research.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In many classrooms correction is the visible proof that teaching is happening: red ink, a rule
> given, a note taken. In others a teacher who corrects openly is thought to be rude, and errors are
> left alone. The 1990 argument cuts across both habits, because it says the correction is not where
> the work happens — the work happens the next time you meet the form in real use and register it.
> Neither the red pen nor the polite silence does that for you. **Ask your group: were you corrected
> openly at school? Did it change anything? Who in your life has corrected you most usefully, and
> how did they do it?**
