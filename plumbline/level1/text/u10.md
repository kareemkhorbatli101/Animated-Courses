### 2 · Read

%%FIG qa10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher, the city and the year with the translation date beneath, a four-row table of intervals against savings marked as a shape not a figure, a small drawing of a falling curve, a boxed paragraph headed one man, nonsense, and a measure of time, a list of four limits, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to students**
> **Subject: the oldest experiment in this book**
> **Source: Hermann Ebbinghaus, *Über das Gedächtnis: Untersuchungen zur experimentellen
> Psychologie*, Duncker und Humblot, Leipzig, 1885; published in English as *Memory: A Contribution
> to Experimental Psychology* in 1913. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> In the early 1880s a German psychologist decided to measure forgetting instead of describing it. He
> made up lists of **meaningless three-letter syllables** and learned them to a fixed standard,
> keeping his own timing with a metronome. Then he waited — twenty minutes, an hour, nine hours, a
> day, two days, six days, a month — and learned the same list again.
>
> What he measured was not how much he could remember. It was how much **time he saved** the second
> time. He called it **savings**. If relearning took far less work than learning, much of the list was
> still there; if it took nearly as long, almost nothing was.
>
> The result is the shape everybody has seen. The loss is **fast at first and then slow**. Most of it
> happens in the first hours and the first day; after that the line bends and then runs almost flat
> for weeks. He also found that the same number of repetitions **spread out over days** was more
> economical than the same number crowded into one sitting.
>
> **ONE MAN, NONSENSE, AND A MEASURE OF TIME**
> There was **one subject**: himself. Nobody else was tested, so nothing in the book tells you about
> the variation between people — and you are a person, not an average of one.
>
> The material was **meaningless on purpose**. He used nonsense syllables precisely so that meaning
> and prior knowledge could not help. That is excellent experimental design and it is also the biggest
> limit on the result: almost everything you will ever learn **has** meaning, and meaningful material
> behaves better than his syllables did.
>
> **Savings** is a measure of relearning **time**, not of recall. A curve of savings is not a curve of
> what you could say out loud. He made no claim about teaching, about schools, or about any
> examination, and he offered no review schedule. The curves printed in study books today are usually
> redrawn, with numbers he never published in that form. And the modern case for spacing out your
> study rests on a large body of later research, not on this book.
>
> Four limits: one subject · no meaning · time, not recall · no advice.
>
> *Our summary of a published book from 1885. The English translation is free online and is short.*

### 1 · *Will* with time clauses

After **when**, **after**, **before**, **until** and **as soon as**, English uses the **present**, not
*will*.

| | |
|---|---|
| **When you come back** to it on Thursday, you **will** remember less. | not *when you will come back* |
| **As soon as you test** yourself, you **will** find the gaps. | |
| You **will** forget most of it **before** you **look** at it again. | |
| **Until you review** it, the line **will** keep falling. | |
| I **will not** remember them **after** a week **passes**. | |

| | |
|---|---|

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **You cannot review on the morning.** Everything in this unit happens in the weeks before, and the
> curve is why the last-week panic produces so little: whatever you learn on the Thursday before a
> Saturday test is sitting on the steep part of the line, and there is no time left to catch it. The
> students who walk in with their vocabulary intact are the ones who were reviewing in November.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> The night before an examination is a cultural institution in many countries: the long session, the
> coffee, the shared room, the pride in it. The 1885 result does not say that night is worthless — it
> says that what you put in then sits on the steep part of the curve, and that the same hours spread
> over three weeks would hold far more. What the night before genuinely does is reduce fear, which is
> not nothing. **Ask your group: in your country, is studying the night before normal, admired or
> hidden? Did anybody ever teach you to review on a schedule instead?**
