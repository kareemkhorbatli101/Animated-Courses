### 2 · Read

%%FIG qa07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a three-row table of coverage against word families, a two-row table comparing written and spoken text, a boxed paragraph headed what coverage is not, a short list of four things the study does not say, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to students**
> **Subject: how many words you need, and where the figure comes from**
> **Source: I. S. P. Nation, *How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?*, Canadian
> Modern Language Review, 2006. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The study counts. It takes frequency lists of **word families** built from a very large collection
> of real English, and asks a single question: if you knew the most frequent one thousand families,
> then two thousand, then three, what share of the words on an ordinary page would you already know?
> That share is called **coverage**.
>
> The answers are not what most students expect. The first thousand families cover about four fifths
> of the running words in ordinary written English. The first two thousand cover a little under nine
> tenths. After that the climb gets slow: every extra thousand families buys less coverage than the
> thousand before it.
>
> The paper reports that about **eight to nine thousand** word families are needed to reach **98 per
> cent** coverage of written text, and about **six to seven thousand** for spoken text. At 98 per cent
> you meet one unknown word in every fifty. The paper treats 98 per cent as the level at which a
> reader can manage **unassisted** — without a dictionary and without help. Around 95 per cent
> coverage, which needs four to five thousand families, a reader can usually follow a text with some
> help.
>
> **WHAT COVERAGE IS NOT**
> Coverage is a count of **running words**, not a measure of understanding. Knowing 98 words in a
> hundred does not mean you understand 98 per cent of the meaning: the two you do not know may be the
> two the question is about. The 98 per cent line itself came from other studies of learners, not from
> this counting exercise, and the paper presents it as a working figure, not a law.
>
> A word **family** counts a base word and the forms built from it as one item, which assumes you can
> handle those forms when you meet them — and that assumption is doing some of the work in the totals.
> Proper nouns and a few marginal words are set aside. And the study is a count of texts, not a study
> of students: it does not say **which** words you need, how long any of this takes, or what any
> individual learner can do.
>
> Four things the study does not say: which words · how long · your reading · your band.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. Read it if your center has access; the counting is the
> interesting part.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Nobody counts your vocabulary in the test.** There is no vocabulary paper and no word list. What
> there is, in Reading and Listening, is a question that turns on one word you may not know — and the
> only defense is having met enough words often enough that the unknown one is rare. This unit will
> not raise your band this month. It tells you what the next two years of reading are for.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In many school systems vocabulary is a list to be learned and tested on Friday, and the number of
> words in the list is the measure of the week. The counting study behind this unit suggests
> something less comfortable: that the words are not equal, that the first two thousand do most of
> the work, and that the last few thousand are learned mostly by meeting them, not by listing them.
> **Ask your group: how were you taught vocabulary at school? Were the words you learned the frequent
> ones, or the ones in that week's text?**
