# Unit 3 · A Band Somewhere Else
%%SUB Six levels, a framework written for a continent, and the sentence in which it says it is not a test%%

A band is a number from one test. A level is a description used by universities, employers and
governments across many countries. They are not the same thing, and the document that defines the
levels says so itself.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qa03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, two source lines giving a framework and its companion volume with their years, a six-row table of levels and groupings, a three-row table of band ranges against levels marked indicative, a boxed paragraph headed what the framework says about itself, a short list of three things it does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to new students**
> **Subject: the six levels, and how bands are mapped onto them**
> **Sources: the Council of Europe, *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages:
> Learning, teaching, assessment*, 2001; and the *CEFR Companion Volume*, Council of Europe, 2020.
> The mapping of bands to levels is published separately by the test owners. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> The framework describes six levels of language ability: **A1 and A2** (basic user), **B1 and B2**
> (independent user), **C1 and C2** (proficient user). Each level is described by **descriptors** —
> short statements that begin *Can understand…*, *Can produce…*, *Can take part in…*. The
> descriptors describe what a learner can do. They do not describe what a learner gets wrong.
>
> The 2020 Companion Volume kept the six levels and added more. It extended the descriptors for
> **mediation** — helping other people understand each other — and for online interaction, and it
> moved away from describing the ideal learner as somebody who sounds like a native speaker.
>
> The test owners publish an **indicative** mapping between bands and levels: roughly, bands 4.0 to
> 5.0 sit at **B1**, bands 5.5 to 6.5 at **B2**, bands 7.0 to 8.0 at **C1**, and bands 8.5 and 9.0 at
> **C2**. A band range covers a level, and a level covers a band range. The two scales do not line up
> point for point, because they were not built for each other.
>
> **WHAT THE FRAMEWORK SAYS ABOUT ITSELF**
> The framework states plainly that it is a **reference** document and that it is **not
> prescriptive**: it is there to be consulted, not obeyed, and the Council of Europe has repeatedly
> said it is not a tool for sorting people. It is not a test and contains no test. Its descriptors
> were calibrated using the judgments of teachers about learners, not against any single examination.
> It does not say how long a level takes, it does not say what a level is worth in a seminar or a
> hospital, and it does not say that two learners at B2 are interchangeable.
>
> Three things the framework does not do: set a pass mark · rank languages · decide who gets in.
>
> *Our summary of two published documents. Read them yourself: both are free.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In some countries one national examination decides school, university and job, and a single number
> follows a person for life. In others a level is a description that an employer reads beside a
> conversation and a reference. The six levels in this unit were written for a continent of many
> languages, for the purpose of comparing courses — not for deciding who gets in. The Council of
> Europe has said so more than once, and admissions pages go on using the levels as a gate anyway.
> **Ask your group: in your country, does one number decide things? Is it fair that a description
> written for comparing courses is used to decide who gets a place?**
