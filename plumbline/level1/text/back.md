# Grammar reference

Every structure in this book, in unit order. The second column is the mistake it prevents, which is
the column worth reading twice.

## Starting Point · Four questions about a score

| | |
|---|---|
| **The test gives you five numbers** | four papers and an overall band, and the fifth is made from the other four |
| **The four questions** | what is this a score of · who decided the number I need · would I get it again · what does it not tell anybody |
| **Two ways of answering question one** | *my English is 6.5* · or *in February I answered 23 of 40 on that paper* |
| **A band with no paper and no date is not a fact** | it is a number about a morning, said as if it were a number about a person |

## Unit 1 · Present simple for facts; *there is* and *there are*

| | |
|---|---|
| **the Listening paper **has** four parts** | present simple for what is true every time the test is run, not *is having* |
| ***There are** three passages in Academic Reading* | not *it has three passages*, which is English but not description |
| ***There is no** break between the papers* | *there is no* and *there are no* for what a paper does not have |
| **the format gives the shape, not the contents** | it does not say your question types, your topics or your raw-score line |

## Unit 2 · Numbers, *out of*, half bands, comparatives of score

| | |
|---|---|
| **I got 23 **out of** 40** | not *23 of 40* |
| **rounded **up to** 6.5** | and an average ending .25 goes up a half band, .75 goes up a whole band |
| **my Reading is **higher** than my Writing** | not *more high*; English builds the comparative into the word |
| **half a band, a whole band, up **by** half a band** | *by* for the size of a change |

## Unit 3 · *Can* for ability; *enough*

| | |
|---|---|
| **I **can** follow a lecture; by June I **will be able to** read a journal** | not *will can* |
| **my Writing is not **good enough** yet** | *enough* after an adjective |
| **I do not have **enough words** for Task 2** | *enough* before a noun |
| **a level is a description; a band is a number** | the mapping between them is published as a guide, and a guide is not an argument |

## Unit 4 · Past simple for reported figures; *most* and *most of*

| | |
|---|---|
| **the mean **was** 5.8 that year** | published figures are about a year that has finished |
| ***Most** test takers sat the Academic test* | *most* + plural noun, with no *the* |
| ***Most of the** students in this class need 6.5* | *most of* + *the* + noun |
| **an average is a fact about a crowd** | and everybody in that crowd chose to be there |

## Unit 5 · *While* and *when* with the past continuous

| | |
|---|---|
| ***While I was writing** answer 7, they **said** answer 8* | the long action in the continuous, the short one in the simple |
| ***What were you doing** when you missed it?* | the question that begins a diagnosis |
| ***listen to**, but **hear*** | *listen* takes *to*; *hear* does not |
| **three blanks in a row is one problem and two casualties** | the mark you lose is the one after the one you argued with |

## Unit 6 · *Must*, *must not*, *may*, *do not have to*; *allowed*

| | |
|---|---|
| **you **must** bring the document you booked with** | necessary |
| **you **must not** bring a watch** | forbidden — and not *must not to* |
| **you **do not have to** bring a pen** | not necessary, which is not the same as forbidden |
| **telephones **are not allowed*** | with a *d*; and *a result **may be withheld*** |

## Unit 7 · *How many* and *how much*; percentages

| | |
|---|---|
| ***How many** words? **How much** vocabulary?* | *word* is countable, *vocabulary* is not |
| ***a few** words, **a little** help* | the countable one and the uncountable one |
| **98 percent · one word in fifty · two in every hundred** | the same figure in three shapes, and no plural *per cents* |
| **coverage is a count of running words** | it is not a measure of how much of the meaning you got |

## Unit 8 · Word families and suffixes; the form the slot needs

| | |
|---|---|
| **select → selection → selective → selectively** | *-ion*, *-ment*, *-ness*, *-ive*, *-ly* |
| **after *the*, a **noun**; after *is*, an **adjective*** | read the three words in front of the gap before you write in it |
| **one sense of a common word does most of its work** | the sense you learned first is often not the sense you will meet |
| **a family counts as one word on a frequency list** | which quietly assumes you can handle all its forms |

## Unit 9 · Comparatives of usefulness; *-ing* as subject

| | |
|---|---|
| **testing is **more useful than** rereading** | and spacing is *better than* massing |
| **rereading is **not as useful as** testing** | *not as … as*, softer and more exact than *less than* |
| ***Testing** yourself works* | an *-ing* form can be the subject, and takes a singular verb |
| **they learned more **by testing** themselves** | after a preposition, always *-ing* |

## Unit 10 · *Will* with time clauses; *the more … the more*

| | |
|---|---|
| ***When you come** back on Thursday, you **will** remember less* | present after *when*, *after*, *before*, *until*, *as soon as* |
| ***The longer** you leave it, **the more** you forget* | both halves keep *the*, and both keep the comparative |
| **savings measured **time**, not recall** | how much less work the second learning took |
| **forgetting is fast and then slow** | so a review comes before you have lost it, not after |

## Unit 11 · Present perfect; *still*, *already*, *yet*

| | |
|---|---|
| **I **have been corrected** three times** | it happened, and it matters now |
| **I **still** say it wrongly** | *still* before the verb; **yet** at the end; you do not need both |
| **I have not noticed it **yet*** | and *I have **still** not noticed it* is stronger |
| **input is what you hear; intake is what you take in** | and a correction with nothing to attach to changes nothing |

## Unit 12 · Review

| | |
|---|---|
| **eleven documents, eleven honest sentences** | the facts move; the sentences do not |
| **five numbers by heart** | 40 and 40, 150 and 250, 11 to 14, 8,000 to 9,000 |
| **the five mistakes this book kept correcting** | *it has* · *more high* · *must not* for *need not* · *test yourself is* · *when I will* |
| **one honest sentence under your own plan** | a plan written in February by somebody who has not sat the test will be wrong by March |

# The British layer

This book is written in American English, and the series carries a switchable British layer: a fixed
list of words is respelled at build time. The list lives with the series rather than in this book.

**This matters less to you than it does to the book.** The published guidance for this examination
accepts **both** British and American spellings in your answers, as long as the spelling you write
is correct in one of them. What it does not accept is a word spelled in neither. So the question in
the test is never *which country's spelling* — it is *is this word spelled correctly at all*. Pick
one and be consistent, because consistency is cheaper to check than a mixture.

What the layer does not touch:

1. **The date.** Write the month in letters where the sentence is prose, and the international format
   where it is data.
2. **A quoted document keeps its own spelling.** Several documents in this book are published in
   British English, and their titles and quoted sentences are printed as published. *Common European
   Framework of Reference for Languages* and *Companion Volume* are titles, not words to be
   respelled.
3. **A journal name and a publisher keep their published form.** *Applied Linguistics*, *Psychological
   Science in the Public Interest*, *Canadian Modern Language Review*, *Über das Gedächtnis* are
   citations.
4. **Money has no symbol anywhere in this series.** Test fees differ by country and so does the
   currency. A number without a symbol is readable everywhere; a number with the wrong symbol is
   readable nowhere.
5. **A name is never respelled.** West, Nation, Coxhead, Dunlosky, Ebbinghaus, Schmidt and Field are
   spelled as they are published.

One word to note. **Programme** keeps that spelling where it appears inside a quoted title, because
changing a word inside a citation renames the document. Everywhere else in this book it is *program*
or, better, a plainer word.

# Sources

Eleven source bodies carry eleven of the twelve reading texts, one per unit; the Starting Point
carries none and Unit 12 is a review of the eleven. Every one is a published document, paper or word
list, cited with its edition or its date, and every reading text prints what the publisher or the
author says the document does **not** cover. No source appears in any other book of this series or in
its five sister series, and no figure is used twice. The series note is in `SOURCES.md`.

| Unit | Source | What the text prints |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The published test format for the Academic test (British Council, IDP: IELTS Australia and Cambridge University Press & Assessment) | It gives the shape of the papers and not their contents: not your question types, not your topics, not your raw-score line, and nothing about you |
| **2** | The published band scale and scores guidance, including the rounding rule and the indicative raw-score guidance | The raw-score guide is described as indicative and is not the table used on your version; the scale does not say that two people with one band speak the same English |
| **3** | Council of Europe, *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages: Learning, teaching, assessment*, 2001, and the *CEFR Companion Volume*, 2020 | It states that it is a reference and is **not prescriptive**; it is not a test and contains none; its descriptors were calibrated on teachers' judgments of learners, not against any one examination |
| **4** | The test taker performance tables published annually by the test owners | The population is self-selected, so a subgroup mean says as much about who applies; the tables do not say why, do not give any individual's score, cannot predict one, and are not safely compared between years |
| **5** | John Field, *Listening in the Language Classroom*, Cambridge University Press, 2008 | A book of argument and classroom method, not an experiment; it reports no trial on test scores, promises no quick fix, and offers no advice about any examination |
| **6** | The notice to candidates and information for candidates published by the test owners | It lists the rules and does not explain them, does not say how a decision about misconduct is made or by whom, and nowhere defines what makes an answer a *memorized* one |
| **7** | I. S. P. Nation, *How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?*, *Canadian Modern Language Review*, 2006 | It counts texts, not students; coverage is running words and not understanding; the 98 percent line comes from other studies and is a working figure; proper nouns were set aside; it does not say which words you need or how long it takes |
| **8** | Michael West, *A General Service List of English Words*, Longman, Green and Co., 1953 | Selected partly on editorial judgment and not frequency alone; the counting is pre-war and mostly written; there is no modern technology in it; it was made for writers of simplified readers, not as a syllabus, and newer lists supersede it for learning |
| **9** | J. Dunlosky, K. A. Rawson, E. J. Marsh, M. J. Nathan and D. T. Willingham, *Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques*, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 2013 | The ratings are judgments of an evidence base, not the authors' own experiment; *low* means the evidence is thin or narrow, not that a technique never works; most studies were on first-language learners of academic content, and the review makes no claim about language tests |
| **10** | Hermann Ebbinghaus, *Über das Gedächtnis: Untersuchungen zur experimentellen Psychologie*, Duncker und Humblot, Leipzig, 1885 (English translation 1913) | One subject, himself; nonsense syllables chosen so that meaning could not help; *savings* measured relearning time and not recall; no teaching advice and no schedule; the curves printed in study books today are redrawn with figures he never published in that form |
| **11** | R. Schmidt, *The Role of Consciousness in Second Language Learning*, *Applied Linguistics*, 1990, with the 1986 diary study | A hypothesis and a position in a live disagreement, not a finding; noticing cannot be measured directly; the diary evidence is one learner reporting on himself; no claim about any examination, no method, and no estimate of how many times a form must be noticed |
| **12** | Review — the eleven above, on one page | No document in this book can tell you what you will get, and none of them was written about you |

Eleven admissions, one per unit, worth reading as a list of their own.

- **Unit 1.** The published format is a description of a shape. Every student who has ever been
  surprised by a question type was surprised by something the document never claimed to tell them.
- **Unit 2.** The number printed largest on the form is the one that is least use to anybody who needs
  a band in each paper. The large print is for the people who want one number.
- **Unit 3.** A framework written to help compare courses across a continent is used daily as a gate.
  The Council of Europe has said more than once that it is not for that.
- **Unit 4.** Everybody in the published tables chose to be there. A mean of two million strangers is
  a planning figure, and it has been quoted at students as a verdict for years.
- **Unit 5.** Most wrong answers in a Listening paper are not vocabulary. They are the question you
  stayed with after the recording had left it, and almost no lesson in the world diagnoses that.
- **Unit 6.** The rule that students break most often is the watch, and nobody who breaks it means to.
  The rule students most want explained — what counts as a memorized answer — is the one the notice
  does not define.
- **Unit 7.** Four thousand extra word families buy the last three percent of coverage. Coverage of 96
  percent still loses the question if the missing word is the one the question turns on.
- **Unit 8.** Somebody chose two thousand words by hand, using judgment as well as counting, and much
  of the world learned those two thousand first. It was a good list. It was still a choice.
- **Unit 9.** The three most popular study techniques in the world came out at the bottom of the
  review, and the technique that feels like failing came out at the top.
- **Unit 10.** One man, one metronome and a list of nonsense, and every review schedule since has been
  drawn on his curve. The schedules are not in his book.
- **Unit 11.** Six corrections, six notebook entries, no change. Then one lecture, and the thing
  stopped. One student in one term is not evidence, and it is still the most useful thing in the unit.

# A note on everything else in this book

Every person, class, center, university, employer, figure and conversation in this book is invented.
Meridian Language Center does not exist; neither do Ashworth University, Calderbrook Institute of
Technology, Northvale College, Harrowgate School of Nursing or Pennine Regional Hospital, and neither
do the fourteen people who appear in the dialogues. **The band each invented organization asks for is
invented too.** The documents, papers and word lists are real and cited; the stories around them are
written to teach the language, and any resemblance to a real organization is accidental.

**No question, passage, recording or prompt from any past examination is reproduced anywhere in this
book.** Every practice item was written for it.

Nothing in this book is advice about your admission, registration, visa or employment. Where a
sentence sounds like a rule — *forty-five minutes early*, *7.0 in each paper*, *six hours with the
book shut* — that is one invented center's practice or one invented student's plan, printed because a
concrete number teaches better than an abstraction. The band you need is the band the organization
asking for it says it needs, on the day you apply. **Ask them, and write down the date.**

And nothing in this book is a prediction. A practice score is what you did on one set of questions on
one morning.

# Closing

You began this book able to say that you needed a 6.5. You end it able to say **which** paper,
against **which** published scale, averaged by **which** rule, read on **which** date, from **which**
document — and what that document admits it does not cover.

Eleven documents, and one habit: find the published page, print the sentence that says what it does
not cover, and write a number where a feeling used to be.

Five sentences to take with you, one from each part of the book.

From Unit 1: **which of these did the document actually say?**

From Unit 2: **what is this a score of?**

From Unit 4: **whose average is this, and who chose to be in it?**

From Unit 9: **how many of those hours had the book shut?**

From Unit 11: **where would I go to hear it?**
