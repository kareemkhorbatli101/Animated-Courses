Before anybody had a computer, somebody chose two thousand words by hand and printed, beside many of
them, how often each of their meanings was actually used. The list is seventy years old. Most of it
is still the first thing you need.

%%FIG qa08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a five-row table of selection criteria, a short extract showing one headword with three senses and a percentage beside each, a boxed paragraph headed where this list stops, a list of four things it does not contain, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to students**
> **Subject: the oldest useful word list, and what it does that newer ones do not**
> **Source: Michael West, *A General Service List of English Words*, Longman, Green and Co., 1953.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> In 1953 a short book was published listing about **two thousand** word families and calling them a
> **general service** vocabulary: the words a learner needs for general purposes, whatever subject
> they go on to study. It drew on word counts made in the decades before it, and it was intended
> mainly for one job — writing **simplified readers**, books that use a small, known vocabulary on
> purpose.
>
> The list was not produced by frequency alone. The published account of the selection names several
> criteria: how often a word appears, how **easy** it is to learn, whether it is **necessary** when
> another word would do, how much it lets a writer **say**, and whether it is the plain word or the
> formal one. One of those is a count. The others are judgements made by people.
>
> Its most unusual feature is this. Beside many headwords it prints, for each **sense** of the word, a
> figure for how much of that word's use that sense accounted for. A common word may have one sense
> that covers most of its appearances and four that cover almost none. A learner who knows the
> frequent sense of a frequent word can read; a learner who learned the rare sense has spent the same
> effort and bought much less.
>
> **WHERE THIS LIST STOPS**
> The counting behind it was done on texts written before the middle of the twentieth century, and
> most of them were written, not spoken. There is no computer in it, no internet, no word for most of
> the objects in the room you are sitting in. The selection was partly editorial: two careful people
> would not have chosen the same two thousand. The sense figures come from one counting exercise and
> are not confirmed anywhere else in the book. And West made the list to help writers produce readers
> — **not** as a syllabus for learners, and not as a test of anybody's vocabulary.
>
> Newer frequency lists exist, built from much larger collections of both written and spoken English
> and kept up to date. Use one of those for your own learning. Read this one to see what a list can be
> when somebody counts meanings and not only words.
>
> Four things it does not contain: modern technology · spoken English in quantity · an order to learn
> in · any claim about a test.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The center's copy is on the shelf; the senses are the part to
> look at.*

### 2 · Choosing the form the slot needs

A gap does not want a meaning; it wants a **part of speech**. Look at the words on each side of it.

| | |
|---|---|
| after *the* or *a* → a **noun** | the **selection** of words |
| after *is* or *was* → an **adjective** | the sense is **frequent** |
| before a noun → an **adjective** | a **frequent** sense |
| after a verb → an **adverb** | it appears **frequently** |
| after a preposition → a **noun** | a question **of necessity** |

**2A  Say what part of speech each gap needs. Then fill it.**

1 The ______ (necessary) of a word was one criterion.
2 This sense is more ______ (frequent) than that one.
3 The list was ______ (part) a judgement.
4 It gives a ______ (measure) for each sense.
5 Two careful people would not choose ______ (identical) the same words.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL1-08-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students write *the frequently sense* and *it appears frequent*.
The slot decides the ending, not the meaning. **Say your partner four sentences and let them stop you
when the ending does not fit the slot.**

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Half the gaps in a Reading paper are grammar, not vocabulary.** A summary-completion or
> sentence-completion gap usually accepts one form of one word, and students who know the word still
> lose the mark by writing the wrong ending. Before you write any word into a gap, read the three
> words in front of it. If the gap follows *the*, it wants a noun, and no amount of knowing the word
> will rescue *the select*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Word lists have a political history. The one in this unit was built in a period when English was
> being taught across a great many countries at once, and a short list was a way of making books
> cheap and lessons possible. That is a real benefit and a real limit: somebody in one place decided
> which two thousand words the world would learn, using judgement as well as counting. Today the
> counting is done by machine on billions of words, and the judgements are still made by people.
> **Ask your group: who decides which words a student in your country learns first? A ministry, a
> publisher, or a teacher?**
