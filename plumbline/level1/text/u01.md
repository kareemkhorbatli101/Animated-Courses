# Unit 1 · Four Papers, One Morning
%%SUB The shape of the test — and the published document that gives you the shape and nothing else%%
%%GOALS
Name the four papers and say what each one asks
Say how long each paper lasts and in what order they come
Read a published test format and find what it does not say
Use the present simple for facts about the test
Use *there is* and *there are* to describe a paper
%%

Four papers. One morning for three of them. The published description of the test tells you the
shape of each paper, down to the minute. It tells you almost nothing about what will be in it.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qa01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the source and the date it was downloaded, a four-row table of the papers with the minutes and the number of questions, a boxed paragraph headed what this page does not tell you, a short list of four things the document is silent about, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to new students**
> **Subject: the shape of the test, from the published format**
> **Source: the test format pages published by the test owners — the British Council, IDP: IELTS
> Australia and Cambridge University Press & Assessment. Downloaded 4 February. Printed here in our
> own words.**
>
> The test has four papers. The published format gives the length of each one, the number of
> questions or tasks in it, and the order.
>
> **Listening** lasts about thirty minutes. There are four parts and forty questions. You hear each
> recording **once**. In the paper test you then get ten extra minutes to copy your answers onto the
> answer sheet. In the computer test you type them as you go and get a short time to check.
>
> **Reading** lasts sixty minutes. In the Academic test there are three passages and forty
> questions. There is **no extra time** at the end. The sixty minutes include writing your answers.
>
> **Writing** lasts sixty minutes and has two tasks. Task 1 asks you to describe information you are
> given. Task 2 asks you to write an essay. Task 2 counts for more of the Writing band than Task 1.
>
> **Speaking** lasts eleven to fourteen minutes and has three parts. You talk to one examiner, in a
> room, and the conversation is recorded.
>
> Listening, Reading and Writing are taken in one sitting, in that order, with no break. Speaking may
> be on the same day or within seven days of it.
>
> **WHAT THIS PAGE DOES NOT TELL YOU**
> The published format is a description of the **shape** of the test. It is not a description of your
> paper. It does not say which question types you will meet, or how many of each. It does not say
> what the passages will be about. It does not say how many correct answers make a band on the
> version you sit. And it does not say anything at all about you.
>
> Four things the document is silent about: your question types · your topics · your raw-score line ·
> your result.
>
> *Our summary of a published format. Read the owners' own pages before you book.*

### 1 · The present simple for facts about the test

We use the present simple for things that are true every time the test is run.

| | |
|---|---|
| **The Listening paper has four parts.** | not *is having* |
| **The test does not give you a break.** | not *is not giving* |
| **You hear each recording once.** | not *you are hearing* |
| **Does the Reading paper have transfer time?** | **No, it does not.** |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In some countries a public examination publishes the number of marks needed for each grade, in
> advance, every year. This test does not work that way. The published documents give the shape of
> the papers and a scale from nought to nine; the number of correct answers that reaches a band is
> set for each version of the test and is published only as a guide. **Ask your group: in your
> country, is the pass mark published before the exam or after it? Which do you think is fairer, and
> why?**
