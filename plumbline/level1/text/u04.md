### 2 · Read

%%FIG qa04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the source and the year it covers with the date downloaded, a four-row table of skill means marked as an example only, a two-row table of two first-language subgroups, a boxed paragraph headed what an average cannot do, a short list of four things the tables do not say, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to new students**
> **Subject: the published test taker performance figures**
> **Source: the test taker performance tables published each year by the test owners. Downloaded 4
> February for the most recent year then available. Printed here in our own words. We have not
> reprinted their figures: look them up, because they change every year.**
>
> Every year the owners of the test publish how everybody did. The tables give a **mean** band for
> each of the four papers and a mean overall band. They are published for all Academic test takers in
> the year, and again cut by **first language**, by **country of origin** and by test — Academic or
> General Training.
>
> The tables are worth one evening of your time, and they show one thing clearly. Across the years we
> have looked at, the mean for **Writing** has been the lowest of the four skill means for Academic
> test takers, and Listening and Reading have been higher than Writing and Speaking. That is a
> pattern in the published figures, not a law.
>
> **WHAT AN AVERAGE CANNOT DO**
> These tables describe about two million people who chose to sit a test. They are not a sample of
> any country's population: nobody is picked at random, everybody is there because they wanted
> something. A subgroup mean therefore says as much about **who applies** from a place as about
> anybody's English.
>
> The tables do not say **why** a mean is where it is. They do not say what any individual got, and
> they cannot be used to predict what you will get: an average is a fact about a crowd, and you are
> one person inside it. The owners note that the figures for different years are not safely compared,
> because the people sitting the test are not the same people.
>
> Four things the tables do not say: why · who · your score · next year.
>
> *Our summary of a published table. Read the owners' own pages, and write down the year.*

### 1 · The past simple for reported figures

Published figures are about a year that has finished. We report them in the **past simple**.

| | |
|---|---|
| The mean for Writing **was** 5.8 that year. | not *is* |
| About two million people **took** the test. | irregular: take → took |
| Listening **had** the highest mean of the four. | |
| The tables **did not say** why. | *did not* + the base form |
| **Did** the mean **change** from the year before? | |

**1A  Put the verb in the past simple and say the sentence aloud.**

2 About two million people ______ (sit) the Academic test that year.
3 The tables ______ (give) a mean for each paper.
4 The owners ______ (not compare) the year with the one before.
5 ______ Writing ______ (have) the lowest mean?

### 2 · *Most*, *most of* and *the majority*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Most students** want a higher band. | *most* + plural noun, no *the* |

| **The majority of test takers** sat the Academic test. | *the majority of* + plural |

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Nothing in these tables will be on your paper.** You will not be asked about means, and no
> examiner will know where you sit in a distribution. The tables are here for one reason: they are the
> cheapest cure for the two sentences that do most damage in a preparation class — *everybody gets
> this* and *nobody gets this*. Both are claims about a crowd, both are checkable, and the answer is
> published.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Published tables of how groups of people performed are read differently in different places. In
> some countries they are used to plan teaching: if one paper is lowest everywhere, hours move to
> that paper. In others the same table is quoted to explain why a group cannot do something, which is
> the one use the figures cannot support, because everybody in them chose to be there. **Ask your
> group: have you ever heard an average used to explain what a group of people is like? Was that a
> fair use of the figure? Why?**
