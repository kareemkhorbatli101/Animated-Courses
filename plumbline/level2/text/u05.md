# Unit 5 · The Brain Guesses Early
%%SUB A model from 1978, the point at which a word becomes the only word, and what its own author changed later%%
%%GOALS
Say what happens in the first sounds of a word
Explain what a recognition point is
Predict the word that must come next

You do not wait for a word to finish. By the third sound you have thrown most of English away, and
by the fifth there is often only one word left. A paper from 1978 gave that process a shape — and its
own author revised it nine years later.

%%FIG qb05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a four-stage table of the process, a short description of the shadowing task, a boxed paragraph headed a laboratory model, revised by its own author, a list of four limits, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: how a word is recognized before it is finished**
> **Source: W. D. Marslen-Wilson and A. Welsh, *Processing interactions and lexical access during
> word recognition in continuous speech*, Cognitive Psychology, **1978**. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> The question the paper asks is small and the answer changed how people think about listening: **at
> what moment does a listener know which word they are hearing?**
>
> The answer the authors give is that recognition is a process of **narrowing**. As the first sounds
> of a word arrive, every word you know that begins that way becomes a candidate — the paper's name
> for that set is a **cohort**. More sound arrives and candidates that no longer fit drop out. At some
> point only one candidate is left, and that is the **recognition point**. Very often it comes
> **before the word has finished**, especially if the sentence has already made most words
> impossible.
>
> Their evidence came from asking people to **shadow** speech — to repeat it aloud as they heard it,
> a fraction of a second behind. Some words in the recordings had been **mispronounced** on purpose.
> Listeners frequently repeated the correct word rather than the one they had actually heard, without
> noticing. These **restorations** happened most when the mispronounced sound came **late** in the
> word and when the sentence strongly limited what could come next — which is exactly what you would
> expect if the word had already been recognized before the bad sound arrived.
>
> For a learner the consequence is immediate. A listener who waits for the end of each word is
> working with the least useful part of it. The beginning carries most of the information, and the
> sentence carries the rest.
>
> **A LABORATORY MODEL, REVISED BY ITS OWN AUTHOR**
> This is a **model** built from laboratory tasks — shadowing and mispronunciation detection — with a
> small number of participants, in English. It is not a measurement of what happens in a test room.
>
> The original version allowed the sentence to influence the cohort from the very start. **The first
> author revised that**, in work published in 1987, so that the initial cohort is formed from the
> sound alone and context acts later. A model that its own author changes is working as a model
> should, and it is also a warning: what you are reading is a position in a continuing argument, not
> a fact about your ears.
>
> It says nothing about **learners**: the participants were native speakers. It says nothing about
> whether recognition can be **trained**, and it offers no exercise, no timetable and no claim about
> any examination.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. Read it if your center has access; the shadowing task is the
> memorable part.*

### 1 · Reduced relative clauses

English drops the relative pronoun and the verb *be*. The result is shorter, commoner in speech, and
harder to hear.

| Full | Reduced |
|---|---|
| the man **who is** standing by the door | the man **standing** by the door |
| the form **which was** given to you | the form **given** to you |
| the students **who were** waiting outside | the students **waiting** outside |
| the module **which is** taught in April | the module **taught** in April |
| anybody **who is** interested in the trip | anybody **interested** in the trip |

> **Read the gap before the recording starts.** If the printed words before a gap are *depends*,

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Finishing another person's sentence is polite in some cultures and rude in others. In languages
> where it shows you are listening, learners often predict confidently and mishear boldly; in
> languages where it is an interruption, learners wait, and waiting costs them in a test that plays
> each recording once. The model in this unit says the prediction is happening whether you approve of
> it or not. **Ask your group: in your language, is finishing somebody's sentence helpful or rude?
> Does that change how you listen?**
