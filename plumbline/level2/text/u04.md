When you mishear English you do not hear nonsense. You hear something else that is also English,
with about the same rhythm, and you believe it. Somebody spent years writing those down.

%%FIG qb04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a three-row table of the kinds of slip with an invented example each, a short list of four findings, a boxed paragraph headed a collection that cannot count itself, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: what happens when a listener gets it wrong**
> **Source: Z. S. Bond, *Slips of the Ear: Errors in the Perception of Casual Conversation*,
> Academic Press, **1999**. Printed here in our own words; the examples in our table are invented,
> not hers.**
>
> For years the author and her colleagues carried notebooks and wrote down **misperceptions** as they
> happened: what a speaker had said, what the listener had heard, and the situation. The result is a
> collection of around nine hundred real slips of the ear, in ordinary casual conversation, almost
> all of them made by people whose first language was English.
>
> Four things in the collection matter to a learner.
>
> **A mishearing is almost always a real phrase.** Listeners do not produce nonsense. They produce
> English — a different sentence, grammatical, plausible in the situation — and they believe it.
>
> **The rhythm survives.** What is heard usually keeps the stress pattern and roughly the number of
> syllables of what was said. The listener is holding on to the shape and losing the detail.
>
> **Word boundaries move.** A very large share of the slips are not wrong words at all: they are the
> right sounds cut in the wrong place. Speech has no spaces, so the cut is something the listener
> supplies, and it can be supplied wrongly.
>
> **Small words go first.** Function words, already flattened, are the ones most often recovered
> wrongly, and the listener does not notice because the sentence still works.
>
> **The last of the four is the uncomfortable one.** In many cases the listener had no idea anything
> had happened. The slip was caught because a second person was there, or because the conversation
> later made no sense. A listener alone with a recording has neither of those.
>
> **A COLLECTION THAT CANNOT COUNT ITSELF**
> This is an **observational collection**, not an experiment. Every slip in it is a slip somebody
> **noticed**. The ones nobody noticed are, by definition, not in the book — so the collection can
> describe what misperceptions look like, and it **cannot say how often they happen**. The author is
> explicit about this.
>
> It is also a collection of **English casual conversation of one period**, largely American, made by
> native-speaking listeners. It does not claim to describe what learners of English mishear, and it
> does not test any explanation: because it is observational, it reports no cause.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The value of it is the shape of the errors, not a number.*

### 1 · Compound nouns and their stress

A compound noun is two words acting as one thing. **The stress goes on the first word.**

| | |
|---|---|

### 2 · Where one word ends

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **If the answer makes a strange sentence, it is probably a slip.** You will not catch most of your
> own mishearings, but you will catch the ones that produce something odd — a phrase that does not
> fit the form, a word that cannot follow the one before it. Read your answer back against the words
> printed around the gap. That one check costs four seconds and catches the loudest kind of error.
