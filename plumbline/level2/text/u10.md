Most listening practice is a test you give yourself. One study took a whole term, taught two groups
the same recordings in two different ways, and found that how you listen can be taught — with the
usual caution attached.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qb10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a five-row table of the cycle, a short description of the two groups, a bar chart of two pairs of bars, a boxed paragraph headed one term, two intact classes, one teacher, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: why we make you compare before we give you the answers**
> **Source: L. Vandergrift and M. H. Tafaghodtari, *Teaching L2 learners how to listen does make a
> difference: an empirical study*, Language Learning, **2010**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Almost all listening practice in the world has the same shape: listen, answer, check, repeat. The
> student finds out their score and nothing else. The study asks whether teaching the **process** of
> listening — rather than giving more things to listen to — makes a measurable difference.
>
> Two groups of learners worked through the same listening material over a term. The **control group**
> did it in the usual way. The **experimental group** worked through a repeated cycle on every text:
>
> | Step | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | **Plan and predict** | before listening, think about the topic and the likely words |
> | **First listen and verify** | listen, write, mark what is uncertain |
> | **Compare** | compare with a partner, **before** the answers are given |
> | **Discuss and solve** | work out together why each disagreement happened |
> | **Listen again and reflect** | listen once more, then write what helped and what to change |
>
> At the end of the term both groups took a listening test. The **experimental group did better** than
> the control group. And within the experimental group, the students who had started as the **less
> skilled** listeners made the **largest** gains.
>
> That second finding is the one that matters in a preparation class. The cycle is not a polish for
> people who are already good. It appears to help most the people who currently get the lowest scores
> — which is the opposite of how most teaching time is spent.
>
> Why would it work? Because the cycle makes the invisible part visible. A student who finds out they
> wrote a day where their partner wrote a time has found a reason, not a score. Step three is
> uncomfortable for exactly that reason, and skipping it removes the whole point of the cycle.
>
> **ONE TERM, TWO INTACT CLASSES, ONE TEACHER**
> This is **one quasi-experimental study**, over one term, at one institution, with one language pair.
> The groups were **intact classes** — whole existing classes, not individuals assigned at random —
> so the two groups may have differed in ways nobody measured.
>
> The outcome was a **listening test**, not a band, and not this examination. The study makes no claim
> about anybody's band.
>
> And the cycle cannot be separated from the **teacher who delivered it**. A method and the person
> running it arrive together, and no study of this kind can hand you one without the other.
>
> The *less skilled gained most* result is a **subgroup finding** from one study, which is the kind of
> result that most often fails to appear again. It is a good reason to try the cycle. It is not a
> promise.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. Read it if your center has access; the five steps are the part
> you can use tomorrow.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Comparing answers with a classmate before the teacher gives the key is, in some classrooms, close
> to cheating; in others it is the normal way to work. Where the teacher holds the answers, students
> learn to wait for authority and to treat a score as the end of the exercise. Where students compare
> first, the discussion does work that no key can do — and it takes longer, and it is noisier, and
> some students hate it. **Ask your group: at school, were you allowed to compare answers before the
> teacher gave them? What happened if you did?**
