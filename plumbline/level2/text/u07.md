# Unit 7 · The Lecture Has a Shape
%%SUB Four versions of one lecture, a study from 1986, and the sentences that tell you what is coming%%
%%GOALS
Tell the difference between a small marker and a big one
Hear where a lecture turns, and write it down
Use discourse markers to signpost your own speech
Use the language of sequence, contrast and restatement
Say why one study with modest effects is still worth knowing
%%

Four groups heard the same lecture. One version had nothing but the content; one had small joining
words; one had sentences announcing what was coming; one had both. Only one of those made a real
difference, and it is the one your course probably never taught you.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qb07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a four-row table of the lecture versions with what each contained, a short description of the recall measure, a boxed paragraph headed one study, modest effects, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: the sentences that give you the shape**
> **Source: C. A. Chaudron and J. C. Richards, *The effect of discourse markers on the comprehension
> of lectures*, Applied Linguistics, **1986**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The study takes one lecture and makes four versions of it. The content is the same in all four.
> What changes is the **signposting**.
>
> | Version | What it contained |
> |---|---|
> | **Baseline** | the content, with no added markers |
> | **Micro-markers only** | small joining words: *well*, *so*, *then*, *but*, *now* |
> | **Macro-markers only** | sentences announcing the structure: *what I want to look at is…*, *that is the first point; let us turn to…* |
> | **Both** | small and big together |
>
> Four groups of learners each heard one version, and their comprehension was measured afterwards.
>
> The result is the useful part. The versions that contained **macro-markers** produced better
> comprehension than the baseline. **Micro-markers on their own added very little.** The small
> joining words, which are everywhere in real speech and which most courses teach, did not help these
> listeners understand the lecture; the sentences that announced the structure did.
>
> There is a plain reason why. A macro-marker gives you a **heading** before the content arrives.
> *Today I want to look at three things* tells you to leave three spaces on the page. *That is the
> first point, now let us turn to the second* tells you that what you have been writing is finished
> and a new space is needed. A listener with headings is filing. A listener without them is writing a
> single long paragraph and hoping.
>
> Small markers do a different job — they hold a sentence together, they buy the speaker a moment,
> they soften a disagreement. They are worth knowing for speaking. They are not where the shape is.
>
> **ONE STUDY, MODEST EFFECTS**
> This is **one study**, published in 1986, with a particular group of learners at one institution,
> on recorded lectures prepared for the experiment. The measure was a **comprehension and recall
> test**, not a band and not a real examination.
>
> The differences between the versions were **real but modest**: the macro-marker groups did better,
> not enormously better, and a single study with a moderate effect is a reason to try something, not
> a proof.
>
> It also cannot tell you what **your** lecturer will do. Some speakers signpost every two minutes;
> some signpost once and never again; some never do it at all, and for those the finding gives you no
> help whatever.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. Read it if your center has access; the four versions are the
> part to look at.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **The first forty seconds of Part 4 are the cheapest marks in the paper.** A lecturer who is going
> to signpost does it at the start. Write the shape down the side of the page before any content
> arrives, and you spend the next three minutes filing instead of hoping. If the speaker does not
> signpost, you have lost nothing but forty seconds of listening you were going to spend on an
> introduction anyway.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Lecturing styles differ sharply between countries. In some traditions a lecturer announces the
> structure, repeats it, and returns to it at the end, because the structure is considered part of
> the teaching. In others the structure is the student's job to find, and announcing it would be
> thought simple-minded. A student trained in the second tradition arrives expecting no help and does
> not hear the help when it is offered. **Ask your group: did your teachers announce the shape of a
> lesson? Did anybody ever tell you to write it down?**
