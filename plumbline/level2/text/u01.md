# Unit 1 · Four Parts, Forty Questions
%%SUB What the published guide says each part contains — and what it refuses to promise%%
%%GOALS

Four parts, ten questions each, and a published guide that tells teachers what **may** be in a paper.
It will not tell anybody what **is** in one, and the difference between those two words is this
unit.

%%FIG qb01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the source and the date it was downloaded, a four-row table of the parts with speakers and context, a six-row list of question types, a boxed paragraph headed what a guide will not promise, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: the four parts, and the question types that may appear**
> **Source: the guide for teachers published by the test owners, downloaded 11 February. Printed
> here in our own words. The guide is revised from time to time: check the date on the copy you
> read.**
>
> The Listening paper has **four parts** and **forty questions**, ten in each part. Every recording
> is played **once**.
>
> The parts are not four pieces of the same thing. **Part 1** is a conversation between two people
> about an everyday matter — booking something, joining something, asking for details. **Part 2** is
> one speaker alone on an everyday subject: a place, an arrangement, a plan. **Part 3** is a
> conversation between up to four people in a study or training context: a project, an assignment, a
> reading list. **Part 4** is one speaker alone on an academic subject — a short lecture.
>
> So the paper moves twice. It moves from **two speakers to one** and back, and it moves from
> **everyday English to academic English**. Students who lose marks evenly across the paper are
> rare; most lose them in a block, and the block has a reason.
>
> The guide names the **question types** that may appear: completion of a form, a set of notes, a
> table or a flow-chart; sentence completion; short-answer questions; multiple choice; matching; and
> the labeling of a plan, a map or a diagram. Each type carries its own instruction about how many
> words you may write, and that instruction is part of the question.
>
> **WHAT A GUIDE WILL NOT PROMISE**
> It says which types **may** appear. It does not say which will appear on your paper, how many of
> each, or in what order. It does not say what any recording will be about. It is a description of a
> design, not a description of a paper, and a student who treats it as a prediction has read it
> wrongly.
>
> It is also **revised**. The edition you read is the edition you read; quoting it without a date is
> quoting nothing. And it is written for teachers: it explains what the paper is for, not how to
> answer it quickly.
>
> *Our summary of a published guide. Read it yourself — it is free, and it is written in plain
> English.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Read the instruction before the recording starts.** Every type carries its own instruction, and
> you get about thirty seconds before each part. Thirty seconds is enough to read six gaps and see
> what kind of word each one wants: a number, a name, a day, a thing. Students who use those thirty
> seconds to worry hear the same recording as students who use them to read, and get fewer marks for
> it.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Directions are given differently in different places. In some cities people give you landmarks —
> *past the mosque, opposite the old bakery* — and in others they give you street names and distances
> in meters. A recording made for an international examination usually gives both, which is
> comfortable for nobody and fair to everybody. **Ask your group: in your city, do people give
> directions by landmark or by street name? What would you find hardest about the other way?**
