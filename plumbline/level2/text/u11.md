# Unit 11 · The Score You Cannot Feel
%%SUB Reliability, the band of uncertainty round any one number, and a coefficient its own inventor later wrote against%%
%%GOALS

Your score is a number. It is not a point: it is a number with a width. A paper published in 1951
gave the profession the coefficient it has used ever since to work out how wide, and its author
spent the rest of his life uncomfortable about it.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qb11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a three-row table of the ideas in plain English, a small worked example of a score with a band round it, a boxed paragraph headed a coefficient its own author came to distrust, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: how much a score would move if you sat another one**
> **Source: L. J. Cronbach, *Coefficient alpha and the internal structure of tests*, Psychometrika,
> **1951**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> A score has two parts: the thing you are trying to measure, and accident. Accident includes the
> particular questions on that paper, whether you slept, whether the one word you did not know
> happened to be the one the question turned on.
>
> **Reliability** is a name for the ratio between those two parts. A highly reliable score is mostly
> signal. A less reliable one carries more accident, which means that the same person, on an
> equivalent paper, next week, would get a different number.
>
> Before 1951 there were several ways of estimating this, mostly by splitting a test in half and
> comparing the halves — but a test can be split in half in very many ways and each split gives a
> different answer. This paper brought them together in a **single coefficient**, and showed that it
> is equivalent to the average of all the possible split-half correlations. That coefficient is the
> one every testing organization in the world has reported since.
>
> What it is **for**, from a candidate's point of view, is this. Once you know how reliable a set of
> scores is, you can work out the **standard error of measurement** — the width of the band round any
> one person's score. Every number on every result form in the world has such a band, whether or not
> it is printed, and knowing that is the difference between *I am a 6* and *I scored 6 on that paper,
> and I might not next time*.
>
> **A COEFFICIENT ITS OWN AUTHOR CAME TO DISTRUST**
> Three things in this paper are regularly misquoted, and all three matter.
>
> It is **not a property of a test**. It is a property of a particular **set of items answered by a
> particular group of people**. The same questions given to a different group yield a different
> figure, so a sentence beginning *this test has a reliability of…* is already loose.
>
> It is **not a measure of whether a test measures one thing**. A high coefficient does not show that
> all the items are about the same ability; that is the commonest misunderstanding of the paper and
> it has been pointed out for seventy years.
>
> It **rises with the number of items**. Add more questions and the figure goes up, whether or not
> the questions are any good.
>
> And the author was uneasy about all of this. **Cronbach wrote critically about how his coefficient
> had come to be used**, in work published after his death, in 2004. A measure whose inventor spent
> fifty years qualifying it is a measure to quote carefully.
>
> This paper contains **no figure for any examination**. It gives a method. What any particular test's
> reliability is, and how wide the band round your own band is, is a question for whoever publishes
> that test — and most published statements about it are general rather than personal.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. The useful sentence is the one about the width.*

### 2 · Hedging without going vague

A hedge should narrow a claim, not empty it.

| Too vague | Hedged and still useful |
|---|---|
| My English is quite good. | I scored between 23 and 28 on six papers. |
| It might be anything. | It is probably within two marks of 25. |
| Maybe I will get a 6. | On these six papers, 6 is the likeliest single band. |
| Sometimes I do better. | My best was 28, in the fourth of six. |
| It depends. | It depends on how many of the answers are spelling. |

**2A  Rewrite each vague sentence so that it keeps a number. Then read both aloud.**

1 My listening is getting better.
2 I usually do quite well in Part 1.
3 My score goes up and down a lot.
4 I might be about a 6.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL2-11-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students hedge by removing the number, which makes the sentence
unusable: *my score moves a bit*. Keep the number and hedge the edges: *my score has moved between

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **The width is not an excuse and it is not a comfort.** It cuts both ways: the morning can go a
> little better than your average and it can go a little worse, and nothing you do at nine o'clock
> changes the width. What narrows it is the number of things you can do reliably rather than
> sometimes. Everything in the first ten units of this book is about turning a sometimes into an
> always, which is the only honest way to move a range.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Public examinations in many countries publish a single mark to the nearest point, and treating that
> mark as exact is a social habit as much as a statistical one: it settles arguments, it fits on a
