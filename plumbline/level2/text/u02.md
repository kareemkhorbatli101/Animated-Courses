%%FIG qb02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a three-row table of the three kinds of speech, a short transcript of one stretch with the speech units marked and the prominent syllables underlined, a boxed paragraph headed what this book is and is not, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: why the words are not where you expect them**
> **Source: Richard Cauldwell, *Phonology for Listening: Teaching the Stream of Speech*, Speech in
> Action, 2013. Printed here in our own words. The three names are his.**
>
> Speech is not a row of words with gaps between them. It is a **stream**. The gaps you think you
> hear are mostly in your head, put there by your knowledge of where words end — which is why you
> can hear the boundaries in your own language and not in English.
>
> The book separates three kinds of speech and gives them names.
>
> The **greenhouse** is a single word, said alone, carefully, in its full form. It is what a
> dictionary recording gives you and what a drill gives you. Every word you have ever learned, you
> learned in the greenhouse.
>
> The **garden** is connected speech that behaves. It follows the rules a textbook teaches: the
> small words weaken, the sounds join in the ways you were shown, and nothing happens that the rules
> did not predict. Most classroom recordings are garden speech.
>
> The **jungle** is spontaneous speech. It is fast and uneven. Words are squeezed into shapes no rule
> predicted, whole syllables go, and the speaker changes direction in the middle of a sentence
> because they are thinking while they talk. Real conversation is jungle, and so is most of what you
> hear in the second half of a Listening paper.
>
> The book's organizing unit is the **speech unit**: a short stretch between two pauses, usually with
> one syllable standing out. The important thing about a speech unit is that the words inside it are
> not equal. One or two carry the message and are said clearly. The rest are compressed into the
> space between them, and those compressed ones are usually the grammar — the articles, the
> prepositions, the auxiliaries.
>
> So the problem is not that you do not know those words. You know them better than you know the
> clear ones. The problem is that you learned them in the greenhouse and meet them in the jungle.
>
> **WHAT THIS BOOK IS AND IS NOT**
> It is a **pedagogic account** written for teachers, with its own terminology, built from close
> listening to recordings of real speech. The three-way division is a **teaching device** — a way of
> describing what you hear — not an experimental finding, and the author does not claim it is one.
> It reports no trial, gives no timetable and promises no gain in any score.
>
> It also does not tell you how long jungle listening takes to learn. It tells you what you are
> listening to, which is a different kind of help, and most students have never been given it.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. If your center has a copy, the recordings that come with it are
> the part to use.*

### 1 · Contractions in writing and in sound

In writing, a contraction is an apostrophe. In speech, it is often all that is left of two words.

| | |
|---|---|
| **I'll** be there by two. | *will* can disappear almost completely |
| **There's** a form at reception. | one syllable for two words |
| **He's** finished the reading. | *he has* |
| **He's** in the library. | *he is* — the same sound, two meanings |
| **We'd** better leave now. | *we had*, in a fixed phrase |

**1A  Write the full form and say both aloud.**

### 2 · Deciding which word a contraction hides

**He's** is *he is* or *he has*. **We'd** is *we would* or *we had*. The rest of the sentence decides.

| | |
|---|---|
| He's **late**. | + adjective → he **is** |
| He's **finished**. | + past participle → he **has** |
| We'd **go** if we could. | + base verb → we **would** |
| We'd **paid** already. | + past participle → we **had** |
| She's **been** waiting. | + *been* → she **has** |

**2A  Say whether each one is *is/has* or *would/had*. Then read it aloud.**

1 He's written to them twice.
2 He's in the seminar room.
3 We'd rather do Part 3 first.
4 We'd finished before the bell.
5 She's been here since nine.

**2B  Listen and check.** %%AUD PL2-02-09%%

**2C  The mistake this causes.** Students write *He's gone yesterday* and *We'd better went*. The
word after the contraction is what fixes it: a past participle after *has*, a base verb after
*would*. **Say your partner four sentences with each and let them correct the verb that follows.**

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Do not wait for the clear version.** Nothing in a Listening paper will be repeated and nothing
> will be slowed down. The one thing you can do on the day is stop listening for separate words and
> listen for the one or two that are said clearly, then take the grammar from what makes sense
> around them. That is not a trick. It is what listeners in every language actually do, and you do it
> already in your own.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Many English courses in the world present the language in its greenhouse form for years: words on
> cards, sentences read aloud by a teacher at half speed, recordings made by actors told to be clear.
> This is kind, it makes lessons possible, and it postpones a problem. In some countries learners
> meet fast English outside class every day and arrive with good ears and shaky grammar; in others
> they arrive with perfect grammar and have never heard a stranger argue in English. **Ask your
