# Grammar reference

Every structure in this book, in unit order. The second column is the mistake it prevents, which is
the column worth reading twice.

## Starting Point · Thirty minutes

| | |
|---|---|
| **Four parts, ten questions each, played once** | the paper moves twice: two speakers to one, and everyday to academic |
| **Four reasons a mark goes** | did not know it · did not hear it in the stream · too slow to write it · still on the question before |
| **Only the first of the four is vocabulary** | which is why three quarters of this book is not a word list |
| **A score with no reason attached cannot be acted on** | *I got 19* is a fact; *I lost eleven in two groups in Parts 3 and 4* is a plan |

## Unit 1 · Prepositions of place and direction

| | |
|---|---|
| ***opposite*, *next to*, *between*, *past*, *along*, *through*** | the six that carry a Part 1 route |
| ***on the left*, *at the end*, *round the corner*** | the three you hear in every map question |
| ***Where exactly? Which side? Opposite what? Can you spell that?*** | the short questions; *where is it exactly situated* is not English anybody says |
| **the guide says which types *may* appear** | not which will, not how many, not in what order |

## Unit 2 · Contractions, and what they hide

| | |
|---|---|
| ***I'll*, *there's*, *he's*, *we'd*, *would've*, *should've*** | in speech these are often all that is left of two words |
| ***he's*** = he is **or** he has | the word after it decides: adjective → *is*, past participle → *has* |
| ***we'd*** = we would **or** we had | base verb → *would*, past participle → *had* |
| **greenhouse, garden, jungle** | single careful words, textbook connected speech, real speech — and you were taught two of the three |

## Unit 3 · Articles and prepositions you could not hear

| | |
|---|---|
| **weak forms: *to*, *of*, *and*, *for*, *was*, *can*** | the commonest words in English, almost never said in full |
| ***a* or *the*** | recovered from whether the thing has been mentioned before |
| ***a lot of people*, *most of the class*** | the *of* was flattened, not absent |
| **elision and assimilation are not carelessness** | they happen in careful speech, and how much depends on speaker, speed and style |

## Unit 4 · Compound nouns; where a word ends

| | |
|---|---|
| **a CAR park, a BUS pass, a GREENhouse** | the stress goes on the first word of a compound |
| **a green HOUSE** | adjective plus noun: the stress moves, and so does the meaning |
| ***a nice cold drink* / *an ice-cold drink*** | the same sounds cut in a different place, and both are real English |
| **a mishearing keeps the rhythm** | which is why it is believable, and why you rarely catch it |

## Unit 5 · Reduced relative clauses; dependent prepositions

| | |
|---|---|
| **the man standing by the door** | *who is* dropped: commoner in speech and harder to hear |
| **the form given to you** | *which was* dropped |
| ***depends on*, *interested in*, *consists of*, *responsible for*** | the word before the gap decides the word in it |
| **recognition usually comes before the word ends** | so the beginning of a word is worth more than the end |

## Unit 6 · Ellipsis in notes; clause to noun phrase

| | |
|---|---|
| **lecture — 4B · bring own ID · deadline → Fri** | notes are a form, not a bad sentence |
| **because the train was delayed → the delay** | a noun phrase is shorter, and shorter is the point |
| **two words unless the instruction allows three** | a long answer is paid for out of the next question |
| **the loop holds speech for a couple of seconds** | and writing is itself a speech-based task |

## Unit 7 · Discourse markers

| | |
|---|---|
| **small markers: *well*, *so*, *then*, *but*** | they hold a sentence together and tell you nothing about the shape |
| **big markers: *what I want to look at is…*, *that brings me to…*, *to sum up*** | each one is a free heading |
| ***in other words*, *that is to say*** | nothing new is coming: stop writing |
| **the first forty seconds of a lecture** | where the shape arrives, if it arrives at all |

## Unit 8 · Stress in nouns and verbs; stress in families

| | |
|---|---|
| **a REcord / to reCORD · an INcrease / to inCREASE** | noun at the front, verb at the back |
| **PHOtograph → phoTOgraphy → photoGRAPHic** | the stress moves when the suffix arrives |
| **a loud syllable is probably a word beginning** | most content words begin strong, which is why the rule works |
| **beGIN, aGREE, reSULT, aLLOW** | which is why it fails, and *sult* is not a word |

## Unit 9 · Comparatives of difficulty; *used to* and *be used to*

| | |
|---|---|
| **easier to understand than · harder to follow than · no easier than** | not *more easy* |
| **I *used to* find it hard** | past habit or state, now over |
| **I *am used to* it · I *am used to* listening to it** | familiar with it now; *be used to* + noun or *-ing* |
| **small and uneven effects** | some combinations helped, some did not, and the authors asked readers not to generalize |

## Unit 10 · Time clauses; reporting what you noticed

| | |
|---|---|
| ***Before you listen*, read every question** | present after *before*, *while*, *after*, *once*, *as soon as* |
| ***I realized that…*, *What helped was…*, *Next time I will…*** | the forms are fixed: *realize that*, and *-ing* after *what helped was* |
| **compare before you check** | the step everybody skips, and the only one that produces a cause |
| **intact classes, one teacher, one term** | which is why the study is a reason to try and not a proof |

## Unit 11 · Modals of probability; hedging a figure

| | |
|---|---|
| ***may*, *might*, *could*, *is likely to*** | and *will* is a claim nobody can make about a future score |
| **within half a band · give or take · about 25** | hedge the edges and keep the number |
| **every score is a number with a width** | the standard error is that width |
| **the coefficient belongs to items and a group** | not to a test; it does not show one thing is measured; it rises with length |

## Unit 12 · Review

| | |
|---|---|
| **thirty seconds, then two words, then move** | the whole book in nine words |
| **the six mechanical rules** | spelling · plurals · exact numbers · clear dates · capitals · the word limit |
| **about 30 of 40 near band 7, about 23 near band 6** | published as a guide, worked out per version, and not your paper's table |
| **count marks, not bands** | a practice score of 23 is 23 answers on one morning |

Four things about the recording in this book, which is a book about recordings.

**The focused-listening tracks are deliberately uneven.** Some are read carefully and some are
recorded at full conversational speed with the hesitations left in, because a book that teaches the
difference between careful and spontaneous speech cannot illustrate it with careful speech only.

**Every number in a focused-listening track is said twice**, because this book is full of figures and
a digit misheard loses the whole exercise.

**The transcripts are printed where the exercise needs them and withheld where it does not.** A
transcript given too early turns a listening exercise into a reading exercise, which is Unit 10's
objection to most listening practice in the world.

**And none of it is a sample of the examination.** This audio is a teaching recording made for this
book. The speakers are not examiners, the speeds are not the examination's speeds, and nothing here
tells you what a real recording sounds like. For that there is only the test owners' own published
sample material. Use it, and use nothing else for that purpose.

# The British layer

This book is written in American English, and the series carries a switchable British layer: a fixed
list of words is respelled at build time. The list lives with the series rather than in this book.

**For a Listening paper this matters more than usual, and less than you think.** The published
guidance for this examination accepts **both** British and American spellings in your answers, as
long as what you write is correct in one of them. What it does not accept is a word spelled in
neither. So the question is never *which country's spelling* — it is *is this spelled correctly at
all*. Choose one system and stay inside it, because consistency is cheaper to check than a mixture.

What the layer does not touch:

1. **The date.** Write the month in letters where the sentence is prose, and the international format
   where it is data. In an answer, write *3 May* rather than *3/5*: in an international paper a
   figure-only date is ambiguous and ambiguity is not a mark.
2. **A quoted document keeps its own spelling.** Several sources in this book are published in
   British English, and their titles and quoted sentences are printed as published.
3. **A journal name and a publisher keep their published form.** *Applied Linguistics*, *Cognitive
   Psychology*, *Language Learning*, *TESOL Quarterly*, *Psychometrika*, *Computer Speech and
   Language* are citations.
4. **Money has no symbol anywhere in this series.** A price in a Listening answer is written as a
   number, because the unit differs by country and a wrong symbol is readable nowhere.
5. **A name is never respelled.** Cauldwell, Brown, Bond, Marslen-Wilson, Welsh, Baddeley, Hitch,
   Chaudron, Richards, Cutler, Carter, Major, Vandergrift, Tafaghodtari and Cronbach are spelled as
   they are published.

# Sources

Twelve source bodies carry the twelve reading texts, one per unit, and the Starting Point carries
none. Every one is a published document, paper or book, cited with its edition or its date, and every
reading text prints what the publisher or the author says the document does **not** cover. No source
appears in any other book of this series or in its five sister series, and no figure is used twice.
The series note is in `SOURCES.md`.

| Unit | Source | What the text prints |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The **guide for teachers** published by the test owners, read 11 February | It names the question types that **may** appear, not which will, how many of each or in what order; it says nothing about the content of any recording; it is revised, so the edition is part of the citation; and it is written to explain the paper's purpose, not how to answer quickly |
| **2** | **Richard Cauldwell**, *Phonology for Listening: Teaching the Stream of Speech*, Speech in Action, **2013** | A pedagogic account for teachers, with its own terminology, built from close listening to recordings; the three-way division is a **teaching device**, not an experimental finding, and the author does not claim otherwise; it reports no trial, gives no timetable and promises no gain in any score |
| **3** | **Gillian Brown**, *Listening to Spoken English*, Longman, **1977**; second edition **1990** | A description based mainly on one variety — educated southern British English of the period — written before large spoken corpora; the second edition revised it rather than replacing it; **no rule in it is absolute**, since elision varies with speaker, speed and style; and it describes the language rather than teaching it |
| **4** | **Z. S. Bond**, *Slips of the Ear: Errors in the Perception of Casual Conversation*, Academic Press, **1999** | Every slip in it is one somebody **noticed**, so the collection cannot say how often misperception happens; it is English casual conversation of one period, largely by native-speaking listeners; it does not claim to describe what learners mishear; and being observational it establishes no cause |
| **5** | **W. D. Marslen-Wilson and A. Welsh**, *Processing interactions and lexical access during word recognition in continuous speech*, **Cognitive Psychology, 1978** | A laboratory model from shadowing and mispronunciation tasks with a small number of native-speaking participants; **the first author revised the role of context in 1987**; it says nothing about learners, nothing about whether recognition can be trained, and nothing about any examination |
| **6** | **A. D. Baddeley and G. Hitch**, *Working Memory*, in *The Psychology of Learning and Motivation*, vol. 8, **1974** | A model, revised continuously since, with a fourth component — the episodic buffer — added by the first author in **2000**, so four parts cannot be cited to the 1974 paper; the evidence is from digit span and reasoning under load, not from writing answers; capacity figures vary with material and measurement; and it is a model of a system, not a technique |
| **7** | **C. A. Chaudron and J. C. Richards**, *The effect of discourse markers on the comprehension of lectures*, **Applied Linguistics, 1986** | One study, one institution, one group of learners, on lectures prepared for the experiment; the measure was a comprehension and recall test rather than a band; **the effects were real but modest**; and it cannot say what any particular lecturer will do — some never signpost at all |
| **8** | **A. Cutler and D. M. Carter**, *The predominance of strong initial syllables in the English vocabulary*, **Computer Speech and Language, 1987** | It establishes a **count** of a vocabulary at the time of counting; that listeners **use** the pattern is an **inference**, not something this paper demonstrates about any individual; the strategy has a known failure in words like *result* and *agree*; and the paper says nothing about learners and contains no exercise |
| **9** | **R. C. Major, S. F. Fitzmaurice, F. Bunta and C. | **10** | **L. Vandergrift and M. H. Twelve admissions, one per unit, worth reading as a list of their own.

- **Unit 1.** Every student who was surprised by a question type was surprised by something the guide
  never claimed to tell them. And almost every student practices the part they are already best at.
- **Unit 2.** Four thousand words, and four words out of nine from four seconds of real speech. The
  words were never the problem, and nobody finds out until the jungle arrives in Part 3.
- **Unit 3.** The commonest words in English are the ones learners cannot hear, because they learned
  them in their full form and meet them flattened.
- **Unit 4.** A mishearing is real English with the right rhythm, which is why you believe it, and why
  the only check is whether the answer fits the printed words round the gap.
- **Unit 5.** Eleven of nineteen blank gaps were answerable from the printed words alone, with no
  recording at all. The student who found that out called it cheating.
- **Unit 6.** Three blanks in a row is one problem and two casualties, and the repair is to write
  fewer words — which feels careless to everybody who has ever been taught to answer in full
  sentences.
- **Unit 7.** The small joining words are the ones courses teach. The sentences that announce the
  shape are the ones that helped, and they arrive in the first forty seconds, free.
- **Unit 8.** The strategy that finds most word beginnings in English is the strategy that produced
  *sult* on an answer line. It worked perfectly and gave the wrong answer.
- **Unit 9.** Two students, one recording, opposite rankings. With two people it is about two people,
  and a rumor about accents is almost always larger than the evidence behind it.
- **Unit 10.** Thirty-eight practice papers bought two marks. One slow cycle found that three of four
  errors came from the wording of the questions, which no score would ever have said.
- **Unit 11.** Everybody believes their highest score, including the people who know better. A number
  has a width, and no result slip in the world prints it.
- **Unit 12.** Forty marks become one band through a table published as a guide. Count marks.

# A note on everything else in this book

Every person, class, center, university and conversation in this book is invented. Meridian Language
Center does not exist, and neither do the fourteen people in the dialogues. **Every transcript,
extract, question page and answer sheet in this book was written for it**, including every example of
mishearing: the collection described in Unit 4 is real, and the examples printed in our table are our
own.

**Every figure attributed to "our own class" is invented** — the four-second counts in Unit 2, the
weak-form counts in Unit 3, the slip tally in Unit 4, the recognition-point figures in Unit 5, the
delay figures in Unit 6, the four versions in Unit 7, the segmentation figures in Unit 8, the accent
matrix in Unit 9, the term-long split in Unit 10 and the six scores in Unit 11. Each of those figures
says on its own face that it is the class's own, with eleven students, and several of the units stop
to say why eleven students cannot establish anything.

**No question, passage, recording or prompt from any past examination is reproduced anywhere in this
book.**

Nothing in this book is advice about your admission, registration or employment, and nothing in it is
a prediction. A practice score is what you did on one set of questions on one morning.

# Closing

You began this book believing that your Listening score was about how many words you knew. You end it
able to say which part of the paper the marks went in, which of four reasons took each one, whether
the word was flattened or the boundary was misplaced, what you were doing with your pen at the time,
and how wide the number is that comes out at the end.

Twelve sources, and one habit: after every wrong answer, ask **why**, and keep asking until the answer
is something you could change on a Tuesday.

Five sentences to take with you, one from each part of the book.

From Unit 2: **which kind of speech was that?**

From Unit 4: **is what I wrote a phrase that fits?**

From Unit 6: **how many words does this gap actually want?**

From Unit 7: **what did the first forty seconds tell me?**

From Unit 11: **what is the width of this number?**
