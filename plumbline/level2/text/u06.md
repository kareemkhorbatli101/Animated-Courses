# Unit 6 · Holding It While You Write
%%SUB A model from 1974, a loop that holds speech for about two seconds, and a fourth part added twenty-six years later%%
%%GOALS

The recording does not wait while you write. Between hearing an answer and getting it onto the
paper there is a gap of two or three seconds, and a model published in 1974 explains why that gap is
so expensive.

%%FIG qb06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the chapter, the volume and the year, a three-row table of the components, a short description of the digit-load experiment, a boxed paragraph headed a model, revised for twenty-six years, a list of four limits, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: the two seconds between hearing and writing**
> **Source: A. D. Baddeley and G. Hitch, *Working Memory*, in *The Psychology of Learning and
> Motivation*, volume 8, **1974**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Before this paper, the standard picture was simple: one short-term store, holding a few items for a
> short time. The authors proposed something else — a **working memory** with separate parts that do
> different jobs.
>
> A **central executive** decides where attention goes. A **phonological loop** holds speech-based
> material: words, numbers, names, anything you could say. A **visuo-spatial sketchpad** holds shapes
> and positions.
>
> The evidence that forced the change came from asking people to do two things at once. If there were
> one single store, then filling it with digits should wreck any other task that needed it. It did
> not. Holding a string of digits made reasoning slower and a little less accurate, but people could
> still reason — far better than a single-store account predicted. So there had to be more than one
> part, and they had to be able to work at the same time.
>
> The part that matters in a Listening paper is the **loop**. It holds speech-based material for a
> short time — a couple of seconds — and then it fades, unless you **rehearse** it by saying it over
> to yourself. And here is the problem. Writing an answer is also a speech-based task. While your hand
> is moving and your mind is spelling, you are using the same loop you were using to hold the next
> thing, and the next thing is arriving now.
>
> This is why **three blanks in a row** is the commonest shape of loss in a Listening paper, and why
> it is not a vocabulary problem and never was. You lost the answer after the one you were writing.
>
> **A MODEL, REVISED FOR TWENTY-SIX YEARS**
> This is a **model**, not a measurement, and it has been revised continuously since. A fourth
> component — the **episodic buffer** — was added by the first author in **2000**. Anybody who cites
> four parts to the 1974 paper is citing something that is not in it.
>
> The evidence is from **laboratory tasks**: digit span, reasoning under load, word lists. Nobody was
> writing answers on an answer sheet. Figures for how much the loop holds vary with the material and
> with how you measure, so a number like *two seconds* is an order of magnitude and not a
> specification.
>
> And it is a model of a system, not a technique. It explains why short answers survive and long ones
> do not. It does not tell you how to write short, and it offers no advice about any examination.
>
> *Our summary of a published chapter. The digit-load experiment is the part worth reading.*

Notes are not bad sentences. They are a different form, with the predictable words left out.

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Write the fewest characters the gap will accept, and move.** The answer you write is not a
> sentence and nobody reads it for style. If the gap wants a place, write the place; if it wants a
> date, write it in figures. The seconds you save are not saved for you — they are spent holding the
> answer that is arriving now. That is the whole of this unit, and it is worth two or three marks a
> paper to nearly everybody.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In many school systems a short answer looks lazy. Full sentences are rewarded, and students learn
> that more writing means more care. A Listening answer sheet is the opposite: it is a list of
> machine-checkable items, nobody reads it for style, and every extra word is paid for out of the
> next question. The habit of writing fully is a good habit in the wrong room. **Ask your group: were
> you taught to answer in full sentences at school? Why do you think a one-word answer looked
> lazy?**
