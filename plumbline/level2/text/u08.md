# Unit 8 · Where the Stress Falls
%%SUB A count published in 1987, a strategy inferred from it, and the one place that strategy fails%%
%%GOALS
Say where most English content words put their stress
Use a strong syllable as a clue to where a word begins
Stress two-syllable nouns and verbs correctly
Move the stress correctly inside a word family
Say why a statistic about a vocabulary is not a fact about a listener
%%

Somebody counted the syllables of English — in a dictionary and in recordings of real speech — and
found that the words that carry meaning nearly all start loud. That one statistic gives a listener a
way of finding the beginnings of words. It also gives them a reliable way of being wrong.

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: why loud syllables are useful**
> **Source: A. Cutler and D. M. Carter, *The predominance of strong initial syllables in the English
> vocabulary*, Computer Speech and Language, **1987**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper does something very simple and very useful. It **counts**. It takes the English
> vocabulary — from a dictionary, and then from a corpus of real spoken English — and asks where the
> stress falls in each word.
>
> The answer is lopsided. The large majority of English **content words** — nouns, verbs, adjectives,
> adverbs — begin with a **strong** syllable: one with a full vowel, said with some force. And in
> **running speech** the proportion is higher still, because the words people actually say most are
> short, and short content words are stressed.
>
> Now put that next to the problem from Units 2 and 3. Speech has no gaps. A listener has to decide
> where words begin. If most content words begin loud, then a listener can use a simple working rule:
> **treat a strong syllable as the likely start of a new content word.** The authors and others call
> this a metrical segmentation strategy, and English gives a listener very good odds on it.
>
> This explains something you have already noticed. In fast speech you hear the loud syllables and
> almost nothing between them — and that is not a failure, it is the strategy working. The loud parts
> are where the words are. The quiet parts are the grammar, and you reconstruct them from what you
> know.
>
> **A COUNT, AN INFERENCE, AND A KNOWN FAILURE**
> What the paper establishes is a **count** of a vocabulary. It is a statistic about **English words**
> at the time of counting, in that dictionary and that corpus.
>
> That listeners **use** the pattern is an **inference** — a reasonable one, supported by other
> experimental work, and not something this paper demonstrates about any individual. A statistic about
> a vocabulary is not a fact about an ear.
>
> And the strategy has a **known failure**. Plenty of English content words do not begin strong —
> *beGIN*, *aGREE*, *reSULT*, *aLLOW* — and when a listener cuts in front of one of those strong
> syllables, they cut a word in half and get two words that are both real English. That is a boundary
> slip, and it is the commonest kind there is.
>
> The paper also says nothing about **learners**: the counting is of the language, not of anybody's
> listening, and there is no training exercise in it.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper. The counting is the part to look at, and it is readable.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Languages differ in where they put stress and in whether they put it anywhere at all. Some fix it
> on the first syllable of every word, some on the last, some on the one before the last, and some
> give every syllable the same weight. A listener trained on a fixed-stress language expects stress
> to mean *word begins here*, and in English that expectation is right most of the time — which is
> exactly what makes the exceptions so expensive. **Ask your group: where does the stress go in your
> first language? Is it fixed or does it move?**
