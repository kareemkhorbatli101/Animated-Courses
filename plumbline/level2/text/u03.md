%%SUB Weak forms, elision, and a description published in 1977 for teachers whose students could not follow real speech%%
%%GOALS

Six of the commonest words in English are almost never said in full. They are not missing from the
recording. They are there, flattened, in the space between the words you did hear.

%%FIG qb03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher, the year and the second edition date, a six-row table of words with their strong and weak forms, three short examples of elision with the dropped sound struck through, a boxed paragraph headed what a description can and cannot do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Listening class**
> **Subject: the words that are there and not there**
> **Source: Gillian Brown, *Listening to Spoken English*, Longman, **1977**; second edition
> **1990**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> This book was written for teachers whose students had learned English carefully and could not
> follow anybody speaking it. Its answer is a description — detailed, patient and unwelcome — of what
> actually happens to English when people talk.
>
> **English has a rhythm.** Stressed syllables arrive at fairly regular intervals, and everything
> between them is squeezed to fit. The words that get squeezed are the **function words**: articles,
> prepositions, auxiliaries, conjunctions, pronouns. They carry grammar, not news, so they lose their
> full vowel and take a short flat one instead.
>
> That short flat form is the **weak form**. *To* becomes a flat vowel. *Of* can shrink to almost a
> single consonant. *And* is often just an *n*. *Was*, *for*, *can*, *have*, *that* and *are* all
> have one. A learner who has only ever heard the strong forms has never heard most of the grammar of
> the language.
>
> Two further things happen. **Elision** is a sound disappearing altogether: the *t* in *next week*,
> the *d* in *old man*, a whole unstressed vowel in the middle of a word. **Assimilation** is a sound
> changing to fit the one beside it, so that *ten bikes* starts to sound like *tem bikes*. Neither is
> carelessness. Both happen in the speech of careful, educated people, at normal speed, every day.
>
> The practical consequence is the one students resist. If a function word has been flattened, you
> very often **cannot hear which one it was**. You recover it from the rest of the sentence: from
> what follows it, from what the speaker is doing, from grammar you already know. Good listeners are
> not people who hear every sound. They are people who need fewer of them.
>
> **WHAT A DESCRIPTION CAN AND CANNOT DO**
> This is a **description**, built mainly on one variety — educated southern British English of the
> period — and written before large spoken corpora and before recording was easy. The second edition
> revised it; it did not replace it. Other varieties of English do some of this differently, and the
> book does not claim to cover them.
>
> **No rule here is absolute.** How much is elided depends on the speaker, the speed and the style.
> The same person says *next week* with and without the *t* on the same afternoon. The book gives you
> what usually happens, not what always does, and a learner who turns it into a rule will mishear
> confidently instead of uncertainly.
>
> And it is a description of the language, not a method for teaching it. It tells you what you are
> failing to hear. It does not tell you how long it will take to hear it.
>
> *Our summary of a published book. The center's copy is on the shelf; the chapters on weak forms
> are the ones to read.*

### 1 · The articles you could not hear

When the article is flattened, the sentence tells you which one it was.

| | |
|---|---|

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Write the word the sentence needs.** When you could not hear whether it was *a* or *the*, *was* or

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Many languages give every syllable roughly the same length, and speakers of those languages hear
> English as a language that eats its own words. English speakers learning those languages have the
> opposite complaint: everything is too even to find the edges. Neither language is lazy; they are
> organized differently, and the organization is what you have to learn rather than the words. **Ask
> your group: in your first language, are the syllables even or uneven? What would an English speaker
> find hard about it?**
