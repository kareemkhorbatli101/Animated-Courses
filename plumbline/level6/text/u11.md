# Unit 11 · What a Score Must Show
%%SUB A set of professional standards from 2014 about what evidence a score needs before it is used%%
%%GOALS

A number was written on your performance and somebody will make a decision with it. A set of
standards published in 2014 says what evidence has to exist before that is legitimate — and the
answer is not about the test alone. It is about the test, the interpretation, and the use, and all
three have to be named.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the three publishing organizations, the volume and the year, a five-row table of the evidence sources, a three-box diagram of test, interpretation and use, a boxed paragraph headed standards, not law, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: what has to be true before a score is used to decide something**
> **Source: *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*, published jointly by the American
> Educational Research Association, the American Psychological Association and the National Council on
> Measurement in Education, 2014. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The document's central move is a reframing that takes a moment to absorb. **Validity is not a
> property of a test.** It is the degree to which evidence and theory support the **interpretations of
> test scores for proposed uses**. A test is not valid; an interpretation, for a particular use, is
> more or less well supported.
>
> That means two things have to be stated before validity can even be discussed: what the score is
> taken to **mean**, and what it will be **used for**.
>
> Five kinds of evidence are set out as bearing on that.
>
> | The source | What it examines |
> |---|---|
> | **test content** | whether the tasks represent the domain being measured |
> | **response processes** | what candidates actually do when they respond, as against what the test assumes |
> | **internal structure** | whether the parts of the test relate to each other as the construct predicts |
> | **relations to other variables** | whether scores relate to other measures, and to later performance, as expected |
> | **consequences of testing** | what follows from using the test, intended and unintended |
>
> Two further commitments run through the document. **Fairness is treated as a fundamental validity
> issue** rather than a separate topic: a score interpretation that holds for one group and not another
> is not a fair test with a validity problem, it is a validity problem. And the responsibility is
> shared: the standards address **test users** as well as test developers, and a user who applies a
> score to a decision the evidence does not support is the one at fault.
>
> For you there are two consequences, and they cut in opposite directions.
>
> **Your score is not a measurement of your English.** It is evidence supporting certain
> interpretations for certain uses, and the owners of the test publish what those are. *My English is a
> 7.0* is a sentence about a number, not about a person.
>
> **And the people using it carry part of the responsibility.** A university that sets 7.0 rather than
> 6.5 for a course has made a decision that, on these standards, it ought to be able to justify with
> evidence about that course. Whether it can is a fair question to ask, and most candidates never
> think to.
>
> **STANDARDS, NOT LAW**
> This is a set of **professional standards**. It has **no enforcement mechanism of its own**: it
> describes what competent practice looks like, and a test publisher or a university may ignore it with
> no direct consequence. Where it has force, that force comes from elsewhere — professional
> expectation, contracts, or the legal systems of particular countries.
>
> It is produced by **three organizations in one country**, and it reflects the measurement tradition
> and the legal context of that country. Testing practice elsewhere is not obliged to follow it and
> often differs.
>
> It is a **consensus document**, which means it is the product of committees and compromise. Where
> the field disagrees, the standards tend to be worded in a way that accommodates the disagreement
> rather than settling it.
>
> It is also an **edition**. The 2014 version superseded earlier ones, differs from them in substance,
> and will itself be superseded; quoting it as timeless would be wrong.
>
> And it **evaluates no particular test**. Nothing in it is about this examination, about any band, or
> about you. The questions it suggests you might ask are ours.
>
> *Our summary of a published set of standards, named with its three publishing organizations and its
> year.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Thresholds are set by institutions and almost never explained. In some countries an applicant may
