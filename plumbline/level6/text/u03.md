# Unit 3 · The Word That Goes With It
%%SUB A book from 1991 arguing that most of what a fluent speaker says is not assembled word by word%%
%%GOALS

*Heavy rain. Strong wind. A big mistake.* Every one of those adjectives is interchangeable in the
grammar and not in the language. A book published in 1991 argued that this is not a detail at the
edge of English but most of it — and that the reason your correct sentences sometimes sound wrong is
that you are building what fluent speakers retrieve.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a two-row table of the principles, a short concordance of five lines with one word centered, a boxed paragraph headed an argument from concordance lines, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: what fluent speakers retrieve instead of building**
> **Source: John Sinclair, *Corpus, Concordance, Collocation*, Oxford University Press, 1991.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book comes out of a dictionary project. Its author spent years reading **concordances** — every
> occurrence of a word in a large corpus, lined up so that the word sits in a column with its
> surroundings on either side — and the book reports what he concluded from doing so.
>
> The central argument sets two principles against each other.
>
> | The principle | What it says |
> |---|---|
> | the **open-choice** principle | a text is a series of slots, and at each slot any word the grammar allows may occur. This is how grammars describe language and how most teaching treats it. |
> | the **idiom** principle | a speaker has available a very large number of **semi-preconstructed phrases**, and normal text is largely built out of them rather than word by word |
>
> The author's claim is that the **idiom principle accounts for more of normal text than the
> open-choice principle does**, and that the open-choice model survives because it is what grammars
> are able to describe.
>
> Two consequences are worth taking.
>
> **Collocation is not decoration.** *Heavy rain*, *strong wind*, *a big mistake*: the adjectives are
> interchangeable in the grammar and not in the language, and a speaker who chooses by meaning rather
> than by habit will be grammatical and audibly foreign. The book's position is that this happens
> because the phrase, not the word, is the unit being stored.
>
> **A word's meaning is partly its company.** Several words the author examines turn out to occur
> overwhelmingly in a particular kind of context — some almost always with something unwelcome, others
> almost always with a quantity. The meaning is not in the dictionary entry; it is in what the word
> habitually appears beside.
>
> For a speaker at your level the practical consequence is uncomfortable. Widening your range by
> collecting single words produces exactly the error this unit began with. Widening it by collecting
> **phrases** — adjective and noun, verb and noun, whole frames like *the question is whether* —
> produces something that sounds like English, because it is what English speakers have stored.
>
> **AN ARGUMENT FROM CONCORDANCE LINES**
> This is an **argument**, built from one lexicographer's reading of concordance lines in **one corpus
> of its period**. It reports **no experiment**: nobody's speech was tested, no learners were measured,
> and no psychological claim about storage was demonstrated.
>
> The central claim is also **not quantified**. *More of normal text than the open-choice principle*
> is a judgment about proportion with no proportion attached, and the book does not offer one.
>
> Parts of it remain **contested**. The idea that words carry a habitual flavor from their company has
> been accepted by many and disputed by others, partly because identifying one requires a human reading
> of the lines rather than a count.
>
> It is also largely about **written text**, and the author was arguing for a **method of making
> dictionaries**, which he says. Nothing in it is about examinations, about speaking under time
> pressure, or about how a learner should build a repertoire.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year. The advice about collecting
> phrases is ours.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Collocations do not translate, and that is the whole difficulty. Every language has pairs that are
> fixed for no reason anybody can give, and a speaker moving between two languages will reach for the
> pattern of the first. There is nothing wrong with the reasoning; the pairing simply is not
> transferable. **Ask your group: name one collocation in your language that makes no sense if you
> translate it word by word.**
