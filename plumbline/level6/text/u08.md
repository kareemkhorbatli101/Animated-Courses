### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a two-column table of core and non-core features, one sentence written three times with different stress, a boxed paragraph headed a proposal, and not what you are assessed against, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: which features of pronunciation carry meaning**
> **Source: Jennifer Jenkins, *The Phonology of English as an International Language*, Oxford
> University Press, 2000. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book starts from a situation that had been largely ignored in pronunciation teaching: most
> conversations in English now take place between people for whom it is not a first language. The
> author recorded such interactions, examined the points at which understanding actually broke down,
> and used them to argue for a reordering of teaching priorities.
>
> What she proposes is a **core**: the features whose absence caused real breakdowns, as against the
> features learners are corrected on which did not.
>
> | In the proposed core | Not in it |
> |---|---|
> | most **consonant sounds** | the two *th* sounds |
> | **consonant clusters**, especially word-initially | the dark *l* |
> | the **length** distinction in vowels | vowel **quality** beyond length |
> | **nuclear stress placement** | word stress, weak forms, assimilation, elision, pitch movement beyond nuclear stress |
>
> The item the author treats as most important is the last one in the left column: **where the main
> stress of the sentence falls**. Moving it changes what the sentence says.
>
> > *I didn't **say** he stole the money.* — somebody else said it
> > *I didn't say **he** stole the money.* — somebody else stole it
> > *I didn't say he **stole** the money.* — he borrowed it
>
> Nine words, three meanings, one difference. For a candidate this is the single most useful thing in
> the unit, and it is in the public descriptor: the Pronunciation criterion asks for **features of
> pronunciation used to convey meaning**, and nuclear stress is what that phrase is about.
>
> The non-core list is the part that provokes arguments, and it is worth being exact about what it
> does and does not say. It does **not** say the *th* sounds are bad English or that nobody should
> learn them. It says that in the recorded interactions, substituting another sound for them did not
> cause breakdowns, so a teacher with limited time has better things to spend it on.
>
> **A PROPOSAL, AND NOT WHAT YOU ARE ASSESSED AGAINST**
> This is explicitly a **proposal**. The author calls the core provisional and revisable, states that
> it rests on her own data set, and invites correction. It is not a standard and it is not a finding.
>
> The data set was **limited**: interactions she collected, in particular settings, between speakers of
> particular first languages. A different set of speakers might break down at different points.
>
> The proposal has been **argued about ever since**, and parts of it vigorously. Leaving word stress
> out of the core is disputed; so is the treatment of the *th* sounds; so is the whole question of
> whether a core can be specified at all independently of who is listening.
>
> And the part that matters most to you: **this is not the standard you are assessed against.** The
> Speaking test's Pronunciation criterion is applied by a trained examiner using the published
> descriptors, and nothing in this book changes them. What the book gives you is a reason to spend
> your remaining practice time on nuclear stress rather than on the *th* sounds — a judgment about
> **priority**, made by us, on the strength of a proposal that was never about examinations.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year.*
