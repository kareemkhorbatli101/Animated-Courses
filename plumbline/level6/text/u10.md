Your remaining errors are not random. At your level there are three or four of them, they come back
in every test, and they obey rules — your rules, not English's. A short paper published in 1972
proposed that this is what a learner's language always is, and the proposal is the most useful
diagnostic tool in this book.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the paper, the journal and the year, a five-row table of the processes, a short note on fossilization, a boxed paragraph headed a proposed construct, and a disputed one, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: what your remaining errors are evidence of**
> **Source: Larry Selinker, *Interlanguage*, International Review of Applied Linguistics in Language
> Teaching, 1972. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper proposes a way of looking at learner language that has shaped the field since. A learner's
> utterances, it argues, should not be treated as a **defective version** of the target language. They
> are the output of a **separate linguistic system** with its own rules — the author's word for it is
> an **interlanguage** — produced by the learner's attempt to express meaning in a language they are
> still acquiring.
>
> The practical consequence is that learner errors are **systematic**: they come from rules, and the
> rules can be stated.
>
> Five processes are proposed as shaping that system.
>
> | The process | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | **language transfer** | a pattern carried over from the first language |
> | **transfer of training** | a pattern produced by the way the learner was taught |
> | **strategies of second-language learning** | the learner's own approaches to acquiring the language |
> | **strategies of second-language communication** | the learner's own approaches to getting a message across |
> | **overgeneralization of target-language material** | applying a rule of the new language beyond where it applies |
>
> The paper's other famous term is **fossilization**: the observation that certain items, rules and
> patterns tend to **stay** in a learner's interlanguage however much instruction, explanation or time
> is applied, and that which items these are often depends on the learner's first language.
>
> For you, the useful half of this is diagnostic and immediate. At band 7 you are not making many
> errors; you are making **three or four errors repeatedly**. Each of them is a rule. Find the rule,
> state it in a sentence, and you have something you can work on — which is not true of *be more
> accurate*.
>
> It also reframes something worth reframing. An error that recurs is not evidence that you are
> careless. It is evidence that you have a grammar, and that one of its rules differs from English's
> at one point. That is a more useful thing to be told, and a truer one.
>
> **A PROPOSED CONSTRUCT, AND A DISPUTED ONE**
> This is a **short theoretical paper** proposing a construct. It reports **no study**: no learners
> were measured, no hypothesis was tested, and the five processes are offered as an account rather
> than established as a list.
>
> **Fossilization has been argued about ever since**, and the arguments are serious. How long does a
> feature have to persist before it counts as fossilized rather than merely stabilized? If the answer
> is *permanently*, how would anybody ever show it, given that a learner might change next year? Some
> writers have held that the term as it stands cannot be tested at all, and others have replaced it
> with *stabilization*, which claims less.
>
> That matters to you directly. **Nobody can tell you which of your errors is permanent**, and anybody
> who does is claiming more than the research supports. The honest statement is that some features are
> very resistant, that resistance varies by first language, and that which of yours is which is not
> something this paper or this book knows.
>
> The five processes also **overlap**, and attributing a particular error to one rather than another is
> usually a judgment. And the paper is of its **period**: it comes near the start of the field it
> helped to create, and a great deal has been proposed since.
>
> And it says nothing about **assessment**. Nothing in it is about bands, examinations, or what a
> marker does with a repeated error.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its journal and its year.*
