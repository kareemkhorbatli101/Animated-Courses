Somebody has told you to reduce your accent. A paper published in 2005 separates accent from two
other things and reports that they come apart — a speaker can sound strongly foreign and be
effortless to understand. Which of the three the examination measures is a question with an answer,
and it is printed in the descriptor.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a three-row table of the dimensions with how each is measured, a short note on listener factors, a boxed paragraph headed a position paper, and operationalized constructs, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: three things that are not the same thing**
> **Source: Tracey M. Derwing and Murray J. Munro, *Second Language Accent and Pronunciation Teaching:
> A Research-Based Approach*, TESOL Quarterly, 2005. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper argues that pronunciation teaching has long been aimed at the wrong target, and it begins
> by separating three things.
>
> | The dimension | What it is | How it is usually measured |
> |---|---|---|
> | **accentedness** | how different a speaker's speech sounds from a local variety | a listener rating on a scale |
> | **comprehensibility** | how much effort a listener has to make | a listener rating on a scale |
> | **intelligibility** | how much the listener actually understood | transcription accuracy, or comprehension questions |
>
> The authors' central empirical point is that these are **partially independent**. They are related —
> a very heavy accent and perfect intelligibility is not the usual case — but the relation is loose
> enough that the three come apart routinely. **A speaker can be strongly accented and highly
> intelligible**, and in the authors' data many are.
>
> The conclusion they draw is about priorities. If the three are distinct, then **accent reduction is
> the wrong goal**: it targets the dimension that matters least for communication and the one least
> likely to change in an adult. What can be improved is comprehensibility and intelligibility, and the
> features that help there are not always the ones a learner has been corrected on.
>
> The paper also insists on something easy to forget: **the listener is half of it.** How familiar a
> listener is with a given accent, how much effort they are willing to make, and what they expect all
> affect the ratings. Comprehensibility is not a property of a speaker; it is a property of an
> encounter.
>
> For a candidate the application is precise and worth being exact about. The Speaking test's
> Pronunciation criterion asks whether you can be **understood with little effort** and whether you
> use features of pronunciation to **convey meaning**. That is comprehensibility and, in part, the
> nuclear stress of Unit 8. **It does not mention accent.** So the two years Unit 8's student spent on
> a single sound were aimed at a dimension the descriptor does not describe.
>
> There is also a listener fact in your favor and worth knowing: your listener is one trained examiner
> who has heard a very large number of accents. On the authors' own account, listener familiarity
> raises comprehensibility ratings — which is a reason to stop worrying about sounding foreign and a
> reason to keep worrying about being followed.
>
> **A POSITION PAPER, AND OPERATIONALIZED CONSTRUCTS**
> This is a **research-based position paper**: it argues for a change in teaching practice, drawing on
> the authors' own studies and others'. It is not itself a single experiment.
>
> The three dimensions are **constructs with chosen measures**, and the measures shape the findings.
> Intelligibility counted as transcription accuracy is not the same thing as intelligibility counted
> as answering comprehension questions, and comprehensibility is a **rating** — a listener's report of
> their own effort, which is not the same as their effort.
>
> The studies behind it were conducted with **particular first-language groups** and **particular
> listeners**, largely in one region. A different set of listeners would produce different numbers,
> which is a point the authors themselves make.
>
> And it is about **pronunciation teaching**, not about examinations. The link to the Pronunciation
> criterion is **ours**, drawn from reading the descriptor; the paper says nothing about this test.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its journal and its year.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Stop working on your accent and start working on being followed.** The criterion does not mention
> accent and your examiner has heard hundreds. What it mentions is effort and conveying meaning —
> which means nuclear stress, vowel length, initial clusters and keeping your runs long enough to
> follow. Those are learnable in weeks. An accent is not, and it was never the thing being measured.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Accent carries social meaning everywhere, and that is why *accent reduction* has been a commercial
> industry for decades. People are judged on accent within their own languages as much as across them,
