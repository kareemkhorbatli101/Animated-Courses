%%FIG qf06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a four-row table of the stages, a four-part diagram of a repair, a boxed paragraph headed a model of speaking a first language, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: the stages between meaning something and saying it**
> **Source: Willem J. M. Levelt, *Speaking: From Intention to Articulation*, MIT Press, 1989. Printed
> here in our own words.**
>
> The book sets out a model of what happens between having something to say and saying it. Its stages
> are these.
>
> | The stage | What it produces |
> |---|---|
> | **conceptualizing** | a **preverbal message**: the content, organized, but not yet in any words |
> | **grammatical encoding** | a structure, with words selected for their meaning and grammar |
> | **phonological encoding** | a plan for the sound of the thing |
> | **articulating** | overt speech |
>
> Two features of the model are directly useful to you.
>
> **The mental lexicon has two parts for every word.** A **lemma** carries the word's meaning and its
> grammatical behavior; a **word form** carries its sounds and its endings. They are retrieved
> separately, which is the explanation for a state everybody has been in: you have the lemma and not
> the form. You know what you mean, you know the word takes an object, you may even know how many
> syllables it has — and the sounds will not come. On this account that is not a memory failure but a
> retrieval that got half way.
>
> That has a practical consequence. In that state you still have the meaning, so **paraphrase is
> available** when the word is not. A speaker who waits for the word loses four seconds; a speaker who
> says what the word means keeps the run going and often retrieves the word a clause later.
>
> **Speakers monitor their own speech.** The model includes a loop by which a speaker hears their own
> output — and also inspects the plan before it is spoken — and may interrupt. The book's analysis of
> self-repairs in recorded speech finds that a repair has a structure:
>
> > **the original utterance** → **the interruption** → **an editing term** → **the repair**
>
> The third element is the one candidates leave out. *It was in 2018 — sorry, 2019.* The word *sorry*
> is doing real work: it marks the break as deliberate. Without it, the same correction is a stumble.
> The book also describes a tendency for speakers to interrupt promptly once trouble is detected
> rather than finishing the faulty stretch.
>
> **A MODEL OF SPEAKING A FIRST LANGUAGE**
> This is a model of **adult first-language production**, built from experimental psycholinguistics
> and from corpora of self-repairs collected in the author's own research tradition and period.
>
> **It is not about second-language speaking**, and the difference is not small: a second-language
> speaker has two lexicons, and the question of how they interact is one this book does not take up. A
> great deal of later research exists on exactly that, and it does not all agree.
>
> The **architecture has been argued about**. How separate the stages really are, whether they run
> strictly in order, and how much later stages can influence earlier ones have all been contested, and
> the author himself revised parts of the account in later work.
>
> And it is **not a method**. Nothing in it tells a learner what to do; the advice about paraphrase and
> editing terms is ours, drawn from the model rather than stated in it.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **One syllable between the break and the correction.** That is the whole technique, and it changes
> how a repair is heard. *Sorry*, *or rather*, *I mean*, *well*: any of them turns a stumble into
> editing, and the descriptors at the top bands describe self-correction as something a competent
> speaker does rather than something a weak one betrays. And when the word will not come, describe it
> and keep going — four seconds of silence inside a clause costs more than any paraphrase.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> What a speaker does when a word will not come differs by language and by setting. In some, a long
