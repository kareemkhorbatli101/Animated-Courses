In Book 3 of this series you were taught a list of five hundred and seventy academic word families,
published in 2000. This unit is built on a 2014 paper that argues that list was built the wrong way.

based on a method its field has moved past.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the journal and the year, a four-row table of selection criteria, a two-column comparison of the 2000 list and the 2014 list, a boxed paragraph headed one corpus, one variety, one period, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: which words academic English actually uses**
> **Source: Dee Gardner and Mark Davies, *A New Academic Vocabulary List*, Applied Linguistics, 2014.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper builds a list of academic vocabulary from the academic section of a very large corpus of
> American English — around a hundred and twenty million words, drawn from nine broad disciplines. The
> resulting list is about three thousand items, and the authors report that it accounts for roughly
> fourteen percent of the words in academic texts.
>
> Four criteria decided what got in.
>
> | The criterion | What it required |
> |---|---|
> | **ratio** | much more frequent in academic than in non-academic text |
> | **range** | occurring across the disciplines rather than in one |
> | **dispersion** | spread evenly rather than clustered in a few texts |
> | **discipline measure** | no single discipline accounting for most occurrences |
>
> The paper is also, explicitly, an argument against the list that preceded it — the one this series
> taught in **Book 3, Unit 5**. Two objections matter here.
>
> **It excluded the commonest two thousand words.** The earlier list set aside the most frequent words
> of general English on the assumption that learners already knew them, and listed only what remained.
> The authors object that many of those common words have distinctively academic **senses** — *figure*,
> *process*, *value*, *rate* — and that leaving them out hides the words a reader most needs.
>
> **It counted word families rather than lemmas.** A word family treats *analyze*, *analysis*,
> *analytical*, *analytically* and *analyst* as one item. The authors argue for the **lemma** instead:
> a headword with its inflections, but a different item for each part of speech. Their reason is that
> knowing one member of a family does not give you the others, and that the grammatical behavior of
> *analysis* and *analytical* is completely different.
>
> For a speaker at this level that second objection is the practical one. You will have met a word in
> one form, be confident about it, and then produce the wrong part of speech under pressure. *The
> analytical of the data* is a band-7 error made by somebody with a band-8 vocabulary.
>
> **ONE CORPUS, ONE VARIETY, ONE PERIOD**
> The list is built from **one corpus** of **American English**, assembled over a particular span of
> years. A list built from British academic writing, or from a different period, would not be
> identical.
>
> The **coverage figure depends on what it is measured against**. Fourteen percent is fourteen per
> cent of the texts in that corpus; against a different collection the number moves, and a coverage
> figure quoted without its corpus means nothing.
>
> The lemma decisions also involve **judgment**. Deciding that a form is a noun here and a verb there
> is a coding choice, and the authors describe the rules they used rather than claiming the rules were
> the only possible ones.
>
> And the authors are clear that this is **a list, not a syllabus**. It is ordered by statistical
> prominence, not by usefulness to a learner, not by difficulty, and not by any order in which
> anybody should study it. Nothing in it is about speaking, about examinations, or about how to widen
> a range in fourteen minutes.
>
> **One further thing, which is about this series rather than the paper.** Book 3 of this course
> taught you the earlier list. We think it was worth teaching, because it is the list most published
> material still uses and because its own author stated its limits. But it is fair to say that you
> were taught a method this paper argues against, and you should know that.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its journal and its year.*
