# Unit 4 · Why the Commonest Words Are Short
%%SUB An observation from 1949 about a trade-off between the speaker's convenience and the listener's%%
%%GOALS
Say why a few words cover most of what you say
Choose the specific word over the general one
Say what the speaker's economy costs the listener
Replace a general word with three specific ones
Say what a regularity is when its explanation is disputed
%%

Your ten commonest words account for a quarter of everything you say, and all of them are short. A
book published in 1949 described the regularity and proposed an explanation: you and your listener
want opposite things, and your vocabulary is the compromise. In an examination, the listener wins —
and you are still speaking as if you were the one being accommodated.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a table of the first ten ranks with their frequencies falling sharply, a two-row table of the economies, a boxed paragraph headed a robust regularity with a disputed explanation, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: why your ten commonest words are the shortest ones you know**
> **Source: George Kingsley Zipf, *Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort*, Addison-Wesley,
> 1949. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book reports a regularity in vocabulary that had been noticed before it and is now named after
> its author. If you count every word in a large body of text and rank them by frequency, the
> frequencies fall away in a very particular shape: the commonest word occurs roughly twice as often
> as the second, three times as often as the third, and so on down a very long tail of words that
> occur once each.
>
> A second regularity goes with it: **more frequent words tend to be shorter**. The words you use most
> are the ones that cost least to say.
>
> The explanation the author proposes is the **principle of least effort**, applied to a conflict
> between two parties.
>
> | Whose economy | What it wants |
> |---|---|
> | the **speaker's** | one word covering as many meanings as possible — ideally a single word meaning everything |
> | the **auditor's** | one word per meaning, so that nothing has to be worked out from context |
>
> Neither gets what it wants. The vocabulary a language settles on is, on this account, the
> **compromise**: a small number of very general, very short, very frequent words, and a very large
> number of specific, longer, rare ones.
>
> For a speaker at your level the application is direct and unwelcome. **Everything that makes speech
> easy for you makes it less informative for your listener.** *Thing*, *good*, *do*, *get*, *a lot
> of*: these are the speaker's economy winning, and in a fourteen-minute test the listener is a marker
> with a criterion about range and precision. The trade-off is real, you have been making it in your
> own favor, and the half band is on the other side.
>
> The practical instruction is therefore simple and effortful. Three times in every answer, take the
> general word you were about to say and spend the extra syllables.
>
> **A ROBUST REGULARITY WITH A DISPUTED EXPLANATION**
> The frequency pattern itself is about as well established as anything in the study of language: it
> turns up in corpus after corpus, in language after language.
>
> **The explanation is not.** Many competing accounts have been offered, including purely statistical
> ones in which processes with no speaker and no effort in them produce the same distribution. That
> matters: if a random process can generate the pattern, the pattern on its own is **not evidence** for
> the principle of least effort. The author's account is a **hypothesis**, and this note calls it one.
>
> The book is also famous for **overreach**. Its author applied the same principle to the sizes of
> cities, the distribution of incomes and much else besides, and those extensions have been criticized
> far more severely than the vocabulary observation.
>
> And the law describes **aggregate text**, not a person. It says nothing about what any individual
> says in fourteen minutes, and nothing about examinations. The claim that your general words are
> costing you a band is **ours**, drawn from the descriptors in Unit 1 — not something the 1949 book
> says or could say.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Three replacements an answer, not thirty.** Replacing every general word produces a performance
> that sounds rehearsed and costs you fluency, which is a quarter of the mark. Three is enough to show
> range in every answer, and it leaves you speaking like a person. Pick your three before you start:
> whichever of *thing*, *good*, *do*, *get* and *a lot of* you know you reach for.

> Every language settles the same trade-off and settles it differently, and the shape of the frequency
