# Unit 5 · What Fluent Speakers Do Not Build
%%SUB A paper from 1983 that set two puzzles and answered them with a very large guess%%
%%GOALS
Say what a lexicalized sentence stem is
Say why fluent speech comes in runs
Use a stem rather than assembling the sentence
Say why fluency and novelty pull against each other
Say what a puzzle is, when its authors call it one
%%

A speaker can produce a grammatical sentence nobody has ever said. A speaker can also produce six
clauses in eight seconds without apparently planning them. A paper from 1983 pointed out that these
two facts are hard to hold at once, and proposed an answer that explains most of what you are trying
to do in Part 2.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the authors, the paper, the collection, its editors, the publisher and the year, a two-row table of the puzzles, a transcript with runs bracketed, a boxed paragraph headed two puzzles, and an estimate, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: what a fluent speaker has already got**
> **Source: Andrew Pawley and Frances Hodgetts Syder, *Two puzzles for linguistic theory: nativelike
> selection and nativelike fluency*, in *Language and Communication*, edited by J. C. Richards and R.
> W. Schmidt, Longman, 1983. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The paper sets two problems that any account of speaking has to deal with at once.
>
> | The puzzle | What is puzzling |
> |---|---|
> | **nativelike selection** | the grammar of English permits an enormous number of ways to say any given thing, and a native speaker reliably produces one of the small number that sound natural. *I want to marry you* and *it is my wish to be wedded to you* are both grammatical and only one of them is said. |
> | **nativelike fluency** | speakers produce long, well-formed, connected stretches of speech in real time, and they appear to do it faster than they could be assembling them word by word |
>
> The answer the authors propose covers both. Speakers have memorized an extremely large stock of
> **lexicalized sentence stems**: partly fixed frames, usually about a clause long, with slots in them.
> *The question is whether ___*. *I don't see why ___*. *What strikes me about ___ is ___*. These are
> retrieved whole and filled, not built.
>
> If that is right, both puzzles dissolve. **Selection** is not a choice among all grammatical
> possibilities, because the speaker is choosing among the stems they have. And **fluency** does not
> require planning nine words in sequence, because most of the nine arrive together.
>
> The authors also looked at what fluent speech sounds like when its pauses are marked. Speech falls
> into **runs** of roughly clause length — in their account something like eight to ten words — with
> hesitation between them rather than inside them. Their phrase for the constraint is **one clause at
> a time**.
>
> For a candidate in Part 2 the application is exact. You have one minute and one to two minutes of
> speech to produce, and you cannot plan two minutes of sentences. What you can do is bring four or
> five **stems** to the task and fill them as you go. A speaker working from stems produces runs; a
> speaker building sentences produces hesitation inside the clause, which is the kind the descriptors
> notice.
>
> It also explains something that has probably been frustrating you. **Fluency and novelty pull
> against each other.** Everything you say that you have said before comes out fast and sounds
> natural; everything genuinely new costs planning time and risks sounding assembled. The way out is
> not to speak only in stored phrases — that is band 6 with a wide smile — but to have enough stems
> that the new content can sit in them.
>
> **TWO PUZZLES, AND AN ESTIMATE**
> The authors call these **puzzles**, which is the honest word. The paper sets out two problems and
> offers a hypothesis; it is not a demonstration, and it says so.
>
> The number of stems is an **estimate of their own**, offered as an order of magnitude rather than a
> count. Nobody has enumerated them, and the figure has been quoted since as though it were measured.
> It was not.
>
> The evidence is largely **observational**: transcripts, examples, and the authors' judgments about
> what sounds natural. There is no experiment and no corpus count.
>
> The planning argument also rests on assumptions about **how much a speaker can hold at once** that
> belong to the psychology of its period. That literature has moved, and the argument moves with it.
>
> And it is about **first-language speakers**. Nothing in it is about learners, about examinations, or
> about what a second-language speaker should do with the minute before Part 2 — that last part is
> ours.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its collection, its editors, its publisher and its
> year.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Write stems in the minute, not ideas.** You already have ideas about the topic; what you do not
> have, under pressure, is the frames to carry them. Four or five stems on the card gives you four or
> five clause-length runs you do not have to build, and the content can arrive in the slots. A
> candidate who writes five ideas spends the two minutes building five sentences; a candidate who
> writes five stems spends it filling them.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every language has its own stock of stems, and they are among the last things a learner acquires
> because they are rarely taught and never obvious. A speaker who has mastered the grammar of a
> language can still be audibly foreign for years simply because the frames are missing — and the
