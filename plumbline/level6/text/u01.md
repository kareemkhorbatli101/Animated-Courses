# Unit 1 · Four Criteria, Spoken
%%SUB What the public Speaking descriptors ask, and the word that appears in three of the four%%

Four criteria, equally weighted, and one word runs through three of them: **range**. At band 6 the
thing holding you down is usually accuracy. At band 7 it almost never is. This unit is about the word
that replaces it.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the source and the date it was downloaded, a four-row table of criteria with what each asks, a two-row comparison of band 7 and band 8, a boxed paragraph headed a scale, not a method, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: the word that runs through three of the four criteria**
> **Source: the published Speaking band descriptors and the public statement of how Speaking is
> assessed, from the test owners' own pages. Downloaded 7 June. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Four criteria, **equally weighted**, each worth a quarter of the Speaking score.
>
> | The criterion | What it asks of you |
> |---|---|
> | **Fluency and Coherence** | speaking at length with little noticeable effort; connecting ideas; hesitation that is about content rather than about language |
> | **Lexical Resource** | the range and the precision of the vocabulary, including the ability to paraphrase |
> | **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** | the range of structures used as well as how accurately they are formed |
> | **Pronunciation** | being understood with little effort; using features of pronunciation to convey meaning |
>
> Read the band 7 and band 8 wording next to each other and the pattern is hard to miss. Around band 7
> the descriptors describe somebody who speaks at length with some hesitation, uses a **flexible**
> vocabulary and a **range** of structures with **frequent** error-free sentences. Around band 8 they
> describe somebody who hesitates only **occasionally** and only to find content, whose vocabulary is
> **wide** and used **precisely**, and whose errors are **rare**.
>
> Three observations follow, and none of them is about making fewer mistakes.
>
> **Range is in three of the four.** Vocabulary, grammar and — in substance — pronunciation all ask
> for variety. Only one criterion is about correctness, and correctness is half of that one.
>
> **Hesitation is distinguished by its cause.** Pausing to find an idea is described differently from
> pausing to find a word. A speaker who never pauses because they only ever say things they can
> already say has bought fluency with range, and the descriptors can see the purchase.
>
> **Paraphrase is named.** The ability to say the same thing another way is in the Lexical Resource
> criterion by name, which means that a speaker who reaches for exactly one phrasing per idea is
> missing a named requirement rather than merely sounding repetitive.
>
> **A SCALE, NOT A METHOD**
> The descriptors describe **what each band looks like**; they do not say how to get there. They name
> no vocabulary, prescribe no answers, give no model performance, and say nothing about which topics
> you will be given.
>
> They are also the **public** versions. Fuller descriptors are used in examiner training and are not
> published; this note does not claim to know what is in them, and neither does this book.
>
> And a descriptor is not a diagnosis. *A narrow range* names what a marker heard. It does not say why
> you have one, and the rest of this book is eleven published attempts to answer that.
>
> *Our summary of a published scale. Read the owners' own pages, and write down the date.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> What counts as impressive speech differs by culture. In some, a speaker who hesitates, corrects
> themselves and thinks visibly is being honest; in others, that is a loss of authority, and fluency
> without pause is the mark of competence. This examination's descriptors distinguish hesitation for
> content from hesitation for language and treat the first as acceptable at the highest bands, which is
> a choice of convention. **Ask your group: in your first language, does pausing to think make a
> speaker sound better or worse?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG qf01-f16 | A desk in June. A transcript lies open with one phrase circled eleven times and two errors marked in a different color, beside the two published descriptor pages with the band 8 row circled, a bar chart of five repeated phrases, a stopwatch, and a cup. A clock shows seven.%%

why?
4 If using a riskier phrase produces one more error and three more words, is that a good trade?
