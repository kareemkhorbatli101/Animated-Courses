### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a three-row table of the dimensions, a two-row table of the dual-mode system, a table of task conditions and what each raises, a boxed paragraph headed a framework, and a contested hypothesis, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: why you cannot do all three in one sentence**
> **Source: Peter Skehan, *A Cognitive Approach to Language Learning*, Oxford University Press, 1998.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book's starting point is that a speaker's **attention is limited**. A second-language speaker
> has to do several demanding things at once, and the account treats spoken performance along three
> dimensions.
>
> | The dimension | What it is usually counted as |
> |---|---|
> | **complexity** | clauses per utterance, subordination, variety of structure |
> | **accuracy** | error-free clauses, or errors per hundred words |
> | **fluency** | speech rate, number and placement of pauses, length of run |
>
> The central claim is a **trade-off**: because the three draw on the same limited attention, an
> improvement in one is often accompanied by a decline in another. A speaker reaching for an elaborate
> structure makes more errors; a speaker monitoring for errors slows down; a speaker going fast says
> only what is already available.
>
> Behind this the book proposes a **dual-mode** system.
>
> | The system | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | **rule-based** | applies grammar analytically: flexible, creative, and slow |
> | **exemplar-based** | retrieves stored chunks: fast and ready-made, and limited to what is stored |
>
> Fluent performance leans on the second; novel and precise performance needs the first. Which is why
> Unit 5's stems make you fluent and will not by themselves make you complex.
>
> The pedagogical half of the book is about **task conditions**: what happens to the three dimensions
> when a task is changed.
>
> | The condition | What it tends to raise |
> |---|---|
> | **planning time before speaking** | complexity, and often fluency |
> | **a familiar topic** | fluency |
> | **a task with a clear structure** | accuracy |
> | **pressure to speak immediately** | fluency at the cost of the other two |
>
> For the Speaking test this is unexpectedly practical, because the three parts have different
> conditions built into them.
>
> **Part 1** is familiar topics with no planning. Fluency is cheap here; complexity is expensive. Spend
> the four minutes on **range of vocabulary**, which costs less attention than subordination does.
>
> **Part 2** gives you a minute of planning, and planning time is the one condition the research
> associates with higher complexity. This is where your long sentences belong.
>
> **Part 3** is abstract and unplanned. Protect accuracy and fluency; take whatever complexity arrives
> without being chased.
>
> That distribution is **ours**, not the book's. What the book supplies is the reason it might work.
>
> **A FRAMEWORK, AND A CONTESTED HYPOTHESIS**
> This is a **synthesis and a framework** drawn largely from task-based research of its period, not a
> single study.
>
> The trade-off hypothesis has been **argued against at length**. Later researchers have reported
> conditions under which two dimensions rise together, and have questioned whether attention is a
> single pool that the three draw from at all. A reader should know that this is a live disagreement
> and not a settled result.
>
> The **measures are operationalizations**. *Complexity* counted as clauses per utterance is not the
> same as complexity counted as variety of structure, and studies that count differently are hard to
> compare. A good deal of the apparent disagreement in this literature is disagreement about counting.
>
> And it is about **classroom task design**, not examinations. Nothing in it is about this test, about
> bands, or about what to do in fourteen minutes.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its publisher and its year.*

### 📝 ON THE DAY

> **Put your hardest structures where the planning time is.** Part 1 is four minutes of easy questions
