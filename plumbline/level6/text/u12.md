Eleven units about range, retrieval, attention and measurement. One question left, and it is the
largest: why does this examination exist in this form at all? A history published in 1995 answers it
with administration rather than science, and the answer is the last thing this series has to say.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG qf12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the author, the book, the publisher and the year, a two-column table of the traditions, a list of administrative pressures, a boxed paragraph headed a history, written by a participant, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **MERIDIAN LANGUAGE CENTER — note to the Speaking class**
> **Subject: why the test is shaped the way it is**
> **Source: Bernard Spolsky, *Measured Words: The Development of Objective Language Testing*, Oxford
> University Press, 1995. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> The book is a history. It traces how language testing moved from the oral examination conducted by
> two people who knew the candidate to the modern apparatus of scales, standardized tasks and
> published bands.
>
> Its central argument is uncomfortable and well evidenced: the form language tests took was driven
> as much by **administrative and political pressure** as by any theory of language. The pressures it
> documents include the need to sort very large numbers of applicants, to do so at a cost somebody was
> willing to bear, to produce a result that could be defended when challenged, and to do it on a
> timetable.
>
> Those pressures favor particular designs. A task that can be marked by a machine, or by a lightly
> trained marker against a scale, survives. A task that requires an expert hour per candidate does not,
> however good it is.
>
> The book sets two traditions against each other.
>
> | The tradition | What it values |
> |---|---|
> | the **psychometric** | consistency, efficiency, and scoring that does not depend on who marks |
> | the **integrative or communicative** | tasks that resemble real language use, even when marking them is slower and less reliable |
>
> The author's view is that the first tradition won most of the twentieth century on grounds that were
> practical rather than intellectual, and that claims to scientific objectivity were often claims
> about **scoring procedure** rather than about language.
>
> For this series the argument applies directly, and this is the last unit, so it will be said
> plainly.
>
> **The examination these six books prepare for is a compromise.** Fourteen minutes, three parts, one
> examiner, nine bands: every one of those is a number somebody chose under constraint. A spoken
> interview marked by a trained human against a public scale is a considerable achievement by the
> standards of the history in this book — it belongs partly to the communicative tradition and costs
> far more to run than a multiple-choice paper. It is also fourteen minutes long because forty was not
> affordable.
>
> **And these six books exist because that examination exists.** Book 5's last unit said so about
> itself; the same is true of all six. If the examination had been designed differently, the units
> would be different units, and some of what you have learned here would be of no use to anybody.
>
> What we would say in the series' defense is this. Every unit of all six books is built on a real
> published document, named with its date, with the limits its own authors state printed beside it.
> Most of those documents are not about examinations at all. A reader who finishes these books knows
> some things about reading, writing, argument, graphics, vocabulary and speech that will outlast any
> band — and knows, of each of them, where it came from and what it does not establish. That was the
> design, and it was chosen for a reason that had nothing to do with the test.
>
> **A HISTORY, WRITTEN BY A PARTICIPANT**
> This is **one historian's account**, and its author was an active participant in the field he is
> describing. That gives him access and a position; both are visible in the book.
>
> A history is **selective** by necessity. It rests largely on institutional records and published
> material from particular countries and particular languages, and other archives would support other
> accounts.
>
> And a history explains **how something came to be**, not whether it is right. That the fourteen
> minutes was arrived at administratively does not show that fourteen minutes is the wrong length. The
> argument from origin to judgment is one the book does not make, and nor do we.
>
> It says nothing about **what to do in the fourteen minutes**. That was the other eleven units.
>
> *Our summary of a published history, named with its publisher and its year.*
