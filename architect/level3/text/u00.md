# Starting Point · What a Quality Argument Is Made Of
%%SUB Four things every argument about quality needs, and the one that is always missing%%
%%GOALS
Review the grammar of Books 1 and 2
Say what a quality argument needs
Ask the four questions of this book
%%

Somebody says the system is too slow. Somebody else says it is fine. Both of them are describing
something real and neither of them has said enough to be checked. This book is about the difference.

### 1 · Six structures you already have

%%FIG c00-f1 | A single review sheet with six boxes. Each box has a heading — the present perfect, modals of possibility, relative clauses, the first conditional, reported speech, and linking words — and one short example sentence from a reliability review.%%

**1A  Read the six examples aloud with a partner. Which two do you use most?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **The present perfect** | The service **has been** slow **since** Tuesday. |
| **Modals of possibility** | A cache **may** be holding an older copy. |
| **Relative clauses** | The part **that** we cannot parallelize is the write. |
| **The first conditional** | **If** we add two servers, the queue **will** shorten. |
| **Reported speech** | The report **states that** the figure is self-reported. |
| **Linking words** | The score is high. **However**, our exposure is small. |

### 2 · Four things a quality argument needs

%%FIG c00-f2 | Four numbered boxes in a column. Each one has a part of an argument named in bold, a line reading what it answers, and a line reading what happens without it, with an arrow at the foot pointing to a box reading now it can be checked.%%

**2A  Read. Then say the four parts to your partner in your own words.**

| | What it answers | Without it |
|---|---|---|
| **1 A quantity** | how much, how many, how often | *slow*, *unreliable*, *good* |
| **2 A unit** | of what — requests, seconds, users, lines | a number nobody can reproduce |
| **3 A period** | over what time — an hour, a month, a year | two true figures that disagree |
| **4 A condition** | under what load, for which users, in which region | an average that describes nobody |

The fourth is usually missing and the third is usually wrong. A figure with all four can be argued
about productively. A figure with two is a mood.

### 3 · The four questions of this book

%%FIG c00-f3 | A card taped inside a laptop lid, printed with four questions in bold, each with one line of smaller type under it explaining when to ask it.%%

**3A  Read the four questions. Which one would have saved you an argument last month?**

> **1  Over what period, and measured where?**
> *Before anybody writes a number of nines in a document.*
>
> **2  Compared with what?**
> *Before *faster*, *better* or *improved* reaches a slide.*
>
> **3  Is that severity or risk?**
> *Before a score decides what gets fixed first.*
>
> **4  What response measure would settle it?**
> *Before a requirement that cannot be tested goes into a plan.*

**3B  Ask your partner all four. Answer from a system you work on.**

### 4 · Two kinds of source in this book

%%FIG c00-f4 | Two columns side by side. The left column is headed a standard and lists three lines; the right column is headed a paper and lists three different lines; an arrow at the foot points to a box reading both have limits, and both say so.%%

**4A  Read the two columns. Then discuss: which kind do you read more often?**

| A standard | A paper |
|---|---|
| gives agreed vocabulary | gives a result and its assumptions |
| has an edition and a date | has authors and a year |
| says what is out of scope | says what it holds only if |

Half this book is standards and half is papers, and the honest reader treats them the same way: find
the sentence about the limits, and read it twice.
