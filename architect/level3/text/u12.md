# Unit 12 · Source, Stimulus, Response, Measure
%%SUB Six parts that turn *it should be fast* into something you can test%%
%%GOALS

Eleven units have been about numbers that mean less than they look. This one is about the sentence
that makes a quality requirement checkable: who or what did something, what they did, in what
circumstances, to which part of the system, what happened, and how it was measured. Six parts. Most
requirements in most companies have two.

%%FIG c12-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, lines giving the book and the technical report with their years, a six-row table of the scenario parts with an example in each, a boxed paragraph on what an evaluation produces, a short list of what the method does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 36
> *Platform team · scenarios and evaluation · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The sources.** The six-part scenario is set out in **Software Architecture in Practice** by Len
> Bass, Paul Clements and Rick Kazman. The evaluation method built on it, the **Architecture Tradeoff
> Analysis Method**, was published as a **technical report by the Software Engineering Institute in
> 2000**.
>
> **The six parts.**
>
> | Part | Question it answers | Example |
> |---|---|---|
> | **source** | who or what? | a customer |
> | **stimulus** | what happened? | opens their order list |
> | **environment** | in what state? | at month end, four times normal load |
> | **artifact** | affecting what? | the order service |
> | **response** | what does it do? | returns the first page |
> | **response measure** | how do we know? | **under 2 s for 95% of requests** |
>
> **Why six.** Each part is there because leaving it out has caused a real argument. Without an
> **environment**, a figure met on a quiet Tuesday is quoted against a month-end complaint. Without
> an **artifact**, nobody knows which team owns it. Without a **response measure**, the requirement
> cannot be failed — **and a requirement that cannot be failed has no content**.
>
> **What an evaluation produces.**
>
> > The method brings **stakeholders** together, collects scenarios, ranks them in a **utility
> > tree**, and examines how the architecture handles the most important ones.
> >
> > Its output is **not a score**. It is four lists: **risks**, **non-risks**, **sensitivity
> > points** — places where one decision strongly affects one quality — and **tradeoff points**,
> > where a decision improves one quality and harms another.
> >
> > **There is no pass, no fail and no number out of ten**, and that is deliberate. An architecture
> > is not good or bad in itself; it is good or bad **for a set of prioritized scenarios**, and the
> > priorities belong to the stakeholders rather than to the architect.
>
> **The expensive part is the room.** The method requires the people who care about the qualities to
> be present and to rank the scenarios themselves. **That is its real cost, and it is also where
> most of its value comes from**: the ranking argument is usually the first time a company finds out
> that two of its stakeholders want opposite things.
>
> **What the method does not do.**
>
> - **Produce a number.** By design.
> - **Prioritize for you.** The utility tree is filled in by stakeholders, not derived.
> - **Tell you what to build.** It finds risks in what you propose.
> - **Work without the people.** An evaluation run by the architecture team alone produces the
>   architecture team's opinions, neatly formatted.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every quality requirement in our documents has six parts, and every
> architecture review ends with four lists and no score.
>
> *Sources: the six-part quality attribute scenario — source, stimulus, environment, artifact,
> response and response measure — as set out in Software Architecture in Practice by Bass, Clements
> and Kazman; and the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method, published as a Software Engineering
> Institute technical report in 2000, whose outputs are risks, non-risks, sensitivity points and
> tradeoff points, with scenarios prioritized by stakeholders in a utility tree. The method produces
> no score and no pass or fail judgment, and it does not prioritize scenarios on the architect's
> behalf.*

<sub>**a sensitivity point** — where one decision strongly affects one quality · **a tradeoff point**
— where improving one quality harms another · **a utility tree** — a structure for ranking scenarios
by importance and difficulty</sub>

### 1 · Reviewing the grammar

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-12-06%%

> **Karin:** A year of these. Read me a requirement you would have written last January.
> **Adeel:** *The system must be highly available and perform well under load.*
> **Karin:** And now?
> **Adeel:** *When the month-end batch runs and load reaches four times normal, the order service
> returns the first page of a customer's order list in under two seconds for ninety-five percent of
> requests.*
> **Karin:** Which is longer, duller and testable, and the difference between those two sentences is
> most of what this book was for. What changed in how you write?
> **Adeel:** Every number has a period and a denominator now.
> **Karin:** That is the first half. The second is the hedging. A year ago you wrote *this will
> improve performance*; now you write *this is associated with* when it is a survey, *likely* when it
> is a judgment, and *about eight hours and three quarters* when precision would be false.
> **Adeel:** You made me take *proves* out of a board paper.
> **Karin:** And the paper survived its first challenge, which the earlier version would not have.
> **Precision about how certain you are is not timidity. It is the only thing that makes you
> believable the third time you say something.**
> **Adeel:** What should I take from the whole book?
> **Karin:** Three sentences. **A figure without a period and a denominator is not a measurement.** A
> **standard tells you what it does not cover, and that sentence is in every one of them if you
> look.** And the last one, which is this unit: **a requirement that cannot be failed has no
> content** — so write the six parts, and you will find out what your team actually meant.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A two-part requirement is easy to write, easy to approve and impossible to fail, and those three
facts are connected. Everybody in the approval chain can agree that the system should be fast,
because nobody is committing to anything. The six parts force a commitment, and the commitment
surfaces conflicts — which is why writing them feels like creating problems rather than solving
them. The deeper lesson of this book is the same in every unit: the useful version of a statement is
the one that can be wrong. A figure with a period and a denominator can be wrong. A judgment with
two reasons can be wrong. A scenario with a response measure can be failed. Teams that get good at
this are not more careful than others; they have simply stopped treating *unfalsifiable* as a safe
place to stand.

> ### Quality scenarios · the order and payment services
> *Platform team · reviewed with stakeholders 26 March · one page*
>
> **Three scenarios, six parts each.**
>
> | | Scenario | Priority |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1** | **When** the month-end batch runs and load reaches **four times normal**, **a customer** opening **their order list** receives **the first page** from **the order service** in **under 2 s for 95% of requests**. | **high / medium difficulty** |
> | **2** | **When** one region is unreachable, **a customer** submitting **a payment** receives **a confirmation or a clear refusal** from **the payment service** within **5 s, for 100% of requests**. | **high / high difficulty** |

>
> **The tradeoff point.** Scenarios 1 and 2 **compete at month end**. **Payments win**: under load,
> the order list **degrades to a cached page up to two minutes old** rather than competing for
