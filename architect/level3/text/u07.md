# Unit 7 · Counting Weaknesses
%%SUB Four numbers you can get from source code, and what a zero means%%
%%GOALS
Use agents and instruments
Explain what an automated code measure counts
Say what a count of zero does not prove
%%

There is a standard that turns source code into four numbers. It does it by counting occurrences of
a published list of weaknesses — not by judging your design, not by checking that the software does
what anybody wanted, and not by looking at anything it cannot read. The numbers are genuinely
useful, and the sentence a team should never say about them is *we scored zero, so we are fine*.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — With a measurement standard, find out exactly what is counted. A measure is
> only ever as broad as its list.

%%FIG c07-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its year, a four-row table of the measures with an example weakness for each, a boxed paragraph on what a count of zero means, a short list of what the measures do not cover, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 31
> *Platform team · automated code measures · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO/IEC 5055:2021**, *Automated source code quality measures*, was published in
> **2021**. It specifies **four measures**, each computed **by a tool** from **source code**, by
> **counting occurrences of a published list of weaknesses**.
>
> **The four measures.**
>
> | Measure | An example of what it counts |
> |---|---|
> | **reliability** | a resource opened on one path and never released |
> | **security** | input used in a query without being neutralized |
> | **performance efficiency** | a query issued inside a loop over a result set |
> | **maintainability** | a module with a very large number of dependencies |
>
> **Where the weaknesses come from.** They are drawn from a **public enumeration of known software
> weaknesses**, each with its own identifier. **So the measures are not opinions**: every counted
> item is a named pattern on a published list, which anybody can look up and argue about on its own
> merits.
>
> **Two kinds of weakness.** Some sit **inside one function** and are easy for a tool to see. Others
> span **several components** — a check performed in one service and relied upon in another — and
> those are the expensive ones. **The standard deliberately includes the second kind**, which is
> what distinguishes it from a simple style checker.
>
> **What a count of zero means.**
>
> > **None of the weaknesses on that list** was **detected by that tool** in **the source code it
> > could read**. Three conditions, all of them real.
> >
> > So a zero is genuine work and a narrow claim. It says nothing about whether the software does
> > what anybody wanted. It says nothing about a design decision that will cost you in two years.
> > And it says nothing at all about the libraries you did not write and the services you only call.
> >
> > **The sentence to avoid is *we scored zero, so we are fine*. The sentence to use names the
> > list, the tool and the scope.**
>
> **A count needs a size.** Four hundred occurrences in ten thousand lines and four hundred in two
> million are different situations. **A count on its own is not comparable with anything**, including
> last quarter's count of the same code after it grew.
>
> **What the measures do not cover.**
>
> - **Functional correctness.** Whether the software computes the right answer is not here.
> - **Design.** A clean count on a bad architecture is entirely possible, and common.
> - **Anything not in source form.** A third-party binary, a model, a managed service: invisible.
> - **Severity for you.** Each weakness is a pattern, not a risk assessment of your deployment —
>   which is Unit 8.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every code measure we report names the standard, the tool, the scope
> and the size, because a bare number in this family has caused two arguments this year and settled
> none.
>
> *Source: ISO/IEC 5055:2021, Information technology — Software measurement — Software quality
> measurement — Automated source code quality measures: four measures for reliability, security,
> performance efficiency and maintainability, each computed automatically from source code by
> counting occurrences of a specified set of weaknesses drawn from a public weakness enumeration,
> including weaknesses that span multiple components. The measures count specified weaknesses in
> readable source code: they do not assess functional correctness, design quality, components not
> available in source form, or the severity of any weakness in a particular deployment.*

<sub>**a weakness enumeration** — a published catalogue of known weakness patterns · **an automated
measure** — a figure computed by a tool without human judgment · **density** — a count divided by
the size of the code</sub>

### 1 · Agents and instruments

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-07-06%%

> **Oksana:** Read me your sentence.
> **Adeel:** *No weaknesses were found.*
> **Oksana:** By whom, or by what? A passive with no agent is the most common way of overstating a
> result in this industry, and usually nobody means to.
> **Adeel:** *No weaknesses were found by the scanner.*
> **Oksana:** Better. Now the reader knows the limit is the scanner's limit. If you want to be
> precise about the thing you held rather than the person holding it, use *using*: *we measured the
> code using the scanner*. **English separates the agent — who did it — from the instrument — what
> they did it with — and a measurement sentence usually needs both.**
> **Adeel:** When would I use *by means of*?
> **Oksana:** In a formal document, where it sounds deliberate. *The count was produced by means of
> an automated scan.* Slightly heavy, and it signals that the method is part of the claim.
> **Adeel:** And is *automatically* enough on its own?
> **Oksana:** It is a useful word and it hides the agent completely. *The code is scanned
> automatically* tells me no person judged it, which is the important part, and tells me nothing
> about what. Pair it: *scanned automatically, by the pipeline, using the standard's weakness list*.
> Three facts in nine words.
> **Adeel:** Should I always name the agent?
> **Oksana:** In a measurement, yes. In an incident report, consider it carefully. **A passive with
> no agent protects somebody, and sometimes that is kind and sometimes it is how a cause goes
> unrecorded for two years.** *A configuration change was made* is a sentence with a person missing
> from it, and in a review your job is to notice.

*What is the difference between an agent and an instrument?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A number that can be produced automatically will be reported more often than a judgment that cannot,
and over time an organization starts to believe the automatic numbers describe the whole of the
thing. This is not a failure of the standards; most of them, including this one, state their scope
carefully in a sentence that nobody carries into the report. What gets carried is the figure. The
result is a familiar shape: the measurable parts of quality receive monthly attention and the
unmeasurable parts — two services that cannot be separated, a schema nobody dares change, a design
that only one person understands — receive none, although everybody can name them. The cheap defense
is to publish the scope beside every automatic number, and to keep a short written list of the
problems your tools cannot see. The second list is harder to produce and it is the one that predicts
your next two years.
