# Unit 2 · What Three Nines Buys You
%%SUB The same percentage over a different period is a different promise%%
%%GOALS
Say percentages and approximate figures
Work out what an availability figure permits
Say what an availability figure does not tell you
%%

Three nines sounds like one promise. Over a year it permits a single outage of nearly nine hours.
Over a month it permits forty-three minutes. Over a week, ten. The percentage is identical in all
three and the commitment is not, which is why the first question about any availability figure is
not *how many nines* but *over what period*.

> ## Meridian · architecture note 26
> *Platform team · availability · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document. ITU-T Recommendation E.800**, *Definitions of terms related to quality of
> service*, in its version of **September 2008**, defines the vocabulary of this unit. It is a
> definitions document: it gives careful meanings and **no targets whatsoever**.
>
> **Two terms people use interchangeably.**
>
> | Term | What the definition says |
> |---|---|
> | **availability** | the ability to be in a state to perform a required function **at a given instant**, or at any instant **within a given time interval** |
> | **reliability** | the ability to perform a required function **under given conditions for a given time interval** |
>
> **So the period is part of the definition, not an afterthought.** Both definitions contain a time
> interval. A figure quoted without one is not a quantity; it is a mood.
>
> **The clause that is always omitted.** The definition of availability ends **assuming that the
> external resources, if required, are provided**. Read that twice. The figure describes the item
> **on the assumption that everything it depends on is working**. A service that is unavailable
> because its network is down may be perfectly available by this definition — and completely
> unavailable to the customer.
>
> **The arithmetic.**
>
> > A year is **8,760 hours**. One tenth of one per cent of that is **8.76 hours**: about **eight
> > hours and three quarters**.
> >
> > A thirty-day month is **720 hours**. One tenth of one per cent is **43.2 minutes**: a little over
> > **forty minutes**.
> >
> > A week is **168 hours**. One tenth of one per cent is **about ten minutes**.
> >
> > **The same percentage, three promises, differing by a factor of about fifty.** And four nines
> > annually is **roughly fifty-three minutes** — less than three nines gives you in a single month.
>
> **One more number worth knowing.** An average across customers can hide a total outage. If one
> customer in two hundred is unavailable for a whole day, the average barely moves — and that
> customer's own availability is **about 99.7 per cent for the year**, which is below three nines
> and far below what they were told.
>
> **What an availability figure does not tell you.**
>
> - **The longest single outage.** This is what customers actually care about, and the percentage is
>   silent about it.
> - **Where it was measured.** Inside your network, at your edge, or from the customer's office:
>   three different numbers, all honest.
> - **What counts as down.** Partly working, slow, working for some requests: all judgment calls
>   made by you.
> - **Who was affected.** An average is not a promise to any individual customer.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every availability figure we publish carries its period, its
> measuring point and its definition of *down*, and we publish the **worst customer** beside the
> average.
>
> *Source: ITU-T Recommendation E.800, Definitions of terms related to quality of service,
> September 2008 version: the definitions of availability and reliability, each including a time
> interval, and the clause that availability assumes external resources are provided. The
> Recommendation defines terms; it sets no targets, prescribes no measurement point and does not
> define what counts as an outage for a particular service.*

### 1 · Percentages and approximation

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-02-06%%

> **Karin:** Read me the figure from your slide.
> **Adeel:** *Availability was 99.94 per cent.*
> **Karin:** Said how?
> **Adeel:** Ninety-nine point ninety-four per cent.
> **Karin:** Ninety-nine point **nine four** per cent. After a decimal point, English says the digits
> one at a time: point nine four, not point ninety-four. It matters, because *point ninety-four*
> makes a listener reach for a pen.
> **Adeel:** And *three nines*?
> **Karin:** Useful in speech and dangerous in writing, because people count them differently under
> pressure. In a document, write the number. In a meeting, say *three nines, which is 99.9 per
> cent*, and you have said it twice in six words.
> **Adeel:** How exact should I be about the downtime?
> **Karin:** Be as exact as the number deserves and no more. *8.76 hours* is arithmetic; nobody
> plans with it. *About eight hours and three quarters* is the same fact in a form a manager can
> hold. **Precision you cannot act on is noise, and it makes a listener trust the rest of your
> figures less, not more.**
> **Adeel:** Which approximations are safe?
> **Karin:** *About* and *roughly* in both directions. *Just under* and *a little over* when the
> direction matters — and it often does, because *just under forty-five minutes* and *a little over
> forty-five minutes* land on different sides of a contract. **Never use *about* when the direction
> is the point.**

*How does English say the digits after a decimal point?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

An availability percentage is the most quoted number in this industry and one of the least
informative, and its popularity is not an accident: it is a single figure, it is easy to put on a
page, and in a large fleet it is almost always flattering. The denominator does the work. Choose a
year and you permit a working day of outage; choose an average across customers and you permit one
customer's disaster to disappear. Nobody chooses those things dishonestly — they are chosen because
annual and average are what the reporting tools produce. The useful discipline is to publish, beside
every average, the figure for whoever did worst, and beside every percentage, the longest single
outage. Those two numbers cannot be met by luck and cannot be met by scale, which is exactly why
they are worth having.

**Discuss:** Which number does your company publish because it is flattering, and what would the
honest companion figure be?

> describes no individual customer's month**, and a single long outage can disappear inside it

>
> **What we do not measure.** The path **between us and the customer**. Their network, their
> equipment and anything in between is outside our edge, and the definition of availability we use
> **assumes those external resources are provided**. Where a customer measures from their own
> office, their figure will be lower than ours, and **theirs is the one they experience**.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Degraded performance.** A month in which everything answered slowly counts
> as fully available here, and that is covered by the response-time note in section 3.
>
> *The average is not a promise to anybody. The worst-customer figure and the longest outage are the
> two numbers that cannot be met by luck.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *what counts as down* row is the one that makes the figure reproducible.
> Without it, two honest teams in the same company will publish different percentages for the same
> month.
