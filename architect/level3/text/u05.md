# Unit 5 · Pick Two, and Then Pick Again
%%SUB A theorem about one bad moment, and a trade-off you make every day%%
%%GOALS
Express concession and trade-off
State what the theorem actually says
Say which choice you make in normal operation
%%

The most quoted result in distributed systems is usually quoted wrongly. It does not say you may
pick any two of three nice properties. It says that when the network splits, you must choose between
two of them — and a later paper pointed out that the choice you make on a normal Tuesday, when
nothing is broken, costs you more over a year than the choice you make during a partition.

> **Reader's Tip** — When a famous result has a slogan attached to it, read the formal definitions
> first. The slogan is usually a reasonable summary of something narrower than people think.

%%FIG c05-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, lines giving the 2000 conjecture, the 2002 proof and the 2012 paper, a three-row table of the formal definitions, a boxed paragraph on what the choice really is, a short list of what the theorem does not say, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 29
> *Platform team · consistency and partitions · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Three publications.** The claim was presented as a **conjecture in a keynote in 2000**. It was
> **proved** by Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch in **ACM SIGACT News** in **2002**. And a further
> trade-off was added by Daniel Abadi in **IEEE Computer** in **2012**.
>
> **The three words, as the proof defines them.**
>
> | Word | The formal definition |
> |---|---|
> | **consistency** | **linearizable**: every operation appears to happen at a single instant, in one order |
> | **availability** | **every** request received by a **non-failing** node must produce a response |
> | **partition tolerance** | the network is allowed to **lose arbitrarily many messages** |
>
> **Read the second definition again.** *Availability* here means **every** request gets a response.
> It is not a percentage and it is not a service-level target. A system that answers 99.99 per cent
> of requests is **not available** in this sense. **The word in the theorem and the word on your
> dashboard are different words that happen to be spelled the same.**
>
> **What the result actually says.**
>
> > In an asynchronous network, no system can guarantee **both** linearizable consistency **and**
> > availability in all runs, **including those in which messages are lost**.
> >
> > So the slogan — *pick two of three* — is misleading in one important way. **A partition is not a
> > property you choose.** It is an event that happens to you. What you choose is **what your system
> > does while one is happening**: answer from one side with a possibly stale value, or refuse to
> > answer until the sides can talk again.
>
> **The 2012 addition.** The later paper observes that partitions are **rare**, and that a system
> makes a second trade-off **all the rest of the time**: it can answer **quickly** from one replica,
> or **wait for several to agree** and answer more slowly. **Latency against consistency, on every
> request of every normal day.** The paper's argument is that this second trade-off explains far more
> about how real systems are designed than the partition case does.
>
> **What neither result tells you.**
>
> - **Which choice is right.** Both are descriptions of what is possible, not advice.
> - **Anything quantitative.** No milliseconds, no percentages, no thresholds.
> - **What happens in between.** A slow network is not a partition and not a healthy network, and
>   the theorem's model does not describe it.
> - **How to resolve conflicts.** If both sides accepted writes during a partition, merging them is
>   your design problem and a hard one.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** For every store we write down two sentences — what it does during a
> partition, and whether a normal read waits for agreement — because until this year we had the
> first sentence for one store and the second for none.
>
> *Sources: the conjecture presented in a keynote in 2000; Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch, Brewer's
> conjecture and the feasibility of consistent, available, partition-tolerant web services, ACM
> SIGACT News, 2002, with its formal definitions of atomic consistency, availability as a response to
> every request at a non-failing node, and partition tolerance as the loss of arbitrarily many
> messages; and Daniel Abadi, Consistency Tradeoffs in Modern Distributed Database System Design,
> IEEE Computer, 2012, adding the latency-against-consistency trade-off in the absence of a
> partition. The results describe what is possible; they recommend nothing, quantify nothing, and do
> not address conflict resolution.*

### 1 · Concession and trade-off

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-05-06%%

> **Priya:** Read me your design note.
> **Adeel:** *The read is fast. The value may be stale.*
> **Karin:** Two sentences and no relationship. A reader has to work out whether the second is a
> cost, a risk or an accident. Join them and say which.
> **Adeel:** *Although the read is fast, the value may be stale.*
> **Priya:** Nearly. You have conceded the wrong half. *Although* introduces the part you are
> playing down, and you have played down the speed, which is the thing you chose it for. Turn it
> round: *the read is fast, although the value may be stale.*
> **Adeel:** So *although* marks what I am admitting.
> **Priya:** And the order matters because readers remember the main clause. Put the benefit in the
> main clause when you are defending a decision and the cost in the concession — and then, because
> you are honest, write the cost out in full somewhere else on the page.
> **Adeel:** What about *whereas*?
> **Priya:** For contrasting two different subjects: *the orders store refuses to answer, whereas
> the catalog answers from one replica.* Not for two facts about one thing. **And never use
> *whereas* where you mean *although* — it is a contrast, not a concession, and lawyers use it
> differently again.**
> **Adeel:** And naming the price?
> **Priya:** *At the expense of* and *in exchange for*, and they point in opposite directions. *Speed
> at the expense of consistency* names what you lost. *Consistency in exchange for twelve
> milliseconds* names what you paid. **The second is the sentence that gets a design approved,
> because it sounds like a purchase rather than a sacrifice.**

*What does* although *mark, according to Priya?*

> **Grammar Note** — *Although* and *despite* are not interchangeable. *Although* takes a clause:

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Three circles on a slide have done more damage than any other diagram in this field, and the reason
is instructive: the slogan is memorable, the theorem is not, and the slogan is wrong in a way that
flatters whoever is quoting it. *We picked availability* sounds like a decision taken by serious
people after deliberation. Usually it means a default was left in place. Underneath this is a more
general pattern worth watching for: a dramatic rare case absorbs the attention a boring frequent
case deserves. Nine minutes of partition get a post-incident review and a diagram; eleven million
ordinary reads a day get neither, although that is where the latency and the staleness your
customers actually experience come from. If you want to find unexamined decisions in any system,
look at what happens on a Tuesday when nothing is wrong.

> | **a normal read** | waits for **three of five** replicas | answers from the **nearest** replica |
> | **what that costs** | **about 12 ms** per read | a value up to **two seconds** old |
> | **chosen by** | Priya, data, March 2024 | Priya, data, March 2024 |
>
> **Why they differ, on purpose.** **An order that half exists is worse than an order that does not
> exist**, so orders refuses. **A product description nine minutes out of date harms nobody**, so the
> catalog accepts. **The settings were previously opposite by accident — they were two products'
> defaults — and the behavior is the same; the difference is that somebody has now chosen it.**
>
> **The rule we were missing.** A catalog record written during a partition that refers to an order
> **that was refused** is **quarantined** on reconnection and listed for review. **Sixty-one such
> records existed in March and nobody had a rule for them.**
>
> **What we are not claiming.** We do **not** say the system is *available* in the sense used in the
> theorem — that would mean **every** request answered, always. **We answer about 99.97 per cent of
> requests in a normal month, which is a different claim with a different word.**
>
> **Where the cost really is.** **Nine minutes** of partition last quarter. **Eleven million reads a
> day.** The twelve milliseconds in row three is paid about four billion times a year; the partition
> behavior applies for nine minutes. **Both rows matter, and the second one matters more often than
> any of us discuss it.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **Writes during normal operation**, and what *acknowledged* means for each
> store. That is section 5.4, and it is the next thing we are going to find we have never written
> down.
>
> *A partition is an event, not a choice. What we choose is what happens during one — and, far more
> often, what happens when nothing is wrong at all.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *where the cost really is* paragraph is the one that changes behavior. Put
> the rare dramatic case and the frequent boring case in the same table with their frequencies, and
> the conversation reorganizes itself.
