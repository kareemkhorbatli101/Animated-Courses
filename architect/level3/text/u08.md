# Unit 8 · Severity Is Not Risk
%%SUB A score that describes a weakness, not your exposure to it%%
%%GOALS
Express probability and certainty
Explain what a base score measures
Say what turns severity into risk
%%

A published scoring system turns a vulnerability into a number out of ten, and that number decides
what thousands of teams fix first. It is a measure of how bad the weakness is in itself. It is not a
measure of how much danger you are in, and the specification says so — in a section almost nobody
reads, immediately before the table everybody uses.

> **Reader's Tip** — Read what a score is *of*. A number out of ten feels like a verdict on your
> system; it is usually a verdict on something else entirely.

%%FIG c08-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the version and its date, a four-row table of the metric groups with what each adds, a boxed paragraph on severity against risk, a short list of what the score does not consider, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 32
> *Platform team · scoring vulnerabilities · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The specification. CVSS version 4.0** was published in **November 2023** by the organization that
> maintains it, succeeding version **3.1** from **2019**. It produces a number from **0.0 to 10.0**
> and a band: **none, low, medium, high, critical**.
>
> **Four groups of metrics.**
>
> | Group | What it describes | Usually filled in? |
> |---|---|---|
> | **base** | the vulnerability **itself** — how it is reached, what it affects | **always** |
> | **threat** | whether a **working exploit** exists and how mature it is | sometimes |
> | **environmental** | what is true in **your** deployment, and what the asset is worth | **rarely** |
> | **supplemental** | extra information — recovery effort, automatability, safety | informational; **changes no number** |
>
> **The sentence that matters.**
>
> > The specification states that the **base score** represents the **severity** of the vulnerability
> > **as such**, and that consumers are expected to use the **threat** and **environmental** metrics
> > to produce something that reflects their own situation.
> >
> > So a base score is a property of the weakness, like the hardness of a metal. **Risk is a property
> > of your system**: it depends on whether anything can reach the vulnerable part, whether an attack
> > exists, and what is behind it.
> >
> > **A ten in a library you ship and never call can be less urgent than a six on your sign-in page**
> > — and a list sorted by base score cannot tell you that.
>
> **Why the base score is the one everybody uses.** Because it **arrives**. It comes in a feed, with
> the finding, already computed. The environmental metrics have to be filled in by somebody who
> knows your deployment, and that person is busy. **The default is not chosen; it is the one that
> requires no work.**
>
> **What version 4.0 added.** A clearer treatment of **exploit maturity** in the threat group, and
> the new **supplemental** group — which deliberately **does not change the score**. That last
> decision is worth noticing: the authors added information that teams need **without** letting it
> move a number, because a number that moves for several reasons stops meaning anything.
>
> **What the score does not consider.**
>
> - **Whether you are exposed.** Reachability is yours to assess.
> - **What the asset is worth.** The score knows nothing about your data.
> - **Probability.** It is not a likelihood of being attacked, and it is not a percentage.
> - **Your capacity.** Eleven criticals and three engineers is a scheduling problem the score cannot
>   help with.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every finding gets three fields filled in by us — reachable, exploit
> in the wild, what is behind it — and the list we work from is sorted by those, with the base score
> as one input among four.
>
> *Source: the Common Vulnerability Scoring System, version 4.0, published November 2023 by its
> maintaining organization, succeeding version 3.1 of 2019: the base, threat, environmental and
> supplemental metric groups; the 0.0 to 10.0 scale and the none, low, medium, high and critical
> bands; the statement that the base score represents severity and is intended to be supplemented by
> threat and environmental metrics; and the supplemental group's informational status, which does not
> affect the score. The score is a measure of severity: it is not a probability, and it does not
> consider exposure, reachability, asset value or remediation capacity.*

adjustment for your own deployment · **a supplemental metric** — information recorded without
changing the score</sub>

### 1 · Probability and certainty

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-08-06%%

> **Oksana:** Read me your risk sentence.
> **Adeel:** *This could possibly maybe be exploited.*
> **Oksana:** Three hedges in one clause, which reads as *I have not looked*. Choose one word and
> commit to it. Where on the ladder are you?
> **Adeel:** It is reachable from the internet and there is a published exploit. So: likely.
> **Oksana:** Then write *exploitation is likely*. One hedge, precisely placed, and now I know what
> you think. **Stacking hedges does not make a statement safer; it makes it unusable, and somebody
> else will have to form the judgment you avoided.**
> **Adeel:** What are the rungs?
> **Oksana:** From the top: *certain*, *almost certain*, *likely* or *probable*, *possible*,
> *unlikely*, *very unlikely*. And two useful phrases at the edges: *may well*, which is a little
> above *possible*, and *cannot be ruled out*, which is the lowest thing you can say while still
> saying something.
> **Adeel:** Is *possible* weak?
> **Oksana:** *Possible* is almost free. Nearly everything is possible, so a sentence built on it
> carries no information unless you add what would make it happen. **Possible** is worth saying when
> you pair it with a condition: *possible if the component becomes reachable*. That is a sentence
> somebody can act on.
> **Adeel:** And *probable* against *likely*?
> **Oksana:** The same meaning; *probable* sounds more formal and is more often argued with. In a
> report to an auditor I use *likely* and then give the two facts behind it. **A judgment with its
> two reasons attached is much harder to dismiss than a judgment with an adjective attached.**

*Why does Oksana object to three hedges in one clause?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A published number is a shield. Sorting a list by score is defensible to anybody — the figure is
public, computed by a documented method, and nobody can accuse you of favoritism. Writing your own
order means writing your own judgment, and a judgment can be wrong in a way that a published number
cannot be blamed for. So organizations drift towards the shield, and the drift is strongest exactly
where the stakes are highest. What breaks the pattern is not courage but format: three columns that
somebody has to fill in, and a sentence with two reasons attached. A judgment with evidence is
nearly as defensible as a number and far more useful, and the teams that write them find that
auditors prefer them — because an auditor's job is to understand your reasoning, and a number out of
ten contains none.

**Discuss:** Where does your team use a published number instead of a judgment, and what would the
judgment have to say?

> | date library | **9.8** | **no — function never called** | no | — | **unlikely while nothing calls it — seventh** |
>
> **Two judgments, written out.** Exploitation of the sign-in finding is **likely**: it is
> **reachable without credentials** and **a working exploit is published**. Exploitation of the date
> library finding is **unlikely while nothing calls the affected function** — **the word *while* is
> deliberate**, and this judgment is **reviewed whenever that library's use changes**.
>
> **Why the three columns are ours.** The specification says the base score is to be supplemented
> with threat and environmental information. **That information exists only here**: nobody outside
> this company knows what we can reach or what our data is worth.
>
> **What we are not claiming.** The score is **not a probability**. Our judgments **are**
> probabilistic and they are **our opinion with the reasons attached**, which is a different and more
> arguable kind of statement — **deliberately so**.
>
> **Capacity.** Three engineers, ten working days. **Six of the eleven will not be addressed this
> fortnight**, and the six are listed below with their judgments, so that the decision is recorded
> rather than discovered.
>
> **Not in this note.** **How we patch.** The process is section 8.4.
>
> *A list sorted by published severity is defensible and not useful. A list sorted by our judgment is
> useful and must be defended, which is why every row has two reasons in it.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *capacity* paragraph is the most adult thing on the page. Every triage list
> has items that will not be done; writing them down converts an omission into a decision.
