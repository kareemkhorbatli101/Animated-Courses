# Unit 10 · Four Principles, Three Levels
%%SUB Conformance belongs to a page and a process, not to a product%%
%%GOALS
Form and use *-able* adjectives
Name the four principles and the three levels
Say what conformance does not promise
%%

Four words organize the whole of web accessibility: perceivable, operable, understandable, robust.
Under them sit guidelines, and under those sit testable criteria at three levels. The structure is
unusually clear, the claims it supports are unusually narrow, and the sentence *our product is
accessible* is not one of them.

%%FIG c10-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the recommendation and its date, a four-row table of the principles with a guideline example under each, a boxed paragraph on what conformance attaches to, a short list of what conformance does not promise, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 34
> *Platform team · accessibility conformance · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document.** The **Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, version 2.2**, became a **W3C
> Recommendation** on **5 October 2023**. It builds on version 2.1 without replacing its structure.
>
> **Four principles, thirteen guidelines, testable criteria.**
>
> | Principle | Means | A guideline under it |
> |---|---|---|
> | **perceivable** | it can be sensed | provide text alternatives |
> | **operable** | it can be used | make everything keyboard accessible |
> | **understandable** | it can be made sense of | make text readable |
> | **robust** | it keeps working | be compatible with assistive technology |
>
> **Three levels.** Each criterion sits at **A**, **AA** or **AAA**. **AA** is the level most
> policies and contracts refer to. The guidelines themselves say that **AAA is not intended as a
> general requirement for entire sites**, because some criteria cannot be satisfied for all kinds of
> content.
>
> **What version 2.2 changed.** It adds **nine new success criteria** — among them requirements about
> **keyboard focus not being hidden**, **alternatives to dragging**, **minimum target size**,
> **consistent help**, **not having to enter the same information twice**, and **authentication that
> does not depend on remembering or recognizing something**. It also **removes one criterion** that
> had become obsolete.
>
> **What conformance attaches to.**
>
> > A conformance claim is made for a **full page**: a page that meets the criteria **except in one
> > region** does not conform.
> >
> > And it is made for a **complete process**: if a task takes a person through several pages, **all
> > of them must conform at the claimed level**, or the claim cannot be made for the process.
> >
> > **So *our product conforms* is not a sentence this document supports.** The supported sentence
> > names pages, or names a process, and a level, and a date.
>
> **What conformance does not promise.**
>
> - **That the product is usable by everybody.** The guidelines state plainly that meeting them
>   **will not address every user need** for every disability.
> - **That a tool can tell you.** Automated checking covers **a minority** of the criteria; the rest
>   require human judgment and, properly, people who use assistive technology.
> - **That it stays true.** A conformance claim describes **a version on a date**. Content changes
>   daily.
> - **Anything about the highest level, site-wide.** See above: the guidelines themselves advise
>   against requiring it as blanket policy.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Our claim names the pages and processes, the level and the date, and
> it is signed by somebody who has completed each process with a keyboard and with a screen reader.
>
> *Source: Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2, W3C Recommendation of 5 October 2023: the four
> principles — perceivable, operable, understandable and robust — their guidelines, and success
> criteria at levels A, AA and AAA; the addition of nine success criteria including requirements on
> focus visibility, dragging alternatives, target size, consistent help, redundant entry and
> accessible authentication, and the removal of one obsolete criterion; the requirement that
> conformance be claimed for full pages and for all pages in a process; and the statement that
> conformance will not address every user need and that level AAA is not recommended as a general
> policy for entire sites.*

### 1 · *-able* adjectives

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-10-06%%

> **Samira:** Read me the four principles.
> **Adeel:** Perceivable, operable, understandable, robust.
> **Samira:** And notice what three of the four have in common. They are **-able** adjectives, built
> from verbs, and they all mean *somebody can do this*. That is not decoration: it moves the subject
> of the sentence from your product to a person.
> **Adeel:** How does the spelling work?
> **Samira:** Mostly *-able*, and a group of older words take *-ible*: *accessible*, *visible*,
> *legible*, *responsible*. There is no rule you can derive — they came into English by a different
> route. **The useful thing is that *-ible* words are a closed set: if you are inventing an adjective
> today, it takes *-able*.**
> **Adeel:** And the silent *e*?
> **Samira:** Usually dropped: *operate* becomes *operable*, *use* becomes *usable*. Kept after a
> soft *c* or *g*: *noticeable*, *manageable*. **And *perceivable* keeps its *e* because it would be
> unreadable otherwise, which is as good a reason as English ever gives.**
> **Adeel:** Are the nouns regular?
> **Samira:** Not at all, and this is where documents go wrong. *Accessible* gives
> *accessibility*. *Operable* gives *operability*. But *perceivable* and *understandable* have no
> comfortable noun in ordinary use — you say *the content is perceivable*, not *the perceivability of
> the content*. **When the noun sounds invented, use the adjective and a person: *a screen reader
> user can perceive the warning*. Shorter, and it names who.**
> **Adeel:** Does that matter?
> **Samira:** It is the whole subject. *The page is accessible* is a claim about a page. *A person
> using only a keyboard can complete the payment* is a claim about a person, and it is the one you
> can go and check. **Every *-able* word in this field is an invitation to name the person and find
> out.**

*Why does Samira prefer naming a person to using an invented noun?*

**1B  Underline six *-able* or *-ible* adjectives in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **-able**, the open set | test**able**, measur**able**, us**able**, maintain**able** |
| **-ible**, a closed set | access**ible**, vis**ible**, leg**ible**, respons**ible** |
| silent *e* dropped | oper**ate** → oper**able**; us**e** → us**able** |
| *e* kept after soft *c*/*g* | notice**able**, manage**able** |
| noun in **-ability** | access**ibility**, oper**ability**, test**ability** |
| better than an invented noun | *a keyboard user can complete it* |

> **Grammar Note** — An *-able* adjective is usually passive in meaning: a *testable* system is one
> that **can be tested**. So the sentence often has a hidden person in it, and naming them makes the
> claim checkable.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Accessibility is the clearest example in this book of a measurement whose subject is a person, and
of what happens when an organization quietly changes that subject. *Can a person using a keyboard
complete this?* is a question with an answer you can watch somebody produce in forty minutes. *Is
the product accessible?* is a question about a product, answerable by a tool, reportable as green,
and nobody has to meet anybody. The second question is more comfortable and it is why automated
scanning is so popular relative to what it covers. The work that actually changes outcomes is
unglamorous and specific: somebody completes each process with the tools real customers use, writes
down where they stopped, and the exception goes in the summary rather than on page nine.

**Discuss:** Who in your company has watched somebody use your product with assistive technology?

> | the **sign-up process** (3 pages) | **AA** | completed by a person **with a keyboard** and **with a screen reader** | 4 March |
> | the **payment process** (4 pages) | **does not conform** | — | 4 March |
>
> **The exception, in the summary.** **Step three of the payment process fails** a criterion about
> alternatives to dragging: the date picker **cannot be operated without a drag**. **A customer can
> complete three pages, including entering card details, before reaching it.**
>
> **Why the exception is here and not in an appendix.** It was correctly described on page nine of
> the audit. **Nobody reads page nine.** Our summaries now carry every exception, because a summary
> that loses them is how an accurate audit becomes an inaccurate claim.
>
> **How we test.** Automated checking covers **about a third** of the criteria. The rest require a
> person, and for each **process** somebody completes the whole task **with a keyboard only** and
> **with a screen reader**. **That takes about forty minutes per process and it is the only step that
> has ever found a failure like step three.**
>
> **What we do not claim.** That the product is **usable by everybody** — the guidelines themselves
> state that conformance **will not address every user need**. That the claim **stays true**: it
> describes **release 7.2 on 4 March**. And we do **not** target the highest level site-wide, as the
> guidelines advise against requiring it as a general policy.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Our internal tools.** None has been tested, and that is honestly recorded
> in section 10.5 rather than omitted here.
>
> *Conformance belongs to a page and to a process. A product does not conform; some of its pages do,
> on a date, at a level, and the rest is a list.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the failing row in the main table, not in a footnote. A table with one
> *does not conform* row in it is trusted; a table of unbroken green is read as marketing.
