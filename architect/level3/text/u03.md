# Unit 3 · Arrivals, Time and Queue
%%SUB Three numbers, and if you know two of them you know the third%%
%%GOALS
Use rates and proportions

There is a result from 1961 that fits on one line and settles a surprising number of capacity
arguments. The average number of things inside a system equals the rate at which they arrive
multiplied by the average time each one spends there. It assumes almost nothing — and the one thing
it does assume is the thing that fails on your worst day.

> right?

> five hundredths of a second is ten.
> **A:** How did you get that so fast?

> nothing about your system.
> **A:** So I need ten workers.
> **B:** You need enough capacity for ten on average, and rather more for the moments when it is

> **B:** Then either the time each one spends there goes up, or the number inside goes up, or both.

> **B:** Then the law stops applying, because it needs a stable system. **Beyond capacity there is

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A paper's value is often in its assumptions. Read what the result needs before
> you read what it says.

%%FIG c03-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the paper, its journal and its year, a three-row table of the quantities with a symbol and a unit for each, a boxed paragraph of worked arithmetic, a short list of what the result does not tell you, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 27
> *Platform team · queues and capacity · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The paper.** John D. C. Little, *A Proof for the Queuing Formula*, published in the journal
> **Operations Research** in **1961**. It gives one relationship, usually written **L = λW**.
>
> **Three quantities.**
>
> | Symbol | What it is | A unit |
> |---|---|---|
> | **L** | the average number **inside the system** | requests |
> | **λ** | the average **arrival rate** | requests **per second** |
> | **W** | the average **time inside**, arrival to departure | seconds |
>
> **The relationship.** **L = λW.** The average number inside equals the arrival rate multiplied by
> the average time each item spends inside. **If you know two of the three, you know the third** —
> and in most systems two of them are easy to measure and the third is the one everybody argues
> about.
>
> **Worked both ways.**
>
> > **Two hundred requests per second**, each spending **fifty milliseconds** inside. Then L = 200 ×
> > 0.05 = **ten requests inside on average**.
> >
> > Now run it backwards. You observe **thirty** requests inside on average, at an arrival rate of
> > **two hundred per second**. Then W = 30 ÷ 200 = **0.15 seconds**. **You have just measured
> > latency without timing anything**, which is the trick worth remembering.
>
> **What it assumes — and it is very little.** The result needs the system to be **stationary**:
> not drifting over the period you measure, with the averages existing. It assumes **nothing** about
> how arrivals are distributed, nothing about how long service takes, and nothing about the order in
> which waiting items are handled. **It is an identity, not a model.** That is why it applies to a
> queue of requests, a warehouse of parcels and a hospital of patients equally.
>
> **What it does not tell you.**
>
> - **Anything about the worst case.** It is an average over a long run. Your ninety-ninth
>   percentile is not in here, and the quantity people are usually complaining about is a
>   percentile.
> - **Anything about variance.** Two systems with identical averages can feel completely different
>   to a user.
> - **Why W is what it is.** The relationship connects the three numbers. It does not explain any of
>   them.
> - **Anything at all about an overloaded system.** If arrivals exceed what the system can handle,
>   there is **no steady state**: the queue grows for as long as the load continues, and the averages
>   the result relies on **do not exist**. **The law does not break on your worst day — it simply
>   stops applying, which is more dangerous, because the arithmetic still looks as if it works.**
>
> **What we do, in one line.** We put all three numbers on the same dashboard, and we mark the point
> at which the system stops being stable, because past that line the arithmetic on this page is no
> longer about anything.
>
> *Source: John D. C. Little, A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW, Operations Research, 1961:
> the identity relating the long-run average number in a stationary system, the long-run average
> arrival rate and the long-run average time in the system, with no assumption about arrival or
> service distributions or about service discipline. The result concerns long-run averages: it says
> nothing about percentiles or variance, and it requires a stationary system, so it does not apply
> where arrivals exceed capacity.*

### 1 · Rates and proportions

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-03-06%%

> **Tomas:** Read me your capacity line.
> **Adeel:** *The service handles 200 requests.*
> **Tomas:** In what? A second, an hour, a day? That sentence is three orders of magnitude wide.
> **Adeel:** *Two hundred requests per second.*
> **Tomas:** Now it is a rate, and a rate always has two units with *per* between them. Requests per
> second. Errors per thousand requests. Deployments per week. **If a number has only one unit, it is
> a count, and a count without a period cannot be compared with anything.**
> **Adeel:** When do I say *a rate of*?
> **Tomas:** When you want the rate to be the subject of the sentence. *A rate of two hundred a
> second is within capacity.* In speech, *two hundred a second* is more natural than *two hundred
> per second*, and both are correct.
> **Adeel:** And proportions?
> **Tomas:** Three ways, and they are not equally clear. *Three percent of requests fail.* *Three
> out of every hundred requests fail.* *One request in thirty-three fails.* The second is the one
> people picture, and the third is the one they remember. **For a small proportion, *one in* is far
> more vivid than a percentage — and that is exactly why you should use it when the number is bad
> and not only when it is good.**
> **Adeel:** What about *on average*?
> **Tomas:** Put it at the front where nobody can miss it. *On average, ten are inside.* It warns
> the reader that a maximum is coming later and that this is not it. **An average with no warning
> label is read as a promise.**

*Why does Tomas say a number with one unit cannot be compared?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The most useful results in this book are the oldest ones, and this is the clearest case: one line
from 1961, no assumptions worth speaking of, and it settles arguments in a minute that otherwise
take weeks. What stops teams using it is not difficulty — it is that it feels too simple to be
allowed in a serious discussion about a modern system. There is a general pattern here worth
noticing: tooling advances very fast and arithmetic does not advance at all, so the fundamentals
become steadily rarer knowledge while the dashboards get better. The teams that look preternaturally
calm during a capacity incident are usually not the ones with the best tools. They are the ones
where somebody has three numbers in their head and does the multiplication before the meeting
starts.

> is 150 ms rather than 40.
>
> **How to sanity-check a claim.** Multiply the claimed rate by the claimed latency. If the result
> is larger than our pool, one of the two figures is wrong. **This has caught three capacity claims
> this year, including one of ours.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **Percentiles and their periods.** Those are section 4, and the argument
> about which percentile matters is still open.
>
> *The relationship is an identity, not a model. It connects three averages and explains none of
> them.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *how we know* column is what makes the note auditable. One figure measured,
> one reported by a tool, one derived — and a reader can see which is which at a glance.
