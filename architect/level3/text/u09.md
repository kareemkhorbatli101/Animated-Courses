# Unit 9 · Ninety-Three Controls
%%SUB A reference list, a statement of what applies, and a certificate with a scope on it%%
%%GOALS

A customer asks whether you are certified. The honest answer has three parts: which standard, what
scope, and which controls you excluded and why. The standard is explicit about all three — it even
requires you to write the exclusions down — and the certificate that customers photograph for their
files usually shows only the first.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — With any certification, find three things: the standard, the scope, and the
> exclusions. A certificate shows the first, states the second in small type, and never shows the
> third.

%%FIG c09-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its edition, a four-row table of the control themes with counts, a boxed paragraph on the statement of applicability, a short list of what certification does not mean, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 33
> *Platform team · certification and controls · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO/IEC 27001:2022** was published in **October 2022**, replacing the **2013**
> edition. It sets out the requirements for an **information security management system** — a set of
> processes, not a set of software.
>
> **Annex A: ninety-three controls in four themes.**
>
> | Theme | Controls |
> |---|---|
> | **organizational** | **37** |
> | **people** | **8** |
> | **physical** | **14** |
> | **technological** | **34** |
> | **total** | **93** |
>
> **What changed from 2013.** The earlier edition had **114 controls in fourteen clauses**. The 2022
> edition has **93 in four themes**, with **eleven new controls** and many of the old ones merged.
> **A smaller number is not a weaker standard**: the reduction is mostly consolidation.
>
> **The statement of applicability.**
>
> > The standard requires an organization to determine the controls it needs from its own **risk
> > assessment**, then **compare that list with Annex A** to verify that **no necessary control has
> > been omitted**, and to produce a document recording **which controls apply**, which do not, and
> > **a justification for each inclusion and exclusion**.
> >
> > So **Annex A is a reference set, not a compulsory checklist.** A control may be excluded — and
> > the exclusion must be **written down with a reason**. That document is where the real
> > information is, and it is almost never the document a customer asks for.
>
> **What is certified, and how much of it.** The certificate covers a **management system** within a
> **scope that the organization itself defines**. A scope may be an entire company or **one function
> in one office**. **The scope is stated on the certificate**, usually in a single line that nobody
> reads, and a certificate whose scope excludes the service you buy tells you almost nothing about
> that service.
>
> **What certification does not mean.**
>
> - **That a product is secure.** The standard certifies processes for managing information
>   security, not the properties of any software.
> - **That all ninety-three controls are in place.** Exclusions are permitted, with justification.
> - **That the scope is what you assumed.** It is chosen by the organization being certified.
> - **That nothing will happen.** A certified organization can be breached, and many have been.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** For every certificate we receive we record the standard, the edition,
> the scope line and whether we have seen the statement of applicability — and for two of our
> suppliers the answer to the last question has been *no* for three years.
>
> *Source: ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Information security, cybersecurity and privacy protection —
> Information security management systems — Requirements, published October 2022, replacing the 2013
> edition: the 93 controls of Annex A grouped in four themes, with 37 organizational, 8 people, 14
> physical and 34 technological; the requirement to determine necessary controls, compare them with
> Annex A to verify that none necessary has been omitted, and produce a statement of applicability
> justifying inclusions and exclusions. Certification covers a management system within a scope
> defined by the organization; it does not certify any product, does not require every Annex A
> control, and makes no guarantee about outcomes.*

reference set** — the list provided for checking · **a statement of applicability** — the record of
which controls apply and why</sub>

### 1 · Exception and applicability

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-09-06%%

> **Oksana:** Read me the line from our statement.
> **Adeel:** *All controls apply. We excluded two.*
> **Oksana:** Two sentences that contradict each other, and an auditor will enjoy that. Join them
> with an exception structure and the contradiction disappears.
> **Adeel:** *All controls apply, with the exception of two.*
> **Oksana:** Correct, and now add the two reasons, because the standard requires them. The grammar
> of exceptions has a job here: it tells the reader that you knew. **An unexplained gap looks like an
> oversight; a stated exception looks like a decision, and the words are the only difference.**
> **Adeel:** When do I use *unless*?
> **Oksana:** For a condition rather than a list. *All controls apply unless excluded in writing.*
> That is a rule with a built-in escape, and it is the shape most policies want. *With the exception
> of* names the exceptions themselves; *unless* names the mechanism by which something can become an
> exception.
> **Adeel:** And *not applicable*?
> **Oksana:** Two words that do a great deal of work and are frequently misused. *Not applicable*
> means the control **cannot apply** to us — a control about physical media when we hold none. It
> does not mean *we have not done it*. **Writing *not applicable* for something you simply have not
> got round to is the single most common finding in an audit, and it is a language mistake before it
> is a compliance one.**
> **Adeel:** What should I write in that case?
> **Oksana:** *Applicable, not yet implemented, planned for the third quarter.* It is uncomfortable
> and it is honest, and auditors are far gentler with it than with a wrong *not applicable*.
> **Adeel:** And *subject to*?
> **Oksana:** For a condition you depend on. *Certified, subject to annual audit.* It tells the
> reader the statement has a maintenance requirement, which is true of everything on this page.

*What is the difference between* not applicable *and* not yet implemented?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A certificate is designed to be read and is mostly used as a symbol. The scope line — the only part
that varies between a meaningful certificate and a decorative one — is set in small type at the
bottom, and procurement processes are built to record that a certificate exists rather than what it
says. This is not anybody's dishonesty; it is what happens when a yes-or-no question meets a
document that answers a different question. The standard itself is unusually honest about its own
limits: it requires exclusions to be justified in writing, it leaves scope to the organization, and
it certifies processes rather than products. Everything a careful buyer needs is in the design.
What goes wrong is at the moment of filing, when a human being with eleven other tasks looks at a
logo and a date and moves on.

**Discuss:** What does your procurement process record about a certificate, and what does it not?

> **What we now ask for.** The **statement of applicability**, or a summary. The standard requires
> every organization to compare its controls with the **93 in Annex A** and to **justify every
> exclusion in writing**, so the document exists.
>
> **If a supplier declines** — which is reasonable, as it is a sensitive document — we ask instead
> for **the count of exclusions and their themes**: *91 of 93 applied, two excluded, both physical.*
> **That sentence is safe to give and tells us a great deal.**
>
> **What we do not claim from any of this.** That a **product** is secure: **no product is
> certified**. That **all 93 controls** are in place: exclusions are permitted **with
> justification**. That nothing will happen: **certified organizations are breached every year**.
>
> **Supplier D, stated honestly.** We have **no evidence either way** about the service we buy.
> **That is not the same as a finding against them**, and the note to the business says so in those
> words.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Our own statement of applicability.** Ours excludes two controls, both
> physical, and it is section 9.6.
>
> *The scope line is the whole of a certificate. Everything else on the page is a date and a logo.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Quote the scope rather than summarizing it. A summary of a scope line is where
> the error always enters, because summarizing means deciding what it covers, which is the thing you
> were trying to find out.

1. The scope is (quoted / summarized).
