%%SUB Where the figures come from decides what you may conclude from them%%

Four numbers about how a team delivers software have become the standard way to compare one
engineering organization with another. They are useful, they are published, and they come from a
survey — which means the sentences you may build on them are narrower than the sentences most people
build. This unit is about those sentences.

> **The sources.** The four measures were set out in **Accelerate** (**2018**) by Nicole Forsgren,
> Jez Humble and Gene Kim, drawing on the annual **State of DevOps** research program, which has
> run since **2014** and publishes a report most years.
>
> **The four.**
>
> | Measure | What it asks |
> |---|---|
> | **deployment frequency** | how often do changes reach production? |
> | **lead time for changes** | how long from code committed to code running? |
> | **change failure rate** | what proportion of changes cause a problem needing a fix? |
> | **time to restore service** | how long to recover when something breaks? |
>
> **Two of them are about speed and two are about stability**, and the central published finding is
> that these do **not** trade off against each other: the groups that deploy most often also recover
> fastest. That is the result worth knowing, and it contradicts the belief most organizations
> operate on.
>
> **Where the numbers come from, and what follows.**
>
> > The figures are **self-reported survey responses** from people who chose to answer a survey about
> > software delivery. The **four performance groups** are produced by **cluster analysis of that
> > year's answers**, which means **the boundaries move between years**. A team can move from one
> > group to the next **without changing anything it does**.
> >
> > The research reports **associations** and argues for **predictive** relationships using
> > statistical modelling. **It is not a controlled experiment.** The authors say so. So the honest
> > sentence is *teams with these characteristics tend to also have those outcomes* — not *doing
> > this causes that*.
> >
> > **This does not make the finding weak.** It makes it a different shape, and the shape decides
> > what you may do with it.
>
> **Three of the four can be improved dishonestly.** Deploy a trivial change hourly and your
> frequency rises. Measure lead time from the final approval and it falls. Define *failure*
> narrowly and your failure rate drops. **Time to restore is the one that resists this, because the
> customer is the clock.**
>
> **What the figures do not show.**
>
> - **Causation.** Nothing here licenses *if we deploy more often, we will recover faster*.
> - **Your industry.** A sample of self-selected respondents is not a census of software.
> - **Comparability between years.** The boundaries are recalculated, so a league-table position is
>   not a stable thing.
> - **Anything about an individual.** These measure a **delivery system**. Using any of them to
>   assess a person is a misuse the authors warn against.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** All four, measured the same way every month, reported together, shown
> as our own trend and never as a group label — and *failure* is defined in writing before anybody
> counts one.
>
> *Sources: Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble and Gene Kim, Accelerate, 2018, and the annual State of
> DevOps reports of the DORA research program from 2014 onwards: the four delivery measures, the
> finding that speed and stability are not opposed, and the performance groups derived by cluster
> analysis of each year's survey responses. The data is self-reported and the respondents are
> self-selected; the research establishes associations and argues for predictive relationships rather
> than reporting controlled experiments; and the measures describe a delivery system rather than any
> individual.*

### 1 · Correlation and cause

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-06-06%%

> **Grace:** Read me the line you want to put in the board paper.
> **Adeel:** *Deploying more often causes faster recovery.*
> **Grace:** Then somebody will ask you for the experiment, and there is not one. The research is a
> survey. What it found is that the two go together, strongly and repeatedly.
> **Adeel:** *Deploying more often is associated with faster recovery.*
> **Grace:** Correct, defensible, and slightly dull — which is the right price. There is a ladder of
> these phrases and it is worth knowing where each rung sits. *Is associated with* and *correlates
> with* claim only that two things move together. *Predicts* claims you can estimate one from the
> other. *Leads to* and *causes* claim a mechanism.
> **Adeel:** Which can I use for the published finding?
> **Grace:** The first three, with the method named. For the last two you need an experiment or a
> mechanism you can describe. **And here you can describe one: a small change is easier to
> understand, so it is easier to undo — that is a mechanism, and you may say so as your own
> reasoning rather than as their finding.**
> **Adeel:** What about hedging?
> **Grace:** *Tends to* is the most useful word in this family: *teams that deploy often tend to
> recover faster.* It admits the exceptions without weakening the point. And *suggests*: *the data
> suggests*. **Use *suggests* when you are reporting somebody else's result and *I think* when you
> are giving your own. Mixing those two is how a team's opinion ends up in a board paper with a
> citation attached.**
> **Adeel:** And if somebody says *correlation is not causation*?
> **Grace:** Agree, and then say what would settle it. **The sentence is true and it is usually used
> to end a conversation rather than improve it. *What would we have to see to be convinced?* is the
> better move, and almost nobody makes it.**

*Which three expressions may Adeel use for the published finding?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Any measure that gets attached to a target changes behavior, and the behavior it changes most is
measuring. Ask for deployment frequency and you will get deployment frequency: some of it real, some
of it produced. That is not dishonesty by the people doing it — it is a predictable response to
being asked for one number out of four that only make sense together. The research these numbers
come from is unusually careful about this, and the care is routinely stripped off in the retelling:
*associated with* becomes *causes*, a cluster from one year's survey becomes a permanent rank, and a
measure of a delivery system becomes a measure of a person. The defense is to report the set, keep
your own trend rather than a league position, and treat the measure that gets worse as the most
interesting line on the chart.

**Discuss:** Which single number is your team asked for, and what would reporting the set change?

> changes have in common.
>
> **Why frequency excludes configuration-only deployments.** Nine such deployments were made last
> quarter that would previously have been bundled into other changes. **Counting them would have
> made the figure forty-one and told you less.**
>
> **What we are not claiming.** We are **not** quoting a performance group. The published groups are
> derived by **cluster analysis of each year's survey responses**, so the boundaries move and a team
> can change group **without changing anything**. We report **our own trend**.
>
> **And on the research.** The published finding is that speed and stability **are associated**, not
> that one causes the other; the data is **self-reported** and the respondents **self-selected**. We
> find the mechanism plausible — **a smaller change is easier to understand and therefore easier to
> undo** — and we say that as our own reasoning.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Anything about individuals.** These four describe a delivery system, and
> using them to assess a person is a misuse the authors themselves warn against.
>
> *Any one of these four can be improved on its own. Three of them can be improved by cutting
> corners. Reporting one of them alone is not a report.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *how it is produced* column prevents the most common dispute, which is not
> about the number but about whether two quarters were counted the same way.
