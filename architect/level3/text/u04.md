# Unit 4 · The Part That Will Not Divide
%%SUB Add workers to a job and one part of it stays exactly as long as it was%%
%%GOALS
Use multiples and factors
Explain the limit on speed-up
Say which assumption a speed-up claim makes
%%

Give a job to twice as many workers and it does not take half as long, because part of it cannot be
shared. A paper from 1967 turned that into a limit: if one twentieth of the work is sequential, you
cannot go more than twenty times faster, however much hardware you buy. A paper from 1988 pointed
out that the first paper assumes something about your problem that is usually false — and both
papers are right.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When two papers disagree, look for the assumption rather than the error. Most
> famous disagreements in this field are two correct answers to two different questions.

%%FIG c04-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, two lines giving the two papers with their years and venues, a table of sequential fractions beside their ceilings, a boxed paragraph on the two assumptions, a short list of what neither paper covers, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 28
> *Platform team · speed-up and scaling · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The two papers.** Gene Amdahl, *Validity of the single processor approach to achieving large
> scale computing capabilities*, in the proceedings of a conference in **1967**. And John Gustafson,
> *Reevaluating Amdahl's Law*, in **Communications of the ACM**, **1988**. Twenty-one years apart,
> and they are usually quoted against each other.
>
> **The 1967 result.** If a fraction of a job **cannot be shared out**, that fraction takes the same
> time however many workers you add. So the speed-up has a **ceiling**, and the ceiling is **one
> divided by the sequential fraction**.
>
> | Sequential part | Ceiling on speed-up |
> |---|---|
> | **1 in 100** | **100 times** |
> | **1 in 20** (5%) | **20 times** |
> | **1 in 10** | **10 times** |
> | **1 in 5** | **5 times** |
>
> **And you reach the ceiling slowly.** With a sequential part of one twentieth, **sixteen workers
> give you about nine times** the speed of one — a little over half the ceiling, for sixteen times
> the hardware.
>
> **The 1988 answer.**
>
> > The later paper does not dispute the arithmetic. It disputes the **question**. The 1967 result
> > assumes the **problem stays the same size** while the machine grows, and asks how much faster
> > the same work finishes.
> >
> > In practice, says the 1988 paper, **nobody buys a bigger machine to do yesterday's work
> > faster**. They do a bigger job in the same time: a finer model, more data, more customers. If the
> > problem **grows with the machine**, the sequential part becomes a **smaller share** of a larger
> > whole, and the achievable speed-up is **close to linear**.
> >
> > **So the two papers answer two different questions**, and the question you are actually being
> > asked decides which one applies. *Will this report finish sooner?* is the first. *Can we handle
> > ten times the customers?* is the second.
>
> **What neither paper covers.**
>
> - **Coordination overhead.** Workers that must agree with each other create work that **grows with
>   their number**. Both papers leave it out, and it is why real speed-up curves **rise, flatten and
>   then fall**.
> - **Cost.** Sixteen times the hardware for nine times the speed may be an excellent deal or an
>   absurd one. Neither paper has an opinion.
> - **Where the sequential part is.** Finding it is the whole of the engineering, and the arithmetic
>   does not help.
> - **Variance.** Both are about the time the whole job takes, not about how that time is
>   distributed.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Before adding workers we write down the sequential fraction, the
> resulting ceiling, and which of the two questions we are answering — because half our scaling
> arguments have been two people answering different questions correctly.
>
> *Sources: Gene M. Amdahl, Validity of the single processor approach to achieving large scale
> computing capabilities, AFIPS conference proceedings, 1967: the bound on speed-up imposed by the
> sequential fraction of a fixed-size problem. John L. Gustafson, Reevaluating Amdahl's Law,
> Communications of the ACM, 1988: the observation that problem size in practice grows with the
> machine, giving a near-linear scaled speed-up. Neither paper models coordination overhead, cost,
> or the distribution of completion times.*

### 1 · Multiples and factors

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-04-06%%

> **Marek:** Read me your result.
> **Adeel:** *With sixteen workers it is nine times more fast.*
> **Marek:** *Faster*, not *more fast* — one syllable adjectives take *-er*. And then there is a trap
> in the number itself. *Nine times faster* and *nine times as fast* mean the same thing to most
> readers, but a careful one will ask whether you mean nine times the speed or ten.
> **Adeel:** Why ten?
> **Marek:** Because *three times taller* can be read as *three times as tall* or as *taller by three
> times its height*. In speech nobody minds. **In a document with money attached, write *nine times
> as fast as one worker* and the ambiguity is gone.**
> **Adeel:** And the formal version?
> **Marek:** *By a factor of*. *The speed-up improved by a factor of nine.* It is the phrase to use
> when a reader may be sceptical, because it sounds like arithmetic rather than enthusiasm. And the
> reverse: *a factor of nine slower*, or better, *it takes nine times as long*.
> **Adeel:** How do I say it the other way round — about time rather than speed?
> **Marek:** Use fractions, and be careful. *Nine times as fast* means *a ninth of the time*. Those
> are the same fact and people mix them up under pressure, usually in a meeting about money. **Say
> the speed and the duration in the same breath: nine times as fast, so a ninth of the time.** Then
> nobody has to divide.
> **Adeel:** And *doubled* or *halved*?
> **Marek:** Precise and convenient, and *doubled* is about the only multiple that nobody
> misreads. For anything else, give the number.

*Why does Marek prefer* nine times as fast as one worker?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Both of these papers are quoted constantly and almost always as weapons: one to argue that scaling
is futile, the other to argue that it is unlimited. Neither author claimed either thing. What
happened is ordinary and worth recognizing — a result with a memorable shape gets separated from its
assumption, and the assumption is the part that decides whether it applies to you. You can watch the
same process happen to any well-known idea in software within about five years of its publication.
The defense is a small habit: when you quote a result, quote its assumption in the same sentence.
*Speed-up is bounded by the sequential fraction, for a fixed problem size.* Eight extra words, and
they are the difference between a useful contribution and a quotation.

**Discuss:** Which result does your team quote without its assumption?

> | speed-up measured with twice the workers | **1.05 times** |
>
> **What that cost.** **Twice the hardware for about four and a half per cent less time.** On the
> fixed-job question, that was a bad trade and we should not have made it.
>
> **Why it is nevertheless the right direction.** On the **growing-job** question the arithmetic is
> different: as the data grows, the shareable part grows and **the merge stays at about an hour and
> ten**. At twice the volume, the same workers finish inside the window. **The hardware we bought
> was wasted this year and will not be next year** — and that sentence should have been in the
> original plan.
>
> **The real work.** **Shorten the merge.** Every lasting gain in this job is in that stage, and no
> amount of hardware touches it.
>
> **What the arithmetic does not tell us.** **Coordination**, which grows with the worker count and
> is why our curve flattened earlier than predicted. **Cost**, which neither paper has an opinion
> about. And **where** the sequential part is — finding the merge took an afternoon of timing, which
> is the only reason this page exists.
>
> **Not in this note.** **The window itself.** Whether four hours is the right deadline is a question
> for the operations team, not for us.
>
> *The bound on speed-up applies to a problem of fixed size. Our problem is not of fixed size, and
> the plan should have said so on its first line.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the question at the top, before the figures. A reader who knows whether you
> mean *sooner* or *more* can evaluate every number that follows; a reader who does not will
> evaluate them against the wrong one.
