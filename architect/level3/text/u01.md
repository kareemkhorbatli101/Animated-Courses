# Unit 1 · Nine Words for Good
%%SUB A published vocabulary for the argument your team has every quarter%%
%%GOALS
Classify and define
Name the characteristics of a quality model
Say what a model does not give you
%%

*Good* is the least useful word in an architecture review. There is a published model that replaces
it with nine characteristics, each divided into sub-characteristics, each with an agreed meaning.
What the model gives you is a shared vocabulary, which is worth more than it sounds. What it does
not give you is a single number, a target, or an opinion about your system.

> ## Meridian · architecture note 25
> *Platform team · the quality model · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO/IEC 25010:2023** is the **second edition** of the product quality model in the
> SQuaRE series. It was published in **November 2023** and replaced the **2011** edition. It gives a
> vocabulary: **nine characteristics**, each divided into sub-characteristics, each defined.
>
> **The nine.**
>
> | Characteristic | One of its sub-characteristics |
> |---|---|
> | **functional suitability** | functional correctness |
> | **performance efficiency** | capacity |
> | **compatibility** | interoperability |
> | **interaction capability** | learnability |
> | **reliability** | **availability** |
> | **security** | non-repudiation |
> | **maintainability** | testability |
> | **flexibility** | scalability |
> | **safety** | fail safe |
>
> **What changed in 2023.** Three things worth knowing. **Usability** became **interaction
> capability**, and gained sub-characteristics for **inclusivity** and **user engagement**.
> **Portability** became **flexibility**, and now includes **scalability** — which until 2023 had no
> home in the model at all, although every architect used the word daily. And **safety** was added
> as a ninth characteristic, with sub-characteristics including **hazard warning** and **fail safe**.
>
> **Availability is a sub-characteristic, not a characteristic.** It sits under reliability. This
> matters in a meeting: a team that reports *availability* as its quality figure has reported one
> part of one of nine characteristics, and the sentence sounds much broader than it is.
>
> **The model does not measure.**
>
> > There is no number anywhere in it. **Measurement is a separate part of the same series**, and
> > choosing what to measure for your own product is a separate activity again.
> >
> > So the model cannot tell you that your system is good, or better than last year, or better than
> > a competitor's. **It can tell you that you and your colleague are arguing about two different
> > characteristics**, which is more often the real problem.
>
> **What the model does not provide.**
>
> - **Targets or thresholds.** None. Not one number.
> - **Priorities.** Nothing here says reliability matters more than maintainability. For your system
>   that is a business decision, and it belongs in a document with a name against it.
> - **Measures.** Turning *testability* into a number is the subject of other documents in the
>   series.
> - **Quality in use.** How well a real person achieves a real goal is a **separate model** in the
>   same series. The product can score well on all nine and still serve nobody.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every quality statement in our reviews names a characteristic, and
> where we can, a sub-characteristic — because *good* has never once survived a follow-up question.
>
> *Source: ISO/IEC 25010:2023, Systems and software engineering — SQuaRE — Product quality model,
> second edition, replacing the 2011 edition: the nine characteristics and their sub-characteristics;
> the renaming of usability as interaction capability and of portability as flexibility; the addition
> of safety; and the position of availability and scalability within the model. The standard defines
> a vocabulary: it provides no measures, no targets, no thresholds and no priorities, and quality in
> use is the subject of a separate model in the same series.*

### 1 · Classifying and defining

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS3-01-06%%

> **Karin:** Read me the first line of your review summary.
> **Adeel:** *The service has good quality and some performance problems.*
> **Karin:** Two statements, one of which means nothing and one of which nearly does. Start again
> with the model, and use its structures. What does the model consist of?
> **Adeel:** *The model consists of nine characteristics, each of which is divided into
> sub-characteristics.*
> **Karin:** Good, and notice what you did: *consist of* for the whole set, *be divided into* for
> the parts. Those two carry most classification in English, and they are not interchangeable with
> *contain*.
> **Adeel:** Why not?
> **Karin:** *Contain* is about what is inside something — a document contains nine paths. *Consist
> of* says *this is everything it is made of*, with nothing left over. **If you write *the model
> consists of nine characteristics*, you have claimed there is no tenth.** That is a stronger
> statement than *contains*, and here it is the true one.
> **Adeel:** And placing one item in a group?
> **Karin:** *Fall under*, or *come under*: *availability falls under reliability*. In a formal
> document, *be classified as* or *be categorized as*. All of them take the group, not the item,
> after them.
> **Adeel:** How do I write a definition?
> **Karin:** *X is defined as Y*, and then say by whom. **A definition with no source is a
> preference with a confident tone.** *Capacity is defined in the standard as the maximum limit of a
> parameter* — now your reader can check you.

*What is the difference between* consist of *and* contain?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A quality model gives a team nine words where it had one, and the first effect is uncomfortable:
arguments get longer before they get shorter. Saying *good* ends a conversation; saying
*recoverability improved and time behavior got slightly worse* starts one, with a trade-off in it
that somebody will have to own. That is why vague quality language survives in companies full of
careful engineers — it is not laziness, it is conflict avoidance with a technical accent. The teams
that adopt a vocabulary successfully usually pair it with one small social change: naming the person
who chose each target. Once a number has an owner it can be renegotiated, and once it can be
renegotiated nobody needs to hide behind an adjective.

**Discuss:** In your company, which quality targets have no owner? What would happen if you asked?
