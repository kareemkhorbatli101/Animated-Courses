# Unit 9 · Seven Claims, All Optional
%%SUB A token says who it is about; it does not say what they may do%%
%%GOALS
Use gerunds and infinitives
Name the registered claims and say which are required
Say what a token does not decide
%%

A token is a small document that travels with a request and says something about who is making it.
There are seven claim names registered for it, and the document that registers them says, of every
single one, that using it is optional. The security of the whole arrangement lives somewhere else
entirely — which is the most misunderstood thing in this book.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Count the requirements. A document with many defined terms and few obligations
> is a vocabulary, and a vocabulary is not a guarantee.

%%FIG b09-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the document number and its date, a seven-row table of the registered claims with one line each, a boxed paragraph on what the document does not decide, a short list of the other documents involved, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 21
> *Platform team · tokens and claims · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document. RFC 7519**, *JSON Web Token*, was published in **May 2015**. It defines a compact
> way of carrying a set of **claims** — named statements — between two parties.
>
> **Seven registered claim names.**
>
> | Claim | Meaning | Required? |
> |---|---|---|
> | **issuer** | who made the token | **optional** |
> | **subject** | who the token is about | **optional** |
> | **audience** | who the token is intended for | **optional** |
> | **expiration time** | after which it **must not be accepted** | **optional** |
> | **not before** | before which it **must not be accepted** | **optional** |
> | **issued at** | when it was made | **optional** |
> | **identifier** | a name for this one token | **optional** |
>
> **Read the third column again.** The document says, of every one of the seven, that using it is
> **optional**. A token with no expiry, no audience and no issuer is a conforming token. **Every
> security property you associate with these claims comes from a decision somebody made in your
> code, not from this document.**
>
> **Two claims that do have teeth — when present.**
>
> > The **expiration time** carries an instruction: the token **must not be accepted on or after**
> > that time. Implementations **may** allow a little leeway for **clock skew**, and the document
> > suggests keeping that small — a couple of minutes, not an hour.
> >
> > The **audience** carries another: if it is present, the party processing the token **must**
> > identify itself with one of the values in it, and **must reject the token otherwise**. This is
> > the check that stops a token issued for one service being presented to another, and it is the
> > one most often skipped.
>
> **Times are seconds, not strings.** The three time claims are **numeric dates**: seconds counted
> from the start of 1970, in universal time. Not a formatted date, not a local time, and not
> milliseconds — a mistake that produces tokens valid for fifty thousand years.
>
> **What this document does not do.**
>
> - **It does not decide what a claim entitles anybody to do.** Nothing here maps a claim to a
>   permission. That mapping is yours, and it is where the real decisions live.
> - **It does not define the signature.** How a token is signed or encrypted, and which algorithms
>   exist, are **separate documents**. A token may be carried in a form that is **not signed at
>   all**.
> - **It does not make the payload secret.** The claims are **encoded, not encrypted**. Anybody
>   holding the token can read them, and the tools to do it are a web page away.
> - **It does not say how long a token should live.** No duration is recommended anywhere.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** We verify the signature with an expected algorithm and a trusted key,
> we reject a token with no expiry or no audience, we allow sixty seconds of skew and no more, and
> the mapping from a claim to a permission is written down in one place with the name of the person
> who approved it.
>
> *Source: RFC 7519, JSON Web Token, May 2015: the seven registered claim names and their meanings;
> the statement that use of each is optional; the requirement not to accept a token on or after its
> expiration time, with the allowance of a small leeway for clock skew; the requirement that a party
> processing a token identify itself with a value in the audience claim and otherwise reject the
> token; and the numeric date representation in seconds since 1970 in universal time. Signature,
> encryption and algorithm definitions are separate documents, and the claims are encoded rather than
> encrypted.*

<sub>**a registered claim** — a claim name with an agreed meaning · **a numeric date** — seconds since
the start of 1970, in universal time · **clock skew** — the difference between two systems'

### 1 · Gerunds and infinitives

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-09-06%%

> **Oksana:** Read me your sentence from the review.
> **Adeel:** *We should avoid to accept tokens without an expiry.*
> **Oksana:** *Avoid* takes the *-ing* form: *avoid accepting*. There is no rule you can derive —
> each verb takes one pattern or the other, and you learn them in groups.
> **Adeel:** Which groups?
> **Oksana:** *-ing* after verbs about habits and reactions: *avoid*, *consider*, *involve*, *risk*,
> *suggest*, *finish*, *keep*. The infinitive after verbs about decisions and attempts: *decide*,
> *agree*, *refuse*, *fail*, *need*, *expect*, *intend*, *manage*. **Decisions take *to*; habits take
> *-ing*.** That covers most of what you will write.
> **Adeel:** And after a preposition?
> **Oksana:** Always *-ing*. *Before verifying the signature.* *Without checking the audience.*
> *Responsible for reviewing the mapping.* If a preposition is in front of it, the verb ends in
> *-ing*, with no exceptions worth remembering.
> **Adeel:** What about *to* on its own?
> **Oksana:** That is the other great use, and it is the most valuable one in a security review: the
> infinitive of **purpose**. *We check the audience **to stop** a token being reused elsewhere.* It
> answers *why*, in three words, where most engineers write two sentences. **A review comment that
> gives the purpose is acted on; one that gives only the rule is argued with.**
> **Adeel:** Are there verbs that take both?
> **Oksana:** A few, and two of them matter. *Start* and *continue* take either with no real change.
> But *remember to check* and *remember checking* are completely different: the first is a thing you
> must do, the second is a memory. **In a document, write the one you mean and read it back.**

*What does Oksana say about verbs after a preposition?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The checks that fail during development get written; the checks that only fail in production do not.
A missing signature check breaks everything on your laptop in the first five minutes. A missing
audience check breaks nothing, ever, until a token arrives from somewhere it was not meant to —
which is exactly the day it matters. This asymmetry quietly shapes every codebase: the protections
that survive are the ones that were annoying early. It is worth saying out loud in a design review,
because it tells you where to look. Ask which of our checks would still pass if we deleted it, and
you have found this year's security work. The honest version of this unit's lesson is not *read the
specification* but *notice which rules nothing enforces*, because those are the ones that are quietly
missing everywhere.

**Discuss:** Which check in your system would you not notice if somebody deleted it?

>
> **What we require.** A token **with no expiry or no audience claim is rejected**. The specification
> makes both claims **optional**; **we do not**.
>
> **Clock skew.** **Sixty seconds**, and no more. A larger window is a longer life for a stolen
> token.
>
> **Times.** The time claims are **seconds** since the start of 1970, in universal time.
> **Milliseconds produce a token valid for fifty thousand years**, and we have seen it twice.
>
> **What a claim does not decide.** A claim is an **assertion by the issuer**. What it entitles
> anybody to do **here** is in the mapping in section 9.4, which is **one file**, reviewed quarterly,
> with the approver's name against each entry.
>
> **What the claims are not.** **Encoded, not encrypted.** Anybody holding the token can read every
> claim. **Nothing confidential goes in a token.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **Key rotation.** That is section 9.6.
>
> *Verifying a signature proves that the token is unaltered. It proves nothing about whether the
> token was meant for us.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Number the checks and give each one a purpose. *Why* in four words per row is
> what stops somebody removing a check they do not understand.
