## How to use this book

Book 1 was about the agreements underneath every field: a date, a character, a number, a code. This
book is about **the promise between two systems** — the interface, the contract, and what happens
when one side changes it.

**Twelve units**, twenty pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | A real specification, with its number, its date and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a long dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `KS2-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Every source is a document somebody has to live with.** A description format, a validation
vocabulary, a method that is neither safe nor idempotent, a caching specification, the syntax of a
URI, an event envelope, a schema resolution rule, a header that announces a shutdown, a token's
seven optional claims, a naming procedure, an error format and two wire protocols that mean exactly
the same thing. Each reading text names **the document, its number, its date, and what the document
says it does not do**.

**The twelve traps are all the same trap.** Somebody read a specification as a promise it never
made. A description is not an implementation. A schema that validates says nothing about meaning. A
media type is a label, not a guarantee. An optional claim is optional. A header that announces a
shutdown obliges nobody. And a new protocol version changes the wire, not the meaning. Half the
integration arguments in this industry are two people quoting the same document at each other,
having read different sentences in it.

**The grammar is the grammar of agreement.** The present continuous for what is happening now
against the present simple for what is always true; articles, because *a schema* and *the schema*
are different claims; the present perfect for what has changed; modals of possibility for what a
cache may do; relative clauses for the part of a URI that is sent and the part that is not; reported
speech for what a document says; the first conditional for what will happen if you add a field;
future forms for a date that is coming; gerunds and infinitives; compound nouns; and the linking
words that make a refusal readable.

**Four sentences you will write until they are automatic.** *Is that the description or the
service?* *What happens to a field you did not mention?* *Is that safe, or idempotent, or neither?*
And *who is obliged by this, and by when?*

Two new voices arrive in this book because interfaces have two sides. **Yara Haddad** is an

units in this book end the way they do.

The company is called **Meridian**, and its customers — **Northwind Rail**, **Alba Health**, **Cobalt
Freight**, **Verano Retail** and **Tarn Energy** — are invented, on purpose.

## Nothing here is official

This is an independent teaching draft. It is **not published or endorsed by any standards body**,
and it carries no official status. Every person, team, company, system, incident and review in it
was written for this book. The specifications quoted in the reading texts are real, and each text
names the document, its date, and the limits its own publisher states.
