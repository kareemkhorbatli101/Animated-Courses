Eleven units have been about promises: what a description promises, what a schema promises, what a
token proves, what a label guarantees. This one is about two documents that changed how messages
travel and deliberately changed nothing about what they mean — and about writing the one page that

> ## Meridian · architecture note 24
> *Platform team · protocol versions · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The documents.** **RFC 9113**, *HTTP/2*, and **RFC 9114**, *HTTP/3*, were both published in
> **June 2022**. The first replaced a document from 2015. Each describes a **way of expressing
> messages on a connection** — and each says, in its own words, that the **meaning** of those
> messages is defined elsewhere.
>
> **Two columns.**
>
> | What changed | What did not |
> |---|---|
> | messages are carried in **binary frames** | the **methods** |
> | several **streams** share one connection | the **status codes** and their classes |
> | field names are **compressed** | the **caching** rules |
> | the newer version runs over a **different transport** | **field names** and what they mean |
> | | **everything your interface document says** |
>
> **One rule that reaches a developer.**
>
> > Field names must be in **lower case**. A message containing an upper-case field name is
> > **malformed** and is to be treated as an error.
> >
> > In practice a library does this for you, and in practice somebody's log parser, test fixture or
> > hand-written request does not. **It is the one line of these documents that will cost your team
> > an afternoon.**
>
> **Why the versions exist.** Several exchanges on one connection, and smaller headers. Both are
> real and both are about **how fast** and **how many** — not about **what**. The newer version also
> changes the transport underneath, which matters on a poor network and matters to the people who
> run the servers.
>
> **Why it matters that nothing else changed.** Because it means an interface can outlive the way it
> travels. **Your document describing methods, status codes, schemas, caching, tokens and errors is
> as true on the new version as on the old.** That is a deliberate piece of design, and it is the
> reason a protocol can be modernized without the world rewriting its documentation.
>
> **What these documents do not define.**
>
> - **The meaning of a method, a status code or a header.** All of that is elsewhere.
> - **Your performance.** Several streams on one connection helps when there are many requests. It
>   is not a promise about any single one.
> - **Whether your clients can use it.** Version negotiation and deployment are operational matters.
> - **Caching, authentication or error formats.** Each is its own document, and each of them is in
>   this book.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** The protocol version is in our operations document, not our interface
> document, and the only interface consequence we have ever had to write down is the lower-case rule.
>
> *Source: RFC 9113, HTTP/2, and RFC 9114, HTTP/3, both June 2022, the first replacing the 2015
> specification: the binary framing and stream multiplexing, the field compression, the requirement
> that field names be lower case and that messages containing upper-case field names be treated as
> malformed, and the transport change in the newer version. Both documents define message expression
> only; the semantics of methods, status codes, header fields, caching and content are defined in
> separate specifications.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The design decision in this unit is quiet and enormous: two new protocol versions that deliberately
changed nothing about meaning. It would have been easy to improve a few status codes while the
bonnet was open, and the cost of that would have been every piece of documentation in the world
needing review. Separating what a thing means from how it travels is what allowed the transport to
be modernized twice without anybody rewriting their interface. The same discipline is available
inside your own company, at a smaller scale, and it is the most useful habit in these two books: keep
the promises in one document and the arrangements in another, and when somebody asks you to add a
line, ask which of the two it changes. Teams that cannot answer that question end up with one
document that says everything and promises nothing.

**Discuss:** In your company, which document would a customer read to decide whether to change their
code? Is it the one you would want them to read?

> | that a **cache** somewhere does not hold an older copy | we publish lifetimes; we cannot clear their equipment |
>
> **Where each promise is written.** Section 3 for fields. Section 11 for problem types. Section 4
> for caching lifetimes. Section 6 for events. This page for the notice period. **If it is not on
> this page or in a numbered section, we have not promised it.**
>
> **How it changes.** A change that requires you to change code gets **four months** and a **named
> contact we expect a reply from**. Everything else is published nightly in the change log.
> **Operational changes — protocol versions, regions, deployment — are in the operations document
> and never require you to act.**
>
> **Not in this contract.** **Price, availability targets and support hours.** Those are in the
> agreement, not in the engineering documents.
>
> *Our error wording, our field order and our protocol version are ours to change. Our field names,
> our problem types and our notice period are yours to rely on. This page exists so that nobody has
> to guess which is which.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The second table is the one that builds trust, and it is the one every team
> leaves out. A document that only lists promises reads as marketing; a document that says plainly
> what you may not rely on reads as engineering.
