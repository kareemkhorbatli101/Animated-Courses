# Unit 6 · Who Says So, and Which One
%%SUB Two fields let a reader recognize an event they have already seen%%
%%GOALS
Use reported speech and *that*-clauses
Name the four fields an event must carry
Say what an envelope does not promise
%%

An event is a message saying that something happened. The envelope around it is small: four fields
that every event must carry, and four more it may. Two of those four do something that matters more
than they look: together they let a reader recognize a message they have already processed. And
nothing in the envelope promises that a message will arrive once, in order, or at all.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A short specification is short because it has pushed the hard parts into other
> documents. Find out which ones before you rely on it.

%%FIG b06-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the specification and its version, a two-part table of required and optional fields, a boxed paragraph on the pair that makes an event unique, a short list of what the specification does not define, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 18
> *Platform team · event envelopes · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The specification.** **CloudEvents**, version **1.0.2**, is a specification published by a
> working group under the **Cloud Native Computing Foundation**. It describes a small envelope: a set
> of named fields that travel with an event, whatever the event is about and whatever carries it.
>
> **Four fields that must be there.**
>
> | Field | What it holds |
> |---|---|
> | the **spec version** | which version of this envelope is in use — *1.0* for this one |
> | the **source** | **where** the event happened, written as an address |
> | the **type** | **what kind** of event it is, conventionally a dotted name |
> | the **id** | which event this is, within that source |
>
> **Four more that may be there.** A **timestamp**, the **content type** of the data, a reference to
> the **schema** of the data, and a **subject** — a short name for the thing the event is about,
> useful to a consumer that must decide whether to care before reading the data.
>
> **The pair that does the real work.**
>
> > The specification requires a producer to ensure that the combination of **source** and **id** is
> > **unique for each distinct event**. That is the whole mechanism, and it is enough: a consumer
> > that has seen a pair before knows it is looking at a copy.
> >
> > The specification notes the consequence directly. Two events carrying the same source and id
> > **may be assumed to be duplicates** by a consumer. **So a producer that reuses an identifier has
> > not merely broken a rule; it has told every reader that two different things are the same
> > thing.**
>
> **What this specification does not define.** This list is longer than the specification and it is
> the reason the specification is usable.
>
> - **Delivery.** Nothing here says an event arrives, arrives once, or arrives at all. That is the
>   business of whatever carries it.
> - **Order.** Nothing here promises that events arrive in the order they happened.
> - **The transport.** How an event travels over a particular protocol is a **separate binding
>   document** for each one.
> - **The data format.** The envelope is agnostic about what is inside it. Even the way the whole
>   event is serialized is a separate format document.
> - **The meaning of your types.** The dotted name is a convention. What *shipment.delayed* means in
>   your company is yours to write down.
> - **Authorization.** Who may publish to a source, and who may read it, is nowhere in here.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every event we publish carries the four required fields with a source
> and an id that are unique together, every consumer we write keeps a record of the pairs it has
> handled, and the delivery promise is written down per transport rather than assumed.
>
> *Source: the CloudEvents specification, version 1.0.2, published by the CloudEvents working group
> under the Cloud Native Computing Foundation: the four required context attributes; the optional
> attributes including time, data content type, data schema and subject; and the requirement that a
> producer ensure the combination of source and id is unique for each distinct event, with the
> consequence that a consumer may treat a repeated pair as a duplicate. The specification defines no
> transport, no delivery or ordering guarantee, no data format and no authorization model; protocol
> bindings and event formats are separate documents.*

### 1 · Reported speech

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-06-06%%

> **Ravi:** Read me your line to the customer.
> **Adeel:** *The spec says: the combination of source and id must be unique.*
> **Ravi:** You have quoted it, which is honest, and you have also made the reader do the work of
> connecting it to their problem. Report it instead: *the specification requires the producer to make
> the combination of source and id unique for each event.*
> **Adeel:** Why is reporting better?
> **Ravi:** Because reporting lets you name **who** is obliged. A quotation is a sentence floating
> in the air. A report has a subject: the producer, the consumer, us. **In an integration argument,
> the person who names the obliged party first usually wins — not by rhetoric, but because the
> argument becomes answerable.**
> **Adeel:** How do I build it?
> **Ravi:** Usually with *that*: *the specification says that order is not addressed*. You can leave
> *that* out in speech, and I would keep it in writing. Some verbs take a different shape: *requires
> somebody to do something*, *allows somebody to do something*. Those are the obligation verbs and
> they are the useful ones here.
> **Adeel:** And when I report what a person said?
> **Ravi:** Then the tense usually moves back one step. *We are sending an identifier* becomes *they
> said they were sending an identifier*. *We have fixed it* becomes *they said they had fixed it*.
> **Adeel:** Even if it is still true?
> **Ravi:** You may leave the tense alone when it is still true, and both are correct. But here is
> the thing worth knowing: **moving the tense back quietly signals that you are reporting rather
> than endorsing.** *They said the identifier was unique* is a statement about them. *They said the
> identifier is unique* leans towards agreeing with them. On an incident call, choose on purpose.

*Why does Ravi prefer a report to a quotation?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

*At least once* is a precise technical promise that almost nobody hears precisely. Read carefully, it
says duplicates are a normal part of the contract and handling them is the reader's job. Read
quickly, it sounds like a reassurance — *don't worry, it will get there* — and whole systems get
built on the second reading. The pattern is worth recognizing because it is everywhere in
distributed systems: *eventually consistent*, *best effort*, *approximately*. Each is an honest
description of a limit, and each is routinely read as a description of a strength. When you publish
a promise in that family, assume the optimistic reading will happen, and say the pessimistic half
out loud: *duplicates are normal; here is the field you compare*. When you consume one, read it as
its authors meant it — as a floor, not a ceiling.

>
> | | |
