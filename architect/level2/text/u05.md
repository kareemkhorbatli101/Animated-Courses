# Unit 5 · Five Parts and a Fragment
%%SUB One of the five parts never reaches your server%%
%%GOALS
Use defining and non-defining relative clauses
Name the five parts of an address
Say what two addresses tell you about one thing
%%

An address has five parts, and the rules for each of them are different. One part is compared
without regard to capital letters and one is not. One of them is cut off before the request is even
sent, which means a value put there never arrives. And two addresses that look different may point
at exactly the same thing, with no way to tell.

%%FIG b05-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the document number and its date, a five-row table of the parts of an address with one line each, a boxed paragraph about the part that is separated off, a short list of what the document does not define, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 17
> *Platform team · addresses · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document. RFC 3986**, *Uniform Resource Identifier (URI): Generic Syntax*, was published in
> **January 2005**. It replaced a document from 1998 and has not been replaced since. It defines the
> **syntax** of an address: what the parts are, which characters may appear where, and how to
> compare two addresses.
>
> **Five parts, in this order.**
>
> | Part | Introduced by | Compared |
> |---|---|---|
> | **scheme** | — (ends with a colon) | **without regard to case** |
> | **authority** | `//` | the host **without regard to case** |
> | **path** | `/` | **with** regard to case |
> | **query** | `?` | with regard to case |
> | **fragment** | `#` | **never sent** |
>
> **The part that never arrives.**
>
> > The fragment is **separated from the rest of the address before the address is dereferenced**.
> > In practice: the client keeps it and the server never receives it. It identifies something
> > **inside** what comes back, and the interpretation is the business of whatever came back.
> >
> > So a value placed there is **invisible to your service** and **fully visible** in the browser,
> > in the history and to anything running in the page. Teams have put tokens there to keep them out
> > of server logs. The effect is the opposite of the intention.
>
> **Characters.** A small set is **unreserved** and may appear anywhere: letters, digits, and four
> punctuation marks. Others are **reserved**, because they have a job in the syntax — the colon, the
> slash, the question mark, the hash sign and several more. A reserved character used as **data**
> must be **percent-encoded**: written as a percent sign and two hexadecimal digits. **A slash inside
> a segment is not a slash; it is encoded, or it is a new segment.**
>
> **Comparing two addresses.** The document gives a **ladder** of methods, from cheapest to most
> expensive: compare the strings as they are; put both into a standard form first; apply what the
> scheme says about its own defaults; and finally, ask the protocol. Each step up finds more real
> matches and costs more. And it states the principle plainly: **a false negative is better than a
> false positive** — better to think two addresses differ when they do not than to treat two
> different things as one.
>
> **What this document does not define.**
>
> - **What an address means.** It defines syntax. What a path segment stands for in your service is
>   your design.
> - **Whether an address can be fetched.** An address identifies; it does not promise to answer.
> - **Whether two addresses identify the same thing.** Two different addresses may well point at one
>   resource, and **nothing in the syntax can tell you**.
> - **Your query conventions.** Repeated names, lists, nested values: all local custom, none of it
>   in here.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Nothing sensitive goes in an address at all, the path identifies and
> the query filters, and anything that compares two addresses normalizes them first and prefers to
> say *different*.
>
> *Source: RFC 3986, Uniform Resource Identifier (URI): Generic Syntax, January 2005: the five
> components and their delimiters; the case-insensitive comparison of scheme and host; the
> separation of the fragment before dereference; the unreserved and reserved character sets and
> percent-encoding; and the ladder of comparison methods, with the stated preference for false
> negatives over false positives. The document defines syntax only, and no syntactic test can
> determine whether two addresses identify the same resource.*

and two hexadecimal digits · **a false positive** — treating two different things as one</sub>

### 1 · Defining and non-defining relative clauses

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-05-06%%

> **Ines:** Read me the sentence you wrote for the customer.
> **Adeel:** *The fragment which is never sent holds the heading.*
> **Ines:** Add two commas and you have written what you mean. *The fragment, which is never sent,
> holds the heading.* Without the commas you are telling me there are several fragments and you mean
> the one that is never sent.
> **Adeel:** So the commas change the meaning.
> **Ines:** Completely. A clause without commas **defines** which one you mean, so it narrows the
> group. A clause with commas **adds information** about something already identified. *The part
> that is never sent* narrows: of the five parts, this one. *The fragment, which is never sent*
> adds: you know which part, here is a fact about it.
> **Adeel:** When do I use *that* and when *which*?
> **Ines:** For a defining clause about a thing, both work and *that* is more common in technical
> writing. For a non-defining clause, **only *which***, and never *that*. For people, *who*. And
> *whose* for possession, with people or things: *a segment whose value contains a slash*.
> **Adeel:** Can I leave the pronoun out?
> **Ines:** When it is the **object** of the clause, yes, and it reads better: *the value we put
> there never arrives.* When it is the **subject**, you cannot: *the part that is never sent* needs
> its *that*. **If you can remove the pronoun and the sentence still works, English prefers it
> removed.**
> **Adeel:** And in a specification?
> **Ines:** Keep the pronoun. A document is read by somebody at eleven at night who is not a native
> speaker. **Clear beats elegant in anything somebody has to act on.**

*What do the two commas change in Adeel's sentence?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Almost everybody in software learns the syntax of an address from examples, and examples teach you
what works rather than what is true. You can build a career on the pattern-matching — a question
mark here, an ampersand there — and never meet the sentence that says one of the five parts is
removed before the request is made. That is not a personal failing; it is how working knowledge
accumulates under time pressure, and most of it is correct most of the time. What makes the gaps
expensive is that they are invisible from inside: nothing in your day tells you which parts of your
model are missing. The cheap remedy is oddly old-fashioned. Once a quarter, read the actual document
for one thing you use every hour. You will find something, and you will usually find it in a
sentence somebody wrote twenty years ago precisely because they knew you would assume otherwise.

> | **query** | **how you want it**: filters, page size, sort order | anything secret **unless** the field is excluded from the log |
> | **fragment** | a position inside the response | **anything our service needs** — it never reaches us |
>
> **Case.** The **scheme** and the **host** are compared **without regard to case**. The **path** and
> the **query** are **case-sensitive**. We generate lower-case hosts and we do not change the case of
> a path we were given.
>
> **Encoding.** A reserved character inside a value is **percent-encoded**. **A slash inside a
> segment is encoded or it is a new segment** — there is no third option.
>
> **Comparing.** Anything that compares two addresses **normalizes both first**, and **prefers to
> report them as different**. Treating two different addresses as one has caused us an incident;
> treating one as two has caused a cache miss.
>
> **Secrets.** **Nothing single-use goes in an address at all.** Reset tokens are in the request
> body. The relevant log fields are excluded in configuration, which is reviewed every quarter.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Our query conventions** — repeated names, lists and ranges. Those are local
> custom and they are section 5.3.
>
> *A value in the fragment is invisible to us and fully visible to the browser, the page and the
> history.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *must never hold* column is the one that earns its space. A note that says
> only what is allowed leaves every other possibility open to a reader in a hurry.
