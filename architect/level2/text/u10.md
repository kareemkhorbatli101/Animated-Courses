# Unit 10 · A Name Is Not a Promise
%%SUB A label tells you how to read the bytes, and nothing about whether they are right%%
%%GOALS
Use compound nouns and word families
Explain the parts of a media type
Say what a label does not guarantee
%%

Every message carries a label saying what kind of thing it is. The label has a published format, a
public registry and a procedure for adding to it, and none of that makes the thing inside the
message correct. This unit is about a naming system that works extremely well at the one job it has
— and about how much weight teams put on a name that was never designed to carry it.

> **Reader's Tip** — A document about naming is really a document about avoiding collisions. Read it
> as an answer to the question *what stops two companies using the same word?*

%%FIG b10-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the document number, its date and its status, a four-row table of the trees with an example each, a boxed paragraph on the syntax suffix, a short list of what a label does not guarantee, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 22
> *Platform team · media types · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document. RFC 6838**, *Media Type Specifications and Registration Procedures*, was published
> in **January 2013**. It is a **Best Current Practice**, which is a kind of standards-track
> document about process rather than about bytes. It says how a media type is named, who may name
> one, and how it gets into the public registry.
>
> **A label has two parts and sometimes more.** A **type** before the slash, a **subtype** after it,
> and optionally **parameters** after a semicolon. The type and subtype are compared **without
> regard to case**, and so are the parameter names.
>
> **Four trees.**
>
> | Tree | Prefix | Who may register |
> |---|---|---|
> | **standards** | none | needs **standards-body** approval or a formal review |
> | **vendor** | `vnd.` | the company or organization behind the format |
> | **personal** | `prs.` | an individual |
> | **unregistered** | `x.` | **nobody registers it**; private or experimental use only |
>
> **So a company does not invent a name in the standards tree.** A format belonging to one company
> belongs in the vendor tree, with that company's name in it. That is not a formality: it is what
> stops two companies registering the same obvious word.
>
> **The suffix.**
>
> > A subtype name may end in a **plus sign** and the name of the **underlying syntax**. Those
> > suffixes are themselves registered, in their own registry.
> >
> > The value of this is practical and large. A tool that has **never heard of your type** can see
> > the suffix and know **how to parse the bytes**. Without it, your type is opaque to everything
> > except software written for it.
> >
> > **The suffix says how to open the box. It says nothing about what is in it.** A document can be
> > perfectly well-formed in the named syntax and complete nonsense in your terms.
>
> **Registration is a procedure, not an inspection.** An applicant fills in a **template**, the
> request is **reviewed**, and the name goes in the registry. The reviewer checks the request and the
> specification. **Nobody tests your software, and nothing about registration means an
> implementation conforms to anything.**
>
> **What a label does not guarantee.**
>
> - **That the content is valid.** Labeling a payload does not check it.
> - **That the sender implemented your format correctly.** A name is a claim by the sender.
> - **What the fields mean.** That is your schema, and the label cannot carry it.
> - **A version.** Nothing in here versions a format. Teams do put version numbers in subtypes, and
>   that is a local practice, not something this document defines.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Our own formats live in the vendor tree with our name in them, every
> one of them carries a syntax suffix, the registered ones are listed in our interface document, and
> a label is treated as a claim about the bytes and never as evidence about the content.
>
> *Source: RFC 6838, Media Type Specifications and Registration Procedures, January 2013, a Best
> Current Practice document: the type and subtype structure and parameters; the standards, vendor,
> personal and unregistered trees and their prefixes; structured syntax suffixes and their separate
> registry; the case-insensitive comparison of type, subtype and parameter names; and the
> registration template and review procedure. Registration records a name and a specification; it
> does not evaluate or certify any implementation, and the document does not define format
> versioning.*

### 1 · Compound nouns

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-10-06%%

> **Tomas:** Read me your heading.
> **Adeel:** *Procedure for the registration of the types of media.*
> **Tomas:** Correct English, and nobody writes like that. In technical English we stack the nouns:
> *media type registration procedure*. Four nouns, no prepositions, and every engineer in the world
> reads it instantly.
> **Adeel:** How do I know which order?
> **Tomas:** Read it backwards. The **last** noun is the thing; everything before it describes it. A
> *media type registration procedure* is a **procedure**. A *registration procedure template* is a
> **template**. **The head noun is always last in English**, which is the opposite of several other
> languages, and it is the single most useful fact about technical vocabulary.
> **Adeel:** Is there a limit?
> **Tomas:** Three is comfortable. Four is normal in our work. At five the reader starts guessing:
> *media type registration procedure review board*. Break it with a preposition at that point: *the
> review board for media type registration*.
> **Adeel:** And the plural?
> **Tomas:** Only the last noun takes it. *Media type**s***, not *medias type*. *Registration
> procedures*. The words in front are describing, so they stay singular — *a two-day review*, not *a
> two-days review*.
> **Adeel:** What about the word families?
> **Tomas:** Learn them in fours: *register*, *registration*, *registered*, *registrant*. *Specify*,
> *specification*, *specified*. *Conform*, *conformance*, *conformant*. **Half of sounding fluent in
> this work is picking the right member of a family you already know.**

*How does Tomas say you should read a stack of nouns?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Naming systems fail in a particular way: they work so well that people forget they are systems. A
media type looks like a word, so it gets chosen like a word — quickly, by whoever is writing the code
on Friday afternoon, from the vocabulary of the problem. The prefix that would have prevented a
collision looks like bureaucratic decoration until the day two companies send you the same name for
different things. There is a general lesson here about namespaces, and it applies to database
columns, metric names, configuration keys and event types just as much: **a name chosen in a shared
space is a claim on everybody else's vocabulary**. The cheap habit is to ask one question before
inventing any identifier that leaves your system — *who else could reasonably choose this word?* —
and if the answer is anybody, put your own name in front of it.

**Discuss:** Which names in your systems live in a shared space without a prefix?

> shared space we do not own. **Two customers have already invented the same name there.** That label
> is being replaced, and the replacement carries our prefix and a suffix.
>
> **Why every label carries a suffix.** A tool that has **never heard of our format** can read the
> suffix and **parse the bytes**. Without one, our payload is readable only by software written for
> us. **The suffix costs nothing and it is the most useful thing on the label.**
>
> **What a label is.** **The sender's claim about the bytes.** Not evidence about the content.
> Validation happens against the schema named in the last column, in the service, every time.
>
> **Private names.** Row three is a private, unregistered name and **will never be registered**,
> because it never leaves our own systems. **That is a legitimate position and it is written down
> here so that nobody registers it by mistake.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **How we version a format.** Nothing in the naming document defines that; our
> own practice is section 10.4.
>
> *Registration records a name and a specification. It tests nothing, certifies nobody, and says
> nothing about whether a sender implemented the format correctly.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Row four says *this is a fault*, in the table, in the shipped document. A note
> that lists only the parts you are proud of is a brochure.
