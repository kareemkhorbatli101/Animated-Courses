# Unit 8 · This Will Stop Working
%%SUB A date in a header is a warning, not a promise — and silence is not reassurance%%
%%GOALS
Use *will*, *going to* and the present continuous for the future
Announce an ending with a date
Say what an announcement does not oblige anybody to do
%%

Everything you publish will one day stop answering. The interesting question is not whether, but
how much notice the people who depend on it get, and in what form. There is a small published way
of putting a date on it — one header, one date — and understanding exactly how little it promises is
what makes it useful.

> **Reader's Tip** — Check what kind of document you are reading. Some documents set requirements.
> Some record a good idea so that everybody spells it the same way. Both are useful; only one
> obliges anybody.

%%FIG b08-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the document number, its date and its status, a two-row table showing the header and the link relation, a boxed paragraph on what the absence of the header means, a short list of what the document does not define, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 20
> *Platform team · announcing an ending · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document. RFC 8594**, *The Sunset HTTP Response Header Field*, was published in **May
> 2019**. Its status is **Informational**. That word is doing real work: the document is **not
> standards-track**. It registers a way of saying something, so that everybody who wants to say it
> says it the same way.
>
> **Two things are registered.**
>
> | | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | the **header field** | a **date** in a response, saying when the resource is **expected to become unresponsive** |
> | the **link relation** | a named link to a resource with **more information** about the ending |
>
> **What the date means.** It says the resource is **likely to become unresponsive** at that point.
> Read that carefully. It is a statement of **expectation**, not a contract. The date **may move**,
> in either direction, and the header on tomorrow's response is simply tomorrow's expectation.
>
> **The silence rule.**
>
> > The absence of the header **means nothing at all**. It does not mean the resource will keep
> > answering. It does not mean no ending is planned. It means nobody has put a date in a header.
> >
> > This is the opposite of how people read it. A client that checks for the header and finds none
> > has learned **nothing**, and a team that relies on the header to warn them has built on
> > somebody else's habit.
>
> **What the document does not define.** The list is long, and the document is honest about it.
>
> - **Any obligation.** Nobody is required to send the header, to honor the date, or to give notice
>   of any length. It is Informational, and that is what Informational means.
> - **What happens afterwards.** A refusal, a redirection, silence: not specified.
> - **Why.** The header carries a date and no reason. The reason belongs on the page the link points
>   at.
> - **A migration.** Nothing here tells a client what to use instead, or how to move. **The
>   replacement is named by you, in prose, somewhere a person will read it.**
> - **Notice periods.** No length is required or recommended. Three days and three years are equally
>   conforming.
>
> **So why use it?** Because it puts a date where a **machine** can find it, on every response,
> without anybody having to read an announcement. Six months from now, somebody who has never heard
> of your announcement will point a tool at your service, and the date will be there. **The header
> is for the person who was not listening when you announced it** — which, eventually, is everybody.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** An ending is announced to people, carried as a date in every
> response, linked to a page with the plan and the replacement, and given a notice period written in
> our own policy, because no document gives us one.
>
> *Source: RFC 8594, The Sunset HTTP Response Header Field, May 2019, an Informational document: the
> header field carrying a date at which the resource is expected to become unresponsive, and the
> registered link relation pointing to further information. The document creates no obligation, does
> not specify the behavior after the date, does not carry a reason, does not describe a migration,
> and recommends no notice period; the absence of the header carries no information.*

### 1 · Future forms

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-08-06%%

> **Grace:** Read me the three sentences you are sending to the customers.
> **Adeel:** *We will retire the old path. We will write to you next week. The new path will open in
> April.*
> **Grace:** Three *wills*, and two of them are weaker than what you actually mean. Which of those
> three is a decision that is already fixed with a date?
> **Adeel:** All of them. The dates are in the plan.
> **Grace:** Then use the forms that say so. *We are retiring the old path on 30 June.* Present
> continuous, with a date: that is English for an arrangement that exists. *We will retire it* sounds
> like an intention you formed while writing the sentence.
> **Adeel:** And *going to*?
> **Grace:** For an intention, especially one you are announcing. *We are going to retire the old
> path* is a decision, slightly less fixed than the continuous. The difference is small and real:
> **the continuous says the diary entry exists; *going to* says the decision does.**
> **Adeel:** When is *will* right?
> **Grace:** For predictions and for promises. *If nobody moves, four integrations will break* —
> that is a prediction, and *will* is exactly right. *We will answer within one working day* — that
> is a promise. **Use *will* when you are predicting or promising, and a dated form when you are
> announcing.**
> **Adeel:** And the new path opening?
> **Grace:** That is a timetable, so the present simple: *the new path opens on 1 April*. It is how
> English writes train departures and published schedules, and it reads as the most certain of all
> four — which is why it is the right form for something already published.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Every company has a process for announcing an ending, and the process is usually satisfied by
sending something rather than by anybody receiving it. That is not cynicism; it is what happens when
a process is written to be completed. The question *did we announce it* has a clean answer and the
question *did they hear it* has none, so the first one becomes the test. The teams that avoid this
have usually added one requirement that is annoying in exactly the right way: for anything that will
break a customer, a human reply is part of the notice. Silence stops counting as consent. It costs
somebody a week of chasing, which is far less than one nightly job stopping at a customer who
telephones at eight in the morning — and it also tells you something you would never otherwise
learn, which is which of your contact records are fiction.

**Discuss:** In your company, what counts as having told somebody? Who decided that?
