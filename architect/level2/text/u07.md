# Unit 7 · The Reader and the Writer
%%SUB Two schemas meet: the one the data was written with, and the one you are reading it with%%
%%GOALS
Use the first conditional
Say what happens when two schemas differ
Say why a default value is not a convenience
%%

Data outlives the code that wrote it. A record written last year is read by a program written this
morning, and the two have different ideas about what fields exist. There is a published set of rules
for exactly that meeting, and almost all of it comes down to one small decision you make when you
add a field: whether you give it a default value.

%%FIG b07-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line naming the specification and the release it belongs to, a four-row table of resolution cases with the outcome of each, a boxed paragraph on the field with no default, a short list of what the specification does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 19
> *Platform team · schema resolution · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The specification.** The **Apache Avro specification**, published with each release of the
> project, includes a section called **Schema Resolution**. It describes what happens when data
> written with one schema is read with another.
>
> **The two schemas.** Data is written with the **writer's schema**. It is read with the **reader's
> schema**. The writer's schema always travels with the data — in the file, or alongside the
> message — so the reader can always work out what the bytes mean. **The question is never *what are
> these bytes*. It is *what do they mean to me*.**
>
> **The four cases for a record.**
>
> | Case | Outcome |
> |---|---|
> | the **writer** wrote a field the reader does not have | the value is **ignored** |
> | the **reader** has a field the writer did not write, **with** a default | the **default is used** |
> | the **reader** has a field the writer did not write, with **no** default | **an error is signaled** |
> | the field was **renamed**, and the reader lists the old name as an alias | it is **matched** |
>
> **The case that fails.**
>
> > Read the third row again, because it is the only one that stops a program. **The danger is not
> > in the data. It is in the reader.** A field added to a reader's schema without a default value
> > makes every record that predates the field unreadable by that reader.
> >
> > And notice who is affected. The team that added the field has new data and sees nothing wrong.
> > The failure appears in whatever reads **history**: a monthly report, a backfill, a training job,
> > an audit. **The gap between the change and the failure can be weeks, and the two will not look
> > related.**
>
> **Numbers can widen.** A value written as a small integer may be read as a larger integer or as a
> floating-point number. A value written as text may be read as bytes, and the reverse. **Widening
> is allowed; narrowing is not**, and that asymmetry is worth remembering when somebody proposes
> *tightening* a type.
>
> **Enumerated values.** If the data holds a symbol the reader's list does not contain, the reader
> uses its **default** symbol if it has one, and otherwise **signals an error**. So the rule about
> defaults applies here too, in a place teams rarely look.
>
> **What the specification does not do.**
>
> - **It does not say whether your change is safe for your users.** It says how two schemas resolve.
>   Whether the result is acceptable is a judgment about your consumers.
> - **It does not version anything.** Two schemas are two documents. Nothing here numbers them or
>   records which came first.
> - **It does not check compatibility for you.** The checks teams rely on live in registries and
>   build tools, which are separate software implementing policies on top of these rules.
> - **It does not tell you what a sensible default is.** That is a question about your business, and
>   the only person who can answer it is the person adding the field.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every field we add carries a default value, chosen deliberately and
> written in the same change, and no schema change is merged until something has read a year-old
> record with the new schema.
>
> *Source: the Apache Avro specification, Schema Resolution section, as published with the project's
> releases: the writer's and reader's schemas; the rule that a field present in the writer's schema
> and absent from the reader's is ignored; the rule that a reader's field absent from the writer's
> schema takes the reader's default value, and that an error is signaled where no default exists;
> enumerated-symbol resolution with a reader default; field aliases; and the permitted numeric and
> string widenings. The specification does not define schema versioning, compatibility checking or
> a registry.*

### 1 · The first conditional

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-07-06%%

> **Priya:** Say what happens if the field has no default.
> **Adeel:** *If you would add a field without a default, old records would fail.*
> **Priya:** You have used the second conditional, which is for things you think are unlikely or
> imaginary. This is neither: somebody is going to do it next week. *If you add a field without a
> default, old records will fail.* Present tense in the *if* half, *will* in the other.
> **Adeel:** So the first conditional is for real possibilities.
> **Priya:** For things that may well happen, and for consequences you are predicting. Compare it
> with the zero conditional you learned last year: *if the first digit is four, the client made the
> mistake* — present and present, because that is always true, a fact about the protocol. Here the
> second half is in the future because it has not happened yet. **Zero for a rule, first for a
> prediction.**
> **Adeel:** Can I use *may* instead of *will*?
> **Priya:** Yes, and you should when you are less sure. *If we deploy this, the report may fail* —
> perhaps. *Will fail* — I am telling you it will. On a review, choose deliberately: **engineers who
> say *will* about everything are eventually ignored about everything.**
> **Adeel:** And *unless*?
> **Priya:** *Unless* means *if not*, and it is shorter: *the backfill will fail unless the field has
> a default*. Useful, and easy to get wrong — do not put a second negative in with it. *Unless the
> field does not have a default* says the opposite of what you meant.
> **Adeel:** What about the order?
> **Priya:** Either way round. Condition first, with a comma. Result first, no comma. Put the
> condition first when it is the part the reader must notice, which in a warning is almost always.

*What is the difference between the zero conditional and the first conditional?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The team that makes a change and the team that suffers from it are rarely the same team, and the gap
between them is usually measured in weeks and in reporting lines. A field added on the eleventh of
March breaks a job on the first of April, owned by somebody who has never met the author, who will
begin by assuming their own data is corrupt. Nothing in that sequence is anybody's fault, and the
pattern repeats in every company above a certain size. What makes it tractable is noticing that the
feedback is not missing but **slow** — the information exists, it just arrives in the wrong place
three weeks late. Teams that get this right do one unglamorous thing: they test against old data, in
the pipeline, every time. It is the only mechanism that makes a distant consequence visible to the
person causing it.

**Discuss:** Whose work breaks weeks after your changes, and how would you ever find out?

> ### Field note · changing a schema
> *Platform team · data document, section 7.1 · 5 March*
>
> **Four changes, four rules.**
>
> | Change | Rule | In the same change |
> |---|---|---|
> | **add** a field | **it must have a default** | one sentence saying what **absent** means |
> | **remove** a field | readers that still want it need a default | a note of which readers still want it |
> | **rename** a field | the reader **must list the old name as an alias** | both names, until every reader is updated |
> | **change** a type | **widening only** — never narrowing | the two types, written out |
>
> **Why the default matters.** A reader that wants a field the data does not contain uses the
> field's default. **If there is no default, the read is an error.** New data is unaffected, so the
> team making the change sees nothing. **The failure appears in whatever reads history**, which is
> usually somebody else's monthly job.
>
> **What a default means.** It is **not a convenience**. It is a statement about **what the absence
> of your field means**: zero, false, or genuinely unknown. **If absent means unknown, the default is
> null and every reader must handle it** — say so in the sentence.
>
> **The test that must pass.** Every schema change **reads a record written before the change**. A
> test that writes and reads with the same schema **cannot find this class of fault**, and most of
> ours were that test until March.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Our compatibility checks in the build.** Those are a tool and a policy, not
> part of the specification, and they are section 7.4.
>
> *The specification tells us how two schemas resolve. Whether the result is acceptable to our
> consumers is our judgment, and this note is where we record it.*

> **Writer's Tip** — *In the same change* is the column that does the work. A rule that can be
> satisfied next week will be satisfied never; tie it to the moment the field is added.
