Most of the work in an interface is describing failure. There is a published format for it: a few
named fields, one of which identifies the kind of problem for a program, and one of which explains
this particular occurrence to a person. Using it is not hard. Deciding what your kinds of problem
*are* is hard, and no document will do it for you.

> **Reader's Tip** — When a format gives you a field for machines and a field for people, notice

%%FIG b11-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the document number, its date and the document it replaced, a five-row table of the members with one line each, a boxed paragraph on the two audiences, a short list of what the document does not decide, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 23
> *Platform team · error bodies · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document. RFC 9457**, *Problem Details for HTTP APIs*, was published in **July 2023**. It
> replaced **RFC 7807** from **March 2016**. It defines a small document for describing a failure,
> carried under its own media type.
>
> **The members.**
>
> | Member | Audience | What it holds |
> |---|---|---|
> | **type** | **a program** | an address identifying the **kind** of problem |
> | **title** | a person | a **short** name for that kind, the same every time |
> | **status** | a program | the status code, **advisory** |
> | **detail** | **a person** | an explanation of **this** occurrence |
> | **instance** | both | an address identifying **this one** occurrence |
>
> **Two audiences, and the rule that follows.**
>
> > The **type** is the stable identifier. A client that needs to behave differently for two kinds of
> > failure should **look at the type**. The **detail** is written for a person, and the document is
> > direct about the consequence: **consumers should not parse the detail for information**.
> >
> > That single division is what lets you improve your wording for ten years without breaking
> > anybody. Give a client only prose, and **your sentences become part of your interface** — which
> > nobody intended and nobody wrote down.
>
> **The type is an address, and it need not answer.** It identifies a kind of problem. **Fetching it
> is not required**, and a client should not automatically go and fetch it. **Where** it does answer,
> it should return documentation a person can read.
>
> **The status is advisory.** It repeats the real status code for convenience. **The real one is the
> response's own**, and if the two disagree, the body is wrong.
>
> **The instance is the cheapest thing on the page.** An address identifying this single failure.
> With it, a customer's screenshot becomes a line in a log. Without it, somebody searches by time
> and hopes.
>
> **Extensions.** A service **may add its own members** — which field was wrong, which value was
> rejected, when to try again. The document gives guidance on them, and **it also now provides a
> registry for common problem types**, so that widely shared failures can be named the same way by
> everybody.
>
> **What this document does not decide.**
>
> - **What your kinds of problem are.** This is the real work, and it is entirely yours. A format
>   cannot tell you whether *insufficient funds* and *limit exceeded* are one kind or two.
> - **Which status code to use.** Not its subject.
> - **How many problems to report at once.** One failure or every failure in the request: your
>   choice, and it belongs in your document.
> - **Your wording.** No vocabulary, no tone, no localization policy.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every error carries a stable type, a short unchanging title, a human
> detail that we rewrite whenever we can improve it, an occurrence identifier that appears in our
> logs, and a named field where the failure concerns one.
>
> *Source: RFC 9457, Problem Details for HTTP APIs, July 2023, replacing RFC 7807 of March 2016: the
> type, title, status, detail and instance members and their intended audiences; the guidance that
> consumers should not parse the detail member for information; the treatment of the type as an
> identifier that need not be dereferenced; the advisory nature of the status member; extension
> members; and the registry for problem types. The document defines a format and does not determine
> an error taxonomy, a status code, a reporting strategy or any wording.*

<sub>**a problem type** — the kind of problem, identified stably · **an occurrence** — one particular
failure · **advisory** — carried for information, not the authority</sub>

### 1 · Linking words

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-11-06%%

> **Ines:** Read me your paragraph.
> **Adeel:** *The type is stable. The detail changes. Do not parse the detail. The title is short.*
> **Ines:** Four true sentences and no relationship between any of them. A reader has to work out
> which of those causes which, and some of them will get it wrong. Put the joins in.
> **Adeel:** *The type is stable. However, the detail changes, therefore you should not parse it.*
> **Ines:** Better, and now one small thing: *however* and *therefore* begin a sentence and take a
> comma. *But* and *so* join two halves of one sentence and do not. Mixing the two patterns is the
> most common punctuation error in technical writing.
> **Adeel:** So *however* is just a formal *but*?
> **Ines:** In meaning, nearly. In position, no. *The detail changes, however you must not parse it*
> is wrong — that needs a semicolon or a full stop before *however*. **Think of *however* and
> *therefore* as standing at the front of a new sentence, and *but* and *so* as living in the
> middle of one.**
> **Adeel:** And *although*?
> **Ines:** *Although* starts a subordinate half and never takes *but* with it. *Although the status
> is in the body, it is advisory.* Not *although it is in the body, but it is advisory* — a mistake I
> read every week, and it comes from languages that pair the two.
> **Adeel:** Which ones should I use in a document?
> **Ines:** *Because*, *so*, *although* and *however* will carry ninety percent of your writing.
> Add *that is* when you are about to restate something more precisely, which in a specification is
> often. **Links are not decoration. They are the difference between four facts and one argument.**

*Where do* however *and* therefore *stand in a sentence?*

**1B  Underline six linking words in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **however** + comma, new sentence | The type is stable. **However,** the wording is not. |
| **therefore** + comma, new sentence | The detail is prose. **Therefore,** do not parse it. |
| **but** / **so** inside one sentence | The detail is prose, **so** do not parse it. |
| **because** — reason | We send an instance **because** it finds the log line. |
| **although** — contrast, no *but* | **Although** the status is in the body, it is advisory. |
| **that is** — restating | The type is an identifier; **that is**, it never changes. |

> **Grammar Note** — *However* at the start of a sentence means *but*. In the middle of a clause,
> *however* can mean *in whatever way*: *however you write it*. The comma decides.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

An error body is the last thing a team designs and the first thing a customer sees, and the two facts
are related. Success has a shape everybody agrees on early; failure is discovered one case at a time,
usually at speed, by whoever hits it. So a mature service often has a careful data model and
thirty-one error kinds that are really four, each written by a different person on a different
afternoon. The part worth noticing is what that does to the people outside: with no stable
identifier, they are forced to depend on your English, and then your English cannot be improved
without breaking them. You end up unable to make your own product clearer. Designing errors properly
is not politeness towards clients; it is how you keep the freedom to rewrite your own words.

**Discuss:** Which of your own sentences has become an interface without anybody deciding?

> **Route on the type.** **Clients must not match on the detail.** The detail is prose written for a
> human reader; **therefore** we rewrite it when we can improve it. **However**, the type and the
> title are frozen the day they ship.
>
> **Extensions we send.** The **field name**, where a failure concerns one field. The **retry-after
> seconds**, where retrying is sensible. Nothing else, **because** every extension is a promise.
>
> **The occurrence identifier.** On **every** error, always. **A customer who quotes it turns an
> afternoon into a minute**, and it is the cheapest field on this page.
>
> **Our types.** **Nine.** They are listed in section 11.3 with one sentence each. **Although** our
> service once returned thirty-one distinct error kinds, twenty-two of them were the same nine
> problems described differently.
>
> **What the format does not decide.** **What our nine types are.** That was a product decision taken
> in March with security and operations, and it is recorded with the names of the people who agreed
> it.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Which status code accompanies which type.** That is the table in section
> 11.4.
>
> *A client given only prose has two choices: match our English, or treat every failure alike. Both
> are our fault, not theirs.*

> depend on and tells you what you are still free to improve.
