# Unit 4 · How Long Is It Good For?
%%SUB If you say nothing, a cache may decide for you%%
%%GOALS

Somewhere between your service and your customer there are caches you have never seen: in a
browser, in a proxy at their office, in a network in front of your own servers. They store
responses and hand them out again. How long they do that is decided by what you put in the
response — and if you put nothing, they are allowed to guess.

%%FIG b04-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the document number and its date, a four-row table of directives with what each one permits or forbids, a boxed paragraph on what happens when nothing is said, a short list of what the document does not decide, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 16
> *Platform team · caching · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document. RFC 9111**, *HTTP Caching*, was published in **June 2022**. It replaced a document
> from **2014**. It describes what a cache **may** store, how long it **may** reuse what it stored,
> and when it **must** check with the server first.
>
> **Fresh and stale.** A cache may reuse a stored response while it is **fresh** — that is, while
> its **age** is less than its **freshness lifetime**. Once the age passes the lifetime, the
> response is **stale**, and reusing it then is a different question with its own rules.
>
> **Four directives that do most of the work.**
>
> | Directive | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | **max-age** | gives the freshness lifetime, in seconds |
> | **no-store** | the response **must not be stored at all** |
> | **no-cache** | it **may be stored**, but **must not be reused** without checking first |
> | **private** | a **shared** cache must not store it; a private one may |
>
> **The two that are confused.**
>
> > **no-store** and **no-cache** are not two spellings of one idea. The first forbids storage. The
> > second permits storage and forbids **reuse without validation**. A team that wants nothing kept
> > anywhere and writes the second has stored the response in every cache along the path and merely
> > required a check before each reuse.
> >
> > The names are unhelpful and they are not going to change. **Read them as *do not keep* and *ask
> > before you serve*.**
>
> **What silence means.** This is the sentence that matters most. If a response carries **no
> explicit expiration**, a cache **may assign a heuristic expiration time** of its own — commonly
> based on how long ago the thing last changed. **So saying nothing is not the same as saying no.**
> A response with no caching information may be reused for a period you did not choose and cannot
> predict.
>
> **Caches you do not own.** A response may be stored by a cache **in the customer's network**, in
> their browser, or in an intermediary between you. You cannot clear those. Everything you control
> is in the response itself, before it leaves.
>
> **One more field worth knowing.** A response may say **which request header fields were used to
> choose it**. Without that, a cache may hand one reader's response to another reader whose request
> differed in exactly the way that mattered.
>
> **What this document does not decide.**
>
> - **What your service should allow.** It describes the rules a cache follows. Which lifetime is
>   right for your price list is your decision, and nobody else can make it.
> - **Whether a cache exists.** A cache is optional. A correct system works with and without one.
> - **How to clear a cache you do not own.** You cannot. You can only choose what you send.
> - **Your own internal caches.** Anything you store inside your service is yours to manage, and
>   none of these rules reach it.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every response carries an explicit lifetime chosen by somebody, even
> when that lifetime is zero, because silence is an answer given by strangers.
>
> *Source: RFC 9111, HTTP Caching, June 2022, replacing the 2014 specification: freshness lifetime
> and age; the max-age, no-store, no-cache and private directives; the permission for a cache to
> assign a heuristic expiration time when no explicit expiration is present; and the use of a field
> naming the request headers that select a stored response. The document specifies cache behavior; it
> does not choose a policy for your service.*

### 1 · Modals of possibility

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS2-04-06%%

> **Marek:** Read me the line from your incident note.
> **Adeel:** *The customer can see an old price.*
> **Marek:** That reads as ability, as if seeing old prices were a feature they have. What you mean
> is that it is possible, and in fact likely. *The customer may be seeing an old price.*
> **Adeel:** So *may* is for possibility.
> **Marek:** *May* is for a real possibility, and it is also the word for permission, which is why
> specifications use it so much. *A cache may store this response* means it is allowed to. *The copy
> may be stale* means it possibly is. Context separates them, and in a document you can usually
> rewrite to make it obvious.
> **Adeel:** And *might*?
> **Marek:** Weaker. *The copy might be stale* — possible, less likely, or you are less sure. On an
> incident call I use *may* when I think it is happening and *might* when I am listing things to
> check.
> **Adeel:** What about *could*?
> **Marek:** *Could* is for a possibility you are raising rather than claiming, and for hypotheses:
> *a proxy could be holding it*. All three are fine in a sentence. The one to be careful with is the
> negative. ***Must not* is a prohibition. *May not* is a refusal of permission. *Might not* is a
> possibility that something is not the case.** Three different meanings, and in a specification
> they are three different instructions.
> **Adeel:** *The cache may not store it* and *the cache might not store it.*
> **Marek:** The first forbids it. The second says perhaps it will not bother. **Write the first when
> you mean a rule, and never write the second in a rule at all.**

*What is the difference between* may not *and* might not?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

*We do not cache that* is a true sentence that answers the wrong question, and it can survive a
two-day incident untouched because nobody is lying. The real question is not what your service does
but what your response permits, and those are different subjects with different owners: one lives in
your code, the other in a header that often nobody has chosen. Behind it is a habit worth naming —
when a customer reports something, teams check their own systems and stop when they find them
innocent. The path between two companies is full of equipment neither of them owns, and the only
thing either side controls is what it says in the message. Teams that get caching right are usually
teams that have learned to answer *what does our response allow* before they answer *what do we do*.

**Discuss:** Where else does your team answer *we don't do that* when the question was *what do you
permit*?

> | published rate card | **1 hour** | shared caches may store | product |
>
> **Which request headers select a response.** Where a response depends on who is asking, it
> **names the request fields used to choose it**. Without that, a cache may hand one reader's
> response to another.
>
> **What we can clear.** **Our own caches only.** A copy held in a customer's network, a proxy
> between us or a browser **cannot be cleared by us**. We can change what we send, from a date we
> announce.
>
> **The two directives that are confused.** One forbids storage. The other permits storage and
> forbids reuse without checking. **We write them out in words in this table** rather than relying
> on the names.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Our internal caches.** What the service keeps in memory is section 4.5 and
> none of these rules reach it.
>
> *A correct client works whether or not a cache exists. A correct response says how long it may be
> reused, even when the answer is zero.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *chosen by* column is doing quiet work. It turns a technical value into a
> decision with an owner, which is what you want when somebody proposes changing it in a year.
