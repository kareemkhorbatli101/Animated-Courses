# Keystone Foundation
%%SUB English for AI-Enabled Software Architects · International Edition · CEFR A2%%

## How to use this book

This is the first of six books for people who design software systems and have to explain them in
English to colleagues, customers and auditors who are not in the same room, the same country or the
same time zone.

This book is **the system and the team**: the formats, codes and keywords that every system in the
world touches, and the English you need to talk about them without being misunderstood.

**Twelve units**, sixteen pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | A real standard or specification, with its edition, its numbers and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a long dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `KS1-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Every reading text is a real published document.** Not a summary of one, and not an invented
example: an International Standard, an IETF Request for Comments, a Unicode version, a published
specification. Each text gives **the document, its edition or date, the figure, and what the
publisher says it does not cover** — because in this work a number without a document and a version
is not information.

**Twelve formats and codes, and the trap in each one.** A date format that has a week-numbering rule
nobody expects. A time offset that is not a time zone. A character set with more than a hundred and
forty thousand characters and four possible byte lengths. A number format in which a simple sum is
not exact. Currency codes with three different numbers of decimal places. A telephone format with a
hard limit of fifteen digits. Country codes that change. Status codes whose classes mean more than
their numbers. A version number in which zero means *no promises*. Three capitalized keywords that
decide whether a sentence is a requirement. Language tags that distinguish one English from another.
And a standard that tells you what an architecture description is.

**The grammar is the grammar of description.** The present simple for what a system does, there
is/there are for what a document contains, countable and uncountable nouns for data, comparatives
for sizes and precision, *have to* and *must* for rules, the present passive for how things are done,
the zero conditional for behavior, and the three modal keywords that a specification uses.

**Four sentences you will write until they are automatic.** *Which document, and which edition?*
*Is that a time zone or an offset?* *How many bytes, and in which encoding?* And *is that a MUST or
a SHOULD?* — the four questions that save more review time than any tool.

## Nothing here is official

This is an independent teaching draft. It is **not published or endorsed by any standards body**,
and it carries no official status. Every person, team, company, system, incident and review in it was
written for this book. The standards and specifications quoted in the reading texts are real, and
each text names the document, its edition or date, and the limits its own publisher states — because
an architect who quotes a standard without its edition has quoted nothing.
