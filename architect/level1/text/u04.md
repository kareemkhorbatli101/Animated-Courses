Type `0.1 + 0.2` into almost any program and print the answer. You will see something that is not
`0.3`. The computer is not broken and the language is not badly written: the standard that nearly
every processor in the world follows cannot store `0.1` exactly, in the same way that you cannot
write one third exactly with a finite number of decimal digits.

> **Reader's Tip** — When a number surprises you, ask which format it is stored in before you ask

%%FIG a04-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its edition, a three-row table of formats with their bits and decimal digits, a boxed paragraph about one tenth in binary, a short box of special values, a paragraph headed what we use for money, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 4
> *Platform team · numbers · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. IEEE 754** is the standard for floating point arithmetic that almost every
> processor and programming language follows. The current edition is **IEEE 754-2019**; the previous
> ones were **2008** and, first, **1985**.
>
> **The two formats you will meet.**
>
> | Name | Total bits | Bits of precision | About how many decimal digits |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **binary32** (single) | **32** | **24** | **6 to 9** |
> | **binary64** (double) | **64** | **53** | **15 to 17** |
>
> **binary64 is the ordinary one.** It is the type behind a plain number in most scripting languages
> and the default floating point column in many databases. **It is more precise than binary32 and it
> is not exact.**
>
> **Why one tenth is a problem.**
>
> > These formats store numbers in **binary**. In binary, `0.5` is exact, `0.25` is exact and `0.125`
> > is exact, because they are halves of halves. **`0.1` is not**, in the same way that **one third
> > is not exact in decimal**: write 0.333 and you are close; write more threes and you are closer;
> > you never arrive.
> >
> > So a program stores the **closest value it can** to `0.1`, which is a very little more than one
> > tenth. It does the same for `0.2`. Add the two closest values and the answer is a very little
> > more than `0.3` — and `0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3` is **false**.
>
> **Special values the standard defines.** Positive and negative **zero**; positive and negative
> **infinity**; and **NaN**, which means *not a number* — the result of an impossible operation such
> as zero divided by zero. **A NaN is not equal to anything, including itself**, which is correct and
> which breaks sorting code written by people who have not read this page.
>
> **What we use, in three rules.**
>
> > **1 Money is not a floating point number.** We store the **number of minor units** as a whole
> > number: a price of twelve point five zero becomes **1250** minor units. Addition of whole numbers
> > is exact. Unit 5 is about which currencies have two minor digits, which have none and which have
> > three.
> > **2 Measurements are floating point numbers.** A weight, a temperature or a distance already has
> > a measurement error much larger than the format's, so binary64 is a good choice — and we
> > **round for display**, not for storage.
> > **3 We never compare two floating point values for equality.** We compare them with a stated
> > **tolerance**: *equal to within 0.000001*.
>
> **What the standard does not do.** It does not tell your language how to print a number, and
> languages differ: the same stored value may print as `0.30000000000000004` in one and `0.3` in
> another. **The value is the same; the printing is a choice.** And the standard also defines
> **decimal** formats, which some databases offer; they are slower and they are exact for decimal
> fractions, which is why financial systems use them.
>
> *Source: IEEE 754-2019, IEEE Standard for Floating-Point Arithmetic, and the earlier editions of
> 2008 and 1985; the binary32 and binary64 interchange formats, with 24 and 53 bits of precision;
> the special values negative zero, infinity and NaN; and the decimal formats the standard also
> defines.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Floating point is the most famous example of a rule that is correct, documented since 1985, and
discovered by every team as if it were a scandal. It is worth asking why. The reason is that the
default is wrong for the commonest case: in most languages the simplest way to write a number gives
you a format designed for measurements, and most business software is full of money. So the default
is fine for the physicist and a trap for the invoice. An architect's job here is small and
unglamorous: decide the type once, write it in the field note with a worked example, and say in one
sentence what rounding is for. Nobody will thank you, because the one-cent difference that never
happens is invisible — and that, by now, you will recognize as the normal shape of this work.

**Discuss:** What is the default in the language your team uses? Who chose it?

> ### Field note · `amount_minor` and `currency`
> *Platform team · interface document, section 5.1 · 13 March*
>
> **What it is.** An **amount of money**. Not a measurement, and not a rate.
>
> **Type.** A **whole number** of **minor units**. No decimal point anywhere in storage or in the
> message.
>
> **Worked example.** Twelve point five zero in a currency with two minor digits is stored as
> **`1250`**. Twelve in a currency with **no** minor digits is stored as **`12`**. Twelve point five
> zero five in a currency with **three** minor digits is stored as **`12505`**. **Unit 5 explains
> which currencies are which.**
>
> **Always with a currency.** `amount_minor` is **never sent or stored alone**. The `currency` field
> carries the three-letter code, and the number of minor digits comes from that code.
>
> **Rounding.** We round **only for display**, and we round at the **last** step. We never round a
> stored value, and we never add two values that have been rounded.
>
> **Comparison.** Two amounts are compared as **whole numbers** and are either equal or not. There
> is **no tolerance** on money.
>
> **Measurements are different.** Weights, temperatures and distances are stored as **binary64
> floating point** numbers and compared **to within a stated tolerance**, which is written in the
