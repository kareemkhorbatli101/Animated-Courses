Two letters stand for a country in almost every system in the world: in an address, in a currency
code, in a language tag, on a form. The letters are assigned by a maintenance agency, they are
withdrawn when a country changes, and they are reserved so that nobody reuses them next year. This
unit is about those three verbs, and about a list that is not yours to invent.

%%FIG a07-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its edition, a three-row table of the code forms with an example each, a boxed paragraph on codes that are withdrawn and reserved, a short list of what a country code does not tell you, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 7
> *Platform team · country codes · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO 3166-1** gives codes for the names of countries and certain other territories.
> The current edition was published in **2020**. The list itself **is maintained by the ISO 3166
> Maintenance Agency**, and changes **are published** as they are agreed — not once a year and not
> on a date you can plan around.
>
> **Three forms for every entry.**
>
> | Form | Example | Used for |
> |---|---|---|
> | **alpha-2** | **GB**, **JP**, **BR** | most systems, addresses, web |
> | **alpha-3** | **GBR**, **JPN**, **BRA** | where two letters are not enough |
> | **numeric** | **826**, **392**, **076** | systems that carry digits only |
>
> **The verbs.** A code **is assigned** to an entry. A code **is withdrawn** when the entry changes
> or is no longer used. A withdrawn code **is reserved**, so that it is not given to a different
> country immediately. Reserved codes are published in categories, and two of them matter in daily
> work: codes that **are exceptionally reserved** at the request of a body that needs them — **UK**
> and **EU** are among these — and codes that **are transitionally reserved** after being withdrawn.
>
> **So UK is not the country code for the United Kingdom.** The alpha-2 code is **GB**. UK is
> reserved, which means it is not available for another country, not that it is the code.
>
> **The list changes.**
>
> > Codes have been withdrawn and new ones assigned within the working lifetime of most systems.
> > Names change more often than codes do, and a name is not an identifier: **store the code, display
> > the name**, and take the name from the list rather than from your own memory.
> >
> > Historical codes are covered by a separate part of the standard, **ISO 3166-3**, so that a system
> > can still read an address recorded under a code that is no longer current. **Old rows do not
> > change when a code does.**
>
> **What a country code does not tell you.**
>
> - **Not a language.** A country can have several, and a language is used in many countries.
>   Language is covered in Unit 11.
> - **Not a currency.** The currency code has its own standard, as Unit 5 said.
> - **Not a tax rule, an address format or a shipping zone.** Those are decided by somebody in your
>   company, and they change for business reasons.
> - **Not a statement about politics.** The standard **is used** to identify names on a published
>   list; it does not decide what a country is, and the names **are taken** from sources outside the
>   standard.
>
> **Subdivisions** — regions, states, provinces — are in **ISO 3166-2**, where the code is written as
> the country code, a hyphen and up to three more characters.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** One service holds the current list with the date of our copy, every
> application asks it, and we store the alpha-2 code and never the country name.
>
> *Source: ISO 3166-1, Codes for the representation of names of countries and their subdivisions —
> Part 1: Country codes, 2020 edition: the alpha-2, alpha-3 and numeric code forms; maintenance by
> the ISO 3166 Maintenance Agency; and the publication of exceptionally and transitionally reserved
> code elements, including UK and EU as exceptionally reserved. Subdivisions are Part 2 and formerly
> used codes are Part 3. The standard does not assign languages, currencies, tax rules or address
> formats, and country names are drawn from sources outside it.*

### 1 · The present passive

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS1-07-06%%

> **Oksana:** Read the first line of your note to me.
> **Adeel:** *We assign the country codes from the standard list.*
> **Oksana:** You do not assign them. Somebody else does, and that is the whole point of the
> sentence. *Codes are assigned by the maintenance agency.*
> **Adeel:** So I should use the passive.
> **Oksana:** Here, yes. The passive is right when the action matters and the actor is either obvious
> or not us. In a standard you will read it constantly: *values are encoded*, *the field is reserved*,
> *the code is withdrawn*. It is not vague writing. It is writing about results.
> **Adeel:** When is it wrong?
> **Oksana:** When you are hiding. *A mistake was made in the formatting function* is passive and
> cowardly. *I used the wrong currency code* is active and three words shorter. **Use the passive for
> standards and the active for yourself.**
> **Adeel:** How do I build it?
> **Oksana:** The verb *to be*, and then the past participle. *Is assigned. Are withdrawn. Is
> reserved.* And if you want to name the actor, add *by*: *by the maintenance agency*. **If you can
> name who did it and it is you, name yourself.**

*When does Oksana say the passive is the wrong choice?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A country list is the most political file in a piece of software, and most teams write it in twenty
minutes. The standard sidesteps the politics honestly: it says that it codes names from published
sources and decides nothing about what a country is. Your product cannot sidestep it, because your
drop-down list is read as a statement, in a way the standard's table is not. The practical defense is
to take the list from the published source, keep the date of your copy, change it when the source
changes, and be able to say all of that in one sentence to anybody who asks — including a customer
who is upset. That answer is boring, which is exactly its value. The teams that get into trouble are
the ones that edited the list themselves, for a good reason, and then could not reconstruct who
decided what.

**Discuss:** Who in your company would answer a complaint about the country list, and what would they
say?
