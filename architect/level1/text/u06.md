# Unit 6 · Fifteen Digits, No More
%%SUB A telephone number is not a number, and it never has more than fifteen digits%%
%%GOALS
Use *have to* and *must*
Describe the parts of an international telephone number
Store a telephone number as text
%%

A telephone number looks like a number and behaves like a word. It can begin with a zero, so you
have to store it as text. It can be written in six ways in six countries, so you have to store one
of them. And it has a hard limit that the standard states in a single line: fifteen digits, no more.

%%FIG a06-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the recommendation and its date, a diagram of a number broken into country code, destination part and subscriber part with a total of fifteen marked beneath, a boxed paragraph on the plus sign, a short list of what the recommendation does not cover, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 6
> *Platform team · telephone numbers · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ITU-T Recommendation E.164** describes the international public telecommunication
> numbering plan. The version we use was published in **November 2010**.
>
> **The limit.** An international number has **at most fifteen digits**. This is the whole number:
> the country code and everything after it. The limit is the same for every country.
>
> **The parts.**
>
> | Part | Digits | Example role |
> |---|---|---|
> | **country code** | 1 to 3 | which country or service |
> | **destination part** | varies | which area or network |
> | **subscriber part** | varies | which line |
>
> **The plus sign is not a digit.** The **`+`** means *dial the international prefix of the place
> you are standing in*. That prefix is **not the same everywhere**: in many countries you dial
> **00**, and in others something else. So the `+` is a short way of saying *this number is
> international, and the reader has to supply the local prefix*. **It does not count towards the
> fifteen.**
>
> **A telephone number is not an arithmetic number.**
>
> > It can begin with a **zero**, and the zero means something. An integer field removes it. Nobody
> > adds two telephone numbers together, nobody sorts customers by them and nobody divides one.
> > **So the field is text.**
> >
> > And the same number can be written in several ways, with spaces, with brackets, with hyphens,
> > with or without a national prefix. **Store one agreed form**, and keep the way a customer wrote
> > it separately if you need it. A duplicate check across four written forms finds nothing.
>
> **What the recommendation does not cover.**
>
> - **How to write a number on a page.** Spacing and punctuation are described in a different
>   recommendation, **E.123**.
> - **Extensions.** The digits for one desk inside a company are **not part of the number**.
> - **Which numbers are mobile.** You cannot tell reliably from the digits alone, and the answer
>   changes when a subscriber keeps a number and moves to another network.
> - **Short numbers inside one country**, including emergency numbers. Those are **national**, they
>   are different in every country, and they are not international numbers.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** One text field, one agreed form beginning with `+`, sixteen
> characters, and no arithmetic ever.
>
> *Source: ITU-T Recommendation E.164, The international public telecommunication numbering plan,
> November 2010 version: the maximum of fifteen digits, the country code of one to three digits, and
> the destination and subscriber parts shown above. Written presentation of numbers is the subject of
> Recommendation E.123; extensions, the mobile or fixed nature of a number, and national short codes
> are outside E.164.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Telling someone their own telephone number is invalid is a small insult delivered by a machine. The
person knows the number; they have had it for years; they give it to their family. Your form says it
is wrong, and the two likeliest outcomes are that they type it again more carefully, or that they
leave. Neither of those tells you that your rule is the problem, which is why these rules survive for
years inside companies that measure everything. The rule itself was almost never written as a
decision about the world — it was written from the numbers somebody could see, in one country, on
one afternoon. An international product has ten or twenty of these, and the honest way to find them
is to ask who has tried to sign up and failed, rather than to look at who succeeded.

**Discuss:** Which field in your own product would reject your own details if you lived elsewhere?
