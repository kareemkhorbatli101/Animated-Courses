# Unit 2 · An Offset Is Not a Zone
%%SUB Plus two hours is a number. Europe/Lisbon is a set of rules that changes%%
%%GOALS
Tell the time and say a time with an offset
Use *there is* and *there are*
Say the difference between an offset and a time zone
%%

`+02:00` is an offset. It is a number: two hours in front of UTC. `Europe/Lisbon` is a time zone. It
is the name of a set of rules — when summer time starts, when it ends, and what the government did in
1992 — and those rules change. If you store the number, you can print a past time. If you store the
name, you can also calculate a future one.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When a value has both a number and a name, find out which one the system
> stores. The number is true for one moment. The name is true for every moment.

%%FIG a02-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a short table comparing an offset and a zone name across four rows, a boxed paragraph about the time zone database and its versions, a line about leap seconds with two dates in it, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 2
> *Platform team · time: what we store · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. UTC** — Coordinated Universal Time — is the time standard the world uses. Local
> times are described as a difference from it: **+02:00** means two hours in front of UTC,
> **-03:00** means three hours behind.
>
> **An offset and a zone are not the same thing.**
>
> | | An offset | A zone name |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Looks like** | `+02:00`, `-03:00`, `Z` | `Europe/Lisbon`, `Asia/Tokyo` |
> | **Is** | a **number** | a **set of rules** |
> | **True for** | **one moment** | **every moment, past and future** |
> | **Changes** | no — it is just a number | **yes**, when a government changes the rules |
>
> **Why this matters in a system.** If you store `+01:00` for a customer in Lisbon, you can print the
> time of a past event correctly. **You cannot calculate next October's local time**, because in
> winter that customer's offset is `+00:00`. If you store `Europe/Lisbon`, you can do both.
>
> **Where the rules live.** The rules for every zone are kept in the **Time Zone Database**, which is
> published by **IANA**. It has a **version** — a year and a letter, such as **2024a** or **2025b** —
> and **it is updated several times a year**, because governments change their rules, sometimes with
> only a few weeks' notice. **A system with an old copy of the database prints the wrong local
> time.**
>
> **Leap seconds.** UTC is kept close to the Earth's rotation, and for that reason **leap seconds**
> have been added from time to time: an extra second at the end of a day. **The last one was added
> on 31 December 2016.** In **2022** the General Conference on Weights and Measures resolved to
> **stop using leap seconds by 2035**. **A system that assumes every minute has sixty seconds has
> been wrong twenty-seven times and will not be wrong again after that.**
>
> **What we store, in three rules.**
>
> > **1** For an **instant** — a log line, an event, a payment — store **UTC** and send it with
> > `Z` or an offset.
> > **2** For a **local plan** — an appointment, an opening hour, a report window — store the
> > **local time and the zone name**.
> > **3** Never store a local time with no zone and no offset. **It is not a time. It is a guess.**
>
> **What the message format does.** The internet profile used in interfaces carries **an offset or
> `Z`**, and it has **no field for a zone name**. That is correct for an instant and not enough for
> a plan: if your interface must carry an appointment, it must carry the zone name in a separate
> field.
>
> *Source: UTC as the international time standard; the IANA Time Zone Database, published in
> versioned releases several times a year; the addition of leap seconds, the most recent on 31
> December 2016; and the 2022 resolution of the General Conference on Weights and Measures to end
> the use of leap seconds by 2035. Zone names are those of the Time Zone Database.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Time is the subject where a team's politeness and its correctness pull in different directions. It is
friendly to write *the call is at nine* to a colleague in your own city, and it is unkind to the five
people reading the same message in five other places. The habit that works in international teams is
small and slightly formal: give the UTC instant first, add one local time with the word *today*, and
let everybody else do their own arithmetic from a number they can trust. The same habit applies to
systems. Nobody has ever been harmed by a log line in UTC. A great deal of harm has come from a
friendly field called *time* with nothing after it — and the harm always lands on the person
furthest from the person who designed the field.

**Discuss:** Whose local time does your team use by default? Who in the team pays for that?
