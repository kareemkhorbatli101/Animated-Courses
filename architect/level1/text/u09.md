# Unit 9 · Three Numbers and a Promise
%%SUB A version number is a promise about compatibility — and zero promises nothing%%
%%GOALS
Use *will* and sequence words
Explain what each of the three numbers means
Say what a version number does not promise
%%

A version number with three parts is not a label. It is a promise, made to everybody who uses your
software, about what will happen when they upgrade. The first number says *this will break your
code*. The second says *this will add something*. The third says *this will fix something*. And a
first number of zero says: *no promises at all, yet*.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Read the rule for *zero* twice. Most arguments about versions are really
> arguments about whether the interface is public yet.

%%FIG a09-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the specification and its version, a three-row table of the three numbers with the rule for each, a boxed paragraph on versions beginning with zero, a short list of what the specification does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 9
> *Platform team · version numbers · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The specification. Semantic Versioning 2.0.0** describes a version number of three parts,
> written **MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH** and separated by dots. Each part is a number, and each one is raised
> for one reason only.
>
> **Three numbers, three reasons.**
>
> | Part | Raise it when | Then |
> |---|---|---|
> | **MAJOR** | you make an **incompatible** change | the two lower parts go to **0** |
> | **MINOR** | you **add** something and break nothing | the last part goes to **0** |
> | **PATCH** | you **fix** something and break nothing | nothing else changes |
>
> **The promise.** A version number tells somebody what **will happen** when they upgrade. If only
> the last number rises, their code **will keep working**. If the first number rises, something
> **will break**, and they have been warned by the number alone, before they read anything.
>
> **Zero is special.**
>
> > A version of the form **0.y.z** means **anything may change at any time**: the public interface
> > **is not to be considered stable**. This is the honest state of most software for most of its
> > life, and it is a useful state to be in. **Version 1.0.0 is the moment you start making the
> > promise.**
> >
> > So *0.9.4* does not mean *nearly 1.0*. It means *no promises*. A team that depends on a 0.y.z
> > version has no complaint when it changes, and a team that publishes one should say out loud that
> > it is not stable.
>
> **Pre-releases.** A label may follow the three numbers, as in **1.0.0-alpha**. A pre-release
> version **has lower precedence** than the plain version: *1.0.0-alpha* comes **before** *1.0.0*.
> Extra build information may also be attached, and **it is ignored** when deciding which version is
> later.
>
> **You must declare your public interface.** The specification says the three numbers describe
> changes to a **declared** public interface. **If you have not written down what is public, the
> numbers mean nothing**, because nobody — including you — can say whether a change was compatible.
>
> **What the specification does not do.**
>
> - **It does not enforce anything.** Nothing stops you from publishing a wrong number. The promise
>   is kept by people.
> - **It does not tell you what is public.** That is your decision and it has to be written down.
> - **It does not cover dates or release frequency.** Shipping weekly and shipping yearly both work.
> - **It does not settle every case.** Whether fixing a bug that somebody relied on is a fix or a
>   break is a judgment, and the specification cannot make it for you.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Decide whether anything breaks, then choose the number — in that
> order.
>
> *Source: Semantic Versioning 2.0.0: the MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH form; the rule that MAJOR is raised for
> incompatible changes, MINOR for compatible additions and PATCH for compatible fixes, with lower
> parts reset to zero; the rule that a major version of zero means the public API should not be
> considered stable and anything may change; the lower precedence of pre-release versions and the
> exclusion of build metadata from precedence; and the requirement that the public API be declared.
> The specification does not enforce compliance, define your public API for you, or prescribe release
> dates.*

before the real one · **to declare** — to write down publicly</sub>

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Raising the first number feels like an admission. In many companies it has to be defended in a
meeting, while shipping the same change as a minor version requires no conversation at all — so the
incentive runs exactly backwards from the engineering. The three numbers were designed to move that
decision out of the meeting and into a rule that anybody can check, and they only work if the number
is chosen before anybody asks how it will look. Where teams get this right, you can usually find one
habit: they say *who* breaks, out loud, in the same sentence as the change. Naming the four teams who
will have to edit code makes the number obvious, and it moves the discussion from the company's
reputation to somebody's Monday morning.

**Discuss:** In your company, who would have to approve a major version, and what would they ask?
