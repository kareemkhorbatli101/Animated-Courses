# Unit 3 · One Character, Four Bytes
%%SUB Sixty-four what? The field limit that means three different things%%

A form says *maximum 20 characters*. A database says `VARCHAR(20)`. A message format says *at most 20
bytes*. Three limits, three numbers, and for a name written in some scripts the three are not the
same number at all: in the encoding the world uses, one character may take one byte or four.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A count of characters is useless without the version of the standard it came
> from. The number goes up every year.

%%FIG a03-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard with two versions and their dates and counts, a four-row table of byte lengths with one example script each, a boxed paragraph about three ways to measure length, a line about two names that look identical, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 3
> *Platform team · text, characters and bytes · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard.** The **Unicode Standard** gives every character a number, called a **code point**,
> written like `U+0041`. The standard has versions, and **the number of characters is different in
> each one**:
>
> | Version | Published | Characters encoded |
> |---|---|---|
> | **15.1** | **September 2023** | **149,813** |
> | **16.0** | **September 2024** | **154,998** |
>
> **A count of characters without a version means nothing.** If a document says *Unicode has about a
> hundred and fifty thousand characters*, ask which version and which year.
>
> **The space is much bigger than the characters in it.** Code points run from `U+0000` to
> `U+10FFFF`, which is **1,114,112** possible values. Most of them are not characters and may never
> be. **The standard is a list, not a range.**
>
> **UTF-8: one code point, one to four bytes.** UTF-8 is the encoding the web uses. It stores
> different code points in different numbers of bytes:
>
> | Bytes | Which code points | Example script |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1** | `U+0000`–`U+007F` | basic Latin letters, digits, punctuation |
> | **2** | `U+0080`–`U+07FF` | letters with accents, Greek, Cyrillic, Arabic, Hebrew |
> | **3** | `U+0800`–`U+FFFF` | most Han, Japanese and Korean characters |
> | **4** | `U+10000`–`U+10FFFF` | historic scripts, many symbols, emoji |
>
> **So a limit needs a unit.** *Twenty characters* can be **twenty bytes** or **eighty bytes**. Twenty
> bytes can be **twenty characters** or **five**. A field described as *short* has not been
> described.
>
> **Three ways to measure one string, all of them reasonable.**
>
> > **Bytes** — what storage and network limits care about.
> > **Code points** — what the standard numbers.
> > **Grapheme clusters** — what a reader sees as one character. One of these may be **several code
> > points**: a letter with a separate accent mark, a symbol with a modifier, a flag made of two
> > regional indicators.
> >
> > The three give different answers for the same name, and **a system that checks with one and
> > stores with another will cut a value in the middle of a character.**
>
> **Two names that look identical and are not equal.** A letter with an accent can be stored as
> **one** code point or as **a letter plus a combining mark**. On the screen they look the same. Byte
> for byte they are different, so a search finds one and not the other. The standard's answer is
> **normalization**: put text into one agreed form before you compare or store it.
>
> **What to write in an interface document.** Three things: **the number**, **the unit**, and **the
> encoding**. *At most 64 bytes, UTF-8.* And if the limit is in characters, say which kind: *at most
> 64 code points*.
>
> *Source: the Unicode Standard, version 15.1 (September 2023), encoding 149,813 characters, and
> version 16.0 (September 2024), encoding 154,998; the code point range U+0000 to U+10FFFF; the
> UTF-8 encoding's one-to-four-byte structure; and the Unicode normalization forms. Counts are per
> version and change with each release.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Every engineer learns about character encodings twice: once in a lesson, and once from a customer
whose name their system could not store. The second lesson is the one that stays. A field limit is
not a technical detail; it is a statement about whose names are welcome, and a form that accepts
twenty Latin letters and five characters of somebody else's script has made a choice even if nobody
in the room meant to make one. The habit that fixes it costs nothing: write the number, the unit and
the encoding, size the storage for the worst case of the promise you made, and reject rather than
cut. Teams that do this stop having the conversation. Teams that do not have it once a year, always
with a customer, and always about a person's own name.

**Discuss:** Has a form ever refused your name or address? What did you do?
