# Unit 10 · MUST, SHOULD, MAY
%%SUB Three words that decide what a reader is allowed to do%%
%%GOALS
Use modals of obligation
Choose between *must*, *should* and *may*
Say when a word is not a rule
%%

Three ordinary English words carry an agreed meaning in technical documents. *MUST* means there is
no choice. *SHOULD* means there is a choice, and you had better understand it. *MAY* means it is
genuinely up to you. The agreement is a document from 1997, and it holds only when a document says
it is using it.

> **Reader's Tip** — Before you read a specification, look for the paragraph at the top that names
> its keywords. If it is missing, every strong word in the document is a guess.

%%FIG a10-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the two document numbers and their dates, a three-row table of the keyword levels with the meaning of each, a boxed paragraph on the middle level, a short list of what the documents do not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 10
> *Platform team · requirement keywords · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The documents. RFC 2119**, *Key words for use in RFCs to Indicate Requirement Levels*, was
> published in **March 1997**. It was updated in **May 2017** by **RFC 8174**, which added one
> important clarification: the special meanings apply **only when the words are in capital letters**.
> Together the two are known as **BCP 14**.
>
> **Three levels.**
>
> | Keyword | Also written | Meaning |
> |---|---|---|
> | **MUST** | REQUIRED, SHALL | an **absolute** requirement |
> | **MUST NOT** | SHALL NOT | an **absolute prohibition** |
> | **SHOULD** | RECOMMENDED | see the box below |
> | **SHOULD NOT** | NOT RECOMMENDED | the same, in reverse |
> | **MAY** | OPTIONAL | **truly optional** |
>
> **The middle level is the interesting one.**
>
> > SHOULD means that **there may exist valid reasons in particular circumstances to ignore a
> > particular item**, but that **the full implications must be understood and carefully weighed**
> > before a different course is chosen.
> >
> > So SHOULD is not a polite MUST and it is not a suggestion. It is permission to differ, **on
> > condition that you have understood what you are doing**. A team that ignores a SHOULD without
> > being able to say why has not followed the document.
>
> **MAY means both sides have work to do.** If an item is optional, then one implementation may
> include it and another may leave it out — and **each has to work with the other**. An
> implementation that omits an option must still be able to talk to one that includes it, and the
> reverse. **Optional does not mean ignorable.**
>
> **Use the strong words sparingly.** The document says the keywords **must be used with care and
> sparingly**, and in particular **only where actually required for interoperation or to limit
> behavior that has potential for causing harm**. A requirement that exists to make one engineer's
> preference binding is a misuse of the word.
>
> **Capital letters matter.** After the 2017 update, **only the capitalized forms carry the special
> meaning**. An ordinary English *must* in a sentence is ordinary English. This is why documents
> write the keywords in capitals, and why a document that wants the meanings **states so in a short
> paragraph at the top**.
>
> **What the documents do not do.**
>
> - **They do not make your document a standard.** Using the keywords is not approval by anybody.
> - **They do not test anything.** Nothing checks whether an implementation follows your MUSTs.
> - **They do not tell you which level to choose.** That judgment is yours, and it is the real work.
> - **They do not apply unless your document says so.** Without the paragraph at the top, a reader
>   cannot tell your keywords from your prose.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Capitals for keywords, the paragraph at the top, and a reason written
> down for every MUST.
>
> *Source: RFC 2119, Key words for use in RFCs to Indicate Requirement Levels, March 1997, and RFC
> 8174, May 2017, together BCP 14: the keyword levels MUST / REQUIRED / SHALL, MUST NOT / SHALL NOT,
> SHOULD / RECOMMENDED, SHOULD NOT / NOT RECOMMENDED and MAY / OPTIONAL; the definition of SHOULD as
> permitting departure where the full implications are understood and weighed; the interoperation
> requirement attached to optional items; the guidance that the keywords be used with care and
> sparingly, only where required for interoperation or to limit harmful behavior; and the
> clarification that the special meanings apply only to the capitalized forms. The documents do not
> confer standards status, provide conformance testing, or choose requirement levels for an author.*

### 1 · *must*, *should* and *may*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS1-10-06%%

> **Mei:** You have written fourteen musts on one page. Read me the fourth one.
> **Adeel:** *The client must send the field names in alphabetical order.*
> **Mei:** What happens if it does not?
> **Adeel:** Nothing. It is easier to read in the log.
> **Mei:** So it is a preference, and you have written it with the strongest word in the language.
> That is *should* at most, and honestly it is *may*. **If nothing breaks and nobody is harmed, it is
> not a MUST.**
> **Adeel:** But then people will not do it.
> **Mei:** Some will not, and that is what *should* means: they are allowed to differ if they have
> thought about it. What you are describing is the real fear — that *should* will be ignored. The
> answer is not to inflate every sentence. **If everything is a MUST, your reader cannot find the
> three that matter, and those three are the ones that keep the system working.**
> **Adeel:** How do I choose?
> **Mei:** Ask one question for each line: what breaks if the reader ignores this? If the answer is
> *two systems stop working together* or *somebody is harmed*, write MUST. If the answer is *it gets
> harder to debug*, write SHOULD. If the answer is *nothing*, write MAY or delete the line.
> **Adeel:** And the lowercase ones?
> **Mei:** Ordinary English. Write *the client must be registered first* in a paragraph of prose and
> nobody treats it as a keyword — which is fine, but **do not rely on it either way. Keywords in
> capitals, prose in lowercase, and the paragraph at the top that says so.**

*What one question does Mei ask about each line?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Writing *MUST* costs the author nothing and costs every reader something, which is why documents
drift towards it. There is also a quieter reason: in many organizations an author who writes *SHOULD*
can be overruled, and one who writes *MUST* cannot, so the keyword becomes a way of winning an
argument that was never held. Readers notice this quickly, and the response is the same everywhere —
they stop reading the levels at all and decide for themselves, which is the opposite of what the
author wanted. The teams who keep the strong word valuable tend to have one small habit: a MUST
requires a sentence saying what breaks without it, written by the author, in the document. A
preference cannot survive that sentence.

**Discuss:** In your organization, is it easier to write *must* than to defend *should*? Who decides?
