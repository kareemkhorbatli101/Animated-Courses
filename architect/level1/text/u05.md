# Unit 5 · Three Letters, Three Decimals
%%SUB Most currencies have two decimal places. Some have none. A few have three%%
%%GOALS

Say how many decimal places a currency has

Unit 4 said that money is a whole number of minor units. This unit answers the next question: how
many minor units are in one unit of currency? The standard gives the answer currency by currency, and
the answer is not always two. Most currencies have two decimal places, some have none, and a few
have three — and a system that assumes two is wrong in about thirty countries.

> **Reader's Tip** — A code list is a living document. Read the date of the copy you are using, and
> find out who publishes the next one.

%%FIG a05-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its edition, a five-row table of currency codes with alpha code, numeric code and minor digits, a boxed paragraph about codes that are added and withdrawn, a line about codes that are not countries, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 5
> *Platform team · currency codes and minor units · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO 4217** gives a code for every currency in the world. The current edition dates
> from **2015**, and the **code list itself is maintained separately and published by a maintenance
> agency**, because currencies change more often than standards do.
>
> **Three things for every currency.**
>
> | Currency | Alpha code | Numeric code | Minor digits |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | euro | **EUR** | 978 | **2** |
> | US dollar | **USD** | 840 | **2** |
> | Japanese yen | **JPY** | 392 | **0** |
> | Kuwaiti dinar | **KWD** | 414 | **3** |
> | Tunisian dinar | **TND** | 788 | **3** |
>
> **So the number of decimal places is a property of the currency, not of money.** Most currencies
> have **two** minor digits. A number have **none**: an amount in yen is a whole number of yen, and
> a price of `1250` in that currency is **one thousand two hundred and fifty yen**, not twelve fifty.
> A few have **three**.
>
> **Why the numeric code exists.** The three-letter code is for people and the three-digit code is
> for systems that cannot carry letters. Both identify the same currency, and **a system should
> accept either and store one**.
>
> **How the alpha code is built.** In most cases the first two letters are the **country code** from
> the standard in Unit 7, and the third letter is the first letter of the currency's name. Not
> always: some codes are historical, and codes beginning with **X** are not countries at all — they
> are used for precious metals, for test values and for units of account.
>
> **The list changes.**
>
> > Codes are **added** when a country introduces a currency and **withdrawn** when one stops being
> > used. A withdrawn code does not disappear from history: payments made in it still exist in your
> > database, and your reports still have to print them.
> >
> > **So a system needs two lists**: the **current** list, for accepting new payments, and the
> > **historical** list, for reading old rows. A system with one hardcoded list fails in one of two
> > ways — it accepts a currency that no longer exists, or it cannot display a payment it took three
> > years ago.
>
> **What the standard does not do.** It does not tell you how to **round in cash**. Some countries
> round cash payments to the nearest five minor units while keeping two minor digits in accounts;
> that is a **national practice**, not part of the code list. And the standard does not give
> **exchange rates**: a rate is a price, it changes by the second, and it comes from a market, not
> from a code list.
>
> **What we store, in one line.** The **whole number of minor units**, the **alpha code**, and
> nothing else. The number of decimal places is looked up from the code when we print.
>
> *Source: ISO 4217, Codes for the representation of currencies, 2015 edition, with the code list
> maintained and published by its maintenance agency; the alpha and numeric codes and minor-unit
> values shown above; the X-prefixed codes for non-country uses; and the addition and withdrawal of
> codes over time. The standard does not define exchange rates or national cash-rounding practices.*

keeps a code list up to date · **to withdraw** — to take a code out of the current list</sub>

> are the smallest mistake in English and the most visible, because a reader sees the number and the

> **Adeel:** Are there irregular ones in our work?
> **Ines:** A few, and you will meet all of them this year. *One index, two indexes* — or *indices*,

> **Ines:** It should, and the field that generates it should take the number and the noun together,
> so that one place says *1 decimal place* and two places say *2 decimal places*. **Every interface
> in the world has one of these, and the fix is always the same size.**

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The two-decimal assumption is the clearest example in this book of a design decision that nobody
made. It arrives with the first currency a company supports, it is copied into a spreadsheet, a
template and a test fixture, and five years later it lives in eleven places and has no author. Then
a customer in a three-decimal currency signs a contract, and a team spends a quarter finding every
copy. What makes this a culture question rather than a coding one is who notices: the error is
invisible in the country where the assumption was made and obvious everywhere else, so the people
who find it are always the people with the least influence over the team that wrote it. An
international edition of anything is mostly this: defaults that were never chosen, and the patience
to go and find them.

**Discuss:** What default in your own systems came from the country your company started in?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Field note · amounts and currencies
> *Platform team · interface document, section 5.2 · 16 March*
>
> **Stored.** Two fields, always together: **`amount_minor`**, a whole number of minor units, and
> **`currency`**, the **three-letter alpha code** from ISO 4217.
>
> **Formatting.** The number of decimal places is **looked up from the code**. It is **not** two by
> default and it is **never** hardcoded in an application.
>
> **Worked examples.**
>
> | Stored | Code | Minor digits | Printed |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | `1250` | **EUR** | 2 | 12.50 |
> | `1250` | **JPY** | 0 | 1250 |
> | `1250` | **KWD** | 3 | 1.250 |
>
> **Same number, three amounts.** One of them is a thousand times the other.
>
> **The list.** The code list lives in **one service**. It is updated from the **published list**,
> and the **date of our copy** is shown on that service's status page. Applications ask the service;
> they do not keep their own copy.
>
> **Two lists, not one.** The service holds the **current** list, used to accept new payments, and
> the **historical** list, used to display old ones. **A code that has been withdrawn must still
> print**, because the payment is still in the database.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Exchange rates.** A rate is a price from a market, it changes by the
