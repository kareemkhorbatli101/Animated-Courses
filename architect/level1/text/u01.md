%%SUB The date format everybody says they use — and the week rule nobody expects%%
%%GOALS
Say and write dates in the international format
Use the present simple for what a system does
Use *on*, *in* and *at* with dates and times
%%

Four digits, a dash, two digits, a dash, two digits. `2026-03-09`. It sorts correctly as text, it
has no ambiguity between day and month, and almost every team in the world says it uses it. The
standard behind it also contains a week-numbering rule, and that rule puts some days of January in
the week of the year before.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Read the name of a standard with its year. *ISO 8601* is a family of documents
> from different years, and the one you mean is the one with the date on it.

%%FIG a01-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a short table of three date forms with one example each, a boxed paragraph about the week rule with two example dates in it, a line about what a profile does, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 1
> *Platform team · how we write dates · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO 8601** is the international standard for writing dates and times. The current
> edition is in two parts, both published in **2019**: **ISO 8601-1:2019**, which covers the basic
> rules, and **ISO 8601-2:2019**, which covers extensions. The 2019 edition replaced the single
> document of **2004**.
>
> **Three ways to write one day.**
>
> | Form | Example | What the parts mean |
> |---|---|---|
> | **calendar date** | `2026-03-09` | year, month, day |
> | **ordinal date** | `2026-068` | year, day of the year |
> | **week date** | `2026-W11-1` | year, week number, day of the week |
>
> All three are **the same day**. The first is the one we use.
>
> **With separators and without.** `2026-03-09` is the **extended** format. `20260309` is the
> **basic** format. Both are in the standard. We use the extended format everywhere, because people
> read it and because mistakes are easier to see.
>
> **A date and a time together.** The time comes after the letter **T**: `2026-03-09T14:05:00`. The
> letter **Z** at the end means the time is in UTC: `2026-03-09T14:05:00Z`. Unit 2 is about what that
> means.
>
> **The week rule, which surprises people.**
>
> > In the standard, the week starts on **Monday**. Day 1 is Monday and day 7 is Sunday. Week 01 of a
> > year is the week that contains **4 January** — which is the same as saying the week with the
> > year's first Thursday in it.
> >
> > So the first days of January can belong to the **last week of the year before**. **1 January
> > 2027 is a Friday, and it falls in week 53 of 2026.** A report for "week 01" is therefore not
> > always a report about January.
>
> **Why the year first.** Two reasons, and both are practical. **It is not ambiguous**: `2026-03-09`
> is the ninth of March in every country. And **it sorts correctly as text**: a simple alphabetical
> sort of a list of these dates puts them in date order, with no special code.
>
> **What the standard does not do.** It does not choose one form for you. A system that uses the
> ordinal date and a system that uses the calendar date are **both** using ISO 8601, and they cannot
> read each other. For that reason the internet has a **profile** of the standard — a smaller set of
> rules — and interfaces usually say *RFC 3339*, not *ISO 8601*. **Saying "we use ISO 8601" does not
> tell another team what your field looks like.**
>
> *Source: ISO 8601-1:2019 and ISO 8601-2:2019, Date and time — Representations for information
> interchange, which replaced ISO 8601:2004; the week-numbering rule that week 01 contains 4 January
> and that the week begins on Monday; and RFC 3339 (2002), a profile of ISO 8601 for internet
> timestamps. ISO standards are sold by their publisher and are not free to read.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Dates are where international teams discover that they are international. In some countries people
write the day first, in others the month, and in others the year — and every one of those habits is
correct at home and dangerous in a shared file. The international format solves the problem for
machines, and it does not solve it for people: a screen that shows `2026-03-09` to a customer is
technically perfect and slightly cold, and a good system stores the long form and shows the
customer's own form. The professional habit is to keep the two apart in your head and in your
documents: **one format for storing and sending, the user's own language for showing.** Teams that
confuse the two produce either unreadable screens or unsortable data, and both are expensive.

**Discuss:** How is a date written where you live? What would you show a customer there?
