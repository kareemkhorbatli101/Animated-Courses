# Unit 11 · The Reader's Language
%%SUB A language tag has parts, and more parts means more specific — not better%%
%%GOALS
Use possessives
Compare one tag with another
Say what a language tag does not tell you
%%

A reader's language is written as a short tag: a language, and then, if you need them, a script and a
region. More parts make the tag more specific, and more specific is not the same as more correct. A
language tag also answers fewer questions than most teams think: it is not the reader's country, and
it does not tell you how to write a date.

%%FIG a11-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the document numbers and dates, a diagram of a tag broken into language, script and region boxes, a four-row table of example tags with what each one means, a boxed paragraph on falling back, a short list of what a tag does not tell you, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 11
> *Platform team · language tags · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The documents. BCP 14 gave us MUST; BCP 47 gives us languages.** It is made of **RFC 5646**,
> *Tags for Identifying Languages*, published in **September 2009**, and **RFC 4647**, on **matching**
> language tags. The subtags themselves live in the **IANA Language Subtag Registry**, which is
> updated as languages, scripts and regions are added.
>
> **The parts, in order.**
>
> | Part | Length | Taken from |
> |---|---|---|
> | **language** | 2 or 3 letters | the ISO language code standards |
> | **script** | **4** letters | the ISO script code standard |
> | **region** | **2** letters, or 3 digits | the country codes of Unit 7, or a UN area code |
> | variant and more | varies | the registry |
>
> **Four examples.**
>
> | Tag | Means |
> |---|---|
> | **en** | English, with nothing said about script or region |
> | **en-GB** | English as written in one region |
> | **zh-Hant** | Chinese in one script, with no region |
> | **sr-Latn-RS** | one language, one script, one region |
>
> **The conventional spelling.** The language part is written in **lower case**, the script part with
> **one capital letter** and the region part in **capitals**. This is a convention that makes tags
> readable; **tags are compared without regard to case**, so a system must not reject a tag because
> of its capitals.
>
> **More parts means narrower, not better.**
>
> > A longer tag is **more specific**. It is not more correct. If you have content for **en** and the
> > reader asks for **en-GB**, the right answer is to give them the English you have, because the
> > language is the part that matters most and the region is a refinement.
> >
> > This is **falling back**: remove the last part of the tag and look again. **en-GB** becomes
> > **en**. A reader who asked for a regional variety and received the plain language has been served
> > well. A reader who asked for one language and received another has not.
>
> **The reader sends a list, in order.** A browser does not send one language; it sends the reader's
> **ordered list of preferences**. **Their order is the answer to the question.** Walk down their
> list, not yours.
>
> **What a language tag does not tell you.**
>
> - **Not the reader's country.** The region part describes a **variety of the language**, not where
>   the person is sitting. Someone may read one regional variety anywhere in the world.
> - **Not the currency.** That has its own standard, as Unit 5 said.
> - **Not how to write a date, a number or a name.** Those belong to a **locale**, which is a larger
>   idea built from other data; a tag is an identifier, not a set of formats.
> - **Not which language a country uses.** Many countries have several, and many languages are used
>   in many countries. **A country is not a language and a language is not a country.**
> - **Not a translation.** The tag identifies what you have. It does not produce it.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Read the reader's list in their order, fall back by removing the last
> part, and never guess a language from a country.
>
> *Source: BCP 47 — RFC 5646, Tags for Identifying Languages, September 2009, and RFC 4647, Matching
> of Language Tags — with subtags maintained in the IANA Language Subtag Registry: the order and
> lengths of the language, script and region subtags; the sources of those subtags in the ISO language,
> script and country code standards and UN area codes; the conventional capitalization and the
> case-insensitive comparison of tags; and the matching scheme that progressively removes subtags. The
> documents do not supply translations, locale formatting data, or any mapping from country to
> language.*

### 1 · Possessives

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS1-11-06%%

> **Hana:** Read me your sentence about the fallback.
> **Adeel:** *We use the language of the reader, or the language of the browser of the reader.*
> **Hana:** Correct, and nobody will finish reading it. Two *of*s in one sentence. Use the apostrophe:
> *the reader's language*, *the reader's browser*.
> **Adeel:** When do I use *of* instead?
> **Hana:** For things rather than people, usually: *the order of the list*, *the length of the tag*,
> *the end of the document*. With people and organizations, the apostrophe: *the reader's language*,
> *the customer's preference*, *Meridian's default*.
> **Adeel:** And for more than one reader?
> **Hana:** The apostrophe goes after the *s*: *our readers' languages*. One reader, *reader's*. Many
> readers, *readers'*. In speech they sound the same, which is why this is a writing problem and not
> a speaking problem.
> **Adeel:** What about *its*?
> **Hana:** *Its* with no apostrophe is the possessive: *the tag and its parts*. **With an apostrophe,
> *it's* means *it is*, and it is the most common mistake in technical English.** Say the sentence
> with *it is* and see whether it still makes sense. If it does not, drop the apostrophe.

*What test does Hana give for* its *and* it's?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Choosing a reader's language from their location is the most common guess in international software,
and it is made by teams who are trying to be helpful. It works for the majority in most countries,
which is exactly why it survives: the people it fails are a minority everywhere and a majority
nowhere, so they never appear as a group in anybody's numbers. They appear one at a time, as
confused individuals, and often they do not complain — they assume they have changed a setting by
accident. The honest alternative costs nothing, because the reader's own ordered list arrives with
every request. The deeper habit worth building is the last line: when you have had to guess, say so
on the page. A product that shows its reasoning can be corrected by the person who knows best.

**Discuss:** Where else does your product guess something about a reader that the reader could have
told you?

> ### Field note · choosing a language
> *Platform team · interface document, section 11.1 · 25 May*
>
> **Where the answer comes from.** The reader's **own ordered list of preferred languages**, sent
> with the request. **Their order decides**, not ours.
>
> **The steps.**
>
> | | Step |
> |---|---|
> | 1 | take the **first** tag on the reader's list |
> | 2 | if we have content for it, show that and stop |
> | 3 | **remove the last subtag** and look again: *en-GB* → *en* |
> | 4 | if still nothing, move to the **next tag on their list** and return to step 2 |
> | 5 | if the list is exhausted, show our default **and say so** |
>
> **The notice.** When we show anything other than the reader's first choice, **one line at the top
> of the page** names the language being shown and offers a way to change it. **A reader who can see
> the choice can correct it.**
>
> **Case.** Tags are compared **without regard to case**. A tag in capitals **is never rejected**.
>
> **What we never use.** **The country the request came from.** A country is **not** a language, and
> the reader's location is **not** the region subtag.
>
> **What a tag does not give us.** The **currency**, the **date format**, the **number format**, the
> **name order**, or a **translation**. Each of those is held elsewhere.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Which languages we translate into.** That is a commercial decision and it
> is listed in section 11.4.
>
> *The region subtag describes a variety of a language. It says nothing about where the reader is.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Numbered steps, and step five is the honest one. Every rule like this needs a
> line that says what happens when the rule runs out, and a note without it will be interpreted by
> whoever is on duty.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. The order comes from (the reader's list / our list).
2. *en-GB* falls back to (our default / *en*).
3. A tag in capitals is (rejected / accepted).
4. The country of the request is (used / never used).
