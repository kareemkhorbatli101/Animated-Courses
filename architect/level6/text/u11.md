# Unit 11 · Three Suppliers Deep
%%SUB Flow-down clauses, tier-one visibility, and the honest limit of any supply chain claim%%
%%GOALS
Concede at length and still conclude
Say what flow-down requirements can and cannot achieve
State the limit of your own visibility
%%

A publication on supply chain risk sets out practices for managing what you cannot see. The central
difficulty is admitted in the document itself: you have a contract with your supplier and none with
your supplier's supplier, so everything beyond the first tier depends on a clause traveling
downwards through agreements you will never read. The English of this unit is the English of
conceding that and still saying something useful.

### 1 · Concessive subordination

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS6-11-06%%

> **Lucia:** Your draft begins half its paragraphs by admitting something. I have been taught never to
> do that.
> **Hana:** You have been taught to write sales material, which is a different genre with different
> readers. **This is read by somebody whose job is to find the weakness in it, and a document that
> concedes nothing hands them the pleasure of finding the concession themselves.**
> **Lucia:** And if they stop reading at the concession?
> **Hana:** That is the real risk, and it is a matter of sentence order rather than content. **A
> concessive clause must come first and the conclusion second: *while nine contracts carry the
> clause, we can verify nothing* ends badly. *While we can verify nothing below tier one, nine of
> eleven contracts carry the clause and six suppliers have attested* ends well.**
> **Lucia:** Same two facts.
> **Hana:** The same two facts, and the English decides which one the reader carries away. **The
> subordinate clause is the one they forgive; the main clause is the one they quote.**
> **Lucia:** What is the difference between *while* and *notwithstanding*?
> **Hana:** Weight. *While* is neutral and I use it most. **_Notwithstanding_ is formal, and it does
> something slightly different: it concedes and then dismisses, which is right when the concession is
> true and irrelevant.** *Notwithstanding the length of the publication, it cannot make a chain
> visible.*
> **Lucia:** And *albeit*?
> **Hana:** Compact, for a qualification rather than an argument. **_Albeit less reassuring, it is the
> only version a customer can rely on._** Eight words doing the work of two sentences. **I allow
> myself one per document, because it is slightly literary and a second one makes the page sound
> pleased with itself.**
> **Lucia:** *Granted that*?
> **Hana:** The strongest, and the one I use when I am about to disagree with somebody who is right.
> **_Granted that the clause is enforceable against our supplier, it gives us no relationship with
> anybody below them._** **You cannot be accused of ignoring the point you have just stated better
> than your opponent did.**
> **Lucia:** That is the whole technique, is it not.
> **Hana:** **Concede first, concede accurately, conclude anyway. And never concede in the main
> clause, because that is the sentence that gets quoted in somebody else's board paper.**

*Why must the concession come first?*

**1B  Underline six concessive structures in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **while** *(neutral)* | **While** we can verify nothing below tier one, nine contracts carry the clause. |
| **notwithstanding** *(concede and dismiss)* | **Notwithstanding** its length, it cannot make a chain visible. |
| **albeit** *(compact qualification)* | **Albeit** less reassuring, it is the only reliable version. |
| **granted that** *(the opponent is right)* | **Granted that** the clause binds our supplier, it binds nobody below. |
| **even though** | **Even though** six have attested, none of the six is verifiable. |
| **for all that** | **For all that**, eleven tiers-one are more than most competitors can name. |
| *the wrong order* | ✗ Nine contracts carry the clause, **although** we can verify nothing. |

> **Grammar Note** — Concede in the subordinate clause and conclude in the main one. The subordinate
> clause is forgiven; the main clause is quoted.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A four-word claim will usually beat four careful paragraphs, and supply chain assurance is the
exception, for a reason worth understanding: the person scoring your answer has tried to map their
own supply chain and failed. They know what lies below the first tier, which is a set of companies
you have no contract with, no relationship with and no right to ask anything of, and a claim to have
assured four tiers therefore reads not as confidence but as evidence that nobody tried. Nor is the
four-word claim usually dishonest — somebody wrote it on a Friday and has never been asked to defend
it. The technique that makes the honest version work is entirely grammatical: concede in the
subordinate clause and conclude in the main one, because the subordinate clause is forgiven and the
main clause is quoted. And finish with something actionable: three supplier names and a renewal date
each. A page of concessions with no action in it is not honesty; it is modesty, and nobody buys
that either.

> *Platform team · for Tarn Energy procurement · 6 June*
>
> **What we can show.** **Contracts with 11 tier-one suppliers.** **9 of the 11 contracts contain
> flow-down requirements.** All **9** were asked whether those requirements were passed on; **6

>
> | | Count |
> |---|---|

>
