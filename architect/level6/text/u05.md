# Unit 5 · Eleven Principles, One Framework
%%SUB Eleven named principles, and the three words where an over-claim hides%%
%%GOALS
Use scope quantifiers precisely
Name the eleven privacy principles
Say why a framework's vocabulary is not a jurisdiction's
%%

A privacy framework names eleven principles and defines the roles around personal information. It is
high-level by design: it implements nothing, requires nothing and belongs to no jurisdiction. The
most dangerous words in any answer about it are *generally*, *where appropriate* and *in most
cases*, and this unit is largely about those. **Nothing in it is legal advice.**

> **A:** The questionnaire asks whether we observe the eleven principles. I wrote *we generally
> observe all eleven*.
> **B:** Then you have written a sentence with a quantifier in it that nobody can check, and **the
> reader's first question is which of the eleven *generally* is covering for.**
> **A:** All of them, roughly.
> **B:** *Roughly* across eleven items can mean one serious gap or eleven small ones, and those are
> different companies. **The honest version counts: we observe seven fully, three in part and one not
> at all.** Eleven items, eleven answers.
> **A:** Which one do we not observe?
> **B:** **Retention.** We have no retention limit on four datasets, which means we observe *use,
> retention and disclosure limitation* in no useful sense for those four. **That is not a *generally*;
> it is a zero with a name on it.**
> **A:** And *where appropriate*?
> **B:** The worst of the three, because it reads like a legal term and is doing no work. **Appropriate
> as judged by whom, against what?** If we mean *for datasets containing personal information*, the
> sentence should say that, and then somebody can check it.
> **A:** The framework uses that kind of language itself.
> **B:** It does, and it has to: it is written for every organization in the world and cannot know
> which cases are appropriate for us. **We can. A framework is permitted to be vague in exactly the
> places where we are not.**
> **A:** Does any of this map to the law?
> **B:** **Not directly, and that is a sentence worth putting in writing. The framework's vocabulary
> is its own — it says *personal information* where a statute may say something else — and it belongs
> to no jurisdiction. Our legal obligations are a separate question with a separate owner.**

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS6-05-06%%

> **Lucia:** I have circled three phrases in last year's privacy answer, and I would like to know
> whether I am being pedantic.
> **Paula:** Read them.
> **Lucia:** *We generally observe all eleven principles.* *Personal information is retained only

> **Paula:** You are not being pedantic. **Those three phrases are, between them, the entire gap

> **Lucia:** Start with *generally*.
> **Paula:** *Generally* across eleven items is unreadable. **One serious failure and eleven trivial
> ones produce the same word, and they are different companies.** The counted version is seven, three
> and one, and the one is retention — which is a zero.
> **Lucia:** And *where appropriate*?
> **Paula:** The one I find most interesting, because it sounds legal and means nothing. **Appropriate
> judged by whom, against what criterion?** If we mean *for datasets containing personal
> information*, that is a checkable sentence. *Where appropriate* is a gesture at a criterion that
> does not exist.
> **Lucia:** *In most cases* is the one I would have caught myself.
> **Paula:** It is the one that would have caught us. **It concedes that some requests are not
> answered and declines to say how many or which, which is the worst of both: the admission without
> the information.** The counted version is ninety-four of a hundred and one, with the seven listed.

> **Paula:** It does, and this is the distinction I want in the note. **It is addressing every

> correct in a framework and dishonest in an answer about one company.**

> **Paula:** One, and we have used it. **_Most of the fields on three forms are used; the unused ones
> are listed in the annex._** **A quantifier with its exception enumerated two lines later is doing
> real work.** A quantifier whose exception is nowhere in the document is hiding something, whether or
> not anybody meant it to.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Three phrases do most of the concealing in corporate documents, and none of them is written with
intent to conceal: *generally*, *where appropriate*, and *in most cases*. Each becomes available at
precisely the moment counting would be expensive — answering eleven principles honestly means
finding eleven separate answers, and a single adverb covers the gap until somebody has time. Nobody
ever has time, the sentence ships, and next year it is the template. The diagnostic is simple enough
to apply to your own writing: find the quantifier, then look for its exception. If the exception is
enumerated nearby — *most fields are used; the unused ones are listed in the annex* — the quantifier
is doing real work. If the exception appears nowhere in the document, the quantifier is hiding
something, whatever its author intended. Frameworks themselves are full of such language and are
entitled to be: a document addressing every organization on earth cannot know which cases are
appropriate for yours. You can, and that asymmetry is the whole of this unit.

> ### Questionnaire response · privacy principles
> *Data group · for Alba Health procurement · 16 May · not legal advice*
>
> **What the framework is.** **ISO/IEC 29100**, a **privacy framework**, first published **2011**,
> with a **second edition carrying 2024**. It is **high-level**: it defines terminology and roles and
> names **eleven principles**. **It specifies no requirements, prescribes no implementation, and maps
> to no jurisdiction's law.** Its vocabulary is its own — it says **personal information** where a
> statute in your market may use a different term with a different boundary.
>

>
> | Principle | Answer | Detail |
> |---|---|---|
> | collection limitation | **part** | **three forms** collect fields nobody reads; **listed in the annex** |
> | data minimization | **part** | consequence of the above |
> | **use, retention and disclosure limitation** | **none** | **four datasets have no retention limit of any kind** |
> | individual participation and access | **part** | answered **in 94 of 101 requests**; **the seven are listed** |
>
> **The zero, named.** **Four datasets have no retention limit** — not a long one, **none**. One is the
> **historical freight movements table, in daily use since 2019**, about which **we also cannot
> establish how the original sample was drawn**. **Both of those sentences are in our own
> documentation and we would rather you read them here.**
>
> **Three phrases we removed from this answer, and why.** Our response last year said *we generally
> observe all eleven principles*, *retained only where appropriate*, and *answered in most cases*.
>
> - ***Generally*** across eleven items **cannot distinguish one serious failure from eleven trivial
>   ones.**
> - ***Where appropriate*** supplies **no criterion and no judge**. Where we mean *for datasets
>   containing personal information*, we now write that.
> - ***In most cases*** **concedes a gap and conceals its size**. It was seven of 101.
>
> **The one quantifier we have kept.** **Most** of the fields on three forms are used; **the unused
> ones are listed in the annex**. **A quantifier whose exception is enumerated nearby is doing work.**
>

> **nothing in this response is legal advice**, and the owner of that question is not the author of
> this page.
>
> **Not in this response.** **Retention periods for the four datasets.** **Those** require a decision
> we have not made, and the decision is due on **31 July**.
>
> *A quantifier whose exception appears nowhere in the document is hiding something, whatever its
> author intended.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Find every quantifier, then look for its exception. Enumerated nearby, it is
> doing work; absent from the document, it is concealing a number you have not found.

**Listen and read.** %%AUD KS6-05-16%%

> Meridian's privacy answer last year read: *we generally observe all eleven principles, and personal
> information is retained only where appropriate.* Twenty-two words.
>
> Counted, the position was seven principles observed fully, three in part and one not at all. The one
> is retention. Four datasets have no retention limit of any kind, and one of them is a table in daily
> use since 2019 about which the company also cannot establish how the original sample was drawn.
>
> Nobody wrote the twenty-two words intending to conceal anything. Counting eleven principles
> honestly requires somebody to go and find eleven separate answers, and *generally* is available in
> the meantime.
>
> Paula's note on the file is four sentences. *The sentence shipped because counting was expensive and
> an adverb was free, and the following year it was the template. The test for any quantifier is
> whether its exception is enumerated nearby: "most fields are used, the unused ones are listed" is
> doing work, and "where appropriate" supplies neither a criterion nor a judge. A framework addressing
> every organization on earth is entitled to that vagueness and we are not, which is the whole
> asymmetry. And we have said in the answer which three phrases we removed, because a reader who sees
> that somebody decided something reads the zero as a finding rather than a weakness.*

2. Why does Paula say nobody intended to conceal it?
3. Do you agree with her last sentence? Which phrase would you remove first?
