# Unit 4 · Practices, Tasks and Examples
%%SUB Four groups, nineteen practices, and the word "notional" doing a lot of work%%

A framework for secure software development sets out nineteen practices in four groups. Each
practice has tasks, and each task has examples described as **notional** — a word chosen carefully,
because the framework is a statement of outcomes rather than a list of tools. Companies mostly use
it as the second thing, which is how a nineteen-row table becomes a purchase order.

### 1 · Participle clauses

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS6-04-06%%

> **Tomas:** Your draft is two pages shorter than mine and says more. I want to know what you removed.
> **Hana:** Subjects and finite verbs, mostly. **Four of your paragraphs each contained two sentences
> that should have been one.** *We reviewed the nineteen practices. We then found that four had no
> owner.* That is *Reviewing the nineteen practices, we found that four had no owner.*
> **Tomas:** Which saves four words.
> **Hana:** Four words a sentence, across a long document, is a page. **But that is not why I do it.
> A participle clause subordinates one action to another, and it tells the reader which of the two is
> the point.** In your version, reviewing and finding have equal weight. In mine, the finding is the
> sentence and the reviewing is context.
> **Tomas:** Is there a cost?
> **Hana:** A serious one, and it is the reason I check every single one. **A participle clause takes
> its subject from the main clause, and if the subjects differ you get a sentence that says something
> absurd.** *Having reviewed the practices, four of them had no owner.* **The practices reviewed
> themselves.**
> **Tomas:** I would not notice that in my own writing.
> **Hana:** Nobody does. **Which is why the rule I use is mechanical: read the participle, then read
> the subject of the main clause, and ask whether that subject did the participle's action.** If it
> did not, write two sentences.
> **Tomas:** And the worse version of the same problem?
> **Hana:** The one that removes a person on purpose, and I have done this without meaning to.
> **_Having been identified, the gaps were scheduled for June._** Identified by whom? Scheduled by
> whom? **Two agents gone in eleven words, and the sentence sounds more professional than the version
> with names in it.** That is the trap: the condensed sentence sounds better and says less.
> **Tomas:** So where do you use them?
> **Hana:** **For the context clause, never for the commitment.** *Having read several hundred
> questionnaires, their team can tell the difference* — fine, the subject is their team and they did
> the reading. **But the four commitments in this note are full sentences with names and dates in
> them, because that is what somebody will be held to.**

*What mechanical check does Hana use on every participle clause?*

|---|---|

> **Grammar Note** — Use participle clauses for context, never for a commitment. They remove

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The word *partially* has been used so widely in procurement answers that it now carries no
information at all: nineteen rows of it and nineteen blank rows tell a reader exactly the same thing.
The people who read these documents professionally read several hundred a year and have long since
stopped seeing the word, which means that careful work can disappear entirely in the output —
somebody reads a nineteen-practice framework twice and then writes the one word that erases the
reading. What does reach them is a named zero: a practice the company does not do at all, with an
owner and a date beside it. The reasoning is not moral but practical. A zero with nothing beside it
is a liability and a reason to score you down; a zero with a name and a date is a plan, and plans are
what a procurement team is trying to buy. The uncomfortable corollary is that you have to decide the
owner before you send the answer, which is why most companies write *partial* instead.
