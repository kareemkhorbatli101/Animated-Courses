> 4. Access is determined by **dynamic policy**, including the observable state of client identity,

> 5. The enterprise **monitors and measures the integrity and security posture** of all owned and
>    associated assets.
> 6. Resource authentication and authorization are **dynamic and strictly enforced before access is

> 7. The enterprise **collects as much information as possible** about current state and uses it to
>    improve its posture.
>
> **The three logical components.** A **policy engine**, which makes the decision; a **policy
> administrator**, which issues or revokes the credential for a session; and a **policy enforcement
> point**, which sits in the path of the request and enables, monitors and terminates it.
>
> **Unpacking the fourth tenet, because it contains six decisions.**
>
> > *"Access is determined by dynamic policy, including the observable state of client identity,
> > application and requesting asset."*
> >

> > application for this purpose; what we can actually observe about a requesting device; how often

> > that policy.** **Six decisions, none of which a vendor can make for us**, inside one noun phrase
> > of fourteen words.

> **Oksana:** The board paper came back with one comment. *Too technical.* It is four pages.

> **Hana:** Nine words, and I count four separate decisions inside it, none of them made. **That is

> **Oksana:** It is how the source document writes.
> **Hana:** It is, and the source document is right to, because it is describing an architecture for
> thousands of organizations it has never met. **A noun phrase that heavy is a container: it holds a
> general shape so that each reader can put their own specifics in.** We are the reader. We are
> supposed to take the specifics out.
> **Oksana:** Take that one apart, then.
> **Hana:** *Continuous* — how often, in seconds? *Device posture* — which three or four properties,

> becomes: *every fifteen minutes we check patch level, disk encryption and whether the agent is
> running, and a failure blocks new sessions to the billing database.***

> **Hana:** Which is longer and can be argued with, costed, and refused by somebody. **The nine-word
> version cannot be refused, because there is nothing in it to refuse. That is why it passed three
> reviews.**

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A phrase that names an architecture will eventually be printed on the side of something you can buy,
and the mechanism is grammatical rather than commercial. Standards and architecture publications
write in heavy noun phrases because they are addressing thousands of organizations whose details they
cannot know: a four-word phrase is a container, and each reader is supposed to put their own
specifics into it. What happens instead is that the container is forwarded. It appears in an internal
paper, then a slide, then a budget line, and at no point does anybody ask how often *continuous*
means, which properties count, or whether a failure blocks or merely warns. The phrase survives every
review precisely because there is nothing in it to refuse. The repair is tedious and takes an
afternoon: underline every noun phrase of more than four words with no verb near it, and ask who
decided each thing inside. The answer is usually nobody, and the finding is worth more than the rest
of the paper.

> ### Board paper · access architecture, revised
> *Platform team · section 3.1 · 9 May · replaces the paper of 14 March*
>
> **Why this paper is four sentences long.** The paper it replaces was four pages and **contained
> seven noun phrases of four or five words each, five of which contained decisions nobody had made.**
> It passed three reviews. **A phrase with nothing in it to refuse is very easy to approve**, and it
> produced a budget line for a thing that cannot be bought.
>
> **What the document is.** **NIST Special Publication 800-207**, *Zero Trust Architecture*, **August
> 2020**. It is **guidance**. It sets out **seven tenets** and a logical architecture of **policy
> engine, policy administrator and policy enforcement point**. **It states that there is no single
> deployment plan and that migration is incremental**, and it describes threats to the architecture
> itself, including the policy engine as a new high-value target.
>
> **The principle, in one line.** **No request is trusted because of where it came from.**
>
> **What that means here, specifically.**
>
> | What we trust today | Why | Who decides the replacement |
> |---|---|---|
> | **4 databases** reachable from anywhere on the internal network | network location | the platform team |
> | **1 shared credential** used by **11 scheduled jobs** | a 2017 convenience | **nobody — this is the finding** |
> | **1 reporting service** that authenticates nothing | **correct in 2014; nothing outside could reach it** | **nobody** |
>
> **One phrase, unpacked, as an example of what the old paper did not do.** *Continuous device
> posture assessment* means, for us: **every 15 minutes we check patch level, disk encryption and
> whether the management agent is running; a failure blocks new sessions to the billing database and
> does not terminate existing ones.** **That sentence can be costed, argued with and refused.** The
> four-word version cannot.
>
> **What we are asking for.** **One application, migrated from June**, with enforcement points and an
> identity service purchased as ordinary products. **We are asking for the budget line named after
> the architecture to be removed**, because no supplier can sell the removal of our own implicit
> trust.
>
> **What this does not achieve.** **It does not eliminate risk.** It moves it, and it creates a new
> concentration of it in the policy engine. **The publication says so, and so do we.**
>
> **Not in this paper.** **The eleven jobs on one credential.** **Those** are a separate paper,
> because splitting them touches three teams and one of them will say no.
>
> *A decision that was right once becomes invisible rather than wrong, and there is no moment at
> which anybody failed.*
