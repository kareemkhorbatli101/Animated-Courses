# Unit 12 · 90.93 and 95.99
%%SUB A review unit: how a standard lives and dies, and the register that outlives the project%%
%%GOALS
Review the structures of Units 1 to 11
Read a stage code and say what it means for a citation
Build a register that survives the people who made it
%%

Every document in this series has a number, an edition and a **status**, and the status is published
as a code. Two of those codes matter more than the rest: one means *reviewed and kept as it is*, and
one means *no longer published*. Four of this company's standing answers cite documents whose codes
nobody has ever looked at, which is where the book began.

> **A:** You said four of our standing answers cite documents that no longer exist as cited. Which
> four?
> **B:** Two cite editions that have been **superseded**. One cites a standard that has been
> **withdrawn** — code **95.99** — and the answer still says *we conform to it*. **And one cites a
> document that is perfectly current and shows code 90.93, which somebody read as *out of date*.**
> **A:** What does 90.93 mean?
> **B:** **Reviewed and confirmed. Still current.** It means a committee looked at it, decided no
> change was needed, and set a new review date. **A confirmed standard is not an old standard; it is
> a standard that has survived a review.**
> **A:** And 95.99?
> **B:** **Withdrawn.** Which is not the same as wrong. **A standard can be withdrawn because it has
> been replaced, because the technology is gone, or because nobody needs it any more.** What it means
> for us is narrow and absolute: **you cannot conform to a document that is no longer published.**
> **A:** How did nobody notice?
> **B:** Because a citation is the one part of a document that looks permanent. **Not once in four
> years did anybody check a code**, and the answers were copied forward each year by careful people.
> **A:** So we check them annually?
> **B:** **We check them, and we record the check. One column for the code, one for the date it was
> checked, one for who checked it.** A register with no date on it is the same problem one layer up.
> **A:** And when something is withdrawn?
> **B:** **We say so, and say what we do instead. *That standard was withdrawn in 2021; our practice
> is unchanged and is described below* is a strong sentence. *We conform to a withdrawn standard* is
> not a sentence at all.**

> ## Meridian · governance note 28
> *Platform team · the status of everything we cite · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The system.** Standards bodies publish a **stage code** for every document: a number pair showing
> where it stands in its life. The codes are **published and public**, and **what they describe is a
> process position rather than a judgment of quality.**
>
> **The stages that matter to a reader.**
>
> | Code | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **60.60** | published and current |
> | **90.20** | under periodic review |
> | **90.92** | to be revised or amended |
> | **90.93** | **reviewed and confirmed — still current** |
> | **95.99** | **withdrawn — no longer published** |
>
> **The two that cause the errors.**
>
> > **What 90.93 does not mean is *out of date*.** It means a committee examined the document,
> > decided that no change was needed, and set a new review date. **A confirmed standard has survived
> > a review, which is more than a recently published one can say.**
> >
> > **Nor does 95.99 mean *wrong*.** A document may be withdrawn because it has been replaced,
> > because the technology it describes has gone, or because nobody needs it. **What it means for a
> > citation is narrow and absolute: you cannot conform to a document that is no longer published.**
>
> **Our four standing answers, checked for the first time.**
>
> | Answer cites | Status | What the answer says |
> |---|---|---|
> | an edition of 2015 | **superseded** | *conforms to the 2015 edition* — true, and nobody asked for that edition |
> | an edition of 2011 | **superseded** | *conforms* — the current edition differs in two clauses |
> | a standard withdrawn in 2021 | **95.99** | ***we conform to it*** — **not a sentence at all** |
> | a current standard | **90.93** | **removed last year by somebody who read the code as *expired*** |
>
> **Having read all four**, the pattern is not carelessness. **Each answer was copied forward
> annually by a careful person, and in four years nobody was asked to check a code.** **Not once did
> a review of these documents include the question *does this still exist?***
>
> **What we have built instead.** A register with four columns: **the document, the edition we cite,
> its status code, and the date the code was checked, with the name of whoever checked it.**
> **Granted that this is administrative work of the dullest kind, it is the only part of our
> governance that will still be true in five years without anybody thinking about it.**
>
> *Source: the harmonized stage codes published by the international standards bodies for documents
> in development and in review, in which 60.60 denotes a published standard, 90.93 a standard
> reviewed and confirmed as current, and 95.99 a withdrawn standard. The codes describe a document's
> position in its life cycle and are not a judgment of its quality; a confirmed standard is current,
> and a withdrawn standard cannot be conformed to.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Six books of documents end at the same place: a sentence somebody wrote because the true sentence was
more expensive. *Supported*, two hundred times, because checking would take a week. *We generally
observe all eleven principles*, because counting means finding eleven separate answers. *Nobody could
have known*, because the accurate version has a name in it. None of the structures that fix these is
difficult English — a cleft, an attribution, a trigger event, a concession in the subordinate clause —
and every one of them costs more words than the sentence it replaces. That, rather than difficulty,
is why the cheap sentence gets written, and why the fix is organizational as much as linguistic:
somebody has to be asked. The smallest possible example is a four-column register of what you cite,
with the date each citation was checked. Nobody is against it. Nineteen of thirty-one rows had never
been checked in four years, and the column most likely to be deleted by somebody tidying up is the
date — which is the only column that makes the rest of it true.

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Register of citations · and what the first check found
> *Platform team · section 12.1 · 6 June · owner: Lucia Ferrara · next check: 6 June next year*
>
> **Why this register exists.** **What nobody had been asked in four years is whether the documents we
> cite still exist in the form we cite them.** The answers were copied forward annually by careful
> people whose task was understood as keeping the prose current.
>
> **The register, in four columns and one more that matters.** The **document**; the **edition we
> cite**; its published **status code**; and **the date the code was checked, with the initials of
> whoever checked it**. **Granted that the last column is administrative work of the dullest available
> kind, a register without it looks exactly like a register that is current — which is the whole
> problem one layer up.**
>
> **What the first pass found, across 31 cited documents.**
>
> | Finding | Count |
> |---|---|
> | published and current (60.60) | 23 |
> | **reviewed and confirmed (90.93) — still current** | 4 |
> | **superseded** — we cite the old edition | 3 |
> | **withdrawn (95.99)** | **1** |
> | **never checked before June** | **19** |
>
> **The two that matter.**
>
> - **One standing answer claimed conformance with a standard withdrawn in 2021.** **You cannot
>   conform to a document that is no longer published.** The replacement sentence: ***that standard
>   was withdrawn in 2021; our practice is unchanged and is described below.***
> - **One current standard was deleted from an answer last year** because a reader took **90.93** to
>   mean *expired*. **It means reviewed and confirmed. A confirmed standard has survived a review,
>   which is more than a recently published one can say.** It is restored.
>
> **What this register is not.** **It is not a quality judgment.** A stage code describes a document's
> position in its life cycle; **withdrawn does not mean wrong, and confirmed does not mean old.** And
> **it says nothing about whether we follow any of these documents** — that is the other twenty-seven
> notes.
>
> **The one remaining criticism, from the reviewer who was in none of the rooms.** **Three of our
> pages name a document and a date and never state what the document cannot do.** **It is one line
> each, it was one line each a year ago, and it is still one line each.**
>
> **Not in this register.** **The nineteen checks themselves.** **Those** are dated between 2 and 6
> June, initialled, and the next pass is due in twelve months.
>
> *Every unit of these six books was about a sentence somebody wrote because the true sentence was
> more expensive.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The last column of any register — the date somebody checked, and who — is the
> one that makes the others true, and the first one anybody tidying up will delete.

> Meridian cites thirty-one external documents. Nineteen of them had never been checked to see

> the entire reason they do not get written. The smallest example is a four-column register that
> nobody is against and that nobody did for four years. And the column most likely to be deleted by
> somebody tidying up is the date, which is the only column that makes the rest of it true.*
