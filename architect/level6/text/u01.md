There are two widely used machine-readable formats for listing the components of a piece of
software, and both have been standardized by international bodies. A customer who asks for one is
usually asking a different question underneath, and the list on its own cannot answer it. What the
list does is make the question askable.

### 1 · Cleft sentences

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS6-01-06%%

> **Lucia:** I have to answer a procurement questionnaire with this note attached, and I want to
> understand why so many of your sentences are back to front.
> **Hana:** Show me one.
> **Lucia:** *What the list does not tell you is whether a vulnerability affects you.* Why not *the
> list does not tell you whether a vulnerability affects you*?
> **Hana:** Both are true, and they behave differently in a long document. **A procurement officer
> reading forty answers is skimming, and in a plain sentence the important words arrive in the
> middle.** The cleft puts the absence first, before anybody has decided whether to read on.
> **Lucia:** It feels theatrical.
> **Hana:** It is theatrical, and I use it no more than twice a page for exactly that reason. **A
> document written entirely in clefts reads as somebody insisting, and after three of them the
> emphasis stops working.** Twice. The two things I would be most unhappy to see misquoted.
> **Lucia:** And the other kind — *it is the generation method that decides*?
> **Hana:** For contrast, when a reader is likely to be focused on the wrong element. **Everybody
> asks which format we use, and the format is nearly irrelevant.** The it-cleft contradicts the
> expected answer without saying *you are asking the wrong question*, which is a sentence I would
> rather not put in writing to a customer.
> **Lucia:** That is a diplomatic use of grammar.
> **Hana:** It is the only diplomatic thing I know how to do. **And there is a third form I use once
> in a document: *all that the file proves is that a component is present*. *All that* makes the
> smallness of a claim audible.** A customer who reads that sentence does not need me to add a
> warning.
> **Lucia:** Is there a version I should avoid?
> **Hana:** The one that sounds defensive. ***What we were never asked to do was…*** **puts the
> emphasis on our innocence rather than on the fact, and a reader hears it.** The cleft should
> foreground information, never a grievance.

*How many clefts per page does Hana allow herself, and why?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A machine-readable file arrives with an authority its contents have not earned. Two lists in the same
format, parsed by the same tool, rendered in the same table, can be an exact record of a resolved
dependency graph and an educated guess about a finished binary — and nothing in the file makes the
difference visible. That is a presentation problem rather than a technical one, and the only place it
can be fixed is the covering sentence a human writes. The same applies to the entries a tool could
not identify. Both widely used formats have somewhere to say *this is unresolved*, and the pressure
to delete those rows before sending the file is enormous, because they are the only ugly thing on an
otherwise clean page. They are also the most informative rows in it: they tell the reader exactly
where your knowledge stops, which is the one thing they cannot obtain from any other source and the
one thing they will remember you for.

**Discuss:** What file does your company send that looks more authoritative than it is?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Covering note · component list for the freight platform
> *Platform team · for Northwind Rail procurement · 3 May*
>
> **What is attached.** A component list for all eleven services of the freight platform, in **both**
> widely used machine-readable formats: **SPDX**, standardized as **ISO/IEC 5962:2021** (which
> specifies **SPDX 2.2.1**; **later project releases exist and are not that edition**), and
> **CycloneDX**, published as **ECMA-424, first edition, 2024**. **Regenerated on every build;**
> the attached files are timestamped **2 May**.
>
> **How each list was produced, because the files do not say.**
>
> | Services | Method | Components | Unresolved |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 9 of 11 | **build-time, from the resolved dependency graph** | 1,847 | **0** |
> | 2 of 11 *(named in the annex)* | **inferred by scanning the finished binary** | 312 | **14** |
>
> **It is the generation method, not the format, that determines what the file is worth.** A
> build-time list records what the build resolved. **An inferred list records what a scanner
> recognized**, which is most but not all of what is present.
>
> **The fourteen unresolved entries.** **We have not removed them.** Both formats provide for an
> entry to be recorded as unidentified, and **what those fourteen rows tell you is exactly where our
> knowledge of those two services stops.** A file without them would parse more cleanly and tell you
> less.
>
> **What this file does not tell you.**
>
> - **What it does not tell you is whether a vulnerability in any listed component affects this
>   product.** That is a separate, machine-readable statement, which **we also produce** and which is
>   described in our note 52.
> - **All that the list establishes is which components are present**, with their versions and
>   relationships. It does not determine your licensing position or ours.
> - **It does not cover anything that is not a software component.** In particular, **the data our
>   models were trained on is not in it**, and **no version of either format currently requires it to
>   be**. We mention this because we expect to be asked.
>
> **What we are doing next, unasked.** **Moving the two inferred services to build-time generation**
> — six weeks — after which the unresolved count should be zero and this note will be shorter.
>
> **Not in this note.** **The three questions you did not ask**, which are whether we are affected by
> anything published this week and how quickly we could tell you. **Those** are notes 52 and 53, and
> we would rather send them now than at renewal.
>
> *A file that parses cleanly and conceals fourteen omissions is worth less than one that parses
> cleanly and declares them.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Use a cleft for the two sentences you would most hate to see misquoted, and no
> more. A page of clefts reads as insistence and the emphasis stops working.

### 3 · Write

Write **190 to 220 words**: a covering note for a file.

**Say what is attached, naming any format with its number and edition year, and noting where an
edition and a current release differ. Say how the file was produced, and name any part produced a
different way. Declare anything unresolved and say why you kept it. Use at least two cleft sentences
to foreground what the file does not tell the reader. Say what is not covered at all. Say what you
are doing next without being asked. End with one thing not in the note.**

**Read your note aloud to your partner first.** Count your clefts: more than three and cut one. Then
check your own against the list, and swap.

> A customer asked Meridian for a bill of materials. The questionnaire contained one item. Underneath
> it were three questions: whether the company knows what is in its product, whether any of it is
> affected by something published, and how quickly it could say so if that changed.
>
> Nine of eleven services produce a component list at build time, from the resolved dependency graph.
> Two produce it by scanning the finished binary. The two files look identical in every customer's
> tool.
>
> Fourteen entries in those two services could not be identified. Both formats provide a way to
> record that. Removing the fourteen rows would have produced a file that parsed more cleanly and
> told the customer less.
>
> Hana's note on the file is four sentences. *A machine-readable file arrives with an authority its
> contents have not earned, and the only place the difference can be stated is the covering sentence
> a human writes. What the fourteen rows tell the customer is exactly where our knowledge stops, which
> is the one thing they cannot get from any other source. We also said that the file does not cover
> what our models were trained on, because no format requires it yet and every questionnaire will ask
> next year. The company that mentions a thing before it is required is the one that looks organized
> when it becomes required.*

2. Why do the two kinds of file look identical?
3. Do you agree with Hana's last sentence? What will your company be asked next year?
