# Unit 2 · Not Affected, and Why
%%SUB Four status values, five justifications, and an assertion nobody verifies%%
%%GOALS
Keep a chain of attribution visible through three parties
Name the four exploitability status values
Say what a "not affected" statement actually asserts
%%

There is a machine-readable way of telling a customer that a published vulnerability in a component
you ship does not affect your product. It has four status values and a fixed vocabulary of reasons.
Every statement in it is an assertion by whoever issued it, verified by nobody, and the most
expensive mistake in this field is repeating somebody else's assertion as your own.

> **A:** Our supplier says their library is not affected by the advisory. Can I send that to the
> customer?
> **B:** You can send it, and **how you word it decides whose problem it becomes.** *The library is
> not affected* is our assertion. *Our supplier states that the library is not affected* is theirs,
> reported by us.
> **A:** Is that not the same information?
> **B:** The information is the same and the liability is not. **If their statement turns out to be
> wrong and we passed it on as ours, the customer's question comes to us and we have nothing behind
> it.** One clause of attribution costs five words.
> **A:** And if they ask what we think?
> **B:** Then we say what we checked. **We confirmed that the vulnerable function is not reached from
> any entry point in our build, and that is our assertion, which we will stand behind.** Two
> sentences, two owners.
> **A:** The format has reasons in it?
> **B:** A fixed list of five, for *not affected*. **The component is not present at all. The
> vulnerable code is not present. The vulnerable code is present but never in the execute path. It
> is reachable but cannot be controlled by an adversary. Or a mitigation is already in place.**
> **A:** Which do we use?
> **B:** The third, and here is why that matters. **The fixed vocabulary forces us to say which kind
> of *no* we mean, and four of those five can be wrong in completely different ways.** A reason
> string is not decoration; it is the thing a customer's security team actually reads.
> **A:** Does anybody verify it?
> **B:** **Nobody. It is an assertion in a standard envelope. The envelope is machine-readable; the
> honesty is entirely ours.**

> ## Meridian · architecture note 52
> *Platform team · exploitability statements · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The format. CSAF version 2.0**, an **OASIS Standard** approved in **November 2022**, which
> carries a **VEX profile** for exchanging **exploitability** information. The **status
> justification** vocabulary was published separately, in a short document dated **June 2022**, by a
> government cybersecurity agency working group.
>
> **The four status values.**
>
> | Status | What it asserts |
> |---|---|
> | **affected** | the vulnerability is exploitable in this product version |
> | **not affected** | it is not — and a **justification is required** |
> | **fixed** | it was, and a release addresses it |
> | **under investigation** | we do not yet know, and we are saying so |
>
> **The five justifications for *not affected*.**
>
> | Justification | In plain terms |
> |---|---|
> | component not present | the vulnerable component is not in this product at all |
> | vulnerable code not present | the component is here; that code is not |
> | **vulnerable code not in the execute path** | it is here and nothing ever calls it |
> | not controllable by an adversary | it runs, but no outside input reaches it |
> | inline mitigations already exist | something already deployed blocks the problem |
>
> **What a statement actually is.**
>
> > **It is an assertion by its issuer, in a standard envelope, verified by nobody.** The format
> > makes the assertion machine-readable and comparable. It does not make it true.
> >
> > **That is why the justification vocabulary matters more than the status.** *Not affected* is one
> > word; *the vulnerable code is present but never in the execute path* is a claim a security team
> > can argue with, and **four of the five reasons fail in completely different ways.** A reachability
> > claim fails when somebody adds a caller. A *component not present* claim fails when somebody adds
> > a dependency.
>
> **The supplier's claim, and how it left this building.** For one advisory in April, **our supplier
> asserted that their library was not affected.** The first draft of our customer note said *the
> library is not affected*. **That sentence adopts their assertion as ours.** The version we sent
> reads:
>
> > *Our supplier states that the library is not affected, on the grounds that the vulnerable code is
> > not present in the distributed build. **We have separately confirmed that the function in
> > question is not reachable from any entry point in our own service**, which is our own assertion
> > and the one we stand behind.*
>
> **Two assertions, two owners, five extra words.** **If the supplier's statement proves wrong, the
> customer knows whose statement it was. Ours still holds.**
>
> **What the format does not do.** It does not verify anything. It does not replace patching — a
> *not affected* statement about today's build says nothing about tomorrow's. It does not express
> severity, which is a different scale in a different document. And **its fixed vocabulary sometimes
> does not fit**, in which case the honest status is **under investigation** rather than the nearest
> available reason.
>

> because the longer version looks as though we do not trust them.
> **Hana:** It looks like that to them. **To the customer it looks like a company that knows which of
> its sentences it can defend**, and that is the reader who matters.
> **Oksana:** And then the advisory was reissued.
> **Hana:** And the supplier's claim turned out to be true for the current build and false for the

> **Oksana:** The difference being that one of those is a mistake and the other is a false statement
> to a customer.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The commonest way an honest company makes a false statement is by adopting somebody else's. A
supplier says a component is unaffected; the sentence is repeated in good faith, loses its
attribution somewhere between an email and a customer letter, and becomes a claim the company has no
basis for and cannot defend. Nobody lied at any point. The fix costs five words and is resisted for
a reason worth naming: attributing a long-standing supplier's claim reads to that supplier as
distrust, and the person writing the letter has to work with them next week. The reader who matters,
though, is the customer's security team, and what they see is a company that counts the hops between
a claim and whoever actually looked at the code. The corollary is the part most people have
backwards: attribute other people's claims carefully precisely so that your own findings can be
stated flat, with no hedging at all. A document that hedges every sentence equally tells its reader
nothing about what you know.

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Statement · advisory handling, April quarter
> *Platform team · for Northwind Rail security · 6 May · machine-readable version attached*
>
> **Format.** The attached file follows **CSAF 2.0**, an **OASIS Standard** of **November 2022**, in
> its **VEX profile**, with status justifications from the vocabulary published in **June 2022**.
> **The format is an envelope. Every assertion in it is ours or is attributed, and the standard
> verifies nothing.**
>
> **This quarter: 41 advisories.**
>
> | Status | Count | Notes |
> |---|---|---|
> | **not affected** — vulnerable code not in the execute path | 19 | **our own assertion**, reachability confirmed per service |
> | **not affected** — component not present | 11 | from the build-time component list |
> | **fixed** | 7 | release named in each case |
> | **affected** | 2 | remediation dates given |
> | **under investigation** | **2** | **six weeks, and we are not converting them to a guess** |
>
> **One statement with two owners, as an example.** For the April library advisory:
>
> > **Our supplier states that** the vulnerable code is not present in its distributed build. **We
> > have confirmed that** the function in question is not reachable from any entry point in our
> > service. **These are two separate assertions with two separate owners**, and the second one is
> > ours.
>
> **Why we word it that way.** That advisory was **reissued six weeks later with a wider version
> range**. The supplier's statement, correct for their current build, **was wrong for the version we
> shipped in March**. Because our letter attributed it, **the correction was a correction to their
> statement; our own assertion was unaffected.** **Had we written *the library is not affected*, we
> would have been correcting a false statement of our own to you.**
>
> **The two still under investigation.** Both concern a component where the **closed justification
> vocabulary does not fit** what we have found. **Rather than choose the nearest available reason, we
> have left the status as *under investigation*.** We would rather be visibly unfinished than
> precisely wrong.
>
> **Shelf life, which this format does not express.** **Every *not affected* statement here describes
> the build dated 2 May.** A reachability claim is invalidated by a single new caller. **We regenerate
> and reissue on every release**, and the file carries its timestamp.
>
> **Not in this statement.** **Severity.** **That** is a different scale in a different document, and
> a *not affected* status says nothing about how serious the vulnerability would be if it did apply.
>
> *An honest company's most likely false statement is somebody else's claim with the attribution worn
> off.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Attribute every borrowed claim precisely, then state your own findings with no
> hedging at all. The attribution is what gives the flat sentence its weight.
