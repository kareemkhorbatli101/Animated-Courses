# Unit 3 · A System, Not a Model
%%SUB A certificate that says nothing whatsoever about how good your model is%%
%%GOALS
Read and rewrite the passive of obligation
Say what a management system standard certifies
Put the responsible person back into a requirement
%%

There is a certifiable standard for managing artificial intelligence. An auditor can examine you
against it and issue a certificate. The certificate is about your **management system**, inside a
**scope you declared**, and it says nothing at all about whether any of your models works. Every
sentence in this unit exists because somebody will read that certificate as though it did.

> **A:** If we get certified, can we tell customers our models are trustworthy?
> **B:** No, and I would like to be very clear about it, because the sentence is tempting. **The
> standard sets requirements for a management system. A certificate says an auditor found that
> system in place within the scope you declared.**
> **A:** It says nothing about the model?
> **B:** Nothing about any model's accuracy, fairness or safety. **It says that you have a policy,
> objectives, assigned responsibilities, documented information, internal audits, a management
> review and a way of handling things that go wrong.** Those are good things. They are not a
> measurement of a model.
> **A:** Then what is a certificate worth?
> **B:** A great deal, correctly described. It is evidence that the arrangements exist and were
> examined by somebody outside. **A company with no management system has nothing to examine, and
> our models were in that position two years ago.**
> **A:** And the scope?
> **B:** The scope is ours to declare, and it is the part people skim. **You can certify one
> business unit, one group of systems, one site. A certificate with a narrow scope is not dishonest;
> a certificate quoted without its scope is.**
> **A:** So what do we say?
> **B:** The standard, its year, the certifying body, the scope line word for word, and then one
> sentence saying what it does not cover. **The last sentence is the one that stops a customer
> putting *trustworthy models, certified* on a slide with our name under it.**

> | Clause | What is required |
> |---|---|
> | **context** | the organization's role (provider, developer, user) and the interested parties |
> | **leadership** | a policy, assigned responsibilities, and commitment from **top management** |
> | **planning** | objectives, risk assessment, and the treatment of identified risks |
> | **support** | competent people, awareness, communication and **documented information** |
> | **operation** | the controls the organization has determined to be applicable |
> | **performance evaluation** | monitoring, **internal audit**, and a **management review** |
> | **improvement** | handling nonconformity and **continual improvement** |
>
> **What a certificate actually says.**
>
> > A certificate states that an accredited body examined the **management system** of a named
> > organization, within a **scope the organization itself declared**, and found it to conform to the
> > standard.
> >
> > **It says nothing about the accuracy, fairness, safety or performance of any model.** It is not a
> > product mark. It is not an assessment of an algorithm. **Two organizations can both hold this
> > certificate and run models of wildly different quality, and the certificate is equally valid for
> > both.**
> >
> > Annexes to the standard provide **controls**, implementation **guidance**, and examples of
> > organizational objectives and risk sources. The organization determines **which controls are
> > applicable** and justifies that determination, as in the information security standard most
> > auditors already apply.
>
> **The passive, and the question it hides.** The standard is written in the passive: *shall be
> established*, *shall be documented*, *shall be reviewed*. **This is correct drafting — the standard
> cannot know your organization chart — but it is useless as an internal instruction.** Our own
> version of each clause names a person. *An AI policy shall be established* became *Chiamaka Obi
> owns the AI policy and reviews it each March*. **Eleven requirements, eleven names, and four of
> them had to be negotiated.**
>
> **Where we are.** We have a policy, a register and a risk method. **We have never run an internal
> audit, and the management review has happened once, informally, in a corridor.** Those two gaps are
> clause 9, and clause 9 is the one an auditor opens first.
>

> Management system. It specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining and

### 1 · The passive of obligation

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS5-03-06%%

> **Hana:** I have rewritten all eleven requirements into our own words, and the rewriting was the
> work. The standard says *an AI policy shall be established*.
> **Mei:** Which is clear enough.
> **Hana:** It is clear and it is unassignable. **A passive with no agent tells you what must exist
> and not who must make it exist, and a requirement that nobody owns will be met by nobody.** The
> standard is right to write it that way — it cannot know our structure. We are wrong to copy it.
> **Mei:** So every requirement gets a name.
> **Hana:** Every requirement gets a name and a frequency. *Chiamaka Obi owns the AI policy and
> reviews it each March.* Active, named, dated. **And four of the eleven took a negotiation, because
> the passive had been protecting somebody.**
> **Mei:** Protecting them from what?
> **Hana:** From being the answer. *Internal audits shall be conducted* sat in our handbook for a
> year and nobody conducted one. **Nobody refused. There was simply no sentence anywhere that said a
> particular person would do it, and so the requirement was true, documented and inert.**
> **Mei:** Is the passive always wrong, then?
> **Hana:** No, and this is the part people overcorrect. **The passive is right when the agent is
> genuinely irrelevant or genuinely unknown**, and in a standard it is both. It is also right when
> the action matters and the actor does not: *the model was retrained in March* is fine. **The test
> is whether a reader could act on the sentence. If somebody has to act and the sentence has no
> actor, rewrite it.**
> **Mei:** And if nobody will accept the name?
> **Hana:** Then you have found something real, and I would far rather find it in a rewriting
> exercise than in an audit. **An unassigned requirement is not a wording problem. It is a vacancy,
> and the passive is how a vacancy stays invisible for a year.**

*What test does Hana give for when to rewrite a passive?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Management system certificates are among the most widely misread documents in business, and the
misreading is always in the same direction: a certificate about arrangements gets quoted as a
certificate about outcomes. The mechanism is not fraud. A certificate is genuine, hard-won and
expensive; the organization is proud of it; and the shortest true description of it — *certified
against a management system standard, within a declared scope* — is nine words longer than the
version that fits on a slide. So the scope line falls off first, then the words *management system*,
and what remains is two words that sound like a verdict on the product. The habit that prevents it is
small and slightly tedious: never state the certificate without the scope line, and always add the
sentence saying what was not assessed. You will be asked to cut that sentence. It is the one doing
the work.

> **What the standard actually requires of us.** Seven clauses: context, leadership, planning,
