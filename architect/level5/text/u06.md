**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS5-06-06%%

> **Yusuf:** I have written the nine unknowns as indirect questions, and I want you to check the
> wording, because I think the wording is doing something.
> **Hana:** Show me one.
> **Yusuf:** The direct question in the paper is *how was the data acquired?* My answer reads: **we
> cannot establish how the data was acquired**. Not *the acquisition method is undocumented*.
> **Hana:** Why does that matter?
> **Yusuf:** Because the indirect question keeps the question visible. **_The acquisition method is
> undocumented_ is a closed sentence; it describes a state of affairs and invites nothing. _We cannot
> establish how the data was acquired_ still has the question inside it, and a reader can see what
> would answer it.**
> **Hana:** Somebody could still find out.
> **Yusuf:** Somebody might. Two of my nine were answered a week after I circulated them, by people
> who recognized the question. **Nobody has ever answered a nominalization.**
> **Hana:** And the word order?
> **Yusuf:** The thing I keep correcting in other people's drafts. **Inside an indirect question you
> do not invert and you do not use the auxiliary: *we do not know how the data **was** acquired*, not
> *how **was** the data acquired*.** And *whether*, not *if*, in anything that will be read by
> somebody outside.
> **Hana:** Why *whether*?
> **Yusuf:** *If* is doing two jobs in English and one of them is conditional. **_We cannot say if
> consent was obtained_ can be misread as a condition. _Whether_ has only the one meaning, and in a
> document about what we do not know I want no sentence capable of two readings.**
> **Hana:** You have thought about this more than I expected.
> **Yusuf:** Because the nine unknowns are the only part of this document anybody will act on. **The
> forty answers are a receipt. The nine questions are an invitation, and an invitation has to be
> phrased as one.**

*Why does Yusuf prefer an indirect question to a nominalization?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Documentation exercises are usually described as overhead, and for a well-run dataset that is nearly
true: forty answers that somebody already knew, written down. The value is entirely in the questions
that cannot be answered, and those are the ones under pressure to be softened. *We cannot establish
whether the people in this dataset were informed* becomes *consent arrangements are not fully
documented*, and the second version is true, unobjectionable and completely inert — nobody has ever
acted on a nominalized deficiency. A question, phrased as a question, with one line saying what would
answer it, behaves differently: it travels, somebody recognizes their own work in it, and it comes
back answered. The other thing these exercises reliably reveal is not in the document at all. It is
how many answers came from interviews rather than records, and therefore how much of your data
provenance is carried in the heads of a small number of people, at least one of whom is always about
to leave.
