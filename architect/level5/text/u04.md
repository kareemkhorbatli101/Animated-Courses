# Unit 4 · Guidance Is Not Requirements
%%SUB One word tells you whether a document can be audited: shall%%
%%GOALS
Use guidance verbs at their real strength
Tell a guidance document from a requirements document
Say why a guidance document cannot be certified
%%

Two documents sit side by side. They are the same length, from the same committee, printed in the
same typeface, and one of them can be audited while the other cannot. The difference is a single
auxiliary verb, used consistently, and it is the most important word in either document.

> **A:** Can we get certified against the risk management document?
> **B:** No, and not because you have not done the work. **It is a guidance document. There is
> nothing in it to be assessed against, because it contains no requirements.**
> **A:** How can you tell, without reading the whole thing?
> **B:** Read any ten sentences and look at the auxiliary verbs. **A requirements document says
> *shall*, over and over, because every *shall* is a thing an auditor can test.** A guidance document
> says *should*, *can*, *may be*. Those are suggestions, and a suggestion cannot fail an audit.
> **A:** So the guidance is worth less.
> **B:** It is worth differently. **The requirements document tells you that a risk assessment shall
> be performed. The guidance tells you what AI risk sources actually look like, which is the part
> you do not know.** One is auditable and thin; the other is useful and untestable.
> **A:** And if somebody asks whether we follow it?
> **B:** Then you say what is true: **we have used its risk sources and its process as the basis of
> our own method, and here is the method.** You do not say conformity, because it does not offer
> conformity.
> **A:** People will still ask for a certificate.
> **B:** They will. **And the answer is not an apology. It is a sentence: this document is guidance,
> no certification exists for it, and here is what we have built with it. That answer has never once
> been challenged.**

### 1 · Guidance verbs and their strength

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS5-04-06%%

> **Nour:** Your method document says *risk criteria should be documented*. Is that us recommending
> something to ourselves?
> **Hana:** It is a copy-paste, and you have caught it. **That sentence is the guidance document's
> sentence, in the guidance document's verb, sitting in a document that is supposed to be our rule.**
> **Nour:** What should it say?
> **Hana:** *The risk criteria are documented in annex 1 and are reviewed by me each March.* Present
> tense, named, dated. **Inside our own method, *should* is a mistake. We are not advising ourselves;
> we are deciding.**
> **Nour:** Is *should* ever right in an internal document?
> **Hana:** Often, and this is the distinction worth having. **Use *should* where you genuinely mean a
> default that a team may depart from with a reason.** *A model should be evaluated on a held-out set
> drawn from the deployment period.* That is a real recommendation: there are cases where you cannot,
> and I want the team to depart and tell me, not to lie.
> **Nour:** So *shall* where you mean it, *should* where you mean it.
> **Hana:** And never *shall* where you cannot audit. **A *shall* that nobody checks is worse than a
> *should*, because it teaches everybody that the document's strongest word means nothing.** We had
> four of those. Three became *should*, and one became a real requirement with an owner.
> **Nour:** What about *must*?
> **Hana:** I avoid it in documents that quote standards, because the standards do not use it and the
> contrast gets confusing. **In an email, *must* is fine and clearer than *shall*. In a method
> document that sits beside a standard, I use the standard's vocabulary so that nobody has to
> translate.**
> **Nour:** And when somebody asks whether we conform to the guidance?
> **Hana:** **There is nothing to conform to, and saying so costs me nothing. We used it. That is a
> stronger sentence than a conformity claim nobody can check, and it has the advantage of being
> true.**

*Where does Hana say* should *is the right verb?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Most internal method documents are written by copying sentences out of standards, and the copying
brings the standard's verbs with it. A standard says *should* because it is advising thousands of
organizations it has never met; your own document says *should* and means nothing at all, because
you are not advising yourself. The result is a page of rules at an indeterminate strength, which
every reader resolves in their own favor. Worse, the same document usually contains a few genuine
requirements written in the same words, and they lose their force by association: a new engineer who
discovers that three of four identically worded rules are decorative will read the fourth one the
same way. The repair is not stricter language. It is honest language — *shall* with a named checker,
*should* with a route for reporting a departure — and the first thing it produces is a flow of
information you were not getting, because under an unfollowable rule the honest answer is unsayable.

**Discuss:** Which rule in your company does everybody quietly not follow?

> | A system **shall not** enter production without a register entry. | the platform team, at release | every release |
>
> **Five recommendations, with a route for departing.**
>
> - A model **should** be evaluated on data drawn from the deployment period. **If you cannot, say so
>   in the register entry; that is not a failure and I would rather know.**
> - Risk criteria **should** be applied before a treatment is chosen, not after.
> - A departure from any recommendation **should** be recorded in one sentence.
>
> **One permission.** A team **may** run the process quarterly rather than monthly where a system has
> not changed.
>
> **What changed in this revision, and why.** This document previously contained **four** *shall*
> sentences. **One was checked by somebody; three were checked by nobody and two were not being done
> at all.** Those three are now recommendations. **An unchecked requirement is not harmless: it
> spends the credibility of every other requirement on the page**, including the register review,
> which is the only rule here that has ever caught anything.
>
> **The hardest page in this method.** The **risk criteria** — which risks we accept, and up to what
> level. **Four sentences. Two weeks.** No technical content whatsoever. The guidance document is
> explicit that the acceptable level of risk is **the organization's own decision**, and for two
> weeks nobody wanted to be the organization.
>
> **Not in this document.** **Per-system risk entries.** **These** are the register itself, and four
> of eleven came from the guidance's risk-source list.
>
> *A rule nobody can follow does not produce compliance. It produces silence.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Give every *shall* a named checker in the same table. If you cannot name one,
> the sentence is a *should* and you have just learned something.
