There is an International Standard that contains no requirements, no processes and no measurements.
It defines words. Committees spent years on it, and the reason is visible in any meeting where two
people use *autonomy*, *transparency* or *bias* to mean different things and agree with each other
for forty minutes.

### 1 · Stipulative definitions

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS5-07-06%%

> **Mei:** You have put definitions at the top of a two-page note. That is a lot of ceremony.
> **Hana:** Three lines, and they are the only reason the two pages work. **Every document I write
> now defines the words it is about to use a great many times, and only those.**
> **Mei:** Why not use the standard's glossary and reference it?
> **Hana:** Because nobody follows a reference. **A definition that is one click away is a definition
> that half my readers do not have, and the half that do not have it are the ones who will act on the
> document.** Three lines on the page costs me nothing.
> **Mei:** And if your definition differs from the standard's?
> **Hana:** Then I say so explicitly, which is allowed and is the part people are nervous about.
> **A standard's definition is stipulative too — it holds for the purposes of that document. I am
> permitted to stipulate differently, as long as I do it in the open and say why.**
> **Mei:** Give me the formula.
> **Hana:** *For the purposes of this document, X means Y.* And where a reader will arrive with
> another meaning, one more clause: **_and not Z_**. The *not* is the half that does the work.
> **_Autonomy means operation without human initiation of each action, and not the system's selection
> among options._**
> **Mei:** Why is the negative half the important one?
> **Hana:** Because a definition competes with whatever the reader already believes. **If I only say
> what the word means, a reader who arrives with a different sense reads my definition as compatible
> with theirs, because people are generous.** The *and not* forces a collision, in a controlled place,
> on page one, instead of in a design review in week six.
> **Mei:** Is there a case for not defining?
> **Hana:** Yes, and I overdid this at first. **Define the two or three words the document turns on.
> A page with eleven definitions reads as a legal document and gets skimmed, and then you have lost
> the two that mattered.**

*Why does Hana say the negative half of a definition does the work?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The most expensive failures in technical work are often not errors but agreements — two groups using
one word for two things, nodding, and building correctly to different specifications. Nobody is
careless and nothing in the document is false; the word simply has two senses and no sentence says
which. Glossaries do not fix this, however good they are: a definition in a separate document is
held by the readers who look things up, and the readers who act on a page are usually not the same
people. What works is small and faintly embarrassing to write — two or three definitions at the top
of the page, each with a clause saying what the word does **not** mean here. The negative clause is
the one doing the work, because readers are generous: shown a positive definition that differs from
their own, most people read it as compatible with what they already thought and carry on. The *and
not* forces that collision onto page one, where it costs a conversation instead of a quarter.

> ### Design note · definitions, before anything else
> *Machine-learning group · section 7.1 · 18 April*
>
> **Why this page comes first.** In a review in January, two teams used the word **autonomy** in two
> senses, **agreed with each other for forty minutes**, and built correctly to different
> specifications. **It surfaced in week six.** Nothing was wrong in either build. **This page is the
> cheapest preventive measure available to us: three lines.**
>
> **Three definitions, for the purposes of this document.**
>
> > **Autonomy** means **operation without human initiation of each action**, **and not** the
> > system's selection among available options. *Readers from the product team arrive with the second
> > sense; that is the collision, and it is deliberate.*
> >
> > **Transparency** means **published information about the system** — its purpose, its data, its
> > evaluation — **and not** an explanation of any individual output, which is **explainability**. *A
> > customer asked us for the first and received four weeks of the second.*
> >
> > **Bias** means **a measured systematic difference between groups**, with a number, **and not** a
> > claim that an outcome is unfair. *The second claim may follow from the first; it requires its own
> > sentence and its own evidence.*
>
> **Where these come from, and where we depart.** All three are adopted from **ISO/IEC 22989:2022**, a
> terminology standard, **with one departure**: we **exclude scheduled triggers** from *autonomy*,
> because a cron-driven run is not what either team is arguing about. **That departure is ours, it is
> stated here, and anybody may argue with it.**
>
> **Why not the glossary.** We have an eleven-page glossary. It is correct, it is linked from the
> template, and **in two years I have not seen anybody open it**. A definition one click away is held
> by the readers who look things up; **the readers who act on a document are usually not those
> people**.
>
> **Why only three.** A page with eleven definitions reads as a legal document and is skimmed, **and
> then you have lost the two that mattered**. These three are the words this design turns on.
>
> **Not on this page.** **The life cycle stage names.** **These** are in section 7.2, adopted from the
> same standard without departure.
>
> *A definition without an "and not" is read as agreeing with whatever the reader already believed.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Two or three definitions, on the page, each with its negative half and a note
> naming the readers who will differ. More than four and you have written a glossary nobody reads.
