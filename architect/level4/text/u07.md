# Unit 7 · What You Cannot Undo
%%SUB A long job broken into steps, each with a step that undoes it — where one can be written%%
%%GOALS
Use the third conditional
Explain compensation, and how it differs from rollback
Say which steps cannot be undone
%%

A paper from 1987 describes how to run a long sequence of steps when you cannot hold everything open
until the end. You break it into steps, and for each step you write a second step that undoes it.
The idea is clean, widely used and has one hard edge: some steps cannot be undone, and the paper
cannot help you with those.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Note what a paper gives up. This one gives up a guarantee most people think
> they cannot live without, and says so in its first pages.

%%FIG d07-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the paper, its authors, its conference and its year, a diagram of five steps with undo steps beneath them, a boxed paragraph on what is given up, a short list of what the paper assumes, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 43
> *Platform team · long sequences and compensation · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The paper.** *Sagas*, by **Hector Garcia-Molina** and **Kenneth Salem**, published in the
> proceedings of the **ACM SIGMOD** conference in **1987**. Its subject is a sequence that takes too
> long to hold open — in their words, a **long-lived transaction**.
>
> **The construction.** Break the long sequence into **steps**. Each step **commits on its own**.
> For each step, write a **compensating step** that undoes its effect. The system then guarantees
> one of two outcomes: **either every step completes**, or the steps that ran are followed by their
> **compensating steps in reverse order**.
>
> **What is given up.**
>
> > **Atomicity.** The sequence is not all-or-nothing. **The intermediate states exist and are
> > visible** — to other parts of your system, to the other companies you called, and to your
> > customer, who may well have received an email about one of them.
> >
> > This is the whole trade. You get a sequence that can run for forty seconds or four hours. You
> > lose the property that nobody ever saw it half done. **Compensation is not rollback: a rollback
> > leaves no trace, and compensation happens afterwards, in public.**
>
> **What the paper assumes.** Three things, and all three matter in practice.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | each **step is itself atomic** | a step that can half-succeed breaks the construction |
> | a **compensating step exists** | the paper takes this as given; in your system it is a design problem |
> | **compensating steps succeed** | if one fails it must be **retried until it succeeds**, so it must be safe to repeat |
>
> **The hard edge.** **Some steps cannot be compensated.** You cannot un-send a notification, un-tell
> a customer a price, or un-dispatch a parcel without cost. For those, the compensation is a
> **second action in the world**: a correction email, a credit, a collection. **That is a product
> decision with wording somebody must approve, and it is not in the paper, because the paper is about
> transactions and this is about people.**
>
> **What follows for the order of steps.** Put the **irreversible steps last**. If the email goes
> before the payment, a failed payment costs you a correction email; if the payment goes first, a
> failed email costs you nothing. **The order of the steps is a design decision that the construction
> itself does not make for you.**
>
> **What the paper does not provide.**
>
> - **Your compensating steps.** They are semantic. Only you know what undoing your step means.
> - **Isolation.** Nothing prevents another process from reading an intermediate state.
> - **A guarantee for irreversible steps.** It assumes compensation exists.
> - **An implementation.** It is a 1987 database paper, not a framework.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every multi-step flow is written out as a table of steps and
> compensating steps, with the irreversible ones marked and moved as late as possible — and a flow
> with an unwritten compensation is not finished.
>
> *Source: Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, Sagas, Proceedings of the ACM SIGMOD conference,
> 1987: the decomposition of a long-lived transaction into subtransactions each with a compensating
> transaction, and the guarantee that either all subtransactions complete or the executed ones are
> compensated in reverse order. The construction relaxes atomicity: intermediate states are visible.
> The paper assumes that each subtransaction is atomic, that a compensating transaction exists, and
> that compensating transactions succeed; it provides no isolation and no treatment of effects that
> cannot be compensated.*

### 1 · The third conditional

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-07-06%%

> **Ravi:** Read me the first line of the incident write-up.
> **Adeel:** *If we write the compensation, we do not telephone four hundred customers.*
> **Ravi:** Both halves are in the present, and the whole thing happened last Tuesday. You need the
> third conditional: *if we **had written** the compensation, we **would not have telephoned** four
> hundred customers.*
> **Adeel:** Past perfect in the *if* half, *would have* in the other.
> **Ravi:** Exactly, and it is the only conditional that is entirely about things that did not
> happen. The second conditional was about now — *if the network were reliable*. This one is about a
> past you cannot change.
> **Adeel:** Is there a risk in using it?
> **Ravi:** One, and it is social rather than grammatical. The third conditional is the tense of
> blame. *If you had tested it, this would not have happened* is a grammatically perfect accusation.
> **In an incident write-up, keep the subject plural and institutional: *if we had written*, *if the
> flow had been documented*. The same tense, and nobody spends the meeting defending themselves.**
> **Adeel:** And *should have*?
> **Ravi:** *We should have written the compensation* is shorter and admits a fault without
> constructing a hypothetical. Useful once. Used four times in a page it reads as self-flagellation
> and stops being information. **Say *should have* once, then switch to *would have* and describe the
> mechanism.**
> **Adeel:** What about mixing the conditionals?
> **Ravi:** Common and correct: *if we had written the compensation, we would not be telephoning
> people today*. Past condition, present consequence. **That mixed form is the most honest sentence
> in most incident reports, because the cost is usually still running.**

*Why does Ravi want a plural subject in the* if *half?*

**1B  Underline four third conditional forms in 1A.**

| | Example |
|---|---|

> **Grammar Note** — The *if* half takes the past perfect and never *would*: *if we had written*,
> never *if we would have written*. The contracted *'d* can be either *had* or *would*, which is why

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The compensating step is written by nobody because it belongs to nobody. The forward path has a
product manager who wants it, a designer who drew it and an engineer who built it. The undo path has
a sentence in a design document, no user story, no screen, and no deadline. It is also the part that
needs the most approval, because for anything irreversible the compensation is an action in the world
— an email, a credit, a telephone call — with wording somebody senior has to agree. So the hardest
part of the flow is the part with the weakest owner. Teams that get this right do one thing: the
compensation is part of the same piece of work as the step, and the step is not finished until the
undo has run in a test.

**Discuss:** Who in your company owns the undo path?

> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | reserve stock | release the reservation (**idempotent**) | **yes, every release** | no |
> | 2 | take payment | reverse the payment | **yes, every release** | no — **but it costs a fee** |
> | 3 | create the order record | mark the order canceled | yes | no |
> | 4 | request dispatch | cancel the pick, if not yet picked | **no — never run** | **after picking, yes** |
> | 5 | **send the confirmation email** | **send a correction email** (wording approved by product) | n/a | **yes** |
>
> **Why the email moved to step five.** It used to be **step two**. On 24 March step four failed on
> four hundred orders, and **four hundred people had already been told their order was confirmed**.
> **If the email had been step five, that failure would have cost nothing.** It was step two because
> that was the order somebody wrote the function in, in 2021. **Nobody had chosen it.**
>
> **What compensation is not.** **It is not rollback.** Each step **commits on its own**, so every
> intermediate state is **visible** — to our own services, to the two companies we call, and to the
> customer. **Compensation runs afterwards, in public.**
>
> **Three things the construction assumes.** That each step is **atomic**. That a compensating step
> **exists**. And that compensating steps **succeed** — so each one must be **safe to repeat**, and
> ours is retried until it does. **The stock release subtracted one until March; run twice, it
> invented stock.**
>
> **The one that is a product decision.** **Step five.** You cannot un-send an email, so its
> compensation is **a second email**, with wording that **product has approved in advance**. **It
> cannot be written at three in the morning by whoever is on call.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **What happens if a compensating step fails permanently.** That is the manual
> procedure in section 7.6, and it has been used twice.
>
> *A compensation that has never run is a hypothesis. Step four's has never run.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *tested?* column is the one that turns this from a design into a status.
> *Never run* is the most useful entry on the page.
