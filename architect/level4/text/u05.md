# Unit 5 · Verifiable, or It Is Not a Requirement
%%SUB Nine qualities, and one of them removes most of what people write%%
%%GOALS
Use noun phrases, and undo nominalization
Name the qualities of a requirement
Say what a standard about requirements cannot do
%%

A standard published in 2018 lists nine qualities a requirement should have. Eight of them are
reasonable and improve a document. The ninth is **verifiable**, and applying it honestly removes more
requirements than the other eight together — including most of the ones your organization has
approved.

> **A:** The requirement is that the system shall be user-friendly and performant.
> **B:** How would somebody check that?
> **A:** They would look at it.
> **B:** Then two people will look at it and disagree, and the argument will happen after the money
> is spent. That is what *verifiable* means: there has to be a test, a measurement or an inspection
> that two people would agree on.
> **A:** It is also two requirements in one.
> **B:** It is, and splitting it is the second easiest improvement in this unit. One statement, one
> demand. The moment a requirement contains *and*, somebody can satisfy half of it and claim
> success.
> **A:** So what do I write?
> **B:** For the second half, a number and a condition. For the first half — *user-friendly* — you
> have to decide what you actually mean, and that is real work rather than writing work.
> **A:** And if nobody can agree what we mean?
> **B:** Then you have discovered something valuable rather than failed at something. **An
> unverifiable requirement is usually a disagreement that nobody has had yet, written down in a form

> **Reader's Tip** — When a standard lists qualities, apply the hardest one first. The list is
> usually ordered for completeness, not for impact.

%%FIG d05-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its edition, a nine-row table of the qualities with a short gloss each, a boxed paragraph on verifiability, a short list of what the standard cannot do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 41
> *Platform team · requirements · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018** covers requirements engineering within the system and
> software life cycle. It is the **second edition**, published in **2018**, replacing the first from
> **2011**. Among other things, it lists the qualities an individual requirement should have.
>
> **Nine qualities of one requirement.**
>
> | Quality | In one line |
> |---|---|
> | **necessary** | removing it would leave a gap |
> | **appropriate** | at the right level for this document |
> | **unambiguous** | two readers take the same meaning |
> | **complete** | it needs nothing else to be understood |
> | **singular** | **one demand**, not two |
> | **feasible** | achievable within the known constraints |
> | **verifiable** | **there is a test, measurement or inspection that would settle it** |
> | **correct** | it accurately states the need |
> | **conforming** | it follows the agreed form and style |
>
> **And five for the whole set.** A set should be **complete**, **consistent** — no two requirements
> contradicting each other — **feasible**, **comprehensible**, and **able to be validated**.
>
> **The one that does the damage.**
>
> > **Verifiable.** Not *is it true* but *could two people agree whether it had been met*. Applied
> > honestly it removes more requirements than the other eight together: *the system shall be
> > user-friendly*, *performance shall be adequate*, *the interface shall be intuitive*.
> >
> > And it removes them for a specific reason. **An unverifiable requirement is usually a
> > disagreement nobody has had yet**, written in a form that postpones it until after the money is
> > spent. Making it verifiable means having the argument now, which is why it is resisted.
>
> **Singular is the cheapest fix.** A requirement containing *and* can be half satisfied and
> declared met. **Split every requirement at every *and*** and your document gets longer, duller and
> dramatically harder to game.
>
> **On the word *shall*.** The standard gives guidance on form, and the convention is worth knowing:
> **shall** for a requirement; **should** for a goal; **will** for a statement of fact or intent; and
> **may** for permission. A document mixing these says four different things in one voice.
>
> **What the standard cannot do.**
>
> - **Write a requirement.** It describes qualities and processes. The content is yours.
> - **Decide what is necessary.** That is a judgment about your users, not a property of a sentence.
> - **Make an unverifiable need go away.** Some real needs are hard to state verifiably, and the
>   honest response is to record the difficulty rather than to write a sentence that hides it.
> - **Require any particular life cycle.** It is a life cycle process standard and **tailoring is
>   expected**; conformance is claimed against declared processes.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every requirement is split at its *and*s and tested against one
> question — *how would two people agree this had been met?* — and the ones that fail it are listed
> as **open questions** rather than rewritten into something unobjectionable.
>
> *Source: ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018, Systems and software engineering — Life cycle processes —
> Requirements engineering, second edition, replacing the 2011 first edition: the characteristics of
> individual requirements, including necessary, unambiguous, singular and verifiable; the
> characteristics of a requirement set, including consistency and the ability to be validated; and
> guidance on requirement language, including the use of shall for a requirement. The standard
> describes processes and qualities: it does not write requirements, determine what is necessary, or
> prescribe a particular life cycle, and tailoring is expected.*

<sub>**verifiable** — able to be settled by a test, measurement or inspection · **singular** —
containing exactly one demand · **tailoring** — adapting the standard's processes to a project</sub>

### 1 · Nominalization, and undoing it

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-05-06%%

> **Mei:** Read me requirement fourteen.
> **Adeel:** *There is a requirement for the provision of notification to the user upon the
> completion of the processing of the payment.*
> **Mei:** Twenty-one words. Now count the verbs.
> **Adeel:** One. *Is.*
> **Mei:** One verb and five nouns that used to be verbs: provision, notification, completion,
> processing, payment. That is nominalization, and it is the single most reliable way of making a
> document sound official and mean nothing.
> **Adeel:** How do I undo it?
> **Mei:** Find the buried verbs and give them back their subjects. Who notifies whom, and when?
> **Adeel:** *The service shall notify the user when the payment has been processed.*
> **Mei:** Eleven words, three verbs, and — the important part — **you had to decide who does it.**
> The nominalized version did not say. That is not an accident of style: **nominalization lets you
> write a requirement without naming the thing that must act, which is exactly why it survives
> review.**
> **Adeel:** So nouns are bad?
> **Mei:** Nouns are essential; nominalization in a *requirement* is dangerous, because a requirement
> must name an actor and an action. In a *description* a noun phrase is often better: *payment
> processing* is a perfectly good name for a thing.
> **Adeel:** Is there a quick test?
> **Mei:** Two. Count the words ending in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance* and *-ity*; more than two in a
> sentence and something is hiding. And look for *there is*, *there are* and *occurs* as the main
> verb. **If the only verb in your requirement is a form of *be* or *occur*, nobody has been asked
> to do anything.**

*Why does Mei say nominalization survives review?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

An unverifiable requirement is approved faster than a verifiable one, and that is not a failure of
rigor — it is what the approval process is optimizing for. *The system shall be intuitive* lets
eleven people in a room agree and leave. *A new user shall complete first sign-in without assistance
in under three minutes* produces an objection from somebody, and the objection is the point. So the
document fills with adjectives, every signature is genuine, and the disagreement surfaces eighteen
months later as a dispute about whether the thing that was built is the thing that was asked for.
Moving those sentences to an *open questions* list feels like admitting failure and is the opposite:
it converts a hidden disagreement into an agenda item, which is the only form in which it can ever
be settled.

> ### Requirements · payment notifications
> *Platform team · section 5.2 · reviewed 24 March*
>
> **How each requirement is written.** **One actor. One *shall*. One action. One measurable

>
> | | Requirement | Verified by | Traced to |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **5.2.1** | **The payments service shall notify** the account holder **within 60 seconds** of a payment being settled. | test 9.2 | Alba Health, January |
> | **5.2.2** | **The payments service shall record** the notification attempt **with its outcome**. | test 9.3 | audit finding 11 |
> | **5.2.3** | **The notification shall name** the amount, the currency code and the settlement time. | inspection | support volume |
> | **5.2.4** | **The service shall retry** a failed notification **three times at increasing intervals**. | test 9.5 | operations review |
>
> **Open questions — not requirements.** Three statements have been **moved off this page** because
> **we cannot say how two people would agree they had been met**:
>
> - *the notification shall be clear* — **what would we measure?**
> - *the experience shall be modern* — **what does this exclude?**
> - *performance shall be adequate* — **adequate for whom, at what load?**
>
> **These are not deletions.** Each is a **real disagreement** that the adjective was concealing, and
> the list is now **Ines's agenda for the April review**.
>
> **Why every requirement names an actor.** A sentence like *there is a requirement for the provision
> of notification* **names nobody**, so nobody can be said to have failed it. **Nominalization is how
> a requirement avoids naming the thing that must act**, and it passes review precisely because it
> offends nobody.
>
> **Language.** **Shall** = a requirement. **Should** = a goal. **Will** = a statement of fact.
> **May** = permission. **This document contains only *shall*;** everything else lives in the
> background section.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Non-functional requirements for the whole platform.** Those are section
> 12, and nine of them are currently open questions.
>
> *Nine of sixty-one requirements survived this review unchanged. Twenty-nine were split at an *and*.
> Eleven became questions. None was deleted.*

> **Writer's Tip** — *Verified by* and *traced to* are two short columns that double the life of a
> requirements document: the first stops it being a wish, the second stops it being deleted by
> somebody who cannot see why it exists.
