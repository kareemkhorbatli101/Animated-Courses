# Unit 4 · Four Levels, One Title, One Date
%%SUB A diagram that needs you standing beside it is not a diagram%%
%%GOALS
Describe levels of detail
Name the four levels and what each one shows
Say what a diagram must carry to be usable alone
%%

Most architecture diagrams are unreadable by anybody who was not at the meeting. They mix two levels
of detail, they have no key, and they have no date. There is a small set of abstractions that fixes
most of this — four levels, each answering one question — and the hardest discipline it asks is the
easiest to describe: say what each box means, and say when you drew it.

> **Reader's Tip** — Notice when a document warns you about its own vocabulary. A word that collides

%%FIG d04-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line naming the model and its author, a four-row table of the levels with the question each answers, a boxed paragraph warning about one overloaded word, a short list of what the model does not provide, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 40
> *Platform team · diagrams and levels · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The source.** The **C4 model**, created by **Simon Brown** and documented publicly since the
> early 2010s. It is a small set of **abstractions**, not a notation and not a standard. Its claim is
> modest: four levels of diagram, each answering **one** question, drawn in whatever shapes you like.
>
> **The four levels.**
>
> | Level | What it shows | The question it answers |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1 system context** | your system as **one box**, with its users and neighboring systems | *what is it, and who uses it?* |
> | **2 container** | the **separately deployable units** inside it | *what are the big moving parts?* |
> | **3 component** | the **major parts inside one** of those units | *how is this one built?* |
> | **4 code** | classes and their relationships | *how is this part written?* |
>
> **The warning about level two.**
>
> > The model's word for a separately deployable unit is **container**, and it was chosen before that
> > word acquired its now-dominant meaning in the industry. **A level-two box is any separately
> > deployable thing**: a service, a mobile application, a database, a scheduled job.
> >
> > The model itself warns about the collision, and the warning is worth repeating in every
> > conversation, because the misunderstanding costs about ten minutes every time and nobody notices
> > it happening.
>
> **The level you probably should not draw.** **Level four.** Code diagrams go out of date within
> days, and the model says plainly that they are rarely worth maintaining — generate them on demand
> if anybody needs one.
>
> **The rule that fixes most diagrams.** A diagram should be **self-describing**: readable **without
> anybody standing beside it**. In practice that means three things, and they take two minutes.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | a **title** | what it shows, **and which level** |
> | a **key** | what each shape, color and line style means |
> | a **date** | when it was drawn, and ideally what version it describes |
>
> **The commonest fault.** **Mixing two levels in one picture** — a service beside one of its
> internal classes, or a team beside a database. Every box on a diagram should be the **same kind of
> thing**, and if you need two kinds, you need two diagrams.
>
> **What the model does not provide.**
>
> - **A notation.** Boxes and lines, in any tool. It is deliberately silent.
> - **A level of detail.** How much goes on a level-two diagram is your judgment.
> - **Any authority.** It is not a standard and nobody will audit you against it.
> - **A method.** It tells you how to draw, not what to build.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** One level per diagram, named in the title, with a key and a date —
> and no level-four diagrams in the repository at all.
>
> *Source: the C4 model, created by Simon Brown: the four levels of system context, container,
> component and code, with their respective scopes; the model's own warning that its use of the word
> container predates and differs from the now-common industry meaning; its guidance that code-level
> diagrams are rarely worth maintaining; and its requirement that diagrams be self-describing, with
> a title, a key and clear labels. The model is notation-independent and is not a standard: it
> prescribes no shapes, no level of detail and no process.*

### 1 · Describing levels of detail

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-04-06%%

> **Nour:** I joined eleven weeks ago, so use me. Read me the caption of your diagram.
> **Adeel:** *Architecture of the payments platform.*
> **Nour:** Which does not tell me what a box is or how much is missing. Try again with a level in
> it.
> **Adeel:** *Payments platform — container level. Each box is a separately deployable unit.*
> **Nour:** Now I can read it, and notice what the second sentence did: it defined a box. That is
> the single most useful sentence on any diagram and almost nobody writes it.
> **Adeel:** How do I describe what is missing?
> **Nour:** Say it, in the caption. *This diagram omits the monitoring systems and the scheduled
> jobs.* **An omission that is stated is an abstraction; an omission that is silent is an error, and
> the picture looks identical either way.**
> **Adeel:** And pointing at the next diagram?
> **Nour:** *One level down*, *zoom in on*, or *expanded in figure 4*. All three are natural. In a
> written document I prefer *expanded in figure 4*, because a reader can follow it without a mouse.
> **Adeel:** What about verbs for the diagram itself?
> **Nour:** *Shows*, *omits*, *distinguishes*, *groups*. A diagram *shows* the deployable units; it
> *omits* the queues; it *distinguishes* our systems from other companies' by color. Those verbs
> are what a key is made of. **And use the present simple throughout — a diagram always shows, it
> never showed.**
> **Adeel:** Even an old diagram?
> **Nour:** Even then, and this is why the date matters more than anything else on the page. The
> diagram shows, in the present tense, something that was true on a date in the past. **Without the
> date, a confident present tense is the most misleading thing in your documentation.**

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The most useful reviewer of a diagram is the person who cannot read it, and they are the one person
never asked. Everybody who built the system knows what the boxes mean, so the key looks redundant to
every single reviewer — and the reader it was needed for is still at a different company. The
practical consequence is that diagram quality in a team is almost entirely determined by whether
somebody newly arrived is asked to read one in their first fortnight, while they still cannot guess.
That window closes fast: within two months a new engineer has absorbed the shared assumptions and
becomes as unable to see the gaps as everybody else. If you want good diagrams, the mechanism is not
a template. It is asking the newest person to read the oldest picture, out loud, in the first week.

**Discuss:** Who is the newest person on your team, and what have they been asked to read?

>
> **Every diagram carries three things.** A **title naming the level**. A **key** defining every
> shape, color and line style. A **date**, with the **release** it describes. **Two minutes, and
> the diagram survives without its author in the room.**
>
> **One level per diagram.** A box at the wrong level is the commonest fault we have. **Fourteen
> boxes of six kinds is two diagrams pretending to be one**, and a stated level is the only thing
> that lets a reviewer push back when somebody adds a box.
>
> **State your omissions.** *This diagram omits monitoring and the scheduled jobs.* **An omission
> that is stated is an abstraction. An omission that is silent is an error — and the picture looks
> identical either way.**
>
> **On the word for level two.** The model's term for a deployable unit **predates its now-common
> industry meaning**, and the collision costs about ten minutes per conversation. **In our documents
> we write *deployable unit*** and mention the model's word once, in a footnote.
>
> **Who reviews a diagram.** **The newest engineer in the team**, out loud, in their first
> fortnight. Everybody else already knows what the boxes mean and therefore cannot see the gaps.
> **That window closes in about two months.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **Which tool to draw in.** The model is notation-independent and so are we;
> the tool is section 4.3 and it is a preference, not a rule.
>
> *An undated diagram does not look old. It looks exactly as authoritative as a correct one, which is
> why the date is the most important thing on the page.*

> **Writer's Tip** — *A box is…* is the sentence to write first. Everything else on a diagram is
> decoration until a reader knows what one shape means.
