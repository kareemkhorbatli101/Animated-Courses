A blog post published in November 2011 proposed five headings for a one-page document recording an
architecture decision. It is not a standard. It has no committee, no edition and no conformance
clause. It has nevertheless changed how a generation of teams writes things down, and the reason is
the fifth heading, which nearly everybody leaves out.

> **B:** What had we already committed to when you decided?
> **A:** We had promised the customer a June date, and we had already built the retry logic.
> **B:** Then that belongs in the record, because without it your decision looks free and it was
> not. A reader in two years will see a choice between two queues and wonder why you took the
> awkward one.
> **A:** And the options I rejected?
> **B:** Named, with one line each. Otherwise somebody will spend a month rediscovering them, and
> the only thing worse than an undocumented decision is a documented decision with the alternatives
> removed.
> **A:** What about the consequences?
> **B:** The last heading, and the one everybody skips. Not *this is good* — what becomes **harder**.

> conclude that the team before them was careless.
> **A:** And if we change our minds?
> **B:** You write a new record and mark the old one superseded. **You do not edit it. A record of a
> decision that has been quietly rewritten is a record of nothing.**

> ## Meridian · architecture note 37
> *Platform team · decision records · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The source.** *Documenting Architecture Decisions*, a short article published by **Michael
> Nygard** in **November 2011**. It is **not a standard**. It has no committee, no edition number and
> nothing to conform to. It proposes one page per decision, under five headings.
>
> **The five headings.**
>
> | Heading | What goes in it |
> |---|---|
> | **title** | a short noun phrase, **numbered**, so that it can be cited |
> | **status** | **proposed**, **accepted**, **deprecated** or **superseded** |
> | **context** | what was true, what limited the choice, what we had already committed to |
> | **decision** | the decision itself, in one or two sentences, in the active voice |
> | **consequences** | what becomes easier **and what becomes harder** |
>
> **The heading that does the work.** **Consequences**, and specifically the half of it that records
> what gets worse. A record listing only benefits is a record of an argument somebody won, not of a
> decision somebody took. **Every real architecture decision costs something, and the record is the
> only place that cost is ever written down while it is still visible.**
>
> **The status field, and why records are not edited.**
>
> > A record is **immutable** once accepted. If the decision changes, you write **a new record** and
> > mark the old one **superseded**, with a pointer to the new one.
> >
> > This is the discipline that makes the collection worth keeping. A file that is quietly rewritten
> > tells you what the team believes **today**, which you could have found out by asking. A sequence
> > of records, including the wrong ones, tells you **how the system came to be like this** — which
> > is the only question an architecture document is ever really asked.
>
> **Where they live.** In the repository, beside the code, in a numbered folder. **A decision record
> in a separate system will be out of date within a year**, because the people changing the code
> will not open it.
>
> **What the practice does not provide.**
>
> - **A threshold.** Nothing tells you which decisions are big enough to record. Teams decide, and
>   most err towards too few.
> - **A template for the rejected options.** The original five headings leave them inside *context*;
>   many teams add a sixth heading, and that is a local choice.
> - **Any authority.** Nobody is obliged to follow it and no auditor will ask for it.
> - **A guarantee that anybody reads them.** A folder of records nobody opens is a filing habit.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** One page per decision, five headings, the consequences honest about
> what got worse, numbered in the repository, superseded and never edited.
>
> *Source: Michael Nygard, Documenting Architecture Decisions, November 2011: the one-page record,
> the headings for title, status, context, decision and consequences, and the practice of superseding
> a record rather than editing it. The article is not a standard: it defines no conformance, sets no
> threshold for which decisions to record, and prescribes no format for recording rejected
> alternatives.*

### 1 · The past simple and the past perfect

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-01-06%%

> **Mei:** Read me the context section.
> **Adeel:** *We chose the single queue. We promised the customer a June date and we built the retry
> logic.*
> **Mei:** Three events in the past simple, in the order you happened to write them. A reader cannot
> tell which came first, and the order is the whole point of a context section.
> **Adeel:** The promise and the retry logic came before the decision.
> **Mei:** Then say so with the past perfect. *We had promised a June date and we had already built
> the retry logic, so we chose the single queue.* Now the sentence has a shape: two things were
> already true, and then a decision followed from them.
> **Adeel:** So the past perfect is for the earlier of two past events.
> **Mei:** For the earlier one, when you are standing at the later one. That is the key: the past
> perfect always has a reference point in the past, and if your reader cannot see what that point
> is, the tense looks like decoration. **Put the decision in the past simple and everything that
> preceded it in the past perfect, and a context section almost writes itself.**
> **Adeel:** And *already*?
> **Mei:** Natural with the past perfect and useful here, because it tells the reader *this was not
> ours to reconsider*. *We had already built the retry logic* is doing real work: it says the cost of
> changing course was not zero.
> **Adeel:** Is there a trap?
> **Mei:** One. When the order is obvious from *before* or *after*, the past perfect becomes
> optional, and overusing it makes a document heavy. **Use it where the order matters and could be
> misread — which in a context section is most sentences, and in the rest of the document is almost
> none.**

*What does Mei say the past perfect always needs?*

**1B  Underline four past perfect forms in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| the decision | We **chose** the single queue. |
| what preceded it | We **had promised** a June date. |

> **Grammar Note** — The past perfect is *had* + past participle, and it is the same for all
> subjects. With two events joined by *before* or *after*, the order is already clear and the past

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A decision record is written at the worst possible moment: just after an argument, by the person who
prevailed, for an audience that already agrees. Everything about that situation pushes the document
towards a list of benefits. The cost is hypothetical, the benefit is next week's release, and
writing *this makes weekly releases impossible for one team* feels like reopening a settled matter.
So the sentence that the record exists to capture is precisely the sentence the moment suppresses.
The fix is procedural rather than moral: ask *what becomes harder?* as a required question in
review, and record the answer even when the author disagrees with it. Teams that do this find
something unexpected — the records become useful to the authors themselves within about a year,
because nobody remembers their own reasoning for long either.

**Discuss:** Who writes your decision records, and who were they written to persuade?

> ### 0017 · One queue for the shipment pipeline
> *Status: **superseded by 0031**, 2 April · originally accepted 14 March*
>
> **Context.** We **had promised** Northwind Rail a **June date**. Two of the three services **had
> already shipped** and were in production. The third **had not been written**. We **did not know**
> that shipment volume would double within a year; the figure we had was last year's.
>
> **Decision.** All three services will publish to and consume from **a single queue**.
>
> **Consequences.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | easier | one piece of infrastructure to operate, monitor and pay for |
> | easier | the third service was written in four days instead of three weeks |
> | **harder** | **the three services can no longer be released separately** |
> | **harder** | **a schema change requires all three teams to agree in the same week** |
>
> **Options we rejected.** **A queue per service**: would not have fitted the June date, because the
> deployment work **had not been started**. **Direct calls between services**: would have required
> retry logic in three places, and the retry logic **had already been built** for the queue.
>
> **Who will live with this.** **The team that releases most often.** At the time of writing that is
> the platform team, monthly. **If any team later needs to release weekly, this decision is the thing
> that will stop them**, and that team should read this record before proposing a change.
>
> *Superseded, not edited. Record 0031 describes what we did about the weekly-release problem and
> what it cost to undo this.*
