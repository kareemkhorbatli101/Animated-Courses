# Unit 10 · Processes, Not a Method
%%SUB A standard that names thirty activities and tells you to do none of them%%
%%GOALS
Use reference words and keep a long document coherent
Explain what a process reference standard is
Say what tailoring means for a conformance claim
%%

There is an International Standard listing the processes in the life of a system, from first
agreement to disposal. It is long, it is dry and it is extremely useful once you understand what
kind of document it is: it names activities and groups them. It does not tell you to perform any of
them, in any order, with any documents.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A long standard needs reference words to hold together. Notice how this note
> uses *the former*, *the latter* and *such* — and notice that a bare *this* is never safe.

%%FIG d10-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its edition, a four-row table of the process groups with examples, a boxed paragraph on tailoring and conformance, a short list of what the standard does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 46
> *Platform team · life cycle processes · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288** covers the processes in the life of a system. The current
> edition carries the year **2023**; the previous one, **2015**, defined **thirty** processes and is
> the edition most of our existing documents reference.
>
> **Four groups of processes.**
>
> | Group | What it covers | Examples |
> |---|---|---|
> | **agreement** | buying and supplying | acquisition, supply |
> | **organizational project-enabling** | what the company provides to every project | infrastructure, portfolio, quality, knowledge management |
> | **technical management** | running the work | planning, risk, **decision management**, configuration, **information management**, measurement |
> | **technical** | doing the work | needs and requirements definition, **architecture definition**, design, integration, verification, transition, validation, operation, maintenance, **disposal** |
>
> **Tailoring, and what conformance means.**
>
> > The standard expects **tailoring**: adapting the set of processes to the project. A two-person
> > team does not perform a portfolio management process, and a standard insisting otherwise would
> > be unusable.
> >
> > So a conformance claim is **not** *we do all thirty*. It is a claim against a **declared set**:
> > these are the processes we perform, this is how we have tailored them, and **this declaration is
> > the thing we conform to**. A claim without a declaration attached means nothing.
>
> **Why the list is worth reading even if you claim nothing.** For two reasons, and **the second is
> the better one**. The first is vocabulary: thirty names that mean the same thing in two companies,
> which makes a conversation with an auditor or a customer much shorter. **The latter** is coverage:
> reading the list tells you what you are **not** doing. **We discovered that we had no decision
> management process and no information management process by reading a list, rather than by it going
> wrong — which is the cheapest way to discover anything.**
>
> **What the standard does not do.**
>
> - **Prescribe an order.** It does not say requirements before architecture, or anything else.
> - **Prescribe a life cycle model.** Stages, milestones and reviews are yours to choose.
> - **Prescribe documents.** It names **outcomes**, not deliverables. Nothing here requires a
>   document with a particular title.
> - **Tell you how.** Each process has a purpose and outcomes. **How** you achieve them is a method,
>   and methods are not in here.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** We declare fifteen of the thirty, we write one sentence for each
> tailoring decision, and we name the two we are missing rather than quietly leaving them off the
> list.
>
> *Source: ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288, Systems and software engineering — System life cycle processes, in the
> edition of 2023 and, for our older references, the 2015 edition which defined thirty processes in
> four groups: agreement, organizational project-enabling, technical management and technical
> processes. The standard is a process reference: tailoring is expected, conformance is claimed
> against a declared and tailored process set, and it prescribes no life cycle model, no order of
> processes, no documents and no methods.*

### 1 · Reference and cohesion

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-10-06%%

> **Nour:** I read your process document last night. Page three has a sentence beginning *This means
> that…* and I could not tell what *this* was.
> **Adeel:** The paragraph before it.
> **Nour:** Which paragraph contains four things. A bare *this* points at whatever the reader
> happens to be holding in their head, and after two pages that is never what you intended. **Give
> *this* a noun: *this declaration*, *this tailoring*, *this group of processes*.**
> **Adeel:** Is a bare *this* always wrong?
> **Nour:** In speech, no. In a document longer than two pages, it is the commonest cause of a reader
> having to go back — and a reader who goes back twice stops reading. *This* plus a noun costs you
> one word and removes the ambiguity entirely.
> **Adeel:** What else holds a long document together?
> **Nour:** *These*, with a noun, for a list you have just given. *Such* for *a thing of that kind*:
> *such a claim means nothing*. And for two items, *the former* and *the latter* — but only when
> there are exactly two, and only when they are close. **If the reader has to count back, name them
> instead.**
> **Adeel:** And *respectively*?
> **Nour:** For matching two lists in order: *planning and architecture are technical management and
> technical processes respectively*. It is precise and it fails badly if the lists are not the same
> length. **Use it when you are sure, and never as decoration.**
> **Adeel:** Is there a simple test for all of this?
> **Nour:** One, and it costs nothing. **Read any sentence beginning with a reference word out loud,
> on its own, without the paragraph before it. If you cannot tell what it refers to, neither can
> your reader on page thirty.**

*What test does Nour give for a reference word?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The gaps a process list finds are invisible from inside any individual team, which is why reading a
list of thirty names can be more informative than a year of working. Each team's own arrangement
works; the absence is only visible from above, and nobody is standing there. This is the real value
of a dry reference standard, and it has nothing to do with conformance: it is a checklist of what
exists in the world, written by people who have seen a great many projects, and an afternoon with it
costs less than almost any audit. The reason it does not happen is not laziness. It is that reading
a list of things you are not doing has no owner and no deadline, and the first person to do it has to
write two sentences that somebody senior must approve.

>
> **How the gaps were found.** By **reading the list**, in an afternoon, by somebody who had been
> here a month. **Not by anything going wrong.** The absence of a process is **invisible from inside
> a team**, because each team's own arrangement works.
>
> **What the standard does not require of us.** **No order of processes. No life cycle model. No
> documents** — it names **outcomes**, and the documents are ours. Anybody citing it to demand a
> deliverable with a particular title is mistaken.
>
> **Not in this note.** **The tailoring notes themselves.** **These** are appendix B, and two of
> them currently read *to be written*.
>
> *A conformance claim without a declaration attached means nothing. This declaration is the claim.*

> **Writer's Tip** — *Declared as not performed* is a column, not an omission. A list with explicit
> gaps is auditable; a list with silent gaps is a document somebody will catch you with.
