# Unit 8 · No Protocol Can Promise It
%%SUB A 1985 proof that something you depend on every day cannot be guaranteed%%
%%GOALS
Express impossibility and limits
State what the 1985 result proves

%%

In 1985 three authors proved that in a certain model, no deterministic protocol can guarantee that a
group of machines will ever agree on anything, if even one of them may fail. Your systems reach
agreement thousands of times a day. Both of those sentences are true, and the space between them is
where every practical consensus system lives.

> **A:** Somebody told me distributed consensus is impossible.
> **B:** That is three words short of the truth, and the three words matter. There is a 1985 proof
> that **no deterministic protocol can guarantee termination** in a **fully asynchronous** system if
> even one machine may fail.
> **A:** But our cluster agrees on a leader every day.
> **B:** It does, because it is not in that model. It uses timeouts, and a timeout is a statement
> about time — the thing the model refuses to assume. **Your cluster works by relaxing an
> assumption, not by beating a theorem.**
> **A:** So the result does not apply to us?
> **B:** It applies exactly. It tells you where your guarantee comes from: not from the algorithm but
> from the assumption about time that the algorithm adds. When that assumption fails — a long
> pause, a slow disk, a network that delays everything by a minute — **your cluster stops making
> progress, and that is the theorem arriving on time.**

> **B:** No, and that distinction is the whole of this unit. A good protocol is never wrong. What
> cannot be promised is that it will **finish**. **Safety can be guaranteed. Liveness cannot, in that
> model, by any deterministic protocol — including one nobody has invented yet.**

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — With an impossibility result, read the model before the conclusion. The model
> is where the result gets its force, and it is also the thing your system does not live in.

%%FIG d08-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the paper, its authors, its journal and its year, a two-row table distinguishing safety from liveness, a boxed paragraph stating the result precisely, a short list of what the result does not say, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 44
> *Platform team · agreement and its limits · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The paper.** *Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process*, by **Michael
> Fischer**, **Nancy Lynch** and **Michael Paterson**, in the **Journal of the ACM**, **1985**. It is
> nine pages long and it is one of the most consequential results in the subject.
>
> **Two properties a protocol can have.**
>
> | Property | Means |
> |---|---|
> | **safety** | it **never** produces a wrong answer — no two machines ever decide differently |
> | **liveness** | it **eventually** produces an answer at all |
>
> **The result, stated carefully.**
>
> > In a **fully asynchronous** system — one with **no bound on message delay** and **no bound on
> > process speed** — **no deterministic protocol can guarantee both safety and termination** if even
> > **one** process may fail by stopping.
> >
> > Read the words that are doing the work. **Fully asynchronous.** **Deterministic.**
> > **Guarantee.** **Termination.** Remove any one of them and the result no longer applies.
> >
> > And note what is **not** claimed: not that agreement is unlikely, not that it usually fails, and
> > not that your cluster is broken. **A protocol can be safe in every execution and merely fail to
> > finish in some of them.**
>
> **Why your systems work anyway.** Because none of them is in that model. Practical consensus
> protocols add **an assumption about time** — usually a **timeout**, which is a claim that a message
> taking longer than *n* milliseconds may be treated as lost. That is **partial synchrony**: delays
> are bounded eventually, though you do not know the bound. **Others add randomness**, which also
> escapes the theorem, because the result is about **deterministic** protocols.
>
> **So the practical reading is this.** Your guarantee does not come from the algorithm. **It comes
> from the assumption the algorithm adds**, and when that assumption fails — a long pause, a slow
> disk, a network delaying everything by a minute — **your cluster stops making progress**. It does
> not give a wrong answer. It stops. **That is the theorem arriving, on schedule.**
>
> **What the result does not say.**
>
> - **That consensus is impossible in practice.** It is reached constantly, under assumptions.
> - **That a protocol might be wrong.** Safety is achievable. It is **termination** that cannot be
>   guaranteed.
> - **Anything about malicious failure.** The model's fault is a process **stopping**, nothing worse.
> - **How long you will wait.** No bounds at all; it is not a performance result.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** Every system that has to agree on something names the assumption it
> relies on and what it does when that assumption fails — because *it will agree* is not a property
> any protocol can offer us.
>
> *Source: Michael J. Fischer, Nancy A. Lynch and Michael S. Paterson, Impossibility of Distributed
> Consensus with One Faulty Process, Journal of the ACM, 1985: the proof that no deterministic
> protocol can guarantee consensus with both safety and termination in a fully asynchronous system
> subject to a single crash failure. The result concerns guaranteed termination in that model: it does
> not show that consensus fails in practice, does not apply to protocols using randomization or
> assumptions about timing, and does not address malicious failure.*

### 1 · Impossibility and limits

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-08-06%%

> **Karin:** Read me your sentence from the design review.
> **Adeel:** *We have not found a way to guarantee that the cluster always decides.*
> **Karin:** Which suggests that somebody might find one next quarter. Nobody will, and the
> difference between those two sentences is the point of this unit.
> **Adeel:** So I should say *cannot*.
> **Karin:** You should say what is true: *no deterministic protocol can guarantee it in that
> model*. That is an absolute statement, it was proved in 1985, and it will not change. Compare it
> with *we have not implemented leader election yet*, which is a statement about us and next
> quarter.
> **Adeel:** How do I tell which I am writing?
> **Karin:** Ask whether more effort would help. If more effort, more money or more time could fix
> it, use *does not* or *has not yet*. If no amount of any of them can, use *cannot* — and then, in
> a technical document, say **why**, because *cannot* without a reason reads as *we gave up*.
> **Adeel:** And the formal versions?
> **Karin:** *It is impossible to* is the strongest and should be reserved for things that have
> actually been proved. *There is no way to* is the same claim in informal register. *No protocol
> can* is the useful one here, because it names the class: it says the limit is not about our
> protocol but about every protocol of that kind.
> **Adeel:** Is there a trap?
> **Karin:** One, and it is everywhere. **An engineer who says *impossible* when they mean
> *difficult* spends the credibility they will need on the day something is genuinely impossible.**
> By then nobody is listening. Save the word.

*What question does Karin use to decide between* cannot *and* has not yet?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Our vocabulary has one word — *failure* — for two completely different events: a system that gave a
wrong answer and a system that gave no answer. The first is a correctness problem and demands fear.
The second is a progress problem and demands a conversation about assumptions. Collapsing them has a
cost that compounds: incident reports become unreadable, the same label is applied to events needing
opposite responses, and engineers learn that reporting *nothing was wrong* sounds like an excuse.
This is one of the places where the right words are not a nicety. *No progress and no wrong answers*
is eight words that classify an event correctly, and the 1985 proof is the reason the category
exists at all — a protocol can be permanently correct and temporarily stuck, and that is not a
compromise but a theorem.

**Discuss:** Does your incident vocabulary distinguish *wrong* from *stuck*?

> **Why there is no correct timeout.** **There is no way to distinguish a slow node from a stopped
> one.** A longer timeout survives longer pauses and takes longer to notice a dead node. **Five
> seconds is a choice about which error we prefer**, made by the platform team in 2022, and it is
> recorded as a decision rather than a setting.
>
> **What to do when you see no progress.** Check for a **wrong answer** first. If there is none,
> **this is a liveness event**: find the pause, do not change the protocol, and do not describe it as
> a failure in a customer message without the word *correct* in the same sentence.
>
> **Not in this note.** **Why storage pauses.** That is the storage team's section 8.4, and the
> cause of the March pauses is still open.
>
> *A protocol can be permanently correct and temporarily stuck. Our vocabulary has one word for both,
> which is why this note exists.*

> **Writer's Tip** — *Safety — guaranteed* beside *liveness — not guaranteed* in the same table is
> the whole note. Two rows, and the next reader knows which kind of event they are looking at.
