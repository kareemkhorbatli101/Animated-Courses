In April 1968 a four-page article in a trade magazine made one claim: an organization designing a
system is constrained to produce a design that copies its own communication structure. It has been
quoted ever since, usually as *the org chart becomes the architecture* — which is close enough to be
useful and wrong in the one word that matters.

> **A:** Our architecture mirrors our org chart. There is a 1968 paper about it.
> **B:** Close. The paper says the design copies the **communication** structure, which is often not
> the org chart at all.
> **A:** What is the difference?
> **B:** Two teams on different branches of the chart who sit together and talk daily will produce
> one well-joined component. Two teams in the same department who have never spoken will produce two
> components with a brittle interface between them. **The chart predicts the architecture badly. The
> pattern of actual conversation predicts it very well.**
> **A:** So if we want a different architecture, we reorganize?
> **B:** That is the popular conclusion and the paper does not make it. It observes a constraint. It
> does not offer a lever.
> **A:** But people do use it as a lever.
> **B:** They do, and sometimes it works. The honest version of the sentence is: *if we change who
> talks to whom, the next design will reflect the new pattern* — not *moving boxes on a chart will
> rewrite the code we already have*. **The existing system was built by the old conversations and it
> does not know you have reorganized.**

%%FIG d02-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the article, its author, its magazine and its date, a boxed quotation of the central claim, a three-row table of consequences, a short list of what the article does not establish, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 38
> *Platform team · organization and design · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The source.** *How Do Committees Invent?*, by **Melvin Conway**, published in the magazine
> **Datamation** in **April 1968**. It is about **four pages long**. It is an argument from
> experience and reasoning, not a measured study, and it has been cited for more than fifty years.
>
> **The claim, in the author's own terms.**
>
> > Organizations which design systems are **constrained to produce designs which are copies of the
> > communication structures of these organizations**.
> >
> > Read the phrase **communication structures**. Not the organization chart: **the pattern of who
> > actually talks to whom**. Those are often different, and where they differ the architecture
> > follows the conversations rather than the reporting lines.
>
> **Three consequences the article draws out.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **a fragmented organization produces a fragmented design** | the design cannot be better joined than the conversations that produced it |
> | **the organizational choice is made early** | and therefore on **less information than any later decision** |
> | **the boundary becomes permanent** | once two groups own two sides, the interface between them acquires defenders |
>
> **Why the early-choice point matters most.** The team structure is usually decided before anybody
> understands the problem — in the first weeks, by whoever is available. That choice then **shapes
> every design that follows**, and it was made with the least information the project will ever have.
>
> **What this means in practice.** Some interfaces in your system exist for **technical** reasons,
> and some exist because **two teams did not talk**. Both look identical in a diagram. **Telling them
> apart requires knowing who built which side, and that information is almost never written down** —
> which is why this unit sits in a book about records.
>
> **What the article does not establish.**
>
> - **That reorganizing will fix an existing system.** The popular use of this claim as a lever is a
>   **later invention**, not in the paper. The existing system was built by the old conversations.
> - **Any measurement.** There is no data in it, no sample, no effect size. It is an observation that
>   has survived because people keep recognizing it.
> - **A direction of cause.** Teams shape systems; systems also shape teams. The article is mostly
>   about the first.
> - **What to do.** It describes a constraint and offers no method.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** When we record a decision about a boundary, we write down **which
> teams owned which side at the time** — because in four years that will be the only way to tell a
> technical interface from an organizational one.
>
> *Source: Melvin E. Conway, How Do Committees Invent?, Datamation, April 1968: the claim that
> organizations designing systems are constrained to produce designs copying their own communication
> structures; the consequence that a fragmented organization produces a fragmented design; and the
> observation that the organizational choice is made early, on incomplete information. The article is
> a short argument from experience in a trade magazine: it reports no measurement, establishes no
> direction of cause, and proposes no method for changing an existing design.*

### 1 · Cause and result

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-02-06%%

> **Grace:** Read me your sentence about the interface.
> **Adeel:** *The interface is brittle because of the two teams never spoke.*
> **Grace:** Two structures in one place. *Because* takes a clause — a subject and a verb. *Because
> of* takes a noun phrase. You have put a clause after *because of*.
> **Adeel:** *The interface is brittle because the two teams never spoke.*
> **Grace:** Correct. And the noun version: *brittle because of the team boundary*. Both are right
> and they emphasize different things — the clause emphasizes the behavior, the noun phrase
> emphasizes the thing.
> **Adeel:** What about *due to* and *owing to*?
> **Grace:** Both take nouns, both are more formal, and there is a rule nobody enforces any more:
> *due to* after a noun, *owing to* at the start of a sentence. Either will pass. **The one to avoid
> is *due to the fact that*, which is four words for *because*.**
> **Adeel:** And for the result half?
> **Grace:** *As a result* and *consequently* start a new sentence and take a comma. *So* joins two
> halves of one sentence and does not. Those are the same two patterns as *however* and *but*, which
> you learned last year — the punctuation goes with the position, not the meaning.
> **Adeel:** Which verbs work?
> **Grace:** *Lead to* and *result in* take nouns: *the split resulted in three services*. *Cause*
> is stronger and should be earned — it claims a mechanism. **And *stem from* is the useful one in a
> document about history: *the boundary stems from a reorganization in 2019*, which says where
> something comes from without claiming anybody intended it.**

*What is the difference between* because *and* because of?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The most consequential architecture decision in most projects is made in its first fortnight, by
whoever is available, before anybody understands the problem — and it is not recorded as a decision
because nobody experiences it as one. Choosing three teams instead of two feels like an
administrative act. It then constrains every design for years, which is the 1968 claim, and because
it was never written down the resulting boundaries look technical to everybody who arrives later.
There is a cheap habit available here and almost nobody uses it: when a team structure is set,
write one paragraph saying what it was, who chose it and why. It is the only decision record in this
book that would be written by a manager rather than an architect, which is probably why it does not
exist.
