# Unit 3 · Config in the Environment
%%SUB Twelve instructions, written as imperatives, from one company's experience%%
%%GOALS
Use imperatives and recommendations
Explain what a methodology is and is not
Say which instructions assume a deployment model
%%

Twelve short instructions published in 2011 shaped how a generation of services was built. They are
written as commands — *store config in the environment*, *treat logs as event streams* — and that
grammar is doing something: it hides the deployment model the instructions assume, and the model is
why three of the twelve now need an argument.

> ## Meridian · architecture note 39
> *Platform team · the twelve instructions · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The source.** *The Twelve-Factor App*, published in **2011** by Adam Wiggins, drawn from
> experience operating a very large number of applications on a single platform service. It is a
> **methodology**: twelve short instructions, each with a paragraph of reasoning.
>
> **The twelve, in short.**
>
> | | Instruction |
> |---|---|
> | 1 | one **codebase** in revision control, **many deployments** |
> | 2 | **declare and isolate** dependencies |
> | 3 | store **config** in the **environment** |
> | 4 | treat **backing services** as attached resources |
> | 5 | strictly **separate** build, release and run |
> | 6 | execute the app as **stateless processes** |
> | 7 | export services by **binding a port** |
> | 8 | **scale out** by adding processes |
> | 9 | fast startup, **graceful shutdown** |
> | 10 | keep development and production **similar** |
> | 11 | treat **logs** as **event streams** |
> | 12 | run administrative tasks as **one-off processes** |
>
> **Why the grammar matters.** Every one is an **imperative**. *Store.* *Declare.* *Treat.* An
> imperative is efficient and it hides its assumptions: it does not say *if you are building a
> stateless web service behind a router*, although **three of the twelve assume exactly that**.
>
> **The three that assume a model.**
>
> > **Processes**, **port binding** and **scaling out** describe a stateless request-handling service
> > that a platform starts, stops and multiplies at will. For that shape they are excellent advice.
> >
> > For a **batch job**, a **stream processor holding an hour of state**, a **scheduled task** or
> > anything maintaining a long-lived connection, they fit badly or not at all — and the instructions
> > do not say so, because in 2011 the author was describing the applications in front of him.
>
> **The one that has been overtaken in part.** *Store config in the environment* was aimed at a real
> problem: **config in the code**, which makes one build unusable in two places. That reasoning has
> aged perfectly. The **mechanism** has aged less well: environment variables are inherited by child
> processes, appear in crash dumps, and are pasted into tickets by well-meaning people. **The
> principle is *keep config out of the build*; the environment is one way to do it and no longer the
> only one.**
>
> **What this document is not.**
>
> - **A standard.** No conformance clause, no certificate, nobody to audit you. *Twelve-factor
>   compliant* is not a thing that exists.
> - **Universal.** It describes one application shape very well.
> - **Measured.** It reports experience, persuasively, with no data.
> - **Finished.** It has not been revised, and the industry has moved around it in places.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** We treat the twelve as **defaults with reasons**, and every departure
> gets one written sentence saying which instruction and why — because a department that has
> departed from six of them silently cannot tell the difference between a decision and an accident.
>
> *Source: The Twelve-Factor App, Adam Wiggins, 2011: the twelve instructions on codebase,
> dependencies, config, backing services, build and run stages, processes, port binding, concurrency,
> disposability, development and production parity, logs and administrative processes. It is a
> methodology drawn from experience on one platform: it defines no conformance, reports no
> measurement, and several of its instructions assume a stateless request-handling deployment model.*

### 1 · Imperatives and recommendations

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-03-06%%

> **Tomas:** Read me the line from your platform guide.
> **Adeel:** *Store config in the environment.*
> **Tomas:** That is a quotation, and it is written as a command. What is missing from a command?
> **Adeel:** The conditions.
> **Tomas:** The conditions, the reasons and the exceptions. An imperative is the most compressed
> instruction in English, and the compression is exactly what gets lost when somebody applies it in a
> situation its author never saw. **A bare imperative in a technical document is a recommendation
> with its conditions deleted.**
> **Adeel:** Should I never use them?
> **Tomas:** Use them where you genuinely mean *always*, and nowhere else. *Never commit a
> credential.* That is a real imperative: no conditions, no exceptions, and the negative form makes
> it stronger again. For everything else there is a ladder.
> **Adeel:** What is on the ladder?
> **Tomas:** From strong to soft: *must* and *never*; the bare imperative; *should* and *ought to*;
> *it is recommended that*; *prefer*; and *consider*. The last two are the ones our documents are
> short of. **Prefer a managed service to a self-hosted one** says *this is the default and you may
> argue*. *Consider caching the result* says *we have thought about it and you should think too*.
> **Adeel:** Is *should* enough for a platform guide?
> **Tomas:** For most of it, yes, and it buys you something important: a *should* with a reason
> beside it invites a written departure, and a bare imperative invites a silent one. **People do not
> argue with commands; they ignore them, and then nobody knows what the system actually does.**
> **Adeel:** And the formal version?
> **Tomas:** *It is recommended that logs be streamed rather than written to a file.* Note the
> subjunctive — *be*, not *is*. It is formal, impersonal and useful in a document that will be read
> by people who did not meet you.

*Why does Tomas say a bare imperative invites a silent departure?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A methodology written entirely in imperatives gets adopted faster than one written in
recommendations, and it gets understood less. That is a real trade, and the author of these twelve
made it deliberately: short commands travel, conditions do not. What happens next is predictable.
Teams adopt the commands, encounter the situations the commands did not anticipate, depart from them
silently — because you cannot argue with a command, only ignore it — and three years later nobody
can distinguish a reasoned exception from an accident. The repair is not more rules. It is to write
down exceptions rather than compliance, in three parts: which rule, what we do instead, why. A
document listing what you follow becomes a checklist somebody fills in. A document listing what you
do not follow is where the engineering is.

> | **3** config in the environment | config is in a **secrets service**, not in variables | variables are **inherited by child processes** and appear in **crash dumps** | one more service in the start-up path |
>
> **Three instructions assume a deployment model.** **Processes**, **port binding** and **scaling
> out** describe a stateless request-handling service that a platform starts and stops at will. For
> a **batch job**, a **stream processor** or a **long-lived connection** they fit badly, and the
> document does not say so because in 2011 its author was describing the applications in front of
> him.
>
> **What is a defect and not a departure.** One application reads config from **a file committed to
> the repository**, so the same build **cannot run in two places** — which is the exact problem
> instruction 3 exists to prevent. **There is no reason and no accepted cost, so it is a defect**,
> and it is in the backlog rather than on this page.
>
> **What this document is not.** The methodology is **not a standard**: no conformance clause, no
> certificate, nobody to audit us. ***Twelve-factor compliant* is not a status anybody can hold.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **Which applications follow which instructions.** That is a spreadsheet, it
> is in section 3.6, and it is the checklist we have decided not to publish.
>
> *A departure has a reason and an accepted cost. A defect has neither. Eleven of ours had neither
> and were being described as preferences.*

> **Writer's Tip** — The *cost we accept* column is what makes a departure defensible. A reason
> explains the choice; an accepted cost proves somebody looked at both sides.
