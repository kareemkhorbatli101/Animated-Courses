# Unit 9 · The Metaphor That Got Reversed
%%SUB A 1992 experience report, and what its author says it did not mean%%
%%GOALS
Distinguish metaphor from literal statement
Say what the original metaphor meant
Say where a metaphor stops being useful
%%

A short experience report in 1992 compared shipping first-version code to borrowing money. The
comparison has been used ever since, usually to mean *we knowingly wrote something bad and will fix
it later* — which is close to the opposite of what the author described, and he has said so.

%%FIG d09-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the report, its author, its venue and its year, a two-column table comparing the original reading with the common one, a boxed paragraph on where the metaphor breaks down, a short list of what it does not provide, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Meridian · architecture note 45
> *Platform team · the debt metaphor · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The source.** An **experience report** by **Ward Cunningham**, presented at the **OOPSLA**
> conference in **1992**, about a financial system his team had built. The famous passage compares
> shipping first-version code to **going into debt**: a little speeds you up, **provided it is repaid
> promptly with a rewrite**, and the danger is in **not repaying**.
>
> **And the author's own correction.** Years later he said publicly that the metaphor was about
> the gap between **the program's structure** and **your current understanding of the business** —
> you ship in order to learn, then you **reshape the program to match what you learned**. He added
> that he was **never in favor of writing code poorly**.
>
> **Two readings.**
>
> | The original | The common use |
> |---|---|
> | we **could not have known** the right structure yet | we **knew** and shipped anyway |
> | shipping is how we **learned** | shipping is how we **hit a date** |
> | repayment = **restructuring to match new understanding** | repayment = **fixing bad code** |
> | the cost is that the program **no longer matches** what we know | the cost is that the code is **unpleasant** |
>
> **These are not the same claim, and the second is the one in every planning meeting.** It is also
> the more comfortable one, because it turns a decision into an instrument: *we took on debt* sounds
> like finance, while *we shipped something we knew was wrong* sounds like a choice somebody made.
>
> **Where the metaphor breaks down.**
>
> > A real loan has four things this does not have. A **principal** you could state. An **interest
> > rate** you could look up. A **schedule** with dates. And a **creditor** who will telephone you.
> >
> > The metaphor keeps the one part that is genuinely true — **it gets harder every month** — and
> > quietly drops the only part that makes a real debt get repaid, which is **somebody who makes
> > you**. That is why *we will pay it down next quarter* is said so often and happens so rarely:
> > **there is nobody on the other end of the sentence.**
>
> **What it does not provide.**
>
> - **A number.** There is no principal and no rate. Spreadsheets that price a code base in money
>   are **inventions on top of the metaphor**, not part of it.
> - **A schedule.** Nothing in the idea says when.
> - **A license to write badly.** The author has said the opposite, in public, more than once.
> - **A measurement.** It is a four-page experience report, not a framework.
>
> **What we do, in one line.** We do not use the phrase. We write **what we did**, **what we now
> know that we did not know then**, and **what reshaping would cost** — three sentences that cannot
> be read two ways.
>
> *Source: Ward Cunningham, The WyCash Portfolio Management System, OOPSLA experience report, 1992:
> the comparison of shipping first-version code to going into debt, repaid promptly by rewriting, with
> the cost of not repaying described as interest; together with the author's later public statement
> that the metaphor concerned the gap between a program's structure and the team's current
> understanding, and that he did not advocate writing code poorly. The source is a short experience
> report: it provides no principal, no rate, no schedule and no method of measurement.*

### 1 · Metaphor and literal language

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD KS4-09-06%%

> **Mei:** Read me the line from your quarterly plan.
> **Adeel:** *We will pay down the technical debt in the invoice module.*
> **Mei:** Every word of that is a metaphor and none of it is checkable. Tell me literally what you
> will do.
> **Adeel:** *We will replace the invoice data model so that one invoice can have more than one
> currency.*
> **Mei:** Which is a sentence somebody can estimate, schedule and refuse. The first version cannot
> be refused, because nobody knows what it commits you to. **A metaphor is a way of agreeing without
> deciding.**
> **Adeel:** So I should never use a metaphor?
> **Mei:** Use them, and mark them. English has a set of signals for exactly this. *So to speak*
> tells the reader you know it is a figure. *In effect* says the comparison is close enough for the
> purpose. *Strictly speaking* introduces the literal version. **The problem is never a metaphor; it
> is an unmarked metaphor, which gets read as a measurement.**
> **Adeel:** And when a metaphor stops working?
> **Mei:** Say so, in those words: *the metaphor breaks down at the creditor*. That sentence is more
> useful than three paragraphs of argument, because it tells the reader exactly which part they may
> not rely on.
> **Adeel:** What about *literally*?
> **Mei:** Keep it for the literal, which in technical writing is most of the time. *The queue
> literally fills up* — it does. **Using *literally* as emphasis is common in speech and in a
> document it costs you the one word you need when something is actually, measurably true.**

*What does Mei say a metaphor is a way of doing?*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

An unmarked metaphor is the most efficient way to get agreement without a decision, and that is why
this one has outlived its author's own correction. *Pay down technical debt* can be approved by
eleven people who each picture something different, costs nothing to say, and commits nobody.
*Replace the invoice data model: three weeks, two million rows migrated* can be refused — which is
what makes it a plan. The useful habit is small: when a metaphor appears in a document, write the
literal sentence underneath it, and if you cannot, you have found out that nobody knows what is
being proposed. The metaphor is still worth keeping for conversations with people outside
engineering, where it does real work explaining why delay has a cost. It is a good door and a bad
room.

> **Where the comparison breaks down.** There is **no principal**, **no rate**, **no schedule** and
> **no creditor**. **Nobody will telephone us in October.** The figure of four hundred thousand in
> last quarter's board paper **was invented in good faith and has no basis**; it has been withdrawn.
>
> **What the 1992 source actually said.** Ship in order to **learn**, then **reshape to match what
> you learned**. The author has since stated publicly that he **did not advocate writing code
> poorly**. **Row one above is the original reading; two other items on our list are not debt in any
> sense and are simply decisions we would take again.**
>
> **Not in this note.** **The other eleven items on the list.** Four of them, on inspection, are
> ordinary untidiness with no cost attached, and saying so is section 9.4.
>
> *The word *debt* does not appear in the plan entry. It appears here once, to explain why we are not
> using it.*

> **Writer's Tip** — *Who is paying for it until then* is the row that changes priorities, because it
> is the only one with people in it. Four hours a month, twice, is a fact a product manager can
