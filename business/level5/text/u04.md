### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Read what a forecast actually said, in its own grammar. *On trend to 145
> billion* is not *will be 145 billion*. The first is a statement about a direction with a figure
> attached; the second is a promise. People who confuse them end up either trusting forecasts too
> much or discarding them entirely, and both mistakes cost the same.

%%FIG s04-f5 | A one-page audit note: a heading, a two-column table comparing what was projected with what occurred, a boxed paragraph headed what the projection actually said, a list of four figures from the later report, a short conclusion in bold, and a source line in small type naming two publications a year apart.%%

> *I. Costa · 11 March · four pages, summarized on one*

> | Economic losses | not stated | **220bn** |
> | Share insured | not stated | about **49%** — highest in the series |
> | Secondary perils' share of insured nat-cat losses | not stated | **92%** |
> | Largest single insured wildfire on record | not foreseen | **Los Angeles, about 40bn** |
>
> **What the projection actually said.** The March 2025 publication described losses as **"on trend
> to" 145 billion dollars**. It was a statement about a rising trend, with a figure attached. It was
> **not** a point forecast for the calendar year, and our budget paper quoted it as though it were.
> **That error is ours, not the forecaster's.**
>
> **What the outturn was.** About **107 billion** — a miss of roughly **26 percent** against the
> figure we used. It was also the **sixth consecutive year above 100 billion**, which is the trend

### 5 · Word study — past modals of deduction

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG s04-f6 | A chart of past modal forms arranged by what they claim: must have for near-certainty, can't have for impossibility, may have and might have for possibility, needn't have for something unnecessary that was done, should have for something necessary that was not, and would have had to for a condition — each with one example.%%

| Form | Claim | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **must have** | near-certain, inferred | They **must have** built it from the trend, not the year. |
| **can't have** | impossible | They **can't have** known about the January fires. |
| **may / might have** | possible | We **might have** paid less with a wider range. |
| **needn't have** | it was done and was unnecessary | We **needn't have** deferred the deductible. |
| **didn't need to** | it wasn't done, or no need arose | We **didn't need to** raise the provision. |
| **should have** | it wasn't done and ought to have been | We **should have** quoted the words exactly. |
| **would have had to** | a condition in the past | Losses **would have had to** double to reach it. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Needn't have* and *didn't need to* are not the same. *We needn't have deferred
> it* means we did defer it and it was unnecessary. *We didn't need to defer it* usually means the
> need never arose. In an audit note the difference is the difference between an admission and a
> description.

**5B  Choose the right form and write the sentence.**

1. They used the trend; you are nearly certain. *(must have)*
2. The January fires happened after publication. *(can't have known)*
3. We deferred, and it turned out to be unnecessary. *(needn't have)*
4. We did not quote the exact words, and we ought to have. *(should have)*

**1B  Underline the two past modal forms. What does each one admit?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **must / can't have + participle** | They **must have** meant the trend. |
| **needn't have + participle** | We **needn't have** paid the higher premium. |
| **should / shouldn't have + participle** | We **should have** quoted the exact words. |
| **could have + participle** | We **could have** planned against a range. |
| **would have + participle** | On that basis we **would have** raised the deductible. |
| **may well have + participle** | They **may well have** been right about the trend. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Could have* has two meanings and context decides: *we could have planned
> against a range* (we had the option and did not take it) or *that could have cost us a million*
> (it was possible and did not happen). If the difference matters, say which: *we had the option
> and did not take it* costs seven words and prevents an argument.

### 2 · Hindsight without blame

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Phrase | What it does |
|---|---|
| **with hindsight** | marks knowledge you did not have at the time |
| **as it turned out** | reports the outcome neutrally |
| **in the event** | the same, more formal |
| **on the information available** | protects the decision while admitting the result |
| **the decision was sound and the number was wrong** | separates the two explicitly |
| **I would have advised the same** | takes responsibility forward, not backward |

> **Grammar Note** — *With hindsight* placed at the front of a sentence frames everything after it
> as unavailable at the time: *With hindsight, the deferral cost us 240,000.* Placed at the end it
> can sound like an excuse added late. The position carries most of the meaning.

### %%AUG c%% 2 · Clear English — admitting and defending in the same breath

**2A  Listen to the model.** %%AUD CL5-04-09%%

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What happens to the person whose forecast missed differs sharply from place to place, and so does
what they are expected to say. In some organizations the professional move is to produce an
unflinching note on your own error before anybody asks, and doing so protects you; in others any
written admission becomes permanent evidence and the expected behavior is silence until the matter
passes. There are differences in where responsibility sits: some business cultures treat a forecast
as the forecaster's property and a bad outcome as their failure, while others treat the decision as
the only thing that can be judged, since the future was never knowable. Even the grammar carries
risk — in English *I should have* is a normal professional phrase, and translated directly into some
languages it sounds like an admission of wrongdoing with consequences attached.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is a written admission of error safe? Who writes one, and what happens
to them?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Variance note — the 2025 insurance decision
> *I. Costa · 11 March · one page*
>
> **What was published, in their words.** In March 2025 the Swiss Re Institute described insured
> natural catastrophe losses as **"on trend to 145 billion dollars"**. The phrase *on trend to* is
> theirs. It describes a direction with a figure attached, and it is not a point forecast for a
> calendar year.
>
> **What we wrote.** Our budget paper said losses **"will be"** 145 billion. **That substitution is
> our error, not theirs**, and it is the only finding in this note I would call a habit rather than
> an outcome.
>
> **What occurred.** About **107 billion** in insured natural catastrophe losses across **190
> events**, or about **118 billion** including man-made events — a variance of roughly **26 per
> cent** against the figure we used, and the **sixth consecutive year above 100 billion**.
>
> **What the same source got right, and we did not use.** Economic losses of **220 billion**, of
> which about **49 percent insured** — the highest share in their records. **92 percent** of
> insured catastrophe losses from **secondary perils** rather than hurricane or earthquake. And the
> **largest single insured wildfire event on record**, the Los Angeles fires at about **40 billion**,
> inside the year that came in below trend. All three matter more to our renewal than the headline.
>
> **The cost, and whose decision it was.** Deferring the deductible increase cost an estimated
> **240,000 euros** in premium. **This is my estimate and I have labeled it as one.** The decision
> was mine to review and I did not challenge it.
>
> **What I would advise now.** **On the information available in March 2025, I would have advised
> the same deferral.** The decision was sound and the number was wrong. **It would have had to** be
> a far worse year for the deferral to pay, and it was not.
>
> **What changes.** We quote hedges exactly. We plan renewals against a range, not a point. We write
> a note like this one every year, whether or not it flatters us.
>
> *Sources: Swiss Re Institute, sigma 1/2025 and sigma 1/2026 (March 2026). A separate Swiss Re
> publication gives 88 percent for wildfire, severe convective storm and flood together — a
> narrower definition than the 92 percent above. The two figures are not in conflict and I have
> not combined them.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Quote the hedge. If the forecaster wrote *on trend to*, *is projected to* or
> *could reach*, those words go in your paper inside inverted commas, because they are the part that
> tells your reader what kind of claim they are relying on. Replacing a hedge with *will be* is the
> most common and most expensive editing decision in business writing, and it is almost always made
> for reasons of style.

### 3 · Write

Write **140 to 180 words**: a variance note on a forecast that missed.

**Quote the hedge exactly. Give the variance as a figure and a percentage. Say whose error each part
was. Label your estimates as estimates. Separate the decision from the outcome. End with what
changes.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Reading an admission out loud is the only way to
hear whether it sounds like an admission or an excuse. Then check your own against the list, and
swap.

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's note as the person who made the decision. Which sentence
would you want removed, and should it be?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you write *but* between the admission and
the defense? Read it both ways and decide which you would send.
