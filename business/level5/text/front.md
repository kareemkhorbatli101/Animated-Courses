# Clearline 4
%%SUB Business English · International Edition · CEFR B2%%

## How to use this book

Clearline 3 was about carrying responsibility. This one is about **persuading and deciding** — and
about the particular kind of honesty that persuasion needs if it is going to survive being checked.

**Twelve units**, sixteen pages each, the same six lessons in the same order as every book in this
series. If you have used any of them, you already know where everything is.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Business Reading** | A real document, and one real number that its own publisher qualifies |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, taught as choices with consequences |
| **4 · Workplace Conversation** | Clear English, a long dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model of the unit's genre, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `CL5-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Every reading text in this book has something wrong with it, and the publisher says so.** That is
the whole design. Unit 1 reads a growth forecast whose own footnotes assume a shipping strait
reopens in March and oil averages ninety-six dollars. Unit 4 reads a projection of a hundred and
forty-five billion dollars against an outcome of a hundred and seven — both from the same
institution, one year apart. Unit 5 reads an unemployment rate that is flat and a median that is
rising, in one report. Unit 10 reads a headline figure its publisher revised from 9.6 trillion to
9.5 between two of its own releases. Unit 11 reads a report that states, in its own front pages,
that its figures cannot be compared with the figures in the report before it.

None of these are scandals. They are what careful measurement actually looks like. The skill this
book teaches is the one that separates people who can be trusted with a number from people who
cannot: **saying the qualification out loud, in the same breath as the figure, without losing the
argument.**

**You will argue against yourself.** Unit 2 is built on it. The strongest case for a decision is the
one that has already said what is weakest about it, and the English for that — *granted*, *while*,
*albeit*, *even if* — is in Lesson 3 of that unit.

**Six units teach you how to say a number is uncertain without saying it is useless.** These are
not the same sentence. *We cannot rely on this* ends a meeting. *This is the best figure available,
it was revised once, and here is what would change my mind* continues one.

**Nothing assumes one country's data.** Where a figure exists only for some economies, the book says
so and makes finding your own the task.

## The people

Ostberg, two years on from Book 3. The decisions have got larger and fewer people now check them.

**Yara Haddad** is operations director at Lindmark Freight and in Unit 1 has to present a plan
built on somebody else's forecast. **Marek Dolny** is finance director and the person who reads the
footnotes. **Chiamaka Obi** heads the program office at Arvo Components and in Unit 2 argues
against her own proposal in front of the people she needs to approve it. **Paula Moreau** is
commercial director. **Sofia Reyes** runs customer operations at Corvid Analytics. **Ravi Menon**
is quality director at Brightwell Pharma. **Nour Khalil** is people director and the one who holds
the conversation in Unit 12. **Hanna Lindqvist** is general counsel and in Unit 8 has to advise on
a dispute with a number missing. **Ines Costa** is head of internal audit. **Wei Zhang** plans the
network and in Unit 3 is asked what the company should be ready for in ten years. **Daniel Okonjo**
runs the Ostberg site. **Anders Holm** is logistics manager. **Jun-seo Park** is at Juno Textiles.
**Tomas Ek** is facilities manager. **Lars Brandt** chairs the board, says little, and reads
everything.

Everything about the people is invented. Every number in a reading text is real, and the text says
where it comes from, what year it describes, what its publisher says about it, and what it does
not show.
