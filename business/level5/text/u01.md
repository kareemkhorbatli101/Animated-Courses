### 5 · Word study — the language of assumption

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG s01-f6 | A chart of six ways to name an assumption, from the plainest to the most formal, each with one example sentence: assuming that, on the assumption that, subject to, contingent on, predicated on, and provided that.%%

| Form | Example | Register |
|---|---|---|
| **assuming (that)** | **Assuming** the strait reopens in March, fuel falls. | neutral, spoken |
| **on the assumption that** | The plan is built **on the assumption that** demand grows. | neutral, written |
| **subject to** | The figure is **subject to** the oil price holding. | formal |
| **contingent on** | Approval is **contingent on** the March reopening. | formal |
| **predicated on** | The whole case is **predicated on** one sentence. | formal, emphatic |
| **provided (that)** | We can staff it, **provided** volumes hold. | conditional |

> **Grammar Note** — *Assuming* and *provided that* put the condition in the air and leave it there.
> *Predicated on* and *contingent on* attach it to something and make it visible. When you want a
> board to notice a condition, use the heavy ones: *this recommendation is predicated on a
> reopening in March* is almost impossible to forget, and *assuming the strait reopens* is almost
> impossible to remember.

### 1 · Cleft sentences — putting the emphasis where it belongs

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL5-01-06%%

> **Yara:** It's three point four *if the strait reopens in March.*

> assumption that the whole plan turns on**, and it isn't in any of our documents.
> **Marek:** Then put it in the first line.
> **Yara:** It is the first line now.

*Yara says two things in the cleft form rather than plainly. What does the form buy her?*

**1B  Underline the two cleft sentences. Say each one plainly, then say the cleft again.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Type | Plain | Cleft |
|---|---|---|
| ***it*-cleft** | The March assumption matters. | **It is the March assumption that** matters. |
| ***what*-cleft** | They only know the headline. | **What they know is** the headline. |
| ***what*-cleft, verb** | We need the assumptions. | **What we need is** the assumptions. |
| **all-cleft** | They quoted only one figure. | **All they quoted was** one figure. |
| ***the reason*-cleft** | We deferred because of March. | **The reason we deferred is that** March is unknown. |
| ***what … do*** | He read the annex. | **What he did was read** the annex. |

> **Grammar Note** — A cleft sentence splits one clause into two so you can choose what lands last
> and loudest. English puts new information at the end, so *it is the March assumption that matters*
> makes the assumption the news, while *the March assumption matters* makes it an aside. Use clefts
> sparingly and on purpose: two in a paper is persuasive, six is a style.

### 2 · Fronting and other emphasis

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **Fronting** | **Two sentences** this whole plan rests on. |
| **Emphatic *do*** | I **do** think the forecast is usable. I just want it quoted properly. |
| **Negative fronting** | **Not once** does the summary mention the strait. |
| **Repetition for contrast** | One assumption. Counted twice. |
| **End-weight** | The part nobody quotes is **the part that decides it**. |

> **Grammar Note** — Negative fronting inverts the verb: *not once **does** the summary mention it*,
> not *not once the summary mentions it*. Emphatic *do* is the cheapest and most useful of these in
> a meeting, because it concedes and holds at the same time: *I do accept the figure — what I
> cannot accept is using it twice.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Whether you may question the assumptions behind a figure, and in front of whom, differs a great
deal. In some organizations reading the annex and saying what you found is exactly what a senior
colleague is for, and doing it in a board meeting is read as diligence. In others the figure has
been agreed before the meeting, the meeting exists to confirm it, and arriving with a footnote is
heard as an attack on whoever brought the number. Some business cultures treat forecasts as
institutional property, so that quoting an assumption against a projection is almost a
constitutional question; others treat all figures as provisional and will happily rework a plan in
the room. There are differences in how conditions are expressed too: in some languages a strong
hedge sounds careful, and in English it can sound evasive, so learners often choose between seeming
vague and seeming blunt.

**Discuss:** Where you work, who is allowed to read the footnote out loud? What happens next?

> assumption about the strait, so if it does not reopen we lose on demand and on cost at the same
> time. **It is the correlation, not the forecast, that I am asking the board to look at.**
>
> **Reversible and irreversible.** The night shift costs about 40,000 to start and can be stopped in
> eight weeks with no penalty. Two vehicles on a four-year lease cannot be stopped at all.
>
> **Recommendation.** Approve the night shift now. Defer the vehicles to the second week of March,
> when the assumption resolves. My estimate of the cost of being two months late is about 60,000;
> of being four years early, about 400,000.
>
> **The date I will know.** March 2027, and I will bring the vehicle decision back in that second
> week whether or not anybody asks me to.
>
> *Source: IMF, World Economic Outlook, October 2026, including the technical assumptions in the
> statistical annex. The projections and the assumptions are from the same publication. The cost
> estimates are mine and are estimates.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the assumption immediately under the figure, not at the end. A reader who
> meets the condition in the second line reads the rest of the paper differently; a reader who meets
> it in the last line has already made up their mind and now thinks you were hiding it. And always
> name the date on which the condition resolves, because that single date converts an argument into
> a plan.
