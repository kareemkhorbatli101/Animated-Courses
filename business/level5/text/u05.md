### 5 · Word study — relative clauses with quantifiers

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG s05-f6 | A chart of quantifier plus of which and of whom forms: most of which, two of which, none of which, half of whom, a third of whom, the figure for which and the largest of which, each with one example sentence drawn from a group of countries.%%

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| **two of which** | Eleven countries, **two of which** are above the median. |
| **most of which** | Nine national rates, **most of which** are published monthly. |
| **none of which** | Four measures, **none of which** I have combined. |
| **half of whom** | Eleven managers, **half of whom** have read the headline. |
| **a third of whom** | 600 staff, **a third of whom** are in one country. |
| **the largest of which** | Three markets, **the largest of which** is Germany. |
| **the figure for which** | Two countries, **the figure for which** is not comparable. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Of which* for things, *of whom* for people, and the verb agrees with the
> quantifier, not with the noun before it: *two of which **are**…*, *none of which **is**…* (or
> *are*, in modern usage). You cannot use *of them* here: *eleven countries, two of them are above*
> is two sentences joined with a comma, which is a comma splice. *Two of which* is the repair.

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL5-05-06%%

**1B  Underline the quantifier clause. Rewrite it as two sentences and say which is better.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **number + of which/whom** | Eleven countries, **two of which** are above the median. |
| **most / some / none + of which** | Four measures, **none of which** I have combined. |
| **fraction + of whom** | 600 staff, **a third of whom** are in one country. |
| **superlative + of which** | Three markets, **the largest of which** is Germany. |
| **the + noun + for/of which** | Two countries, **the figures for which** are not comparable. |
| **many / few + of whom** | Eleven managers, **few of whom** read the annex. |

> **Grammar Note** — These clauses are non-defining: they always take a comma, and they can never
> use *that*. *Eleven countries that two of them are above* is wrong twice. Keep the quantifier
> first: *two of which*, not *of which two*, unless you are writing deliberately formal prose.

### 2 · The language of aggregation

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Phrase | What it tells the reader |
|---|---|
| **weighted by** | which member counts for more, and why |
| **on an unweighted basis** | every member counted once |
| **the simple average** | the same, plainer |
| **the median country** | the middle one, size ignored |
| **across countries / within a country** | between members, or inside one |
| **dispersion has widened** | the members are becoming less alike |

> **Grammar Note** — *Across* and *within* are not interchangeable and the difference is often the
> whole finding: *inequality across countries* is how countries differ from each other; *inequality
> within a country* is how people inside one differ. A sentence that does not say which is not
> describing anything.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How a group figure is received depends on whether people believe the group is a real thing. In some
organizations a regional or global average is treated as the authoritative number and a national
figure as special pleading, so a manager who argues from local data is heard as resisting the
center. In others the national figure is sovereign and the group average is politely ignored. There
are differences in what may be said about pay at all: in several countries pay levels are collective
matters, negotiated nationally and not open to a company's discretion, so a group framework arrives
as a legal problem rather than a management choice. And openness differs — in some workplaces
publishing eleven national figures side by side would be normal and useful, and in others it would
be read as inviting comparison and resented by whoever is at the bottom.

**Discuss:** Where you work, which carries more authority — the group figure or the local one? Who
decides?

> **The decision this note is for.** The size of one pay envelope, and the way it is split across
> eleven countries. **The envelope is sized on the group figure. The split is not.**
>
> **Two summaries of the same market.** The **OECD-wide unemployment rate** was **4.9 percent** in
> May 2026 and has been at or below 5.0 percent for over four years, projected near that level
> through 2027. The **median unemployment rate across OECD countries** was **5.7 percent**, **up
> from 5.4** a year earlier. Both are in the same publication.
>
> **What each one answers.** The 4.9 percent is **weighted by the size of each labor market**, so
> the largest economies dominate it; it answers *what is happening across the OECD as a population?*
> The 5.7 percent is the **middle country** with size ignored; it answers *what is happening in a
> typical member country?* **Taken together they say the group is becoming less alike**, which is
> the finding neither figure carries alone.
>
> **Three figures I am including although nobody asked for them.** Employment growth is projected at
> **0.3 percent** in 2026 and **0.6** in 2027. Average annual real wage growth was **2.2 percent**
> in Q1 2026, against **2.7** a year earlier — **growing and slowing**. And in about **one third of
> OECD countries** real wages remain below their level of five years ago.
>
> **Our eleven countries.** **Eleven countries, none of which is the weighted average.** Nine
> publish a comparable national rate, **two of which** are above the OECD median and **four of
> which** are below. **Two publish nothing comparable, and for those two I am recording that I do
> not know** rather than substituting the group figure.
>
> **What I am asking for.** One envelope, sized on 4.9 percent. Eleven allocations, made on national
> figures. An explicit line in the board minute stating that the group figure was used for the size
> and for nothing else.
>
> *Source: OECD Employment Outlook 2026, July 2026, for the weighted rate, the median and the wage
> and employment figures. Country-level rates are from national statistical offices and are not
> harmonized with the OECD series; I have not adjusted them.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Write the two figures as two sentences with no conjunction between them. The
> moment you join them with *but*, you have told the reader which one you believe, and the whole
> point of the paper is that you believe both. *However* and *although* do the same damage more
> politely.
