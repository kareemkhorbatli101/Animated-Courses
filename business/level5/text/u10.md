Marek's hedging policy quotes a single figure: global foreign exchange turnover of nine point six
trillion dollars a day, up twenty-eight percent. He took it from the (1) ______________ release at
the end of September, which is exactly where it was. In December the same institution published its
quarterly review with the final data, and the figure there is nine point five trillion, up
twenty-seven percent. The difference is about one percent of the total and one percentage point of
the growth rate, which is nothing at all for the purpose Marek is using it for, and which is
nevertheless the reason his paper is now wrong in a way that matters. It is wrong because it quotes
a figure without a version. If the board reads nine point six in March and somebody looks the figure
up, they will find nine point five, and the question will not be about one percent: it will be about
whether anything else in the paper was taken from a superseded (2) ______________ . The repair takes
nine words. *Nine point six trillion dollars a day as published on 30 September; nine point five in
the December review.* Two figures, two dates, no choice made, nothing to find. Sofia has pushed him
further than that. The survey also breaks the total down — spot turnover at three trillion dollars a
day, thirty-one percent of the total against twenty-eight in the previous survey, and outright
forwards at one point eight trillion, nineteen percent against fifteen — and it covers fifty-two
(3) ______________ jurisdictions. More importantly, it measures turnover in a single month, April,
every three years. Everybody who quotes it says *a day*, which is correct, and almost everybody
silently treats it as a figure for the year, which it is not. An April in which something unusual
happened is an April that sets the figure for three years. So the paper's second repair is a sentence
saying what the survey measures: one month, every three years, in fifty-two jurisdictions, and not
(4) ______________ .

### 5 · Word study — the language of versions

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG s10-f6 | A chart of version phrases: as published on, as at, as reported in, has since been revised, now stands at, superseded by, and preliminary against final, each with one example sentence and a note on where it goes in the sentence.%%

| Phrase | Example |
|---|---|
| **as published on** | 9.6 trillion **as published on** 30 September. |
| **as published in** | 9.5 trillion **as published in** the December review. |
| **as at** | The figure **as at** 30 September was 9.6 trillion. |
| **as reported in** | **As reported in** the preliminary release, turnover rose 28%. |
| **has since been revised** | The figure **has since been revised** to 9.5 trillion. |
| **now stands at** | The final figure **now stands at** 9.5 trillion. |
| **superseded by** | The preliminary release is **superseded by** the December data. |

> **Grammar Note** — *As at* fixes a point in time for a measurement: *as at 31 December*. *As of*
> is used the same way in American English and also means *from now on*: *as of Monday, we charge
> more*. If both readings are possible, *as at* for the measurement and *from* for the change: it
> removes the ambiguity in one word.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence with the phrase in brackets.**

2. It is now 9.5 trillion. *(has since been revised)*
3. The preliminary release no longer applies. *(superseded by)*
4. The measurement point is 31 December. *(as at)*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **has been revised** | The figure **has been revised** to 9.5 trillion. |
| **has since been revised** | It **has since been revised**, in the December review. |
| **was subsequently revised** | The preliminary figure **was subsequently revised** downwards. |
| **now reads / now stands at** | The final data **now reads** 9.5 trillion. |
| **is superseded by** | The September release **is superseded by** the December data. |
| **remains as published** | The breakdown **remains as published** in September. |

> **Grammar Note** — The passive here is not evasion, it is accuracy: the figure was revised by the
> publisher as part of a normal process, and naming them in every sentence would imply an event. Use
> the present perfect — *has been revised* — when the new figure is the one now in force, and the
> past simple — *was revised in December* — when you are narrating what happened.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Attitudes to revised figures differ, and so do the consequences of quoting one. In some organizations
a revision is an ordinary event and quoting both versions is simply good practice; in others any
change to a published figure is treated as an error by somebody, and naming the earlier version
invites the question of who used it. Statistical culture varies at national level too: some agencies
revise frequently and publish revision histories as a matter of pride, while in other systems a
published figure is politically final and a revision is a sensitive matter. There are differences in
document discipline as well — in some companies a policy is a living document with a version number
on every page, and in others the same file is edited for years with no record of what changed, which
makes *as published on* almost impossible to reconstruct after the fact.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is a revised figure a routine event or somebody's mistake? What happens
to the documents that used the old one?

> ### Hedging policy — amendment to the figure on page 2
> *M. Dolny with S. Reyes · 14 January · one page*
>
> **The same figure, in two documents.** Global foreign exchange turnover averaged **9.6 trillion
> dollars a day** in April 2025, **up 28 percent**, **as published in the preliminary release of 30
> September 2025**. The same institution's **December 2025 quarterly review** gives **9.5 trillion**,
> **up 27 percent**, as final data.
>
> **What is material and what is not.** The difference is about **1 percent** of the total and one
> percentage point of the growth rate. **For sizing our hedging limit it is immaterial.** **The
> absence of a version note is material**, because an undated figure cannot be checked and will
> eventually be read as ours rather than the publisher's.
>
> **What the survey measures.** Turnover in **a single month — April — once every three years**,
> across **52 reporting jurisdictions**. The figure is correctly a **daily average for that April**.
> It is **not** an annual figure. The next observation is in 2028, so an unusual April sets the
> headline for three years.
>
> **The breakdown, which matters more to us than the headline.** **Spot: 3.0 trillion a day, 31 per
> cent of turnover**, against 28 percent in the previous survey. **Outright forwards: 1.8 trillion a
> day, 19 percent**, against 15 percent. **Our exposure is forward-weighted**, so the rise in the
> forwards share is the part of this survey that bears on our policy, and it is the part nobody
> quotes.
>
> **Exact replacement wording for page 2.** *"Global foreign exchange turnover averaged 9.6 trillion
> dollars a day in April 2025 as published in the preliminary release of 30 September 2025, and 9.5
> trillion as published in the December 2025 quarterly review. The survey measures a single month
> every three years across 52 jurisdictions."*
>
> **Why we are not choosing between the two figures.** Three other documents quote the September
> figure. If we switch silently, those three cannot be distinguished from documents using a different
> figure altogether. **Two figures, two dates, no choice made** — and all four documents to be
> amended identically.
>
> *Source: BIS Triennial Central Bank Survey 2025, preliminary release 30 September 2025, and BIS
> Quarterly Review, December 2025. Both are the same institution's publications.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Draft the replacement wording in full, inside quotation marks, and put it in the
> amendment. Whoever makes the change is not you, is working at speed, and will otherwise improvise a
> sentence that loses either a date or the measurement basis. Writing the exact words takes you two
> minutes and removes the only step where this can go wrong.
