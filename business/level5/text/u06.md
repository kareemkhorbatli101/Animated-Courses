means for Paula is simple and annoying. If a country was fortieth out of fifty and is now
fifty-second out of a hundred and one, the second position is better in proportion and worse in

the two positions is not a (3) ______________ comparison, because the denominator moved. There are
three things she can honestly do. She can quote this year's position with this year's total — the
figure and its denominator in the same breath. She can compare the countries that appear in both

sixty-one out of a hundred means the same thing whoever else is in the table. What she cannot do is
draw a line between two ranks and call it progress, and she has spent an hour trying to find a
fourth option.

### 5 · Word study — like-for-like language

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG s06-f6 | A chart of comparison phrases arranged from strongest claim to weakest: on a like-for-like basis, on a constant sample, on a comparable basis, as reported, as restated, and not directly comparable — each with one example sentence and a note on what it promises.%%

| Phrase | What it promises |
|---|---|
| **on a like-for-like basis** | same members, same definitions, both periods |
| **on a constant sample of 50** | same members, and you have said how many |
| **on a comparable basis** | definitions aligned; weaker than like-for-like |
| **as reported** | the figure as published at the time, not adjusted |
| **as restated** | the figure republished on the new basis |
| **not directly comparable** | an explicit refusal to compare |

> **Grammar Note** — *Like-for-like* is hyphenated before a noun — *like-for-like growth* — and
> written *on a like-for-like basis* as a phrase. Say what is held constant, every time: *on a
> like-for-like basis* with nothing after it is a claim without content, and readers who work with
> figures hear it as a warning rather than a reassurance.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence using the phrase in brackets.**

1. Comparing only the fifty economies in both editions, the score rose. *(on a constant sample)*
2. The two ranks cannot be set against each other. *(not directly comparable)*
3. This is the figure as it was published in 2024. *(as reported)*
4. The 2024 figure has been republished on the new basis. *(as restated)*

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **compared with** | 101 economies, **compared with** 50 in 2024. |
| **relative to** | Our score rose **relative to** the regional median. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Compared with* and *compared to* are both standard, and *compared with* is

> your benchmark.

### 2 · Refusing a comparison without refusing the data

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Move | Language |
|---|---|
| **Name the problem** | The denominator changed · The sample more than doubled |
| **Refuse precisely** | These two ranks are not directly comparable |
| **Keep the data** | The underlying scores are comparable, and they rose |
| **Offer the honest version** | On a constant sample of 50, two of the three improved |
| **Price the honesty** | It is a longer sentence; I would still send it |
| **Put the untidiness in your own footnote** | because whoever checks will find it anyway |

> **Grammar Note** — Refuse the comparison, not the source. *This index is unreliable* invites an
> argument you cannot win and loses you a useful series. *These two positions are not directly
> comparable, and here is what is* keeps the source and ends the discussion.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Rankings carry very different weight from country to country. In some economies an international
business ranking is a matter of national policy, quoted by ministers, with real consequences for
investment promotion, so a company's slide that says *we rose eleven places* can be repeating a
government's own talking point, and arguing with it in a meeting is not a technical remark. In
others such indices are treated with open skepticism and quoting one at all marks you as naive.
There are internal differences too: some organizations prize the vivid slide and regard
qualification as weakness, while in others a slide without its denominators would be returned
unread. And where the ranking is produced by an institution a country has a political relationship
with, the number can be unusable whatever its quality.

**Discuss:** Where you work, how is an international ranking received? Would you be thanked or
corrected for adding the denominator?

> **What cannot be said.** **No movement in rank between the two editions is a finding about a
> country.** Fifty-one economies entered the table between them. A market that was 40th of 50 and is
> 52nd of 101 has risen from roughly the 80th percentile to the 51st while appearing to fall twelve
> places. **The apparent fall and the apparent rises are both artifacts of the denominator.**
>
> **What can be said instead, and what each costs.** This year's rank **with this year's total** —
> honest, but gives no trend. A comparison restricted to the economies in **both** editions —
> **on a constant sample of 50, two of our three markets improved** — a real like-for-like
> comparison, at the cost of a smaller sample which must be stated. Or the **underlying scores out
> of 100**, which are comparable whoever else is in the table, at the cost of being less vivid.
>
> **On the published totals.** 50 plus 62 is 112, not 101, and the eventual target is given two
> ways. The publisher is building the series in tranches and says so, so this is untidiness rather
> than error. **I am putting it in our footnote because anybody who checks will find it, and I would
> rather it were a sentence about the index than a question about our slide.**
>
> **Recommendation.** Use the scores. State the coverage. Keep the constant-sample finding. Remove
> the arrows.
>
> **What I am not saying.** I am not saying the index is unreliable. It is the best comparable
> assessment available for these markets, and we should go on using it.
>
> *Source: World Bank, Business Ready (B-READY) 2024 and 2025, and the published plan for the 2026
> tranche. All coverage figures are the publisher's own.*

> **Writer's Tip** — End a note like this with *what I am not saying*. Readers who have just been
> told they cannot draw a trend assume, every time, that you are attacking the source, and they will
> defend it instead of hearing you. Two sentences at the foot of the page prevent the whole
> argument, and they are the two sentences most writers leave out because they feel obvious.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. The interim edition covers (50 / 101) economies.
2. The further tranche is (62 / 112).
3. On a constant sample, (two / three) markets improved.
4. The eventual target is given as about (170 or 180 / exactly 170).
