### 5 · Word study — near future and far future

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG s03-f6 | A chart split into a near column and a far column: the near column holds is set to, is due to, is projected to and is likely to; the far column holds may well, could, is expected to have, by then and within the decade, each with one example sentence and a note on how much it claims.%%

| Horizon | Forms | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Near, confident** | is set to · is due to · is scheduled to | Tariffs **are set to** rise in March. |
| **Near, qualified** | is projected to · is likely to · looks set to | Volumes **are projected to** fall 2 per cent. |
| **Far, possible** | may well · could · might | Liability rules **may well** change by 2033. |
| **Far, cumulative** | will have · will have been | By 2036 we **will have been** operating a decade under them. |
| **Far, framed** | by then · within the decade · at some point before | **Within the decade**, insurers will have repriced it. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Is set to* and *is due to* claim a schedule, so they belong to the near
> horizon and look foolish at ten years. *May well* claims about a sixty per cent chance in most
> speakers' usage — stronger than *might*, weaker than *will*. Use the cumulative forms for the far
> horizon: *by 2036 we will have been doing X for a decade* says something concrete about a distant
> year without pretending to know what happens in it.

### 1 · The future perfect, and the two horizons

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL5-03-06%%

> **Yara:** What do you want me to do about a risk that's thirtieth?
> **Wei:** Nothing this year. **By 2036 we'll have been carrying other people's AI liability clauses

> **Yara:** That I can approve.

*Wei asks for almost nothing. Why is that more likely to work than asking for a program?*

**1B  Underline the future perfect continuous. What does it describe that the simple future cannot?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **will + verb** | a point in the future | Tariffs **will rise**. |
| **will have + past participle** | completed before a future point | By March they **will have risen** twice. |
| **will have been + -ing** | duration up to a future point | By 2036 we **will have been** trading under them for a decade. |
| **is set to / due to** | scheduled, near | The rule **is due to** take effect in June. |
| **may well / could** | possible, far | Insurers **may well** reprice it. |
| **by then** | ties a second clause to the first | … and **by then** the template will be ten years old. |

> **Grammar Note** — The future perfect needs a future reference point, and English wants it stated:
> *by 2036*, *by the time the rule lands*, *before the next review*. Without it the tense floats and
> readers assume you mean now. *By the time* takes the present, not the future: *by the time it
> **takes** effect*, never *by the time it will take effect*.

### 2 · Future in the past — what we expected then

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| **was going to** | In 2016 this **was going to** be solved by regulation. |
| **would** (reported) | The last review said volumes **would** double. |
| **was due to / was expected to** | The rule **was due to** take effect in 2024. |

> **Grammar Note** — This is how you quote an old forecast without endorsing it: *the 2024 register

### %%AUG c%% 2 · Clear English — saying a horizon before a risk

**2A  Listen to the model.** %%AUD CL5-03-09%%

> *Over two years — trade. Over ten — weather.* — the horizon first, then a half-beat, then the risk
> *Thirtieth now. Fifth by the 2030s.* — two short sentences; let the gap do the work
> *That's what people expect, not what's likely.* — flat tone, no emphasis anywhere

the wrong thing. Name the horizon, pause, then name the risk. It takes half a second and prevents
most of the argument.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How far ahead an organization is expected to plan varies enormously, and so does who is allowed to
talk about it. Some business cultures treat a ten-year view as the proper subject of senior
management and consider quarterly thinking a sign of weakness; others treat long-horizon talk as
avoidance of the results you owe this year. Family-owned and state-owned organizations often plan
over decades as a matter of course, while listed companies answer to a reporting rhythm that makes
anything beyond three years hard to fund. There are differences in how uncertainty is voiced too: in
some languages a flat statement about 2036 is normal and heard as vision, while in English the same
sentence without a hedge can sound like a claim to know the future, and a listener will test it.

**Discuss:** Where you work, how far ahead may you talk about? Who would be taken seriously doing
it, and who would not?

> *W. Zhang · 2 February · one page*
>
> **Two lists, not one.** Over a **two-year** horizon, surveyed leaders rank **geoeconomic
> confrontation first**, misinformation and disinformation second, and cyber insecurity sixth. Over
> a **ten-year** horizon the order changes: **extreme weather first**, misinformation **fourth**,
> and the adverse outcomes of AI technologies **fifth**.
>
> **The biggest riser.** Adverse outcomes of AI technologies sits at **30th over two years and 5th
> over ten** — the largest movement between the horizons in the survey. Misinformation is the only
> risk in the top five of both.
>
> **What this evidence is.** A perception survey of **over 1,300 leaders and experts**, published
> **14 January 2026**, 21st edition. It records what informed people **expect**. It does not measure
> probability, and I am not presenting it as one.
>
> **What follows for the next two years.** Almost nothing. The near-horizon list is the one we
> already manage, and I am not proposing to move resource away from it.
>
> **What follows for the next ten.** One change, now, while it is cheap: a 38-word clause in the
> contract template on liability where a decision in the chain is taken by a model rather than a
> person. Legal has drafted it. **By the mid-2030s every counterparty will have written their own
> version**, and **by then** ours **will have been** the only template without one.
>
> **What I cannot claim.** This **may well** go the other way, and the clause **may** sit unused for
> a decade. That is what a 38-word clause is for. If I were asking for a budget I would owe you
> better evidence than a survey of expectations.
>
> **Revisit.** February 2028, with both lists, or sooner if AI risk enters the two-year top ten.
>
> *Source: World Economic Forum, Global Risks Report 2026, 14 January 2026, drawing on the Global
> Risks Perception Survey of over 1,300 leaders and experts. Rankings are of respondents'
> expectations.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Match the size of your request to the quality of your evidence. A perception
> survey will buy you a sentence in a template; it will not buy you a program, and asking for one
> on that evidence is how people learn to ignore your briefings. The discipline is uncomfortable
> because the far-horizon risks are the ones that frighten you most.

### 3 · Write

Write **140 to 180 words**: a two-horizon risk briefing.

**Give both lists. Name the biggest riser with both positions. Say what kind of evidence you are
using. Ask for one cheap thing. Say what you cannot claim. Give a revisit date.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Reading it out loud is the quickest way to hear
whether you have asked for more than your evidence can carry. Then check your own against the list,
and swap.

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's briefing as somebody with three urgent problems this
quarter. Would you approve the request, and how long did it take you to decide?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you ask for more than the evidence
supports? What would you have to find to justify the bigger ask?

> Five years on, the clause is in every Lindmark contract and has been invoked once, in a dispute
> about a routing decision that cost a customer eleven days. It settled in Lindmark's favor in
> four weeks, and Hanna's view is that without the clause it would have taken a year. On that
> arithmetic the thirty-eight words were worth several hundred thousand.
>
> The uncomfortable part is the rest of the briefing. Wei also wrote, in the same paper, that
> extreme weather was first on the ten-year list, and he asked for nothing at all about it, because
> he could not think of anything cheap. Two depots have since flooded. Nobody has criticized him;
> the flooding was not foreseeable at a depot level and the insurance paid. But the clause got
> written because a small, clever action existed, and the weather risk got nothing because one did
> not — and that is not the same as a judgement about which risk was larger.

1. Did Wei's briefing do its job?
2. Is "I could not think of anything cheap" a reason or an excuse?
3. How should a briefing handle a risk ranked first that you have no cheap answer to?
