### 5 · Word study — hypothetical forms

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG s08-f6 | A chart of hypothetical structures from plainest to most formal: if we arbitrated, if we were to arbitrate, were we to arbitrate, suppose we arbitrated, had we known, and if it had not been for, each with one example sentence and a note on register.%%

| Form | Example | Register |
|---|---|---|
| **if + past** | **If we arbitrated**, we would tie up 9 million. | neutral |
| **if + were to** | **If we were to arbitrate**, I would apply for expedition. | more tentative |
| **inversion: were we to** | **Were we to arbitrate**, I would apply for expedition. | formal, written |
| **suppose / supposing** | **Suppose** the tribunal took three years. | spoken, inviting |
| **had + subject + participle** | **Had we known** the clause named this seat, we would have priced it. | formal, past |
| **if it had not been for** | **If it had not been for** the clause, we would be in court. | emphatic, past |

> **Grammar Note** — Inversion replaces *if*: *were we to arbitrate*, *had we known*, *should you
> prefer*. You cannot keep both: *if were we to arbitrate* is wrong. Inversion raises the register
> sharply, which makes it useful in written advice and faintly theatrical in speech.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence with the form in brackets.**

2. We did not know the clause named Paris, so we did not price it. *(had we known)*
3. Imagine the tribunal took three years. *(suppose)*
4. The clause saved us from the courts. *(if it had not been for)*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **were + subject + to** | **Were we to** proceed, costs would start at 400,000. |
| **had + subject + participle** | **Had we** negotiated the seat, I could answer precisely. |
| **should + subject + verb** | **Should the tribunal** order disclosure, the timetable moves. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Should* in *should the tribunal order disclosure* means *if it happens*, not
> obligation, and it is the standard way to raise a possible future event in formal writing. Keep it
> for events that may well happen; use *were … to* for ones you think unlikely.

### 2 · Distancing and the language of advice

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Function | Language |
|---|---|
| **Advising without ordering** | My advice would be · I would be inclined to · I would hesitate to |
| **Labelling your own inference** | This is my inference, not a published figure |
| **Marking the limit of your knowledge** | I am not in a position to say · The figures do not reach that |
| **Giving a range you will defend** | I would plan on between X and Y, and I will not write either down |
| **Separating advice from decision** | The decision is yours; my advice is X |
| **Refusing to invent** | I can estimate. I cannot call my estimate a statistic. |

> **Grammar Note** — *I would be reluctant to* is stronger than it sounds and weaker than *I advise
> against*. In writing that may be read years later, choose the one you mean: a reader in a dispute
> will treat *reluctant* as permission and *advise against* as a warning, whatever you intended.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What counsel is expected to say, and in what form, varies greatly. In some legal and business
cultures advice is expected to arrive as a recommendation with a number attached, and a lawyer who
answers *not published* is thought to be withholding; in others the careful statement of what is
unknown is the professional product, and a confident figure would be treated as negligence. Where
disputes are resolved differs too — arbitration is normal in some industries and almost unknown in
others, and in some jurisdictions a local court would be faster and cheaper than any institution.
Attitudes to settlement vary most of all: in some business cultures proposing a settlement is a
normal commercial act, and in others it is read as an admission that your claim is weak, which
changes what a sixty-one per cent recovery looks like to the person you are proposing it to.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is proposing a settlement a sign of strength or weakness? Who is allowed
to propose one?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Advice — the Teval claim
> *H. Lindqvist · 18 April · one page*
>
> **The figure I do not have.** **How long an arbitration takes is not in the published statistics**
> I am relying on. They give case counts, values, sectors and award types. **I can estimate a
> duration and I will not call my estimate a statistic**, because a figure written in this note will
> be read in two years as the institution's and it would be mine.
>
> **What is published.** **894** new cases registered — 881 under the Arbitration Rules, 13 under the
> Appointing Authority Rules. **1,869** pending at year end, a record, against **1,789** after
> **841** new cases the year before. **607** draft awards approved: **444** final, **118** partial,
> **45** by consent. **169** new expedited cases, up from **152**. Construction and engineering
> **246 cases (28%)** and energy **128 (15%)** — together **43%**. Average amount in dispute **just
> over 50 million dollars**, down from **130 million**.
>
> **My inference, labelled as mine.** New cases rose by 53; pending cases rose by 80. **The stock is
> building faster than the inflow**, which is consistent with longer cases, and equally consistent
> with slower tribunal constitution or with more complex matters arriving. **This is an inference
> from two published numbers. It is not a duration.**
>
> **What bears directly on our decision.** Our claim is **9 million euros** against an average
> amount in dispute of just over 50 million, so we are a small case here and the **expedited
> procedure** is both available and growing. Ours is a construction and engineering dispute — the
> largest single category. **Nothing about this case is unusual, which is itself useful to know.**
>
> **Recommendation.** One settlement approach at **5.5 million** before filing. **Should it be
> refused**, file and apply for expedition in the same week. **Were we to run the full procedure**, I
> would budget costs from **400,000 euros** upwards and I cannot price the time.
>
> **For the contract policy.** **Had we negotiated the seat** in the 2023 agreement rather than
> accepting the counterparty's draft, this advice could have been precise. That belongs in next
> year's policy review and I have raised it there.
>
> **The decision is the board's. My advice is to try the 5.5 million.**
>
> *Source: ICC Dispute Resolution statistics for 2025. All counts, values and sector shares are the
> institution's published figures. No average duration appears in them, and I have not supplied one.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Write *I have not supplied one* in the source line. It is four words, it is the
> most useful sentence in the note, and it is the sentence that protects both you and the reader when
> somebody looks for the duration figure later and does not find it. Absence that is stated is
> information; absence that is silent is a trap.

### 3 · Write

Write **140 to 180 words**: advice on a decision whose key figure is not published.

**Name the missing figure in your first line. Give what is published. Label any inference as yours.
Use at least two hypothetical forms. Reach a recommendation. End by saying what you have not
supplied.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Reading the opening line out loud is the only way to
hear whether naming the gap sounds confident or evasive. Then check your own against the list, and
swap.
