### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When two numbers from the same organization do not agree, they are usually
> counting **different things**. Before you decide somebody is wrong, find out what each one is
> counting, and over what year.

%%FIG o12-f5 | A printed briefing sheet: a heading, two large percentages side by side in boxes with small print beneath each, a short paragraph between them, a note box headed these are not the same measurement, and a source line at the foot.%%

> ## Two numbers, one report
> *Lindmark Freight · staff briefing, 20 November*
>
> We were asked at the last meeting how many people in the world work without a contract. Here is
> what we found, and why it is not a simple answer.
>
> The International Labour Organization said in January 2026 that about **2.1 billion workers**
> are expected to hold informal jobs in 2026 — work with little or no social protection, rights or
> job security.
>
> A summary of the same release says that informal work was **51.1 per cent of all employment in
> 2025**.
>
> One news report described the 2.1 billion as "around 60 per cent" of the world's 3.6 billion
> workers.
>
> **These are not the same measurement.** 2.1 billion out of 3.6 billion is about 58 per cent, not
> 51.1 per cent. The two figures count slightly different things over slightly different years:
> one is a **share of employment**, the other is a **count of workers**. Neither is wrong and
> neither is a mistake.
>
> **What we will put in writing.** When we quote this, we will give **one** figure, say what it
> counts, and give the year: *"About 2.1 billion workers are expected to be in informal
> employment in 2026 (ILO)."* We will not write "around 60 per cent", because we did not count it.
>
> *Figures: ILO, January 2026 release; UN Geneva summary of the same release.*

<sub>**informal work** — work without a contract or legal protection · **a share** — out of every
hundred · **social protection** — sick pay, pensions and similar</sub>

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | will / 'll + verb | I'**ll** send it today. |
| **Negative** | won't + verb | I **won't** be there before ten. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Will* has no *-s*: *she will send*, never *she wills*. And the short form

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What an email **is** differs. In some business cultures an email is a record and should be short,
with no greeting beyond a name. In others an email without a polite opening and closing is cold
or even rude, and the business comes third. Naming an owner in front of a group is normal in some
workplaces and embarrassing in others, where the manager assigns the work afterwards and
privately. And in some cultures a written deadline is a firm commitment; in others it is an
expression of intention and everybody knows it will move.

**Discuss:** In your workplace, how long is a normal email? Who writes down who does what?
