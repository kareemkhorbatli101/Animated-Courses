# Clearline — cited sources, book by book

Every figure quoted in a reading text in this series is real, published, dated and named in the
text where it is used. Where two published summaries of the same thing disagree, both are printed
and the disagreement becomes the task. Nothing else in these books is real: every company, person,
document and transaction is invented.

Checked by search in November 2026.

- **Unit 12 is built on a disagreement, and says so.** 2.1 billion out of 3.6 billion is 58%, not
  51.1%. The two numbers come from the same organization in the same month and count slightly

  numbers are not the same thing*, and asks the learner which one they would put in an email.

  readers to. The text gives the year.

## Notes on honesty

- **Unit 10 is built on a number nobody can confirm.** The 1,474,118 figure for ISO 9001
  certificates in 2024 comes from secondary reports of the ISO Survey, which is now published
  through IAF CertSearch rather than as a public table. Two of those reports give different
  figures for 2023 — 837,978 and 1,250,243 — and the apparent jump is most likely a change in how
  certificates are counted, not a change in the world. The unit prints the figure, prints both
  baselines, and says plainly that it cannot tell you which is right. That is the point of the
  unit: an audit is about evidence, and the number at the top of this page does not have any.
- **Unit 11 is two true sentences that point in opposite directions.** The accident rate fell in
  2025 and the number of people killed rose. Both are in the same IATA report. The unit teaches
  the learner to say both.
- **Unit 8 is about a forecast that moved.** The OECD's 2027 figure was 3.1% in June 2026 and 3.0%

  home if they do so for at least half the days in a four-week period. News reports of the same
  release give the EU average as 8.8%, 8.9% and 9%, and Finland's as 20.5% or 20.9%. The unit
  prints "about 9%" and asks the learner why a one-decimal difference is not worth arguing about
  while the definition is.
- **Unit 2 quotes a caveat the ADB printed about its own figure.** The ADB says the gap may partly
  reflect cyclical factors rather than lack of access, and that no comprehensive dataset of trade
  finance demand and supply exists, so the estimate rests on survey modelling. The unit prints
  that sentence beside the figure.

## Notes on honesty

- **Unit 11 is the clearest case in the series of one business measured two ways.** International
  parcel post tonnage fell 16 per cent while the number of items rose 38 per cent. Both are true
  and they describe the same traffic: people are sending more parcels and each one weighs less. A
  report that quotes only the tonnage says the business is shrinking; one that quotes only the
  items says it is growing. The unit prints both and makes the learner write the sentence that
  contains them both. It also keeps the UPU and IPC figures visibly apart, because they are
  different organizations measuring different populations over different periods.
- **Unit 5 separates value from volume, which is the commonest honest-looking error in business
  reporting.** The WTO's own October 2026 update has services trade up 14 per cent in value in one
  quarter and a 2026 **volume** forecast cut to 3.3 per cent. Neither figure is wrong. Quoting one
  as though it were the other is.
- **Unit 9's headline contains its own counter-example.** Global FDI rose 6 per cent; flows to
  lower-middle-income countries fell 5 per cent; and excluding conduit flows the rise was 4 per
  cent rather than 6. The unit prints all three and asks which one belongs in a paper arguing for
  investment in a lower-middle-income country.
- **Unit 7 is three numbers for one subject, and the gap between them is the point.** The law
  averages 67, its enforcement 53, and the systems that would make it work 47. The unit's task is a
  policy that admits the difference between what a rule says and what a company actually does.
- **Unit 12 is a count that depends on what you are counting.** "Nearly 50 jurisdictions on the
  journey" and "21 jurisdictions have adopted" are both current and both defensible, because one
  counts intention and the other counts enactment — and even enactment is often partial. The unit
  prints both with their dates and asks the learner which they would put in an annual report, and
  what sentence they would put beside it.
- **Unit 4's figure is a simple average of providers, not of money sent.** The World Bank computes
  its 6.49 per cent by averaging the cost charged by each provider in its database, so a provider
  used by almost nobody counts as much as a provider used by millions. The unit says so, and asks
  what you would say to a finance director who wanted to budget on it.
- **Unit 10 mixes two vintages on purpose.** The birth and death rates are for 2024; the most
  recent EU-wide five-year survival figure describes businesses born in 2014. The unit prints both
  years beside the figures and makes the mismatch the task.

## Notes on honesty

- **Unit 4 is a published forecast that missed, by its own publisher's later figures.** Swiss Re's
  *sigma* 1/2025 said insured natural catastrophe losses were "on trend to 145 billion dollars" for
  2025; *sigma* 1/2026 reports about 107 billion. Nobody did anything wrong: a projection made in
  March describes a year that had not happened. The unit prints both, asks what a 26 per cent miss
  on a one-year projection should do to how the next one is used, and refuses the conclusion that
  forecasts are useless.
- **Unit 1 is a forecast whose assumptions are published and are the story.** The IMF's October 2026
  projections assume the Strait of Hormuz reopens in March 2027 and an oil price averaging 96
  dollars in 2026. The growth number everybody quotes is downstream of two sentences almost nobody
  quotes. The unit makes the learner quote the assumptions.
- **Unit 5 is an average and a median disagreeing about direction.** The OECD-wide unemployment rate
  was 4.9 per cent and near a four-year low; the median across OECD countries rose from 5.4 to 5.7.
  The first is weighted by the size of each labour market, so large countries dominate it; the second
  treats every country alike. Both are in the same report. "Unemployment is flat" and "unemployment
  is rising in most countries" can both be true.
- **Unit 9 is one institution publishing two different versions of the same measure.** The IEA's
  *World Energy Outlook 2026* gives electricity at 23 per cent of final energy consumption today
  and growing four times faster than total energy; *Electricity 2026* gives 21 per cent in 2025 and
  at least 2.5 times as fast. Different publications, scenarios, horizons and baselines. The unit's
  task is the sentence that cites one of them properly rather than averaging the two.
- **Unit 10 is a headline revised by its own publisher.** 9.6 trillion dollars a day became 9.5, and
  "up 28 per cent" became "up 27", between a preliminary release and the final data. Both figures
  were correct when published. The unit teaches the learner to write *as published on 30 September*
  rather than to choose.
- **Unit 11 says in the report itself that it cannot be compared with the report before it.** The
  ILO's coverage estimates changed method, data availability and country revisions, so the rise from
  42.8 to 52.4 per cent is partly real and partly measurement, and the ILO says so. The unit prints
  that sentence and makes the learner write the caveat that has to travel with the figure.
- **Unit 6 is a ranking whose sample is still growing.** B-READY covered 50 economies, then 101, with
  62 more to come and a target of about 170 — or about 180, depending which World Bank page you read.
  The published counts do not reconcile neatly. A country's position cannot be compared with last
  year's while the denominator moves, and the unit's task is to say so in a slide somebody wanted to
  use for exactly that.
- **Unit 7 is a ranking built from 79 indicators across 139 economies**, in which a five-place move
  is well within what a small change in one indicator can produce. The unit prints the top five and
  asks what a five-place rise is evidence of.
- **Unit 8 quotes what the statistics do not contain.** The ICC's 2025 figures give case numbers,
  values, sectors and award counts, and the figure that matters most to anybody deciding whether to
  arbitrate — **how long a case takes** — is not in the material most commentators quote. The unit
  makes that absence the finding.

# Clearline 5 (Book 6) · B2+ to C1

Twelve units, twelve different source bodies, nothing reused from Books 1 to 5. The organizing idea
of this book is **the gap between an instrument and what it can actually do**: a guideline with no
power to compel, an index with margins of error wider than its rankings, a compliance score that
says nothing about effect, a commitment that was removed, a projection with a prediction interval,
and a measure of tax that is not a measure of tax paid.

## Notes on honesty

- **Unit 3 is a measure that is not the thing it is named after.** The OECD's effective tax rates are
  forward-looking policy measures built from the tax code, not a record of tax paid. The unit prints
  the publisher's warning, then makes the learner write the sentence that distinguishes *the rate the
  law implies* from *the money that left the company*, because the two are routinely confused in
  board papers and the confusion always favours whoever is presenting.
- **Unit 5 is the cleanest case in the series of compliance without effect.** Technical compliance
  with the 40 Recommendations improved by roughly twelve percentage points over a decade while the
  average effectiveness of measures stayed low, and the publisher says both. The unit also prints
  the admission that the FATF does not reassess effectiveness after a jurisdiction leaves the grey
  list, so the index **assumes** graduates reached at least a moderate level: an assumption inside a
  score, stated by the people who built it.
- **Unit 6 is a ranking whose own publisher tells you not to rank with it.** A 90% confidence
  interval on a governance estimate is the estimate plus or minus 1.64 standard errors, and the World
  Bank's guidance is to consider those margins when comparing. The unit's task is the sentence
  *these two countries cannot be distinguished on these data*, which is a finding and sounds like a
  failure.
- **Unit 7 is an index its own factsheet says is not a diagnostic tool.** The LPI is a perception
  survey, its weights come out of principal components analysis rather than policy judgement, the
  2023 edition stopped asking respondents about their own country, and the published economy counts
  differ between the index (139) and the data portal (171). The unit keeps the index and refuses the
  diagnosis.
- **Unit 8 is a count of promises and a count of deliveries, and they are not the same count.**
  10,051 companies hold validated targets; 239 had commitments removed in one year for missing a
  24-month deadline; of those, 17 had net-zero targets validated in the following year and a half.
  The unit prints all three and teaches *have yet to*, which is the only honest tense for a promise
  that has not been kept and has not been broken either.
- **Unit 4 prints two different counts of the same year's new cases**, 60 and 57, and explains the
  difference: the smaller figure omits the Additional Facility filings. Neither number is wrong. The
  unit makes the learner write the citation that survives meeting the other one.
- **Unit 9 is one quantity measured two ways, where the two ways diverge for a physical reason.**
  Seaborne trade grew 2.2 per cent in volume while tonne-miles rose 5.9 per cent, because ships went
  round the Cape instead of through the Suez Canal. Nothing about demand explains the gap. The unit's
  sentence is the one that attributes the divergence to distance rather than to trade.
- **Unit 10 is a dispute mechanism with no power to compel anybody.** Agreement was reached in 24 per
  cent of all concluded cases and 57 per cent of mediated ones in 2024, against 73 per cent in 2023
  and 36 per cent in 2022, and the commonest reason for no agreement is that one side refused to take
  part. The unit teaches the modality of soft law — *is expected to*, *is encouraged to*, *is not
  binding on* — and asks what a company owes a process that cannot make it do anything.
- **Unit 12 separates a probabilistic projection from a scenario, and keeps two institutions
  apart.** The population projection carries a published 95 per cent interval of 9.0 to 11.4 billion
  around 2100 and has moved 6 per cent between vintages. The freight outlook is a scenario: "nearly
  double by 2050" in one edition and "a factor of 2.6" in an earlier one, on different baselines and
  different policy assumptions. The unit prints both and refuses to put them in the same table.
