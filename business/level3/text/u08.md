### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A forecast is a sentence with a date on it. The same institution will publish
> a different number three months later, and both will be correct as of their publication. If a
> report quotes a forecast without saying when it was made, the writer has either not noticed or is
> hoping you will not.

%%FIG q08-f5 | A page from a planning paper: a heading, a table of three projections with their publication dates, a line chart with two diverging lines from the same starting point, a boxed paragraph headed what we plan on, and a footer giving the source and the review date.%%

> ## Arvo Components · planning assumptions for next year
> *P. Moreau · 20 March · four pages, this is page 2*
>
> | Published | Projection for next year | Note |
> |---|---|---|
> | June 2026 | **3.1%** | baseline |
> | June 2026 | **1.8%** | alternative scenario: prolonged energy disruption |
> | September 2026 | **3.0%** | current baseline, **revised down** from 3.1% |
>
> The OECD's September interim report projects global growth of **2.9 per cent this year and 3.0
> per cent next year**, with the next-year figure revised down from 3.1 per cent. G20 headline
> inflation is projected at **4.1 per cent this year**, easing to **3.6 per cent next year**. The
> United States is projected at 2.2 and then 2.1 per cent; China at 4.5 and then 4.2.
>
> **What we plan on.** We plan on **3.0 per cent — the September projection — and we review in
> March.** We are not planning on 1.8 per cent. If energy disruption returns, the June alternative
> scenario is the relevant one, and under it we would hold the two Q2 hires and defer the second
> line. That decision is written down now so that it does not have to be invented in a hurry.

> these are projections, not measurements, and the institution itself revised the next-year figure

**1B  Underline the cause and result links. Which fact is doing the most work?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **because / as + clause** | We deferred the hires **because** the scenario changed. |
| **due to / owing to + noun** | **Due to** the revision, we rechecked the plan. |
| **so + clause** | The figure fell, **so** we rechecked the plan. |
| **therefore / as a result** | The figure fell. **As a result**, we rechecked the plan. |
| **which meant + clause** | The figure fell, **which meant** rechecking the plan. |
| **This is why …** | **This is why** we review in March. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Due to* and *owing to* take a noun; *because* takes a clause. "Due to the
> energy prices rose" is one of the commonest errors at B1 and it is always noticed. Fix it either
> by adding a noun — *due to higher energy prices* — or by changing the word — *because energy
> prices rose*.

### 2 · Contrast, and admitting the other side

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| The figure is 3.0 per cent. | The figure is 3.0 per cent, although it was 3.1 in June. |
| We are confident. | We are confident of the direction, however the size is uncertain. |
| The risk is low. | Despite the low probability, the 1.8 per cent case would cost us two hires. |
| Everything is fine. | Nothing has changed in our plan, whereas our assumption has. |

> **Grammar Note** — A report with no contrast links in it is a report that has not examined
> itself. *Although*, *however* and *despite* are the words that show you have read the evidence
> against your own conclusion — and they are what makes a reader trust the rest.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Report writing conventions differ a great deal, and the differences are about where the argument
sits. In some business cultures the recommendation goes first and the reasoning follows, and a
report that makes the reader wait is thought evasive; in others the reasoning must come first and
leading with a conclusion looks arrogant or under-evidenced. Attitudes to hedging differ too: in
some languages and workplaces qualifying a figure is a mark of rigour, and in others it reads as a
lack of confidence or an attempt to escape responsibility. There are also differences in who may be
named: in some organizations a report says "I recommend" and carries a signature, and in others it
says "it is recommended" and carries none, because the institution, not the author, is speaking.

**Discuss:** Where you work, does the recommendation come first or last? Do reports say *I*?

> ### Planning assumptions for next year — summary
> *P. Moreau · 20 March · four pages; this page is the whole argument*
>
> **The finding that matters most.** Our carrier rate, agreed in January, **reopens if fuel moves
> more than eight per cent** in either direction. That is in clause 7.2 of the contract. If it
> reopens, our freight cost assumption for the whole year is void. **I do not know how likely an
> eight per cent move is**, and nobody I have asked does either; what I do know is that it is the
> single thing in this plan that could move most and the only one we have not prepared for.
>
> **What we plan on.** Global growth of **3.0 per cent — the OECD's September projection — and we
> review in March.** It was 3.1 per cent in June. We have chosen the September figure because it is
> the most recent, not because it is the one we prefer.
>
> **What would change it.** The June alternative scenario, in which energy disruption is prolonged,
> gives 1.8 per cent. **Under that scenario we would hold the two Q2 hires and defer the second
> line.** That decision is taken now, in writing, so that it does not have to be taken quickly.
>
> **As a result of all of the above**, I recommend: approve the plan at 3.0 per cent; ask the
> carrier for a fixed rate with a wider band, even at a small premium; and bring the review forward
> to February.
>
> **What this report cannot tell you.** Whether fuel will move eight per cent. Whether the
> September projection will hold. Anything at all about our two largest customers' own plans, which
> I have asked for twice and not been given.

> **Writer's Tip** — Lead with the thing you are least sure about, if it is also the thing that
> matters most. It feels wrong — the instinct is to lead with what you can defend — but a reader
> can act on an honest uncertainty and cannot act on a confident irrelevance.

Write **100 to 140 words**: a one-page summary.

**Use at least two cause or result links and one contrast link. Put a date on every figure. Include
a decision made in advance for one scenario. End with what the report cannot tell you.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Hearing it is the quickest way to find the sentence
that does not work. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Which of you has hedged more? Is hedging honesty or
