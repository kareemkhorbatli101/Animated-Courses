### 1 · *if* with modals

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL3-02-06%%

> **Hanna:** What would ninety days cost you?
> **Supplier:** We'd stop. Our bank lends against thirty-day invoices. It won't lend against
> ninety.
> **Hanna:** … Nobody told me that.
> **Supplier:** Nobody asked. If you need the cash, we could do two percent for fourteen days.
> **Hanna:** That's worth more to us than the ninety was.

*Hanna's question changed the conversation. What was different about it?*

**1B  Underline the conditional forms. Which one is an offer, and which is a consequence?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **if + present, will** | a real, likely result | **If** you fix the price, we **will** sign. |
| **if + present, can / could** | what is possible | **If** you pay in 14 days, we **can** offer two percent. |
| **if + past, would** | hypothetical — testing an idea | **If** you took ninety days, we **would** stop. |
| **what would … ?** | asking the other side to cost it | **What would** ninety days **cost** you? |
| **unless** | if … not | We can't sign **unless** the price holds. |

> **Grammar Note** — *If you take ninety days, we will stop* is a threat. *If you took ninety days,
> we would stop* is arithmetic: the past form makes it hypothetical, which lets both sides examine
> it without either of them committing. In a negotiation that distance is the whole value of the
> second conditional.

### 2 · Trading instead of conceding

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Conceding | Trading |
|---|---|
| All right, sixty days. | Sixty days, as long as the order is firm for twelve months. |
| We'll do two percent. | Two percent if you pay in fourteen days. |
| Fine, we'll absorb the freight. | We'll absorb the freight on full pallets only. |
| Yes, we can bring it forward. | We can bring it forward if we may split the delivery. |

> **Grammar Note** — Never give anything away in a flat sentence. Attach a condition to every
> movement, even a small one. *Yes, if* teaches the other side that there is a price; a bare *yes*
> teaches them there is more where that came from.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Negotiating style varies more than almost anything else in business, and the differences are easy
to misread as rudeness. In some cultures the first price is an opening position and not haggling is
thought careless; in others the first price is the price and a counter-offer is mildly insulting. In
some places a long relationship is itself a concession and asking for a discount puts it at risk;
in others the relationship is what makes hard bargaining safe. Silence after an offer means *I am
considering it* in one country and *you have offended me* in another. And in many cultures the real
negotiation happens before or after the meeting, between two people who already know each other, so
the person across the table may not be the person deciding.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is the first price the price? Who really decides?

> **Subject: Terms — a different question, and two alternatives**
>
> Dear Ms Weber,
>
> I have asked three times for ninety-day terms and you have said no three times, so I am going to

>
> I ask because I may have been solving the wrong problem. What we need is not ninety days as such;

> stop raising it, and we will find the cash elsewhere.
>
> Two alternatives, either of which would work for us:
>
> 1. **Sixty days**, as long as our order is firm for twelve months. That gives you the volume
> certainty you asked for in January.
> 2. **An early-payment discount.** If you would rather have the money sooner, we can pay within
> fourteen days against a discount — we would accept anything from one and a half percent, and
> at two percent I would move our other lines to you as well.
>
> I would rather have the second one than the first, which is the opposite of what my last three
> emails said, and I am telling you that because guessing wastes both our weeks.
>
> **What I am not asking for.** I am not asking you to hold stock, and I am not asking for a price
> reduction on top of either option.
>
> Could we have fifteen minutes on Thursday? If Thursday is difficult, any morning this week.
>
> Kind regards,
> Hanna Lindqvist · Head of contracts · Lindmark Freight

%%FIG q02-f14 | A negotiation in progress in a glass-walled room, seen from outside. Fourteen people in total: four at the table, two on each side, one standing at a flipchart listing three options, two more waiting outside the glass with folders, three at a nearby desk cluster watching, and two at a coffee point talking quietly with a printed chart between them. On the table, two sheets of paper and a calculator.%%

**In your team, answer.**

1. How many people are at the table?
2. What is on the flipchart?
3. Who is waiting outside, and why might they be there?
4. Which of these fourteen people has the real authority, do you think?

### B · Write a team summary

> This is a negotiation. Four people are at the table. If one side moves on price, the other will
> have to move on terms.

Write eight sentences. Make four of them conditional. One person writes, everybody checks.

### C · Report

Read your summary to another team. They must turn one of your sentences into a trade instead of a
concession.

### D · Team project — the deal

| Role | Who | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Buyer** | | wants terms and has a walk-away point |
| **Seller** | | has one hard limit that is real and one that is a tactic |
| **Observer** | | counts concessions on each side and who attached conditions |
| **Presenter** | | reports the deal and what each side gave |

Fifteen minutes. The Observer may not speak until the end, and then must say which side traded and
which side simply gave in.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD CL3-02-16%%

%%FIG q02-f16 | An office late in the day. A signed one-page agreement with a highlighted threshold line. On the screen, a cash-flow chart with a deeper dip in the third week of each month. Beside it, a printed email thread of four messages. Through the window, a lit yard and a parked van.%%

> The discount was agreed at two percent over four thousand units and has saved Lindmark about
> thirty-one thousand in eight months, which is more than ninety-day terms would have been worth.
> Marek is pleased. Two things sit underneath it. The first is that paying in fourteen days means
> Lindmark's own cash leaves earlier every month, and the third week of the month is now tighter
> than it was — which is exactly the problem Hanna was trying to solve, moved rather than fixed.
> The second is that the supplier's bank facility is still only thirty days, and if Lindmark ever
> goes back to normal terms the supplier is in the same position as before, with one customer who
> now knows precisely how fragile they are. Hanna has not told anybody that last part.

1. Was this a good deal?
2. Should Hanna tell her own company how fragile the supplier is?
3. What does a buyer owe a supplier who has shown them their cash-flow chart?
