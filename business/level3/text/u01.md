**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL3-01-06%%

**1B  Underline the two present perfect continuous forms. What does each one tell you?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **present perfect continuous** | it started earlier and still goes on | I'**ve been asking** since Monday. |
| **present perfect simple** | a completed result, no time | I'**ve asked** four people. |
| **past simple** | finished, with a time | I **asked** Paula on Tuesday. |
| **negative** | | We **haven't been getting** answers. |
| **question** | | **How long have** you **been** waiting? |

> **Grammar Note** — Use the **continuous** for the activity and the **simple** for the result.
> *I've been asking* says you are still asking. *I've asked four people* says you have an answer to
> report. In a status update you usually need both: *I've been chasing since Monday and I've had
> two replies.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How much a brief is expected to say varies enormously. In some business cultures a short brief is a
compliment: it says *we trust you to work out the detail*, and asking for a written measurement can
look like a lack of confidence or a search for somebody to blame. In others a project without a
written measurable objective would not be approved at all, and the person who started work on a
four-line brief would be considered at fault. There are also differences in who may define scope:
in some places only the sponsor does, and in others the project manager is expected to write the
definition and have it confirmed. And in many organizations the real brief is the conversation and
the document is a formality — which is fine until the people in that conversation leave.

**Discuss:** Where you work, how detailed is a brief? Who writes down what success means?
