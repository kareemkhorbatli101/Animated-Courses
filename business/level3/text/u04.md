### 1 · *was going to* and *will now*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL2-04-06%%

> **Wei:** We were going to ship on Thursday. The carrier has rolled us.
> **Customer:** When did you know?
> **Wei:** Tuesday, half past eight.

*Wei tells the truth about Tuesday. What would have happened if he had not?*

**1B  Underline the future and past-future forms. Which one carries the apology?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **was going to + verb** | We **were going to** ship on Thursday. |
| **will now + verb** | We **will now** ship on the 21st. |
| **is / are due to** | The vessel **is due to** sail on the 21st. |
| **won't … until** | It **won't** arrive **until** the 28th. |
| **I'll let you know by** | I'**ll let you know by** four. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Was going to* is the most useful form in this unit, because it names the plan
> that has been lost. Customers are rarely angry about a new date; they are angry about discovering
> that the old one disappeared days ago. Say both dates in the same sentence and the conversation
> is about logistics rather than about you.

> **Grammar Note** — An update with no time in it is not an update. The sentence that buys you the

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How bad news travels is one of the sharpest cultural differences in working life. In some
workplaces flagging a risk early is a professional duty and a person who hides a slipping date is
in serious trouble; in others an early warning is read as weakness or as passing a problem upwards,
and you are expected to arrive with the solution. Directness differs too: in some countries "the
sailing has gone" is the correct first sentence, while in others opening with the bad news is
shockingly blunt and the news is placed after context and an apology. There are differences in who
may be told, as well: in some organizations you must inform your own manager before the customer,
and doing it the other way round is a serious error, whatever the customer would prefer.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is early bad news rewarded or punished? Who must be told first?

> Dear Ms Oduya,
>
> **Our Thursday sailing has gone.** The carrier rolled our booking. I knew on Tuesday morning and

> that was my mistake.
>
> **What was going to happen.** Sail Thursday 6th, arrive 13th.
>

>

>
> **What I need from you.** Nothing today. If the 28th is impossible for any of the four remaining
> lines, tell me which and I will price air on those too.
>
> **What happens next.** I will confirm the air booking by 16:00 today and the sailing by 16:00
> Monday. From now on, on this account, you hear from me on the day I know something — including
> the days when what I know is nothing.
>
> Kind regards,
> Wei Zhang · Network planning · Lindmark Freight

> **Writer's Tip** — Four headings and one admission. The headings mean the reader can act after
> line one; the admission — *I knew on Tuesday* — is what stops the next six months being about
> trust instead of freight. Never let somebody else be the one who works out the date you knew.
