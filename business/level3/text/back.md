# Grammar reference

Every structure in this book, in unit order. The second column is the mistake it causes, which is
the column worth reading twice.

## Starting Point · the three habits

| | |
|---|---|
| **A number carries four things** | the figure, the unit, the date, and where it came from |
| ***I don't know, and I'll find out by …*** | the sentence that costs nothing and buys everything |
| **Say the tense you mean** | *we send it* and *we're sending it* are different promises |
| **Review** | present, past, passive, modals, first conditional |

## Unit 1 · Present perfect continuous; *for* and *since*

| | |
|---|---|
| **have / has been + -ing** for an activity still going on | *I've been asking since Monday* |
| ***for*** + a length · ***since*** + a point | *for eleven days* · *since Monday* |
| **State verbs stay simple** | *I'm knowing* · *we're needing* |
| **Continuous for the activity, simple for the result** | a status update needs both |

## Unit 2 · *if* with modals; *unless*, *as long as*

| | |
|---|---|
| **No *would* in the *if* clause** | *if you would take ninety days* |
| ***unless*** = if … not; one word does the work of three | |
| **Second conditional distances a threat** | *if you took ninety days, we would stop* is arithmetic |
| **Attach a condition to everything you give** | a bare *yes* teaches them there is more |

## Unit 3 · Quantifiers; approximation

| | |
|---|---|
| ***fewer*** things you count · ***less*** things you measure | *fewer orders, less delay* |
| ***a few*** = some · ***few*** = hardly any | one is a Tuesday, the other is a success |
| ***percent*** ≠ ***percentage point*** | 13.5 to 20 is 6.5 points and about 48 percent |
| **Round for a slide, exact for the appendix** | and say which you are doing |

## Unit 4 · Future forms for changed plans

| | |
|---|---|
| ***was going to*** names the plan you have lost | say both dates in one sentence |
| ***will now*** · ***is due to*** · ***we expect to*** | three different degrees of certainty |
| **An update with no time in it is not an update** | *I'll come back to you at four, even with nothing* |
| ***by*** a point · ***until*** a stretch | *by Friday* and *until Friday* are different promises |

## Unit 5 · Reported speech

| | |
|---|---|
| present → past · past → past perfect · *will* → *would* | *he said he would call* |
| **Keep the present if it is still true** | *she says the seals are missing* |
| ***asked me to*** + infinitive · ***asked whether*** | *she asked me not to transfer her* |
| **Reported speech is the grammar of being believed** | read the history back, with dates |

## Unit 6 · *by*, *until*, *within*; time prepositions

| | |
|---|---|
| ***by*** at or before · ***until*** up to then · ***within*** inside that time | |
| ***end of day*** is not a time | in four zones it names four moments, two of them past |
| **Every written time carries its zone** | especially in a recurring invitation |
| **Convert the time for the other person** | ninety seconds of work, one fewer missed meeting |

## Unit 7 · Non-defining relative clauses

| | |
|---|---|
| **Commas change the meaning**, not just the punctuation | one coordinator, or several |
| **Not *that*** in a non-defining clause | *Ines, ~~that~~ who handles customs* |
| ***which*** can refer to a whole clause | *nobody replaced him, which is how she took it on* |
| **A reference made of adjectives says nothing** | one fact and one clause beats four paragraphs |

## Unit 8 · Linking: cause and result

| | |
|---|---|
| ***due to*** / ***owing to*** + noun · ***because*** / ***as*** + clause | *due to the energy prices rose* |
| ***so*** · ***therefore*** · ***as a result*** · ***which meant*** | four ways to say the result |
| ***although*** / ***whereas*** + clause · ***despite*** + noun or *-ing* | |
| **A report with no contrast links has not examined itself** | |

## Unit 9 · The second conditional

| | |
|---|---|
| **if + past simple, *would*** | *if the pipe split, we would lose a day* |
| ***were*** for all persons in formal writing | *if the log were read weekly* |
| **if + past perfect, *would have*** | looking back at what you could have known |
| **A risk without a number is a feeling** | money and days, or say you do not know |

## Unit 10 · *used to* and *would*

| | |
|---|---|
| ***used to*** for states and habits · ***would*** for repeated actions only | *he would be the manager* |
| ***didn't use to*** · ***did you use to*** | no *d* in the negative and the question |
| **Habit in *used to*, single event in the past simple** | one mixes into the other and changes the claim |
| **Ask for a record, not a defense** | *what record would show me that?* |

## Unit 11 · Articles

| | |
|---|---|
| **First mention *a*, after that *the*** | the rule that fixes most of the rest |
| **General plurals and uncountables take nothing** | *operators know* · *safety comes first* |
| **Jobs take *a* / *an*** | *she is an engineer* |
| **Articles are never stressed** | stress one and you change the meaning |

## Unit 12 · Present perfect against past simple

| | |
|---|---|
| **Perfect for the result, past simple for the occasion** | *I've halved it; the change went in in February* |
| **A finished time forces the past simple** | *I've reported it on 11 March* |
| ***since*** / ***for*** with the perfect | *it has held since February* |
| **Three sentences for what went wrong** | what happened, what you did, what has changed |

## Five honesty notes, printed here because they are printed in the units too

- **Unit 10 is built on a number that cannot be checked, and that is the lesson.** The figure of
  1,474,118 ISO 9001 certificates comes from secondary reports of the ISO Survey, which is now
  published through IAF CertSearch rather than as a public table. Two of those reports give
  different figures for 2023 — **837,978** and **1,250,243** — a difference of more than 400,000.
  The most likely explanation is a change in how certificates are counted when the data moved
  systems, not a change in the world, but neither the unit nor anybody in it can demonstrate that.
  So the unit does what an auditor does: it prints the figure, prints both baselines, states where
  the chain of evidence breaks, and says in writing that it is not asserting the figure is wrong.
  There is no answer key for this one because there is no answer.
- **Unit 11 is two true sentences that point in opposite directions.** In 2025 the airline accident
  rate fell, from 1.42 to 1.32 per million flights, and the number of people killed rose, from 244
  to 394. Both come from the same IATA report. A rate can improve while a total worsens — fewer
  accidents across more flights, and more people aboard the aircraft that did crash. The unit's task
  is to write one sentence containing both facts, and the *Reader's Tip* says plainly that anybody
  who gives you only one of them is selling you something.
- **Unit 8 is about a forecast that moved while nobody was looking.** The OECD's projection for
  2027 was 3.1 percent in June 2026 and 3.0 percent in September, and June's own alternative
  scenario put it at 1.8 percent. None of those is an error. The unit's model report quotes the
  most recent figure **with its publication date**, names the scenario it is not planning on, and
  writes down in advance what the company would do if that scenario arrived.
- **Unit 6's figure depends entirely on its definition.** Eurostat counts somebody as usually
  working from home if they do so for at least half the days worked in a four-week reference
  period, so a person working two days a week at home is not counted at all. Reports of the same
  release give the EU average as 8.8, 8.9 or 9 percent, and Finland's as 20.5 or 20.9. The unit
  prints "about 9 percent" and teaches the learner to ignore the decimal and interrogate the
  definition.
- **Unit 2 quotes the caveat the ADB printed about its own estimate.** The Bank says the gap may
  partly reflect cyclical factors rather than lack of access, and that no comprehensive dataset of
  trade finance demand and supply exists, so the figure rests on survey modeling rather than on
  counted transactions. The unit prints that sentence beside the figure, because a statistician who
  publishes their own limitations has earned the courtesy of having them quoted.

**Everything else in this book is invented.** Ostberg is not a real city. Lindmark Freight, Arvo
Components, Brightwell Pharma, Corvid Analytics, Juno Textiles, Sable &amp; Finch, Harbour Mutual,
Terrace Hotels, Baptista Metalúrgica and every supplier, carrier and customer named here are not
real companies. Every character, brief, contract, case, register, finding, itinerary and appraisal
in these pages was written for this book. Where a document would in reality depend on the law of a
particular country — notice periods, interview questions, pay transparency, record retention,
expenses, after-hours contact — the book says so and makes finding your own answer the task.

# Closing

You began this book able to get the work done. You end it able to turn "make it better" into
something you can be measured on; to ask what your own request would cost the other side; to put
one figure in front of a room so that they remember it; to say that a date has moved on the day you
know; to take a call about somebody else's mistake and be useful inside a minute; to write a time
that nobody in four countries can misread; to describe a job honestly enough that the right person
applies; to write a page a board can act on without stripping the dates off your figures; to cost a
risk in money and days; to trace a number and say out loud where the chain breaks; to ask a host
what you should have done differently; and to describe a year of your own work including the part
you would rather leave out.

Three sentences to take with you. From Unit 4: **I knew on Tuesday.** From Unit 10: **I cannot
verify this, and here is what I can see.** And from Unit 11, the question almost nobody asks and
almost everybody answers: **what should I have done differently today?**

None of the three is advanced English. All three are hard to say, in any language, and saying them
is most of what people mean when they call somebody professional.
