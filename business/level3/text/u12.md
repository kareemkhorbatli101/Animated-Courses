### 5 · Word study — present perfect against past simple

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG q12-f6 | A chart contrasting the two tenses on a timeline: past simple marked at a single point in the past with a time expression beneath it, and present perfect drawn as an arrow from a point in the past to now, with four paired examples and a note that a finished time expression forces the past simple.%%

| Tense | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **past simple** | a finished event, with a time | The change **went in** in February. |
| **present perfect** | a result that still stands | The rate **has held** every month since. |
| **present perfect** | experience, no time given | I'**ve hosted** two external audits. |
| **past simple** | the detail of one occasion | The 2025 audit **took** three days. |
| **present perfect + since / for** | continuing | I'**ve been** in this role **for** four years. |

> **Grammar Note** — In an appraisal you need both, in this order: *present perfect* for the
> achievement, *past simple* for when and how. "I've cut the deviation rate by half. The change
> went in in February and it has held for thirteen months." Give only the perfect and it sounds
> vague; give only the past and it sounds finished.

**5B  Choose the right tense.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **present perfect — the result** | I'**ve reduced** the deviation rate by half. |
| **past simple — the occasion** | The change **went** in in February. |
| **present perfect + since** | It **has held** **since** February. |
| **past simple + finished time** | I **reported** it on 11 March. |
| **present perfect — experience** | I'**ve hosted** two external audits. |

> **Grammar Note** — A finished time expression — *last year, in March, on the eleventh, in 2024* —
> forces the past simple. *I've reported it on 11 March* is wrong. The present perfect is for
> results with no time attached, or with *since*, *for*, *already*, *yet* and *ever*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Appraisals are among the most culturally specific rituals in working life. In some countries you
are expected to state your achievements plainly and asking for a promotion is normal; in others
self-promotion is unseemly and the correct form is to credit the team and let your manager make the
case for you. Direct criticism varies just as much: in some workplaces a manager says the difficult
thing in the room, and in others it would be delivered through a third person, or implied so
carefully that a foreign employee misses it entirely. Written ratings mean different things —
"meets expectations" is a good outcome in some systems and a warning in others. And in some
cultures discussing pay in the same meeting as performance is standard, while in others the two are
deliberately separated.

**Discuss:** Where you work, do you state your own achievements? Is pay discussed in the same
meeting? **And find out:** does a rating of "meets expectations" help or hurt where you are?

**Use the present perfect for results and the past simple for occasions. Name one objective you did

want.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Hearing it is the quickest way to find the sentence
that does not work. Then check your own against the list, and swap.
