### 5 · Word study — the second conditional

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG q09-f6 | A chart of the second conditional: if plus past simple on the left, would plus infinitive on the right, with four examples and two notes, one saying that were is used for all persons in formal English and one contrasting the first and second conditionals on a likelihood scale.%%

| Pattern | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **if + past simple, would** | unreal or unlikely | **If** the line **stopped**, we **would** lose 90,000. |
| **if + past simple, could / might** | possible result | **If** it **froze** again, we **might** lose a day. |
| **if I were you** | advice | **If I were** you, I'**d** insulate the pipe. |
| **unless + past simple** | if … not | **Unless** we **insulated** it, it would happen again. |
| **would have** (past) | looking back | We **would have** avoided it **if** we **had** asked him. |

> **Grammar Note** — First conditional for things you expect; second for things you are examining.
> *If the pipe freezes, we will lose two days* says you think it will freeze. *If the pipe froze, we
> would lose two days* examines the cost without claiming it is likely — which is exactly what a
> risk register is for.

**1B  Underline the conditional forms. Which ones look back, and which look forward?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **if + past, would** | **If** the pipe **split** again, we **would** lose a day. |
| **if + past, might / could** | **If** it **happened** in July, we **might** not even notice. |
| **if + past perfect, would have** | **If** we **had read** the log, we **would have** known. |
| **were** for all persons | **If** the log **were** read weekly, this **wouldn't** happen. |
| **would + stative** | We **would know** about nine other things. |

> **Grammar Note** — *If I was* is normal in speech and *if I were* is expected in writing and in
> anything formal. In a risk register, use *were*: it signals that you are examining a scenario
> rather than predicting one, which is the difference between a professional document and a
> warning.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Who may raise a risk, and how, differs sharply between workplaces. In some organizations anybody
may stop a job or escalate a concern, and a person who saw a problem and stayed quiet is held
responsible; in others raising a risk outside your own level is a serious breach of order, and the
correct route is to tell your supervisor and leave it there. Written records play different roles
too: in some cultures the log entry is the protection, and in others a written complaint is read as
an accusation and a quiet word is the proper form. There are differences in how uncertainty is
received as well: saying "I don't know what that would cost" is professional in some rooms and
unacceptable in others, where you are expected to produce a number.

**Discuss:** Where you work, who may raise a risk? What happens to somebody who writes it down?
