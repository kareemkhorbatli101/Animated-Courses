> **Reader's Tip** — Before you use a long-horizon figure, ask whether it is probabilistic or
> conditional. A projection answers *how likely?* A scenario answers *what would follow if?* Planning
> against the wrong one is not a small error: it is planning against a question nobody asked.

### 5 · Word study — scenario conditionals

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG t12-f6 | A chart of scenario expressions: under a scenario in which; on current policies; were X to hold; on the assumption that; in the central case; and all else equal — each with one example sentence and a note on what it claims.%%

| Form | Example | Claims |
|---|---|---|
| **under a scenario in which** | **Under a scenario in which** policies tighten, demand is 11% lower. | consequence, not likelihood |
| **on current policies** | **On current policies**, freight activity rises by a factor of 2.6. | consequence of inaction |
| **were X to hold** | **Were the current ambition to hold**, tonne-kilometres would roughly double. | consequence, hypothetical |
| **on the assumption that** | **On the assumption that** fuel prices stay in range … | a named input |
| **in the central case** | **In the central case** the peak falls in the mid-2080s. | the likeliest of a set |
| **all else equal** | **All else equal**, a longer route raises cost per tonne. | one variable isolated |

> **Grammar Note** — Scenario sentences use *would*, not *will*: *were the scenario to hold,
> tonne-kilometres would double*. Writing *will* converts a conditional consequence into a
> prediction, which is the single commonest way a scenario becomes a forecast in somebody's slide.
> Note also the inverted form *were X to hold*, with no *if*, which is the standard register for this
> in formal writing.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence as a scenario conditional with the form in brackets.**

1. If policies tighten, demand falls 11 per cent. *(under a scenario in which)*
2. If nothing changes, activity rises 2.6 times. *(on current policies)*
3. If the current ambition holds, tonne-kilometres double. *(were X to hold, with would)*
4. The peak is in the mid-2080s, most likely. *(in the central case)*

| Pattern | Example |

### 2 · Projections, scenarios and the word *will*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Confuses the two | Keeps them apart |
|---|---|
| Freight will double by 2050. | **Were the current ambition to hold**, freight **would** roughly double by 2050. |
| Population will be 10.2 billion in 2100. | Population **is projected at about 10.2 billion** in 2100, **with a 95% interval of 9.0 to 11.4**. |
| The outlook says demand rises 2.6 times. | **The 2021 edition**, **on current policies**, has activity rising by a factor of 2.6. |
| The projection was wrong by 6%. | The 2100 figure **has been revised** about 6% between editions. |
| Policy will reduce demand 11%. | **Under a high-ambition scenario**, 2050 demand **would be** about 11% lower. |

> **Grammar Note** — *The projection was wrong* is almost always the wrong sentence for a long-horizon
> figure, because nothing has happened yet. What has happened is a revision between vintages, and
> *has been revised* reports it without implying error. Keeping that distinction is what allows you
> to go on using the series after it moves.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Planning horizons differ enormously, and so does the status of a long-horizon number. Family-owned
and state-owned organizations frequently plan over decades as a matter of course and treat a
twenty-five-year figure as a serious object; listed companies answer to a reporting rhythm that makes
anything beyond three years difficult to fund, so long-horizon work is often done to satisfy an
external requirement rather than to guide a decision. There are differences in how uncertainty may be
expressed in public: in some business cultures a published range is read as rigour, and in others as
an admission that the organization does not know, which is held to be a failure of leadership. And
where a government publishes its own long-term projections, a company that uses a different vintage
or a different scenario may be making a political statement without intending one.

**Discuss:** How far ahead does your organization plan? Is a published range a strength or a
weakness there?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Network strategy brief — the 2050 question
> *W. Zhang · 28 November · one page*
>
> **Section 1 — the projection. Probabilistic; no policy in it.** World population stood at **8.2
> billion in 2024** and **is projected to peak at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s**, easing to
> **about 10.2 billion by 2100**, **with a published 95% interval around 2100 of 9.0 to 11.4
> billion** — a range of **2.4 billion**, more than a quarter of today's population. **The 2100
> figure has been revised about 6% lower — some 700 million fewer people — than the projection of a
> decade ago. That is a revision between vintages, not an error**, and it is the normal behavior of
> the instrument.
>
> **Section 2 — the scenarios. Conditional; policy in them, no probability.** **Were the current
> ambition scenario to hold**, worldwide freight tonne-kilometres **would roughly double between 2019
> and 2050**. **On current policies**, an **earlier edition** of the same outlook has freight activity
> rising **by a factor of 2.6** by 2050. **Under a high-ambition regional scenario**, 2050 demand
> **would be about 11% lower** than the current trajectory.
>
> **Why these three may not be combined.** **Different editions, different baseline years, different
> policy assumptions.** **There is no average of them that means anything.** And the 11% is the
> point: **a figure that policy can move by 11% is, by construction, not a prediction.**
>
> **What each instrument should decide.** **Capacity against the interval** — how much to build, and
> how reversibly. A berth is a 40-year asset and a crane a 15-year one; an interval 2.4 billion wide
> says build the 15-year things and lease the 40-year ones wherever we can. **Policy exposure against
> the scenarios** — which three assumptions we are betting on, and which of them are held by
> governments rather than by us.
>
> **What I am not providing, and why.** **A single 2050 figure.** **Entered once, it would be quoted
> for twenty-five years without its interval and without its scenario**, by people who were not in the
> room, and in 2035 somebody would tell the board that the strategy assumed it. **I would rather be
> asked for it twice than supply it once.**
>
> **Review.** The population projection is revised on a published cycle and the outlook publishes
> new editions; **I will bring the next vintage of each to the board rather than wait to be asked**,
> and I will say what moved.
>
> *Sources: UN DESA, World Population Prospects 2024, for the population figures and the interval.
> ITF Transport Outlook, 2023 and 2021 editions, for the freight scenarios — two institutions, kept
> separate because they are separate.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Write the sentence that says what you are not providing, and the reason, in the
> document itself. A blank with an explanation is a decision; a blank without one is an omission, and
> the next person to open the file will fill it. This is the last Writer's Tip in the series and it is
> the same point as the first one: the qualification travels with the figure, or somebody else carries
> it for you.
