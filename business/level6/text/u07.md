Lindmark's standard proposal has a page headed "corridor reliability" and the page quotes an
international logistics index. The index is a serious piece of work. Its international edition
covers a hundred and thirty-nine economies across six (1) ______________ , it is built from

the weights that combine the six are derived by principal components analysis rather than chosen by
hand. The 2023 edition stopped asking respondents about their own country, which removes a known
bias, and added separate indicators of the actual speed of trade. None of this is the problem. The
problem is in the publisher's own factsheet, which states that the index is **not** a
(2) ______________ tool but a general metric about the efficiency of supply chains. Lindmark's
proposals use it as a diagnostic: they quote a national score and promise a corridor. Mei's
objection has three parts and the third is the one that will matter if anybody ever litigates. First,
a national score is an average over a country and the corridor Lindmark sells runs through one port
and one inland crossing. Second, the index is a (3) ______________ measure: it records what
professionals believe about a country, which is informative and is not a record of what happened to
any consignment. Third, Lindmark has the direct data. Two years of clearance times for its own
consignments on that corridor, measured in hours, consignment by consignment, with the variance
visible. That is a worse-looking number and a better promise, because it describes the thing the
customer is buying. There is also a small citation trap in the material. The index's own edition
covers a hundred and thirty-nine economies, while the publisher's data portal lists a hundred and
seventy-one economies for the overall score series across all years, and published (4)

### 5 · Word study — purpose and fitness clauses

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG t07-f6 | A chart of purpose expressions: designed to, intended as, for the purpose of, with a view to, so as to, serves as a proxy for, and is not designed to — each with one example sentence and a note on whether it describes design or use.%%

| Form | Example | Describes |
|---|---|---|
| **is designed to** | The index **is designed to** benchmark national environments. | design |
| **is intended as** | It **is intended as** a general metric. | design |
| **is not designed to** | It **is not designed to** predict one corridor. | design, negative |
| **for the purpose of** | We cite it **for the purpose of** country comparison. | use |
| **with a view to** | We collected clearance times **with a view to** pricing. | use, forward |
| **so as to** | We publish both counts **so as to** avoid a reconciliation question. | use |
| **serves as a proxy for** | The score **serves as a proxy for** data we do not have. | use |

> **Grammar Note** — *So as to* and *in order to* both express purpose and both take an infinitive;
> *so as not to* is the usual negative — *so as not to overstate the claim* — where *in order not to*
> is also possible but heavier. Do not write *for to*: in English purpose takes either *to* alone or
> *for* plus a noun or *-ing* form, never both.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence with the form in brackets.**

1. The index benchmarks countries; that is what it is for. *(is designed to)*
2. It cannot predict one route. *(is not designed to)*
3. We print both counts to prevent a question. *(so as to)*
4. The score stands in for data we do not hold. *(serves as a proxy for)*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **is designed to / was designed to** | The index **was designed to** compare countries. |
| **is not designed to** | It **is not designed to** set a service level. |
| **is intended as / was never intended as** | It **was never intended as** a diagnostic tool. |
| **for the purpose of + noun/-ing** | cited **for the purpose of** country comparison |
| **with a view to + -ing** | collected **with a view to** pricing the route |
| **so as to / so as not to** | printed **so as not to** invite a reconciliation question |

> **Grammar Note** — *With a view to* always takes an *-ing* form: *with a view to pricing*, never
> *with a view to price*. *For the purpose of* takes a noun or an *-ing* form in the same way. The
> infinitive belongs with *designed to*, *intended to*, *in order to* and *so as to*. Mixing the two
> patterns is the single commonest error in formal purpose clauses.

> **Grammar Note** — When you replace a proxy with a direct measure, give the spread as well as the

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What may be promised in a commercial proposal is regulated differently from place to place, and so
is the expectation behind a number. In some markets a published service level is understood as a
target and nobody is surprised when it is missed; in others the same sentence is read as a
contractual commitment and a missed figure is a breach. Procurement culture varies too: some
customers run formal verification, demand the underlying data and will exclude a bidder whose
figures cannot be reproduced, while others treat proposals as persuasion and expect optimism. There
are also differences in how national rankings are received by the country concerned — a proposal
quoting a low score for a customer's own country can cause real offense, even where the score is
accurate and publicly available.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is a figure in a proposal a target or a promise? Who decides?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Corridor reliability — proposal page, revised
> *M. Chow · 24 January*
>
> **What we measure, and over what.** Clearance at the port of entry for **our own consignments** on
> this corridor, **1,480 consignments over 24 months**, measured in hours from arrival to release.
>
> | | Hours |
> |---|---|
> | Median | **31** |
> | 75th percentile | **48** |
> | **90th percentile** | **96** |
> | Worst month in the period (July last year), median | **74** |
>
> **Why we print the tail.** **The customer is planning around the bad week, not the median week.** A
> median alone would be a proxy for the distribution, and **a proxy is what we are replacing**.
>
> **The country benchmark, used for the purpose it was designed for.** The Logistics Performance
> Index places this economy in the upper band of the **139 economies** it covers, across **six
> components**, weighted by principal components analysis, from perception ratings by logistics
> professionals rating up to **eight overseas markets**. **It was designed to benchmark national
> logistics environments. It was not designed to predict the performance of one corridor**, and its
> own factsheet states that it *"is not considered a diagnostic tool, but rather a general metric
> about the efficiency of supply chains."*
>
> **What we therefore do not claim.** We do not claim a clearance window on the strength of a national
> score. **The only figures in this page that describe your consignments are ours**, and every one of
> them can be reproduced from our records on request.
>
> **A note on the published counts.** The index's own edition covers **139 economies**; the
> publisher's data portal lists **171** for the overall score series across all years, and published
> respondent counts differ between sources (**652 professionals in 115 countries** in one, **nearly
> 1,000** in another). **We print this so as not to be asked about it later**; none of it affects the
> figures above, which are our own.
>
> *Source: World Bank, Connecting to Compete 2023, its factsheet and its data portal, for the index
> description and the quoted statement of purpose. All clearance figures are Lindmark's own.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Replace a proxy with a direct measure **and its spread**, and say why you are
> printing the spread. A median on its own is a new proxy wearing better clothes, and the reader who
> plans on it will be disappointed in the same month every year. Printing the ninetieth percentile
> costs you the easy sentence and buys you the renewal.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

3. The sample is (115 / 1,480) consignments.
4. The index covers (139 / 171) economies in its own edition.
