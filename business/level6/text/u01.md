### 5 · Word study — extraposition and *it*-structures

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG t01-f6 | A chart of it-structures grouped by function: allocating a duty with it is for and it falls to; leaving something open with it remains to be seen and it is not yet clear; blocking an inference with it should not be inferred; and evaluating with it would be wrong to — each with one example sentence.%%

| Function | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Allocate a duty** | it is for X to · it falls to X to | **It is for the board to** approve the budget. |
| **Leave open** | it remains for X to · it is not yet clear whether | **It remains for** the committee **to** decide how. |
| **Block an inference** | it should not be inferred that · it does not follow that | **It should not be inferred that** a target is a quota. |
| **Evaluate** | it would be wrong to · it is open to the board to | **It would be wrong to** read 29 as a target. |
| **Report a position** | it is the case that · it is accepted that | **It is accepted that** chair representation lags. |
| **Postpone a long subject** | it is … that/to | **It is** surprising **that** no jurisdiction requires it. |

> **Grammar Note** — Extraposition moves a heavy subject to the end and puts *it* in its place:
> *That no jurisdiction requires it is surprising* becomes *It is surprising that no jurisdiction
> requires it.* English strongly prefers the second, because a long subject before the verb makes a
> reader hold too much in mind. In governance writing *it is for X to* is the standard formula for
> allocating a duty, and its great virtue is that the duty cannot be written without naming X.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence using the structure in brackets.**

3. A target is not a quota. *(it should not be inferred that)*
4. That the chair figure lags is accepted. *(extrapose it)*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **it is for X to + verb** | **It is for the board to** appoint the managing director. |
| **it falls to X to + verb** | **It falls to** management **to** settle claims below 500,000. |
| **it remains for X to + verb** | **It remains for** the committee **to** source candidates. |
| **it is open to X to + verb** | **It is open to** any director **to** require a vote. |
| **it is not for X to + verb** | **It is not for** this document **to** set an executive target. |
| **it should not be inferred that** | **It should not be inferred that** a target is a quota. |

> **Grammar Note** — *It is for X to* allocates a duty. *It is open to X to* grants a right. *It is
> not for X to* excludes X from the question entirely. These three are not stylistic variants: in a
> dispute two years later they produce three different answers, and nobody will reread the
> paragraph they sit in.

### 2 · The impersonal sentence, and what it hides

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Hides the actor | Names the actor |
|---|---|
| A decision will be taken. | **It is for the board to** decide. |
| Approval will be obtained. | **The managing director will obtain** approval. |
| The matter shall be considered. | **The audit committee shall consider** the matter. |
| It is felt that the risk is low. | **The risk committee considers** the risk low. |
| Steps will be taken. | **Operations will** replace the seals **by 30 April**. |

> **Grammar Note** — The agentless passive is not always evasion: *the figure was revised* is right
> when the reviser is irrelevant. It becomes evasion the moment the sentence imposes a duty, because
> a duty with no bearer is unenforceable. The test is one question: **if this does not happen, who
> has failed?** If the sentence cannot answer, rewrite it.

### %%AUG c%% 2 · Clear English — allocating a duty out loud

**2A  Listen to the model.** %%AUD CL6-01-09%%

> *It's for the board.* — the stress falls on **board**, not on *for*
> *That one falls to me.* — short, flat, and it ends the question
> *It isn't for me to decide that.* — the stress is on **me**, and the sentence is a boundary

In speech, *it is for* and *it falls to* do the work that a job title cannot. Saying *that one falls
to me* in a meeting is heard as taking responsibility; saying *that's my area* is heard as claiming
territory, and the two sentences mean almost the same thing.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Board structures differ in kind, not only in degree, and a document written for one will not work in
another. In some jurisdictions a single board holds both supervision and management, so a
non-executive director sits beside the executives they oversee; in others the two sit in separate
bodies that meet separately and whose members may not overlap at all, which changes what a reserved
matter even means. Employee representation on the supervisory body is mandatory in several
countries, normal in some and unknown elsewhere. Shareholder votes on pay are binding in some
jurisdictions and advisory in others, and about three quarters of jurisdictions require one of the
two. There are differences in register too: in some business cultures a document that names who acts
reads as professional, and in others as an accusation waiting to be made.

**Discuss:** How is your board structured? Would a document that names a person by role be read as
clarity or as blame?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Board terms of reference — reserved matters and delegation
> *Y. Haddad with G. Mensah · 11 February · extract*
>
> **Reserved to the board.** **It is for the board to** approve the annual budget; to approve any
> commitment exceeding **2,000,000 euros**; to appoint and remove the managing director; to approve
> the remuneration policy; and to approve any related-party transaction of any size.
>
> **Delegated.** **It falls to the managing director to** approve commitments below 2,000,000 euros,
> to settle claims below **500,000 euros**, and to set terms of employment below executive level —
> **in each case reporting to the board at its next scheduled meeting**, which is the obligation that
> makes the delegation safe.
>
> **Board composition target, and the argument for it.** The board adopts a target of **30% women
> among its own members by 2029**. **It should not be inferred that this figure derives from the
> 52-jurisdiction average of 29%**; an average is not an argument, and had the average been 40% we
> would not have proposed 40%. The argument is our own: the long lists for our last four board
> searches were **38% women**, so 30% is reachable from the candidate pool we can address. **It would
> be wrong to build a nine-year target on four searches**, which is why the figure is 30 and not 38,
> and why **it falls to the nomination committee to report the long-list composition of every search
> to the board**, so that the basis of the target is tested as it goes.
>
> **What this document does not decide.** **It remains for the nomination committee to** determine
> how candidates are sourced. **It is not for this document to** set any target for the executive
> team, whose candidate pool differs and which will be addressed separately.
>
> **What should not be inferred.** **It should not be inferred that** a target is a quota: nothing
> here obliges the board to appoint a candidate it judges less suitable. **It should not be inferred
> that** the company is subject to a disclosure obligation: **65%** of surveyed jurisdictions require
> listed companies to disclose board gender composition and **we are not listed**, so we disclose by
> choice and say so.
>
> **One figure we are not quoting.** The survey reports that representation in **board chair**
> positions lags behind the board average. **It does not give a figure we can quote in this form, so
> none appears here.**
>
> **Review.** **It falls to the general counsel to** bring this document back to the board in
> February 2028.
>
> *Source: OECD, Corporate Governance Factbook 2025, 52 jurisdictions, for all comparative figures.
> The 38% long-list figure is Lindmark's own, covers four searches, and is a small sample.*

> paragraph and remove the three arguments you can most easily predict.
