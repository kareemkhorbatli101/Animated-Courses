### 5 · Word study — *have yet to* and the perfect aspect

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG t08-f6 | A chart of non-delivery forms arranged by what they leave open: have yet to, has not yet, has still not, never did, and is no longer expected to — each with one example sentence and a note on whether the possibility remains open.%%

| Form | Example | Leaves it open? |
|---|---|---|
| **have yet to + bare verb** | We **have yet to submit**. | yes — expected, not done |
| **has not yet + participle** | We **have not yet submitted**. | yes, slightly flatter |
| **has still not + participle** | They **have still not submitted**. | yes, with impatience |
| **has since been + participle** | The commitment **has since been removed**. | reports what followed |
| **had not been + participle by** | **Had not been validated by** July. | closes at a stated date |
| **did not + bare verb** | They **did not submit**. | closed; finished |

> **Grammar Note** — *Have yet to* is the only one of these that is both accurate and neutral about
> blame. It states that the thing has not happened and that it is still expected, which is exactly
> the position of most unfinished commitments. Note the form: *have yet to **submit***, never *have
> yet to submitting* and never *have yet submitted*.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence with the form in brackets.**

3. The target was not validated before July. *(had not been … by)*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **have yet to + bare verb** | We **have yet to submit** the targets. |
| **has been + participle** (record) | The submission **has been drafted**. |
| **remains + adjective** | Scope 3 data **remains incomplete**. |
| **has since been + participle** | The commitment **has since been removed**. |
| **as at + date, had + participle** | **As at** July, **17 had had** targets validated. |
| **falls due / closes** | The window **closes** in nine weeks. |

> **Grammar Note** — Use the present perfect for the record so far — *the submission has been
> drafted* — and the past perfect with *as at* for a position at a stated earlier date — *as at July,
> 17 had been validated*. Mixing them produces a timeline nobody can follow, which is why status
> reports so often contain sentences that are each true and together meaningless.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How a missed commitment is treated varies enormously. In some business cultures a public status
showing non-delivery is a serious reputational event and companies will submit incomplete work to
avoid it; in others the same status is regarded as ordinary and nobody outside a specialist team
notices. Internally the differences are larger still: some organizations expect status reports to be
optimistic as a matter of courtesy, so that a report saying *we have yet to submit* is read as
disloyal, while in others any report containing the phrase *on track* without a date and a measure
would be sent back. There are also differences in who owns a commitment — where sustainability or
compliance commitments are made by a central function and delivered by business lines, the function
that reports the failure is rarely the one that caused it.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is a status report expected to be optimistic? What happens to the person
who writes an accurate one?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Board paper — our emissions target commitment
> *P. Raman · 3 October · one page*
>
> **Two counts, and the difference between them.** The initiative's dashboard lists **10,051
> companies with validated targets** and **12,647 with targets or commitments**. **The difference is
> about 2,600 organizations that have promised and have yet to deliver.**
>
> **The published rule, in the body's own terms.** A company that does not submit targets within **24
> months** of its commitment is given the status **"commitment removed"**. The status is **published**
> and **reversible**: a company **returns to validated status on successful validation of its
> targets**.
>
> **What happened to those who missed.** In one year **239 major global companies had commitments
> removed**. **As at July of the following year, 17 of the 239 had had net-zero targets validated
> since removal, and 14 had had near-term targets validated.** **For most of the 239, removal has
> not so far been a short administrative interruption.**
>
> **Our own position.** Lindmark **committed 22 months ago**. The submission **has been drafted**.
> Scope 3 data **remains incomplete** for **two of our four business lines**. **We have yet to
> submit.** The window **closes in nine weeks**.
>
> **Three options.** **Submit on current data** — near-term targets only, two lines estimated;
> expect queries and possible rejection. **Request additional time**, if the policy permits; status
> uncertain in the interval. **Allow removal and resubmit later** — the status becomes published, and
> **17 of 239 is the benchmark for what follows**.
>
> **My recommendation, and the judgment in it.** Submit within the window with the two estimated
> lines flagged as estimated. **I would rather be rejected on a submission than removed on a
> silence**, and I record that this is a judgment and not a calculation.
>
> **What I will not write.** **I will not describe this project as "on track."** *On track* means the
> dates will be met on current performance. They will not. A paper that said otherwise would make
> every other sentence in it worth less, including the ones the board needs.
>
> *Source: Science Based Targets initiative target dashboard and Trend Tracker, and its commitment
> and target statuses document, for the counts, the 24-month window and the removal and reinstatement
> rules.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Write the sentence beginning *what I will not write*. It costs two lines, it
> converts a reporting choice into a visible decision, and it is the only way a reader can tell the
> difference between a status report that is cautious and one that has been managed. Boards remember
> who wrote it.

### 3 · Write

Write **150 to 190 words**: a status paper on an undelivered commitment.

**Give the promise count and the delivery count if you have them. State the rule and its
consequence. Report what happened to those who missed, with an *as at* date. State your own position
using *have yet to* and at least two perfect forms. Give options with consequences. Say what you will
not write.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Reading *we have yet to* aloud is the quickest way
to find out whether you have softened it into something else. Then check your own against the list,
and swap.

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's paper as a board member who wants to approve a budget and move

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you write *on track*? Say what it would
have to mean, with a date and a measure, and decide whether it is true. **What do you think should
happen** to somebody who writes it when it is not? And why does almost everybody write it anyway?
