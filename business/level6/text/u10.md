### 5 · Word study — the modality of soft law

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG t10-f6 | A chart of modal expressions arranged from strongest to weakest: is required to, shall, is expected to, should, is encouraged to, is invited to, and may — each with one example sentence and a note on whether it creates an obligation.%%

| Form | Example | Creates an obligation? |
|---|---|---|
| **is required to / shall** | The employer **shall** provide rest breaks. | yes, in law |
| **is expected to** | Enterprises **are expected to** engage in good faith. | no — a standard of conduct |
| **should** | Enterprises **should** respect human rights. | no — normative, not enforceable |
| **is encouraged to** | Parties **are encouraged to** use mediation. | no — weaker than *should* |
| **is invited to** | The company **is invited to** attend on 4 February. | no — a request |
| **may** | The contact point **may** publish recommendations. | a power, not a duty |
| **has no power to** | The contact point **has no power to** compel attendance. | an express limit |

> **Grammar Note** — *Shall* creates a duty; *should* states a norm; *is expected to* states a norm
> and names who holds the expectation. In an instrument these are chosen deliberately and never
> interchangeable, and in a company's own documents the same distinctions apply: writing *suppliers
> should* where you mean *suppliers shall* gives away a contractual right in one word.

**5B  Rewrite each sentence with the form in brackets, and say whether an obligation results.**

1. Enterprises engage in good faith. *(are expected to)*
2. The contact point publishes a statement. *(may)*
3. The employer provides rest breaks. *(shall)*
4. The contact point cannot compel attendance. *(has no power to)*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **has no power to + verb** | The body **has no power to** order disclosure. |
| **is expected to + verb** | Enterprises **are expected to** engage in good faith. |
| **is not obliged to + verb** | We **are not obliged to** attend. |
| **is invited to + verb** | We **are invited to** attend on 4 February. |
| **may + verb** (power) | The body **may** publish recommendations. |
| **is binding on / is not binding on** | The guidelines **are not binding on** us. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Not obliged to* and *not expected to* are completely different positions:
> the first means nobody can make you, the second means nobody thinks you should. In a voluntary
> process you are usually *expected to* and *not obliged to* at the same time, and a company that
> hears only the second half of that sentence tends to be surprised by the published statement.

### 2 · *Shall*, *will*, *should* and *may* in an instrument

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Word | In an instrument it means |
|---|---|
| **shall** | a duty on the named party |
| **will** | a statement of what is going to happen, often the drafter's own side |
| **should** | a norm, not enforceable |
| **may** | a permission or a power |
| **must** | a duty, commonly used for rules addressed to everybody |
| **is to** | a duty, slightly softer than *shall*, common in modern drafting |

> **Grammar Note** — The dangerous one is *will*. *The supplier will provide monthly reports* reads
> like a duty and in some drafting traditions is treated as a mere prediction. If you mean a duty,
> write *shall* or *must*; if you are describing your own future conduct, *will* is right and carries
> no obligation on the other side.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Voluntary mechanisms work very differently depending on who takes them seriously locally. In some
countries a published statement from a national contact point is covered by the press, raised by
investors and asked about in tenders, so declining to engage has real commercial consequences; in
others the same statement is read by almost nobody and the mechanism is close to dormant. There are
differences in who files: in some jurisdictions complaints are brought mainly by trade unions, in
others by non-governmental organizations, and the identity of the filer changes what engagement
looks like. And companies themselves differ: some treat any invitation to a voluntary process as a
matter for the board, and others as a matter for a communications team, which is usually a mistake
when the underlying allegation concerns compliance with somebody's actual law.

**Discuss:** Where you work, would a published statement be noticed? By whom?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Note to the board — the specific instance
> *H. Lindqvist · 12 November · one page*
>
> **What is alleged.** **It is alleged that** three shifts of agency staff at a subcontracted
> warehouse worked without the rest breaks required by the local law of that country. The complaint
> **has been filed** with a national contact point under an international set of guidelines for
> responsible business conduct.
>
> **What the body may do.** Decide whether to **accept** the complaint for further examination; offer
> **good offices** and, with both parties' agreement, **mediate**; **publish a final statement**;
> make **recommendations**.
>
> **What the body may not do.** It **has no power to compel** our attendance, to **order** disclosure,
> to **award** anything, or to **enforce** anything in any court. **The guidelines are non-binding.
> We are expected to engage in good faith. We are not obliged to.**
>
> **Published outcomes, three years, unaveraged.** Most recent year: **52 cases closed**, of which
> **32 (58%)** had been accepted for further examination; agreement in **24% of all concluded cases**
> and **57% of concluded cases where mediation occurred**. Previous year: **73%** of mediated cases.
> Year before: **36%**, on **41** instances closed. **These are samples of a few dozen cases moving by
> nearly 40 points. I have not averaged them and I would not plan on any one of them.**
>
> **The one figure I would rely on.** **The commonest reason concluded cases reached no agreement was
> that one party refused to engage — 64% of the cases without agreement.** Refusal is permitted and
> is the largest single cause of failure. **If we decline, we are not avoiding a risk; we are electing
> to be that 64%.**
>
> **What the final statement will say.** **If we engage:** that Lindmark participated, what was
> discussed, and whether agreement was reached. **If we decline:** that Lindmark declined to engage.
> **There is no version in which no statement is published.**
>
> **The part that is not about this process at all.** **The guidelines are not law. The rest breaks
> are.** If agency staff worked without legally required breaks at a site we subcontract, that is a
> compliance matter and not a reputational one, and it exists whether or not we attend in February.
> The agency's records **are incomplete for two of the three shifts**.
>
> **Recommendation.** Establish the facts, beginning with the agency's records, as the first item.
> **Attend the meeting — and not in order to manage the statement.** Attend because the fastest route
> to the facts is a room containing the people who filed the complaint.
>
> *Source: OECD annual reports on the activity of National Contact Points for Responsible Business
> Conduct and the accompanying data annexes.*

> **Writer's Tip** — End a note about a voluntary process with the part that is not voluntary. Readers
> who have just been told that nothing can be enforced will file the whole matter under reputation,
> and the sentence *the guidelines are not law; the rest breaks are* is the one that stops them. Put
> it last, where it is remembered, not first, where it is read as an introduction.

### 3 · Write

Write **150 to 190 words**: a note on a process that cannot compel you.

**Put the allegation in the pleading register. Give what the body may and may not do. State the
instrument's non-binding character and the expectation alongside it. Give published outcome figures
with sample sizes and do not average them. Say what will be published either way. End with the part
that is not voluntary.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Reading *we are not obliged to* aloud is how you
hear whether you stopped there. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's note as a director who wants to hear that nothing can be
enforced. Where does the note stop you?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you treat the matter as reputational? Say
the two-sentence version — the instrument, then the law — and decide which comes first in your note.
**Why do you think** a voluntary process gets taken more seriously in some organizations than in
others? What would make yours take it seriously?
