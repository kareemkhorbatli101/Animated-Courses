**2C  Complete the paragraph.**

The slide is already written and scheduled, and the figure on it is correct. A well-known annual
barometer reports trust in "my employer" at seventy-eight per cent among employees, which is ahead
of business at sixty-four, non-governmental organizations at fifty-eight, media at fifty-four and
government at fifty-three; employers are trusted in twenty-seven of the twenty-eight countries
measured. Nour has three objections and none of them is that the figure is wrong. The first is the
(1) ______________ : that question was asked only of people who are employed, so seventy-eight per
cent is not a statement about the population of any country, and a slide that drops the word
*employees* turns a precise finding into a vague one. The second is attribution: the study is
(2) ______________ by a communications firm, conducted in thirty-minute online interviews over four
weeks, and reports what people say about themselves. That does not make it unreliable, and it does
mean the sentence has to name who produced it and how, because a reader who finds that out later
will decide you were hiding it. The third is the one that actually matters internally. The survey
measures trust in *my employer* across twenty-eight countries. It says nothing whatsoever about
Lindmark. Putting it on an internal slide invites four hundred people to read it as a claim about
this company, and if their own experience differs they will conclude either that the figure is false
or that management believes something untrue about them — and the second is worse. Nour's rewrite
keeps the figure, names the source, states the base, and then does the thing the slide was avoiding:
it prints Lindmark's own staff survey figure beside it, which is sixty-one per cent, and says that
the difference is seventeen points and that nobody has yet asked why. The barometer's other finding
has gone onto the slide too, because it explains more than the headline does: seven in ten
respondents report being unwilling or hesitant to (3) ______________ somebody with different values,
background or sources of information, and two thirds of employees worry that trade policies and
tariffs will hurt their employer. Those two sentences describe the mood Lindmark's own figure sits
inside, and neither is a compliment to anybody. Nour's position is that a communication which quotes
a flattering external average and hides an unflattering internal number is not a communication but
an advertisement, and that people can (4) ______________ the difference in about four seconds.

### 5 · Word study — attribution and evidence

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG t02-f6 | A chart of attribution forms arranged from strongest to weakest claim: X reports that, according to X, on X's own account, X is said to, reportedly, and it is claimed that — each with one example sentence and a note on how much the writer is taking on.%%

| Form | Example | What the writer takes on |
|---|---|---|
| **X reports that** | The barometer **reports that** trust stands at 78%. | nothing beyond accuracy of quotation |
| **according to X** | **According to** the barometer, trust stands at 78%. | the same, slightly more distant |
| **on X's own account** | **On the publisher's own account**, the base is employees. | signals it is their claim about themselves |
| **as measured by X** | trust **as measured by** a 28-country online survey | names the instrument, not just the source |
| **X is said to** | The index **is said to** be comparable. | distance, often doubt |
| **reportedly / it is claimed that** | Trust has **reportedly** risen. | clear doubt; unsuitable for a board paper |

> **Grammar Note** — *Reportedly* and *it is claimed that* carry audible doubt, which makes them
> dishonest when you have no doubt and merely want distance. If you believe a figure, write *X
> reports that* and name X. If you do not believe it, say why. The one thing you may not do is
> borrow the authority of a figure while declining responsibility for it.

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **X reports that + clause** | The barometer **reports that** employers are trusted in 27 of 28 countries. |
| **according to X, …** | **According to** the barometer, business stands at 64%. |
| **on a base of + noun** | 78%, **on a base of** employees only. |
| **as measured by + noun** | trust **as measured by** a 30-minute online interview |
| **on X's own account** | **On the publisher's own account**, the figures are self-reported. |
| **X says nothing about Y** | The barometer **says nothing about** Lindmark. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Says nothing about* is the most useful sentence in this unit and the one
> learners never produce. It is not a criticism of the source: a survey of twenty-eight countries is
> not supposed to say anything about one company. Writing the sentence protects the source and the
> reader at the same time.

### 2 · Restrictive and non-restrictive modification

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Sentence | What it means |
|---|---|
| Employees **who trust their employer** stay longer. | **restrictive** — only those employees |
| Employees, **who trust their employer**, stay longer. | **non-restrictive** — all employees do both |
| 78% **of employees** trust their employer. | the base is employees |
| 78% **of people** trust their employer. | a different and false claim |
| The figure, **which is self-reported**, stands at 78%. | adds information about the one figure |
| The figures **that are self-reported** are in the annex. | distinguishes some figures from others |

> **Grammar Note** — Two commas change the meaning completely, and in English the non-restrictive
> version makes a claim about **all** the members of the group. *Employees, who trust their employer,
> stay longer* says that employees trust their employer — which is exactly the overclaim this unit
> is about. Commas are not decoration in a sentence that carries a figure.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What may be said internally about trust, and by whom, varies a great deal. Where works councils or
union representation are strong, an internal communication quoting a flattering external figure
while omitting the company's own survey is likely to be raised formally, and the company will be
asked for the internal number in writing. Elsewhere internal communication is treated as a
management instrument and nobody expects it to be balanced. There are differences in how survey
results are read, too: in some countries a trust score of sixty-one would be regarded as poor and in
others as unusually good, because response norms differ and people in some cultures use the top of a
scale sparingly. That makes cross-country comparison of self-reported sentiment particularly
unreliable, and it is a limit the publishers of such studies generally acknowledge.

**Discuss:** Where you work, who could require management to publish an internal figure? Has it
happened?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Note on the trust slide
> *N. Khalil · 7 March · one page*
>
> **The figure, quoted accurately.** The **2026 Edelman Trust Barometer reports** trust in "my
> employer" at **78%** among employees, ahead of business (**64%**), NGOs (**58%**), media (**54%**)
> and government (**53%**), with employers trusted in **27 of the 28** countries measured.
>
> **Producer, base, method, dates — in one sentence.** The barometer is **commissioned by a
> communications firm**, the figures are **self-reported**, the employer question has a base of
> **people who are employees**, and fieldwork was **30-minute online interviews conducted between 23
> October and 18 November 2025**.
>
> **What it says nothing about.** **The barometer says nothing about Lindmark.** No respondent works
> here, and it was never intended to measure one company. **It would be wrong to place it beside our
> logo without its source**, because a figure on a company slide is read as a company claim.
>
> **Our own figure, beside it.** Our November staff survey puts trust in Lindmark as an employer at
> **61%**, on a **71% response rate**, **measured by a different instrument and therefore not
> comparable in method**. **The gap is 17 points, and we have not yet asked why.**
>
> **Two further findings, which explain more than the headline.** **Seven in ten** respondents report
> being unwilling or hesitant to trust somebody with different values, background or sources of
> information. **Two thirds of employees** worry that trade policies and tariffs will hurt their
> employer. That is the mood our 61% sits inside, and it flatters nobody.
>
> **The risk I am managing.** Not that staff will disbelieve the 78%. **That they will conclude
> management believes something untrue about them** — which is worse, lasts longer, and is available
> to anybody who filled in our own survey.
>
> *Sources: 2026 Edelman Trust Barometer, global report, 28 countries, fieldwork 23 October to 18
> November 2025. The 61% is Lindmark's own November staff survey and is not comparable in method.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Print your own less flattering figure beside the borrowed one, and label the two
> as different instruments. You lose the slide's shine and you gain the only thing that makes an
> internal communication work at all, which is that the reader cannot catch you out with something
> they already know. The sentence *we have not yet asked why* is worth more than the figure.
