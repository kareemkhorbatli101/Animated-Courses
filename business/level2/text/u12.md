### 1 · Past continuous with past simple

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL2-12-06%%

> **Daniel:** So the year was good.
> **Paula:** The year was good. While we were fixing the invoices we lost a customer, and I'd
> rather the board heard that from me.
> **Daniel:** They'll ask why.
> **Paula:** They should. Salas went on hold in July. They were waiting to be paid themselves when
> the rule caught them.
> **Daniel:** The rule we wrote.
> **Paula:** The rule we wrote and I still think is right.

*Paula could leave Salas out of a good report. Why doesn't she?*

**1B  Underline the past continuous forms. What is each one doing in the sentence?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **was / were + -ing** | We **were chasing** earlier all year. |
| **while + continuous** | **While** we **were fixing** the invoices, we lost a customer. |
| **when + simple** | **When** the rule caught them, they **were waiting** to be paid. |
| **as + continuous** | **As** the error rate **was falling**, the complaints stopped. |
| **negative** | We **weren't planning** to lose anybody. |

> **Grammar Note** — Use the continuous for the situation and the simple for the event. The event
> is usually what you are reporting; the continuous tells your reader what else was true at the
> time, which is often where the explanation lives.

**2A  Study the chart.**

| What you do | Example |
|---|---|
| **Give both figures** | One report says 56 percent; the other says 55.0. |

> **Grammar Note** — "Approximately" is not the same as "I don't know". Use *approximately* for a

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Admitting uncertainty in a report is read very differently from place to place. In some business
cultures writing "I cannot tell you which figure is right" is a mark of seriousness, and a
confident single number would be treated as careless. In others it reads as weakness or as pushing
the decision upwards, and a manager is expected to choose, state the number and carry it. There are
also strong differences in how bad news is placed: some readers expect the problem first and the
achievement second; others would find that ungrateful and expect the year's good news to be
established before anything difficult appears. None of this changes the arithmetic — but it changes
where on the page the arithmetic goes.

**Discuss:** In your workplace, how would a paragraph admitting two conflicting figures be
received? Where would bad news sit on the page?

## Team Up & Review

### A · Look at the picture

%%FIG p12-f14 | A company year-end meeting in a large room. Sixteen people: one presenting at a screen showing a target table, four at a front table with papers, two by a wall chart of the year, two carrying boxes to a new office area, one at a coffee table talking to two others, two looking at two documents held side by side, and two at a window with a view of a new building. A banner with the year on it.%%

**In your team, answer.**

1. How many people are at the front table?
2. What is on the screen?
3. What are the two people with documents doing?
4. What do you think is in the boxes?

### B · Write a team summary

> This is a year-end meeting. There are sixteen people. While one person was presenting, two others
> were comparing documents.

Write eight sentences. Use *while* or *when* in at least four. One person writes, everybody checks.

### C · Report

Read your summary to another team. They must add one thing that went wrong in the year you describe.

### D · Team project — the board paper

| Role | Who | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Manager** | | sets the three targets and keeps to twenty-five minutes |
| **Writer** | | writes the one page, five sections, no more |
| **Checker** | | finds the sentence the board will attack and strengthens it |
| **Presenter** | | presents in three minutes and takes two hostile questions |

Invent a company year with three targets, two met and one missed, one unplanned good thing, one
unplanned bad thing, and two conflicting figures for next year's growth. Write the page. Another
team is the board.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD CL2-12-16%%

%%FIG p12-f16 | A quiet office in late December. A single page on a desk with two percentages visible and one circled. Beside it, a thin annex stapled at the corner. Through the window, a lit yard and a parked van. On the wall, a calendar with June ringed.%%

> The paper went to the board with one sentence on the page and both reports in Annex A, and June
> is ringed on Paula's calendar. Three things are worth saying about it. The first is that she will
> probably never find out which report was right, because by June her own order book will be better
> evidence than either and she will use that instead. The second is that the Salas paragraph is now
> quoted by other managers as the reason not to write honest papers, because the director asked
> about it for eleven minutes. The third is that Paula has started keeping a file of every figure
> she has had to choose between, with the date and the reason — fourteen so far this year — and
> nobody has asked to see it, and it is the most useful document in the building.

1. Was the honest paragraph worth eleven minutes of questions?
2. If her own order book disagrees with both reports in June, what should she do?
3. Why might nobody ask to see Paula's file, and should somebody?
