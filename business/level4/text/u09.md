### 5 · Word study — *unless*, *provided that*, *in case*

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r09-f6 | A chart of four conditional connectors with a small diagram against each: unless shown as a stop condition, provided that as a permission condition, in case as a precaution taken beforehand, and as long as as a continuing condition, with one example each and a note that in case is not the same as if.%%

| Connector | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **unless** | if … not — a stopping condition | **Unless** we have 140,000 by month 15, we exit. |
| **provided that** | only on this condition | **Provided that** it arrives, stage two proceeds. |
| **as long as** | the same, less formal | **As long as** the orders hold, we continue. |
| **in case** | as a precaution, **beforehand** | We'll keep the lease short **in case** we exit. |
| **if** | a condition | **If** we exit, we write off 40,000. |

> **Grammar Note** — *In case* is not *if*. "We'll take an umbrella in case it rains" means you take
> it **before** you know. "We'll take an umbrella if it rains" means you wait and see. In an
> investment paper, *in case* is the language of precautions and *if* is the language of outcomes,
> and mixing them up makes a careful plan sound indecisive.

**1B  Underline the conditional connectors. Which one stops the project?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Use |
|---|---|
| **unless + present** | the stopping condition |
| **provided that + present** | the continuing condition |
| **in case + present** | a precaution taken in advance |
| **if + present, will** | a consequence |
| **by month 15** | the point at which the condition is tested |

> **Grammar Note** — A trigger needs three things: a **figure**, a **date**, and an **action**.
> "Unless orders improve, we will review" is not a trigger, because none of the three is there.
> "Unless we have 140,000 by month 15, we exit" is one, and it can be tested by somebody who was
> not in the room.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How investment decisions are justified differs by organization and by market. In some companies a
paper is expected to argue one side and the board's job is to attack it; in others a paper that
contains the argument against itself is read as unconvinced, and the author is expected to bring
conviction rather than balance. Staged commitments are standard practice in some sectors and
regarded as a lack of seriousness in others, particularly where a local partner or government
expects a full commitment as a sign of good faith — and in several markets a phased entry with a
written exit rule would itself damage the relationships the investment depends on. There are also
real legal differences: what you may own, who must be a local partner, how profits may be taken
out, and what notice you must give to leave.

**Discuss:** Where you work, should a proposal contain the argument against it? **And find out:**
in a market you deal with, are there rules about local ownership or about taking profits out?

> will decide at month 15" instead. What I do not want is a number everybody treats as
> indicative.
>
> **What we are not asking you to believe.** Any forecast about that country. I have not made one
> and I would not trust mine. You are approving a first stage and a stopping rule.

> **Writer's Tip** — The paragraph that makes a staged proposal work is the one that says the
> trigger is a rule rather than a guide, and offers to replace it with judgement if that is what
> the board actually wants. It is the only way to find out in September what everybody will claim
> to have meant in month fifteen.
