**2C  Complete the paragraph.**

The service agreement says Lindmark will deliver "a twelve percent improvement in delivery
performance within twelve months". Nobody defined delivery performance, which is not negligence;
it is what happens when a sentence is written by two people who each know what they mean. There are
four candidates. **On the promised day**: Lindmark is fourteen percent better, because the planning
changes last year did exactly this. **Within a two-day window**: eleven percent better, just under
the promise. **Complete first time** — the whole order in one delivery, nothing to follow — is
flat, because the improvement came from splitting deliveries, which helps the first two measures and
hurts this one. And **by weight delivered on time** it is three percent worse, because the heavy
items are the ones that get split. All four are real and all four are calculated from the same
(1) ______________ . Wei's position is the only honest one available: he has met the promise on two
measures, missed it on one and gone backwards on one, and he will say so in that order rather than
pick the measure he won on. What makes this unit matter beyond the contract is that the same
situation exists in a global dataset Wei looked up while preparing: international parcel post
(2) ______________ fell sixteen percent while the number of items rose thirty-eight. The business
did not shrink by sixteen percent and did not grow by thirty-eight. People are sending more parcels
and each one weighs less, and either figure alone is a lie by omission. What Wei writes into the
revised agreement is one line defining the (3) ______________ , one defining the denominator, and
one saying which of the four measures the twelve percent applies to. It takes four sentences and
it is the difference between a review meeting and a (4) ______________ .

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When a weight measure and a count measure move in opposite directions, the
> business has not grown or shrunk: its **mix** has changed. The sentence that describes it has to
> contain both numbers, and it is always longer than either headline.

> *W. Zhang and the customer's operations manager · for signature*
>
> **2.1 Definition.** "Delivery performance" means **the number of orders delivered on the promised
> day** (numerator) **divided by the number of orders dispatched in the same month** (denominator),
> expressed as a percentage. Orders canceled by the customer are excluded from both.
>
> **2.2 The commitment.** A **twelve percent relative improvement** on the baseline of 78.4 per
> cent, measured monthly and assessed on a three-month average.
>
> **2.3 What this clause does not measure.** Three other things we also report, which are **not**
> the commitment:

> | Measure | Against baseline |
> |---|---|
> | On the promised day *(the commitment)* | **+14%** |
> | Within a two-day window | +11% |

### 5 · Word study — noun phrases, and un-nominalizing

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r11-f6 | A chart with two columns, nominalized and un-nominalized: four long noun-phrase sentences on the left and the same content expressed with verbs and named actors on the right, with a note that nominalization hides who does what.%%

| Nominalized | With verbs and actors |
|---|---|
| **The implementation of the revised definition** will take place in April. | **We will start using** the new definition in April. |
| There has been an **improvement in delivery performance**. | **We delivered** 14 percent more orders on the promised day. |
| **The splitting of deliveries** has had an **impact on completeness**. | **We split more deliveries**, so **fewer orders arrived complete**. |
| **A review of the measurement methodology** is required. | **Somebody has to decide which of the four measures counts.** |

> **Grammar Note** — Nominalization — turning a verb into a noun — makes writing sound formal and
> removes the actor. It is useful in a definition clause, where you want the thing rather than the
> doer: *delivery performance means the number of orders delivered…*. It is harmful everywhere
> else, because *an improvement has occurred* does not say who improved what, and a sentence with
> no actor cannot be checked.

|---|---|

> **Grammar Note** — Use noun phrases where you are naming a thing that has to be defined, and

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Measurement is more cultural than it looks. In some business cultures a supplier is expected to
present the best defensible figure and the customer's job is to challenge it; in others presenting
anything other than the full picture is a breach of trust that ends the relationship. Attitudes to
definitions differ too: in some markets a detailed definition clause is normal and welcomed, while
in others asking to define a promise in writing implies that you expect to be cheated. And there
are real differences in who may contradict a customer's own figures — in several business cultures
telling a customer their number is based on a different denominator, however politely, is
unacceptable from a supplier and must be routed through a senior person on each side.

**Discuss:** Where you work, may a supplier correct a customer's figures? Would asking for a
definition look like distrust?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Service report — October, and a proposal about the definition
> *W. Zhang · for Ostberg Retail · one page*
>
> **The commitment measure, first.** Orders delivered on the promised day, as a share of orders
> dispatched: **+14 percent against the 78.4 percent baseline**. That is the measure your
> agreement implies, and on it we have met the twelve percent.
>
> **Three measures that are not the commitment, and one of them is worse.**
> | | Against baseline |
> |---|---|
> | Within a two-day window | +11% |
> | Complete first time, nothing to follow | **flat** |
> | By weight delivered on time | **−3%** |
>
> **Why, in plain terms.** **We hit the promised day by splitting more orders.** That means more of
> your orders arrive in two deliveries instead of one, so *complete first time* has not improved,
> and because we split the heavy items most often, *weight on time* has gone backwards. **We
> improved the measure the contract implied and made the thing your warehouse actually feels
> slightly worse.**
>
> **Your figures and mine.** Your October figure is by weight. It is right. Mine is by order count
> on the promised day. It is also right. Same data, two denominators.
>
> **What I propose for next year.** (1) Define it in schedule 2: numerator, denominator, exclusions,
> period, baseline. (2) If *complete first time* is what your warehouse cares about, make that the
> commitment — and I am telling you now that twelve percent on that measure will take me about
> **eighteen months**, not twelve. (3) Let me report all four every month, with the commitment one
> at the top.
>
> **What I am not doing.** I am not asking you to accept the fourteen percent as the answer. I am
> asking you to choose the measure, with the cost of choosing it in front of you.

> **Writer's Tip** — Lead with the measure the customer feels rather than the one you won on, and
> when you explain a trade-off, use verbs and name yourself as the actor: *we hit the promised day
> by splitting more orders.* A nominalized version — *the improvement was achieved through
