### 5 · Word study — the modals of rules

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r07-f6 | A chart of regulatory modals in five rows: must for an obligation on the reader, shall for an obligation on the organization, may for a permission, will for a statement of what happens, and should for advice with a warning that should promises nothing, each with one clause as an example.%%

| Modal | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **may** | permission — the reader is allowed | Any employee **may** apply. |
| **shall** | obligation on the organization | The manager **shall** respond within 14 days. |
| **must** | obligation, usually on the reader | The response **must** state the criterion. |
| **will** | a statement of what happens | The decision **will** be recorded. |
| **should** | advice — **no obligation at all** | Managers **should** consider the request fairly. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Should* has no force. A clause that says a manager *should* respond promptly
> has promised nothing and cannot be breached. If you mean it, write *shall* with a number of days
> beside it; if you do not mean it, do not write it. This single substitution is the difference
> between most useless policies and most usable ones.

### 1 · The zero conditional in rules

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD CL4-07-06%%

> **Hanna:** "If a request is refused, the response must state which criterion it fails."
> **Nour:** Not "should"?
> **Hanna:** Never "should". Somebody refused a request in 2023 with the word "unfortunately" and
> no reason, and there was nothing in the policy they had broken.
> **Nour:** So "must".
> **Hanna:** "Must", and the register, so that in a year we can count the refusals by criterion.

*Hanna says there was nothing the 2023 manager had broken. Whose fault was that?*

**1B  Underline the conditional and the modals. What does each modal commit whom to?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **if + present, present** | a rule that is always true | **If** a request **is** refused, the reason **is** recorded. |
| **if + present, must / shall** | a rule with an obligation | **If** it **is** refused, the response **must** state why. |
| **where + present** | the same, more formal | **Where** a request is refused, … |
| **unless + present** | the exception | **Unless** the employee agrees, the date **shall not** change. |
| **provided that** | a condition on a permission | A manager **may** grant it **provided that** cover exists. |

> **Grammar Note** — Rules use the zero conditional because they describe what is always the case,
> not what might happen: *if a request is refused* — not *would be*, not *will be*. And *where* is
> the formal equivalent of *if* in regulatory writing; it means the same and signals that you are
> writing a rule rather than describing an event.

### 2 · Making a rule testable

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Untestable | Testable |
|---|---|
| Requests will be considered. | The manager shall respond in writing within 14 days. |

> **Grammar Note** — A rule is testable if somebody could tell, a year later, whether it was
> followed. Add three things to any clause to make it testable: a **named role**, a **number of
> days**, and a **written output**. If a clause has none of the three, delete it; it is decoration.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Policies carry different weight in different places. In some organizations a written policy is the
authority and a manager who departs from it is at fault; in others the written policy is a
statement of intent and real decisions are made by relationship and seniority, so an employee who
cites a clause at their manager has made a serious mistake. Appeal routes differ sharply too: in
some cultures appealing above your manager is a normal procedural right, and in others it is a
declaration of war whatever the policy says. Flexible working itself is regulated in some countries
and entirely discretionary in others, and in several the right to request it, the deadline to answer
and the permitted grounds for refusal are all set in law rather than by the employer.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is the policy the authority or the intention? Would you appeal above
your manager? **And find out:** does the law where you are give a right to request flexible
working, and does it set a deadline?

> ### Flexible working policy, version 2
> *H. Lindqvist · effective 1 June · review date 1 June next year*
>
> **Definitions.** *Flexible working*: a change to when, where or how long you work. *Decision
> maker*: your own line manager. *Working days*: excluding weekends and public holidays at your
> location.
>
> 1. Any employee with six months' service **may** apply, on the form at Annex A, up to twice a
> year.
> 2. The decision maker **shall** respond **in writing within 21 days** of receiving the form.
> 3. A request **shall** be assessed against **these four criteria only**: coverage of the work;
> effect on colleagues; cost; and whether the arrangement can be measured.
> 4. **If a request is refused, the response must state which of the four criteria it fails, and
> why.** A refusal that does not do so is a breach of this policy.
> 5. The employee **may** request review by the site manager within **10 working days**; the site
> manager **shall** respond within a further **10**.
> 6. Every decision, granted or refused, **shall** be recorded in the decisions register, with the
> criterion cited for each refusal.
>
> **What this policy does not cover.** Pay; permanent contract changes; and employees with under
> six months' service, whose requests a manager **may** grant informally and **shall** record.
>
> **Why the register exists.** So that in a year we can count refusals by criterion. If all of them
> cite the same criterion, that tells us something about the criterion. And if the people who apply
> and the people who are promoted turn out to be two different lists, we will know in a year
> rather than in five.
>
> **What I am not claiming.** This policy will not make managers say yes. It will make them say
> why, on paper, within 21 days, against a list they cannot add to — and it will let somebody count

> **Writer's Tip** — Write the register clause before you write the rule. The question that

### 3 · Write

Write **120 to 160 words**: a policy of five or six clauses.

**Use *may*, *shall* and *must* for the right people. Write at least two zero conditionals. Put a
number in every obligation. Remove every *should*. Include a register clause and a review date.**

**Read your draft aloud to your partner first.** Hearing it is the quickest way to find the sentence
that does not work. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's policy as a manager who does not want to say yes. Where is
the gap you would use?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Whose policy is more usable, and whose would your own
management accept? If those are different, what would you do?
