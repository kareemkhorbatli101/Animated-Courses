### 5 · Word study — the past perfect

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r08-f6 | A chart of the past perfect on a timeline: a reference point in the past marked by eleven thirty, with earlier events drawn to its left and labeled had happened, and a note contrasting it with the past simple used for the reference point itself.%%

| Pattern | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **had + past participle** | earlier than another past point | By 11:30 the warehouse **had been** on paper for six hours. |
| **past simple** | the reference point itself | At 11:30 somebody **connected** the three facts. |
| **had been + -ing** | how long, up to that point | They **had been picking** from paper since 06:15. |
| **by the time + past simple** | names the reference point | **By the time** anybody **called** it an incident, … |
| **hadn't … until** | when it finally happened | Nobody **hadn't** — *no*: Nobody **had** reported it **until** 11:30. |

> **Grammar Note** — You only need the past perfect when you are telling a story out of order. In a
> timeline that runs forwards, the past simple is enough and the past perfect is noise. Use it the
> moment you look back from a point: *by 11:30, the warehouse had already keyed nothing in for six
> hours.*

|---|---|

### 2 · Telling a story in order, and out of it

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Purpose | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| the timeline itself | past simple, in order | At 04:50 two scanners stopped. At 05:20 the ticket was logged. |
| the finding | past perfect, looking back | By 11:30 the warehouse **had been** on paper for nearly seven hours. |
| what was not known | past perfect negative | Nobody **had connected** the three facts. |
| what was lost | past perfect passive | The 05:00 restore point **had been** overwritten. |
| what follows | present | **We now** require three unrelated faults to be escalated. |

> **Grammar Note** — An incident report needs all three: the past simple for the sequence, the past
> perfect for the gap, and the present for what changes. Reports that use only the past simple read
> as a list of times and bury the one sentence anybody needs.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How an incident is handled depends heavily on what happens to the person who raises it. In some
organizations anybody may declare an incident and a false alarm costs nothing; in others only a
senior person may use the word, and a junior employee who escalates and turns out to be wrong is
remembered for it — which produces exactly the delay in this unit. Reporting obligations also
differ by law and by sector: many countries require notification of certain breaches to a regulator
within a fixed number of hours, and that clock starts at detection, which makes "when did you
detect it?" a legal question and not only an operational one. There are also strong cultural
differences in blame: in some workplaces an incident review names people, and in others naming
anybody guarantees that the next review will be less honest.

**Discuss:** Where you work, who may declare an incident? What happens to them if they are wrong?
**And find out:** does the law where you are set a notification deadline, and what starts the clock?

> would **not** have prevented the breach. I would rather say that than let anybody think speed
> alone was the answer.
>
> **Actions.**
> | Action | Owner | By |
> |---|---|---|
> | Rule: two unrelated system faults within 2 hours are reviewed together | IT provider | 10 days |
> | A named escalation route from the warehouse that is not the help desk | D. Okonjo | 14 days |
> | Supplier-portal credentials rotated and unique | IT provider | 7 days |
> | Paper workaround rehearsed once a quarter, timed | T. Ek | next quarter |
>
> **What this report does not explain.** How the credential was obtained. We know where it was
> valid; we do not know how it was taken, and we may never.

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the finding above the timeline. A chronological report makes the reader do
> the analysis, and most readers stop at row six. And name what earlier detection would *not* have
> changed: an incident report that implies speed would have fixed everything will produce a
> fortnight of alarm and no structural change.
