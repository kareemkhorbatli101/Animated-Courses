The direct cost of a pallet leaving Lindmark is 1.38: the labor to pick and wrap it, the
consumables, the carrier's charge. That is a real number and it is the one people quote, because it
is the only one nobody argues about. Add the (1) ______________ — the building, the lighting, the
heating, the planning team, the insurance, the three people in finance — and divide them by the
number of pallets going out this year, and the figure becomes 1.61. That too is correct. But the
overhead is mostly (2) ______________ , so it does not fall if volume falls: at last year's volume
the same overhead divided by fewer pallets gives 1.74. All three are right, and which one is
*useful* depends entirely on the question. For a quote on new business you need 1.61, because the
new business has to carry its share of the building. For a decision about whether to accept one
extra pallet tonight on a truck that is already going, you need something closer to 1.38, because
the building is paid for either way. And for a warning to the board about what happens if volume
drops, you need 1.74. The dangerous thing is not any of the numbers; it is giving one of them
without saying which question it answers. Marek's rule, written at the top of the costing sheet, is
four words: **state the volume assumed.** Every unit cost in the building now carries it, and the
sales team has stopped quoting 1.38, which was costing about forty thousand a year in work won at
a price that could not pay for the (3) ______________ it used. The number in a quote is not the
(4) ______________ cost. It is the cost at a stated volume, and the two are not the same thing.

### 5 · Word study — quantity language

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r03-f6 | A chart of quantity expressions in six rows: per unit, out of, in every, a ratio expressed as to, as a share of, and per thousand, each with one worked example from a costing sheet and a note on which is clearest when spoken aloud.%%

| Expression | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **per** | 1.61 **per** pallet · 0.18 **per** kilowatt-hour | the commonest, and the clearest |
| **out of** | 19 **out of** 27 member states | use for counts, not for rates |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What a price is understood to include varies widely, and so does what may be discussed. In some
business cultures a quotation is expected to be a single number and breaking it down looks evasive;
in others an itemized cost is the normal basis of negotiation and a single figure will simply be
challenged. Attitudes to overheads differ too: in some markets it is normal for a buyer to argue
that your head office is not their problem, while in others carrying a share of it is taken for
granted. Payment and price are also linked in different ways — in some places the price assumes a
payment period and quoting one without the other is incomplete, and in others they are negotiated
separately and bundling them is a tactic.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is a price broken down or given whole? Would a customer argue about
your overhead?

> **What this note does not tell you.** The cost of a part-load pallet, which is higher and which I

> **Writer's Tip** — A costing note is read by people who will take one number out of it. Write it

> say in one bold line which figure must never leave the building. You are not writing arithmetic;
> you are writing instructions for its use.
