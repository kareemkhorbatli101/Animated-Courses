### 5 · Word study — verb + *-ing* or *to*-infinitive

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r10-f6 | A chart of verb patterns in three groups: verbs followed by -ing such as avoid, consider, involve and risk; verbs followed by to plus infinitive such as decide, agree, refuse and arrange; and verbs that take both with a change of meaning, such as remember, stop and go on, each with one example.%%

| Group | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **+ -ing** | avoid, consider, involve, risk, delay, suggest | We **considered keeping** the line open. |
| **+ to-infinitive** | decide, agree, refuse, arrange, aim, promise | We **decided to close** it. |
| **+ object + to** | ask, tell, allow, require, remind | We **asked them to place** a final order. |
| **both, same meaning** | begin, start, continue | We **began winding** / **began to wind** it down. |
| **both, different meaning** | remember, forget, stop, go on | We **stopped making** it. / We **stopped to make** a note. |

> **Grammar Note** — The pair that matters most here is *stop*. *We stopped making it* means
> production ended. *We stopped to make it* means you paused in order to make it. In a closure
> notice, that distinction is the entire message, and getting it wrong changes the meaning
> completely rather than sounding slightly odd.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Closing something touches employment law, notice, and strong local expectations, and all three
differ. Consultation requirements vary enormously: in some countries changing eleven people's work
triggers a formal process with representatives and minimum periods, and announcing it in a room
first would itself be a breach; in others it is entirely at the employer's discretion. Customer
notice is sometimes contractual and sometimes a courtesy, and in several markets a long-standing
supplier simply stopping is read as a serious breach of relationship whatever the contract says.
The order of telling also carries meaning: in some cultures informing staff before customers is
obviously correct, and in others informing a major customer last is an insult that outweighs
everything else.

**Discuss:** Where you work, who must be told first? **And find out:** does the law where you are
require consultation before changing people's work, and how much notice?

> ### DL range — closure plan
> *C. Obi · decision 3 October · last production 31 March*
>
> **The decision, and the awkward part of it.** We are discontinuing the DL range. **It is
> profitable today.** It would not be after the 80,000 of machine work needed within two years, and

> **The order of telling, with dates.** (1) The **eleven people** whose work changes — in a room, by
> me, **Tuesday 7 October**. (2) The **four customers** — dated letters, twelve months' notice,
> **Friday 10 October**. (3) Everybody else internally — the same Friday, after the letters. (4)
> Nothing public until all three are done. **I delayed step 1 by three weeks and two of the eleven
> had already heard. That is the one thing in this plan I would do differently.**
>
> **What customers get.** Twelve months' notice. A last-time buy from **2 January to 28 February**.
> **Spares until 2031**, in writing. Technical answers by email for two years, from a **named
> person** rather than an inbox.
>
> **The money.** Residual stock of about 31,000, written down across three quarters rather than one,
> agreed with finance.
>
> **The physical and the records.** The machine cleaned, covered and recorded — in nine years
> somebody will ask what it was. Records archived **with a note saying what they are**.
>
> **What has no procedure.** Telling the eleven people. There is no clause anywhere about doing it
> first, in a room, or at all. I suggest there should be, and I have drafted one.
