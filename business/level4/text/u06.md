### 5 · Word study — *should have* and *be supposed to*

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r06-f6 | A chart of past criticism and expectation forms in five rows: should have, shouldn't have, was supposed to, wasn't supposed to, and needn't have, each with an example and a note on whether the thing happened or not.%%

| Form | Did it happen? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **should have + participle** | no, and it was a mistake | I **should have** declared it. |
| **shouldn't have + participle** | yes, and it was a mistake | I **shouldn't have** accepted it. |
| **was supposed to** | no — an expectation unmet | I **was supposed to** register it within a week. |
| **wasn't supposed to** | it was against the rule | He **wasn't supposed to** offer cash. |
| **needn't have + participle** | yes, and it was unnecessary | I **needn't have** worried; it was 15 euros. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Needn't have done* and *didn't need to do* are different: *I needn't have
> declared it* means you did and it was unnecessary; *I didn't need to declare it* means you did
> not and did not have to. Both appear in compliance conversations and they say opposite things
> about whether anything happened.

**1B  Underline the forms. Which describe rules and which describe judgement?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Use |
|---|---|
| **should have** | your own judgement, afterwards |
| **was supposed to** | a rule or an expectation, usually somebody else's |
| **shouldn't have** | a mistake that happened |
| **needn't have** | something unnecessary that happened |
| **was meant to** | the same as *was supposed to*, slightly softer |

> **Grammar Note** — *Should have* is about judgement and *was supposed to* is about rules, and
> people choose the wrong one under pressure. "I was supposed to tell you" blames the procedure.
> "I should have told you" takes responsibility. In a disclosure conversation the second one ends
> the conversation and the first one extends it.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

This is the area where good people most often get into trouble abroad, because the rules are legal
in one place and cultural in another. In some countries a gift at the start of a relationship is
not optional and refusing it is a serious insult; in others accepting anything at all from a
supplier is a disciplinary matter. Thresholds differ enormously, and so does what counts: in some
systems hospitality you consume with the giver is treated quite differently from a gift you take
home. Several countries' laws reach across borders, so the rule that binds you may not be the rule
of the country you are standing in. And facilitation payments — small sums to speed a routine
approval — are customary in some markets and criminal under the law your employer is subject to,
which is a conflict you cannot solve by being polite.

**Discuss:** Where you work, what may you accept? **And find out:** what does the law that applies
to your employer say about gifts abroad and about facilitation payments? Whose law is it?

> ### Declaration — hospitality received, and a recusal
> *R. Menon to H. Lindqvist, copied to D. Okonjo · 19 April · register entry 2026-14*
>
> **What was offered.** Two tickets to the Ostberg Classic on 11 May, one hotel night, and rail
> travel, by K. Novak of Novak Packaging, whom I have worked with since 2020.
>
> **Estimated value.** About 400 in total: tickets about 240, hotel 120, travel 40. I have estimated
> the tickets from the face value printed on them.
>
> **What I have done.** Accepted the tickets and the travel; **asked Novak to invoice Brightwell
> for half the hotel and half the tickets**, which he has agreed to and which he suggested
> extending to the tickets himself. Registered the same morning. Told my manager in person rather
> than by email.
>
> **The part that matters more than the hospitality.** I am scheduled to conduct the review of
> Novak Packaging's supply contract in September. **I have asked I. Costa to take that review
> instead of me.** I do not believe either of us would behave differently, and that is not the
> test: the test is what this register and that review look like read together in November.
>
> **What I should have done differently.** I took two days to raise this. The policy allows a week
> and two days is within it, so nothing is late; I am recording it because the two days were spent
> deciding whether to mention it at all, and that hesitation is the thing worth knowing about. If
> it took me two days, it will take somebody else a week, and some of them will decide not to.
>
> **One recommendation.** Our policy sets a threshold for **gifts** and does not mention
> **hospitality**, which is why this entry required judgement rather than a rule. I suggest a
