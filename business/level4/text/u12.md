### 5 · Word study — hedging and commitment

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r12-f6 | A chart of commitment language on a scale from weakest to strongest: we are exploring, we intend to, we aim to, we expect to, we have committed to, and we have done, with a note against each saying what evidence it requires.%%

| Form | What it claims | Evidence it needs |
|---|---|---|
| **we are exploring** | almost nothing | a meeting |
| **we intend to** | a decision, no plan | a decision somewhere |
| **we aim to** | a target | a target with a date |
| **we expect to** | a forecast | a reason for the forecast |
| **we have committed to** | money or a contract | a signed order or a budget line |
| **we have + done** | a completed fact | evidence, and a date |

> **Grammar Note** — Each step up the scale requires evidence that the step below does not. The
> commonest failure in corporate reporting is using *we are* for everything, which claims the
> strongest thing on the weakest evidence. If you cannot produce the evidence in the right-hand
> column, move down one row.

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **we have + participle + date** | We **replaced** the compressors **in June**. |
| **we have committed to** | We **have committed to** a heat recovery unit; it is ordered. |
| **we expect to** | We **expect to** complete metering by March. |
| **we aim to** | We **aim to** reduce energy use by 30 per cent by 2032. |
| **we intend to** | We **intend to** review the fleet. |
| **we are exploring** | We **are exploring** on-site generation. |

> **Grammar Note** — Put the finished things first with dates, and the intentions last with the
> word *intend* in them. A reader who reaches your intentions after four verifiable facts will
> believe them. A reader who meets your intentions first, dressed as achievements, will disbelieve
> the facts as well.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What a company is expected to publish about itself varies enormously, and the direction of travel
is not the same everywhere. Some jurisdictions have made disclosure mandatory for large companies
and are phasing it down to smaller ones; others have adopted a standard voluntarily; others have
adopted part of one; and in several, reporting of this kind is viewed as a cost imposed from
outside. Expectations about tone differ too: in some business cultures a statement is expected to
be aspirational and reading it literally would be naive, while in others any gap between a claim and
its evidence is a legal risk. And the audience differs — many companies publish mainly because one
large customer requires it, which makes the customer's jurisdiction the one that matters rather
than their own.

**Discuss:** Where you work, is this kind of reporting required, voluntary or unknown? **And find
out:** which jurisdiction's rules would reach your employer first — your own, or your largest
customer's?

> **What we have done.** We **replaced** the compressors at the main site in **June** and the

>

>
> **What we intend.** We **intend to** review the vehicle fleet; a board paper goes up in January.

> 2032 figure yet**, which is why this sentence says *aim* and not *will*.
>
