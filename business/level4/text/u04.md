### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Find out whether an average is **simple** or **weighted** before you use it. A
> simple average of providers treats a service nobody uses the same as one used by millions. It is
> not a wrong number; it is a number about providers rather than about money.

%%FIG r04-f5 | A payment reconciliation: two statements side by side, one showing an amount sent and a single fee line, the other showing a smaller amount received, with three annotated arrows between them labelled rate spread, intermediary charge and receiving charge, and a total difference at the foot expressed in money and as a percentage.%%

> ## Lindmark Freight · payment reconciliation, supplier KE-204
> *M. Dolny · 7 May · eleven months of payments compared line by line*
>
> | | Amount |
> |---|---|
> | Sent, per month | 4,000 |
> | Our bank's disclosed fee | 12 |
> | **Arrived, per month** | **3,841** |
> | **Difference** | **159, or about 4.0%** |
>
> **Where the difference goes.** About 96 is the gap between the rate our bank used and the market
> rate that morning — the **spread**, which never appears as a line. About 33 is an intermediary
> bank in a third country. The remainder is the receiving bank's charge and the disclosed fee.
>
> **What it costs over a year.** About 1,900. The supplier has been pricing to cover it, so we have
> been paying it twice: once as a deduction and once inside their price.
>
> **How this compares.** The World Bank tracks the cost of sending money across borders. In the
> first quarter of 2025 the **global average cost of sending 200 dollars was 6.49 per cent**, up
> from 6.26 per cent in the previous quarter. Sending to sub-Saharan Africa averaged **8.78 per
> cent**.
>
> *Source: World Bank, Remittance Prices Worldwide, Q1 2025. Read the method before you use it: the
> global average is a **simple average of the total cost charged by each provider** in the
> database, expressed as a percentage of the amount sent. A provider used by almost nobody counts
> as much as one used by millions, and the figure is for a 200-dollar personal transfer, not for a
> 4,000 commercial payment. It tells us the order of magnitude. It does not tell us what we should
> be paying.*

### 5 · Word study — the future perfect

**5A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG r04-f6 | A chart of the future perfect on a timeline: a point now, a later point marked by Friday, and an action completed before that later point, with four example sentences and a note contrasting it with the simple future.%%

| Pattern | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **will have + past participle** | finished before a future point | The funds **will have cleared** by Friday. |
| **by the time + present** | names that future point | **By the time** it arrives, three banks **will have taken** a share. |
| **won't have** | not finished by then | It **won't have cleared** before the weekend. |
| **will have been + -ing** | how long, at that point | By June we'**ll have been paying** this for a year. |
| compare: **will** | simply later | It **will clear** on Tuesday. |

> **Grammar Note** — *By the time* takes the **present**, not the future: *by the time it arrives*,
> never *by the time it will arrive*. And the future perfect is the natural tense for payment and
> project work, because almost everything in both is about what will be finished before something
> else happens.

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **will have + past participle** | We'**ll have paid** about 1,900 by the year end. |
| **by the time + present simple** | **By the time** it **clears**, two days will have gone. |
| **by + a date** | **By 30 June** the new route will have been running for a month. |
| **will have been + -ing** | We'**ll have been using** this route for a year. |
| **won't have** | They **won't have** received it before Tuesday. |

> **Grammar Note** — Use the future perfect when you want your reader to look back from a future
> point: *by the time the audit happens, we will have fixed it.* It is the tense of a promise with a
> deadline, which makes it the most useful tense in this book and the one most learners avoid.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Money across borders is where business practice, banking regulation and courtesy all differ at
once. Which side bears the charges is a convention in some markets and a negotiation in others, and
the three standard options on a payment instruction mean quite different things to a supplier
depending on what they are used to. Invoicing currency carries status as well as cost: in some
sectors invoicing in your own currency is normal and in others it is read as pushing the risk on to
the customer. There are also real differences in what can be said: asking a supplier directly
whether they have priced in a deduction is a reasonable commercial question in some cultures and an
accusation in others. And in several countries moving money takes documents and days that a sender
elsewhere never sees.

**Discuss:** Where you work, who bears the bank charges? Would you ask a supplier what they have
priced in?
