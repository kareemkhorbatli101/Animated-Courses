# IELTS 2026 Preparation Course — the complete series

**Eight books, 80 units, 400 cycles.** Academic **and** General Training in
every book. Built from one source, gated the same way, and graded so that
each book starts where the one below it stops.

The series is now complete: the two Foundation books (below 4.0 and
4.0–4.5) are built, and the gap between them and Volume 1 is measured
rather than assumed — see *The bridge into Volume 1* below.

## What a unit looks like

Every unit is five cycles, and **no cycle belongs to one module**. Each one
is a complete pass: reading, listening, speaking, writing, grammar,
vocabulary, expressions, transitions.

- **Cycle 1** — two short General Training texts + a Part 1 listening
- **Cycle 2** — the four-short-texts task
- **Cycle 3** — a General Training work text + the unit's figure
- **Cycles 4 and 5** — Academic passages

Every unit covers Listening Parts 1–4. Questions are numbered continuously
through the unit, across reading and listening alike, exactly as the paper
numbers them.

## What the series teaches

Skills and technique, not hacks. Receptive before productive. Tied
throughout to the Cambridge band descriptors, because **this is not just a
language test.**

The reading method is one loop, taught in Volume 1 unit 1 and used 300
times after it: **orient, load the questions, then read one paragraph at a
time.** Underline and keep reading. Headings before detail. Out-of-order
questions used as a memory aid rather than fought. Passages split wherever
a paragraph runs past about 150 words, so that a paragraph is always a
thing you can hold.

Listening parallels Reading step for step. Brainstorming and outlining are
taught extensively before any essay is asked for. The eleven essay
structures and the ten Task 1 principles are introduced at the pace each
level can carry, with 1D, 2D and hybrid Task 1 organisation. Speaking runs
on the 2-to-5 rule. **No templates anywhere.**

## How the level of each book is proved

Two numbers are measured on every academic passage and every timed paper:
the share of running words from the first 1,000 most frequent English words
(`k1`), and the share beyond the first 2,000 plus the academic list
(`beyond`). Each volume has its own window, and the gate is the two sets
measured **together**.

| Book | k1 | beyond | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1 | 74.7% | 9.7% | in window |
| Foundation 2 | 71.9% | 12.2% | in window |
| Volume 1 | 70.1% | 13.4% | in window |
| Volume 2 | 69.4% | 14.7% | in window |
| Volume 3 | 67.0% | 17.1% | in window |
| Volume 4 | 64.9% | 19.3% | in window |
| Volume 5 | 64.8% | 20.7% | in window |

### The syntax ladder

Two more numbers are measured on the same prose and gated the same way:
the mean sentence length, and the -tion/-ment/-ity/-ness rate, which is
the best single marker of academic density.

| Book | mean sentence | window | longest sentence | nominalisation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Foundation 1 | 16.8 | 16.5–18.5 | 31 | 1.8% |
| Foundation 2 | 18.8 | 17.6–19.6 | 37 | 2.0% |
| Volume 1 | 19.6 | 19.4–21.4 | 39 | 2.9% |
| Volume 2 | 20.7 | 20.2–22.2 | 46 | 3.4% |
| Volume 3 | 21.6 | 20.9–22.9 | 50 | 3.9% |
| Volume 4 | 22.7 | 21.6–23.6 | 54 | 4.3% |
| Volume 5 | 23.3 | 22.3–24.3 | 56 | 4.7% |
| Volume 6 | 24.0 | 23.0–25.0 | 55 | 5.5% |

Three things are gated: each book inside its own window, no sentence
anywhere over its book's ceiling, and — with `density.py --ladder` — the
eight measured means strictly rising, each at least 0.4 words above the
book below.

This is a **relative** gate and is stated as one. Unlike the vocabulary
window, it does not rest on a property of English; it gates the claim
this series makes, which is that each book is harder than the one below.
It is not a weak gate: it failed six of the eight books the day it was
written, and the worst failure was that the B1 book had the longest
sentences in the series and the C1 book did not. About 275 sentences were
split or merged to fix that. `IELTS_Syntax_Ladder_Report.md` has the
whole account, including what the gate still does not prove.

---

The two productive bands do **not** scale down with the cover band: a model
answer has to satisfy the rubric at any level, so the writing models in
Volume 1 are held to the same standard as those in Volume 6. The two
Foundation books are the single exception, and deliberately so: neither
prepares a candidate to sit the paper, and asking an A1 writer for 150
words teaches padding, which is then hard to unteach.

## The bridge into Volume 1

The two Foundation books exist to answer one question with a number: can a
student who finishes them open Volume 1 and read it?

`ledger.py` measured the gap — the word **types** a reader with the first
1,000 English words meets in Volume 1 that no book in the series teaches,
with proper nouns dropped and one entry per stem family. **The gap was 984
types.** `alloc.py` assigned every one of them to a Foundation cycle, by
theme: a word that lives in Volume 1's food unit is taught in the
Foundation unit about food.

| | |
|---|---|
| Gap into Volume 1, before either Foundation book | 984 types |
| Taught in Foundation 1 | 561 head words |
| Taught in Foundation 2 | 423 head words |
| **Gap remaining** | **0** |

`readiness.py` (gate 7) then measures the result on the finished books: a
Foundation 2 leaver meets **11.4 unknown types** in a mean Volume 1
academic passage and **18 in the worst of the twenty**, against ceilings of
12 and 20. **PASS.**

## What the ten-phase fix added after the first delivery

The eight books were delivered, then measured against two external
critiques and a page-by-page visual pass. That produced
`IELTS_Fix_Plan.md`: ten phases, each with a gate written **before** the
content it governs, and an invariance contract that fails the build on
any change a phase did not declare. All ten have shipped.

| what the books gained | where |
|---|---|
| a line for every wrong option — what that option actually is, and where in the text it came from | every item with a printed option list, in the units and in the timed papers: **7,475 lines** |
| a band-6.5 second model answer, with a list of what the band-9 answer does that it does not | the cycle-5 Task 2 of all 60 units in Volumes 1–6 |
| a Part 2 long-turn model, scaled by book from 130–180 words to 255–320 | every unit, all eight books |
| a Part 3 follow-up set, three exchanges rising to four | every unit from Volume 3 |
| a pronunciation and fluency page | every unit, all eight books |
| "Find the fault" — a band-6 paragraph with three planted errors, each with its correction and its reason | the third cycle of all 80 units |
| a second Reading paper and a second Listening paper | Volumes 1–5: ten papers, 400 new questions |
| exam-length reading inside the units, not only in the papers | 62 reads of 700+ words from Volume 3 up; one stamina read each in Volumes 1 and 2 |
| matching sentence endings | one set a book, Volumes 1–6 |
| listening plan and map labelling | one set a book from Volume 3 |
| both verification types taught in every book | 56 items retyped between True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given |

Two numbers are worth stating because they are the ones a candidate feels.
**Speaking** was 6.2–13.6% of a unit and falling as the books got harder;
it is now 12.1–20.4%, with a floor of 12% enforced in every unit of every
book. And the answer-key page, which once said that a wrong option is
explained *where it is tempting*, now says **"Every wrong option is
explained, because a letter on its own teaches nothing"** — and
`check.py` fails the build if one is missing.

`IELTS_Fix_Plan_Completion_Report.md` has the phase-by-phase record, the
measurements above per book, the three defects the invariance contract
caught, and the five things the plan did **not** fix.

## Gates every book passes

| Gate | What it proves |
|---|---|
| `check.py` | bands, continuous numbering, Listening Parts 1–4 in every unit, head-word seams against every other volume, explanation length |
| `density.py` | the register window above |
| `book.py` | the build itself |
| `omit.py` | zero omissions — every passage, script turn, model, question and explanation reaching the file |
| `gate6.py` | every run of text inside its box, nothing printed under 7.5px |
| `density.py --ladder` | the syntax ladder above: eight books, rising |
| `readiness.py` | gate 7, on the pair of Foundation books: a leaver can read Volume 1 |
| `gate8.py` | a block of text is inside its own box, measured rather than eyeballed |
| `freeze.py` / `regress.py` | the invariance contract: nothing changes that a phase did not declare |

All nine pass on all eight files in this package. One command runs them:
`bash runall.sh <phase>`, which ends `SUITE: ok` or names what failed.

Gates do not replace looking at the page. Three figure defects in the
shared renderer and one in the Foundation 2 cable schematic were found by
rendering pages and reading them, not by a gate — a gate proves text is
inside its own box, not that a caption is clear of its neighbour.

## Known open items

**None blocking.** The syntax window, carried as the one open item through
eight deliveries, was closed on 17 November: it is now gated, and the
prose of six books was changed to pass it. The ten-phase fix plan closed
the rest, and the three things it left are improvements rather than
defects.

**The wrong-option lines are gated for presence, not for truth.**
`freeze.py` records the `wrongs` field as a count, so a line rewritten at
the same length moves nothing in the baseline. Each of the 7,475 lines was
written against the passage in front of the author and checked for
presence and length; a sampled read by a second person is the obvious next
step. Two answer keys also cite a paragraph one short of the one their
explanation describes — in both the keyed option is still the best of the
four, so neither was changed under a phase whose declaration did not
permit it. Both are named in the completion report.

**Question-type distribution is now gated — this was the open item and
it is closed.** `IELTS_Balance_Plan.md` set out six further phases (10 to
15) and `IELTS_Balance_Report.md` records what they did.
`ielts/qtypes.py` classifies every item from its own shape, never from
its printed label; `ielts/gate10.py` holds the census against a floor, a
ceiling and a rule that every type must be taught as a set of at least
three questions in one exercise. The gate reported 80 failures when it
was written and reports **PASS** on all eight books now. The worst two
cases it found — Volume 6 running 62% of its listening as multiple
choice, Foundation 2 running 99% of its listening as gap completion — are
gone, and the Foundation books, which between them had put 593 of 593
listening questions into one task type, now teach all six.

What the gate proves is that every type is taught in every book and that
none dominates. It does **not** prove the shares match a current
Cambridge paper: the floors and ceilings are a declared design target,
because this container reaches package registries and nothing else and
cannot sample a real paper. The gate says so in its own docstring, and
the balance report says so twice.

**Every book now carries all four timed papers.** Fourteen Writing and
Speaking papers were written in phase 15, for Volumes 1 to 5 and both
Foundation books; before that only Volume 6 had them. The option pools
are still smaller than the real paper's: matching information offers four
candidate paragraphs where the real task offers every paragraph.

The remaining item is the one carried from the start. The syntax ladder is
a relative gate: it proves this series is internally graded, not that
Volume 4's 22.7 words a sentence is the right figure for band 6.5 in the
real exam. Settling that needs the mean sentence length and
nominalisation rate of twenty official Cambridge Academic passages, per
band — a measurement this container cannot make, because its network
policy reaches package registries and nothing else. If it is ever made,
the window centres move to it and the ladder stays.
