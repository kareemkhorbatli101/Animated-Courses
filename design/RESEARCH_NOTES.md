# Phase 0 · Research notes

What was looked at, what it says, and what each finding changes in the design.
Where a claim is contested or thinly sourced, that is stated. Nothing here is
treated as settled because it was convenient.

---

## 1. Specificity in ESP course design

**Finding.** Wide- and narrow-angle design is a continuum, not a binary, and the
degree of specificity has been debated for decades. Needs analysis is the one
point of agreement: it anchors design, and it is also the instrument for
revising and evaluating a course, not just starting it. I found no large
empirical study comparing narrow- and wide-angle outcomes, so claims that
narrow is simply better are not supportable.

**Design consequence.** The course goes narrow on *tasks and lexis* (the chair,
the counter, the record, the lecture) and stays wide on *transferable
sub-skills* (defining, sequencing, hedging, checking understanding). Where the
evidence is absent, the design declares a choice rather than citing authority
for it.

## 2. Vocabulary load per lesson

**Finding.** Commonly cited figures are 5–10 new words per lesson, and 8–12 in a
sixty-minute lesson; Nation's course-design target of roughly 1,000 word
families a year works out to about five words per contact hour. All of these
reach me second-hand through reviews and practitioner sources rather than from
the primary texts, so they are rules of thumb, not constants. There is no agreed
ideal number, and the answer depends on whether "learned" means recognition or
production.

**Design consequence — and this is the single most important correction to the
First Edition.** Each unit carries:

- a **productive core of 8–10 items** that the learner is expected to say and
  write, and that the tests and the unit review actually assess; and
- a **receptive set of up to 10–14 further items** met in context, glossed, and
  assessed only for recognition.

The First Edition tested all twenty productively, which is well above every
figure above. The split is declared per unit in the data, not left implicit.

## 3. Spacing and retrieval

**Finding.** This is the best-evidenced strand of the lot. A meta-analysis of 48
experiments found spacing has a medium-to-large effect on L2 learning overall,
smaller for vocabulary than for grammar (g ≈ 0.34 vs 0.56), but with a large
benefit from *longer* gaps specifically for vocabulary retention (g ≈ 1.27).
Expanding versus equal intervals is **unsettled**: one L2 study found a small
advantage for expanding gaps, general-memory work found no significant
difference, and an L2 synthesis found equal and expanding statistically
equivalent. Number of sessions and gap length matter more than the shape of the
schedule, and with three or more sessions the relative spacing appears not to
matter.

**Design consequence.** Three or more encounters per productive item, with the
later gaps long rather than short — not an elaborate expanding algorithm the
evidence does not support. Concretely: first encounter in the unit, second in
the unit review, third in the review unit after a gap of several units, fourth
in the book's end review. The recycling gate counts encounters and gap length,
and deliberately does not optimise interval shape.

## 4. Academic listening, and why most EAP listening material fails

**Finding.** A corpus study comparing EAP listening coursebooks with 160 real
lectures from an academic spoken corpus found that books stress identifying main
points but give little training in the markers lecturers actually use to signal
importance, and that the examples given are few and prototypical (the canonical
"the important point is"). Separately, reviews report that signalling cues can
support L2 listeners' comprehension and recall, but hedge heavily: there is no
single rule that holds across lectures.

**Design consequence.** The student series teaches importance marking from a
*range* of real forms — repetition, reformulation, slowed delivery, "so what
this means for you in clinic is", "you'll be asked this" — not a list of four
textbook phrases. Scripts are written to carry those markers deliberately, and
the teacher's book says which marker each script is exercising. This is a
genuine differentiator, cheaply obtained.

## 5. Medical terminology and morphology

**Finding.** Sources agree on the method — teach word parts before whole terms —
but disagree on order: some argue suffix-first because terms are decoded from
the suffix backwards, others root-first, others grouping roots by body system so
related roots reinforce. The commonly quoted claim is that 50–150 word parts
unlock the majority of clinical vocabulary. The sources are overwhelmingly
commercial study guides; the retention claims are asserted without studies
behind them.

**Design consequence.** Morphology is taught in the **student** series as a
decoding strategy, grouped by system (which is the ordering with a defensible
rationale rather than a tradition), introduced from the first book and recycled
on the same schedule as lexis. It appears in the practitioner series only where
a practitioner must *read* a term, not build one. No claim about numbers of
terms unlocked appears anywhere in the books.

## 6. What published dentistry English actually teaches

**Finding.** The available materials are mostly university manuals and online
units rather than major coursebooks. A representative manual moves: medical
professions → body systems → parts of a tooth → types of teeth → dental diseases
→ periodontal disease, gingivitis, caries, pulpitis → instruments, with a grammar
supplement at the back. Another textbook runs fourteen units, each in four
parts, with a glossary. Online units are organised by specialty (endodontics,
orthodontics) or around the dental team and the parts of the dental unit.
Chairside verb lists exist and are usefully organised by agent — what the
clinician does, what the patient does, what the tooth does.

**Design consequence.** Two things to copy and one to avoid. Copy: the
disease/condition sequence, which is how the field itself organises the content;
and the agent-split verb treatment, which is a neat solution to a real problem.
Avoid: the grammar-supplement-at-the-back structure, which detaches grammar from
use. Our grammar is drawn out of the unit's phrase bank in every unit.

## 7. Assessment orientation (OET)

**Finding.** Writing is assessed on six criteria, with purpose first — whether
the reason for the letter is clear from the start and sustained — then content,
conciseness and clarity, genre and style, organisation and layout, and language.
The referral letter is the dominant task at roughly 180–200 words. Speaking is
role-play with an interlocutor playing a patient or relative, assessed on
linguistic criteria plus clinical communication criteria. Assessors do not count
errors; they match performance to descriptors.

**Design consequence.** The practitioner writing strand is built so that its
third task at each level is a referral-shaped letter, and the writing checklists
are worded as the criteria are worded — purpose first. Speaking role cards give
the interlocutor a character and a concern, not just a prompt. We are not
building an exam course, but aligning the genres costs nothing and makes the
books usable for candidates.

## 8. Dental curriculum, year one

**Finding.** Across several dental schools the first year is consistent: gross
anatomy, head and neck anatomy, histology and oral histology, embryology,
physiology, biochemistry, microbiology and immunology; dental/oral anatomy with
morphology and occlusion; dental materials science or biomaterials; and a
two-semester preclinical laboratory course introducing materials, instruments
and laboratory procedure. Infection prevention, basic instrumentation,
ergonomics and isolation are taught in simulation in the first year.

**Design consequence.** This is the spine of *English for Dental Students*
books 1–3, and it settles the mirroring question from the plan: the student
series is sequenced by this, not by the patient visit. Books 4–6 move into
supervised clinical work, which is where the two series converge and where
practitioner material is legitimately reused.

## 9. Dental instruments: classification

**Finding.** Classification varies by source. A common functional scheme is
cutting (hatchets, chisels, hoes, excavators), condensing, plastic, finishing
and polishing, isolation, and miscellaneous (mirrors, probes, scissors, pliers).
A diagnostic/exploring group holds mouth mirror, explorer/probe, tweezers,
retractors. Elevators and forceps are extraction instruments in most schemes,
though one source files forceps oddly. Black's nomenclature names an instrument
by purpose, manner of use, working-end design and shank shape, which is why
names like "bin-angle spoon excavator" are built the way they are.

**Design consequence.** The inventory uses the functional grouping, flags that
schemes differ, and teaches Black's naming logic in the **student** series as a
decoding pattern — it is a morphology lesson in disguise and exactly the kind of
thing a student is examined on.

## 10. Tooth notation

**Finding.** FDI two-digit notation is the international standard, ISO 3950
("Dentistry — Designation system for teeth and areas of the oral cavity"): first
digit quadrant, second digit position from the midline; primary teeth take
quadrants 5–8. The Universal system (1–32, primary A–T) is US practice; Palmer
notation uses a quadrant symbol plus a number and is preferred in the UK. The
systems collide — "11" through "18" mean different teeth in FDI and Universal —
and a dot is sometimes inserted in FDI codes to disambiguate. Historical dates
for ADA recommendations conflict between sources and are not used.

**Design consequence.** Both series teach **FDI as the default** and explicitly
teach the collision, because an international learner will meet all three. A
notation figure appears in Book 1 of each series and is recycled. The collision
is a genuinely useful teaching point and no existing material we found covers it.

## 11. Teacher's books: what the market expects

**Finding.** The conventional contents are an answer key, an overview of the
language aims, unit-by-unit step-by-step notes with warm-up and wrap-up ideas,
background notes, audio scripts, extra activities including mixed-ability and
fast-finisher options, photocopiable supplementary pages at the back, and
progress tests. The stated purpose is efficiency: teachers are short of time.

**Design consequence.** Ours carries all of that and adds four things the
convention omits and this subject needs: pronunciation notes for the unit's
medical lexis, domain notes for the non-specialist teacher, two explicit timing
routes, and the mixed-group problem (practitioners and students in one room).
