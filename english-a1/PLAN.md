# Wayfare 1 — the A1 level

**The full plan, second pass.** A1 of our general-English series, modeled on *American
Headway Starter* (OUP, 3rd edition) and its British parent *Headway Beginner*, written
from scratch: 17 units, 932 exercises, a specified visual for every one of them, and
7,509 checks.

> **What the second pass added.** The first pass fixed the syllabus and listed every
> exercise. This one adds the three things it was missing: **a visual for every
> exercise**, drawn from a 28-type system in which no visual is allowed to be
> decorative; **a check architecture** of 7 checks per exercise and 5·(u+1) per unit,
> so that the checking of unit *u* includes five targeted cross-checks against every
> unit before it; and **the evidence** the whole design rests on, researched before
> the checks were written rather than after (§7). §19 lists the eleven things this
> pass found wrong in the first one.

---

## In one page

**The book.** 17 units (an on-ramp plus 16), 4 review Loops, 100 teaching hours. 798
lexical items on a budget of 48 a unit. 42 grammar points, enumerated in Appendix A.
16 pronunciation points, each revisited twice. 34 functions. 16 videos rendered by the
engine this repository already has. 592 Student Book exercises (536 in the units, 56
in the Loops) and 340 Workbook exercises — every one named, typed, visualized and
checked in this document.

**The visuals.** 932 of them, one per exercise, from 28 types in five functional
classes. The research on coursebook images says more than half of the pictures in
published ELT material are decorative (§7.7). Ours cannot be: a visual that no rubric
refers to and that carries no task data fails check **E6** and the exercise does not
ship. Every visual declares its class — presentational, task-bearing, referential,
form-making or organizational — and five of the 28 types exist only to make an
invisible thing visible: stress, sound, intonation, the shape of a paradigm, the shape
of a transaction.

**The checks.** Three layers, 7,509 passes.

| Layer | Per unit of measurement | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Per exercise, 7 checks | 932 exercises | **6,524** |
| Per unit, 5 intra + 5 against each earlier unit = 5·(u+1) | 17 units | **765** |
| Per Loop, 5 intra + 5 against each of its four units | 4 Loops | **100** |
| Book-level, twelve families of ten | the book | **120** |
| | | **7,509** |

The middle layer is the new idea. Unit 1 is checked five times on its own and five
times against Unit 0. Unit 16 is checked five times on its own and five times against
each of the sixteen units before it: 85 checks. By the end of the book the checking is
mostly *cross-checking*, which is where cohesion failures actually live — a word
re-taught, a character who changed jobs, a key pattern repeated, a text that got
easier instead of harder, a 7-gram shared with an earlier unit.

**The evidence.** Nine findings decided the check list before it was written (§7):
Mayer's twelve multimedia principles; the 6-to-20 range for vocabulary encounters and
the spacing meta-analysis behind it; the 2025 finding that interleaving *hurts* low
achievers without blocked practice first; the 95–98 per cent lexical-coverage
threshold; Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez's 31 item-writing guidelines; Tomlinson's
materials principles; the research on decorative images; WCAG 2.2's contrast and
colour rules; and the CEFR Companion Volume's A1 descriptors.

**What I still need from you.** The five decisions in §18.4, unchanged: the name,
sixteen units or fourteen, where the video sits commercially, how far the Arabic
edition goes, and American or British English.

## 0. How this was researched

Two passes, both from a container with no general outbound web access: a search tool
returns summaries of pages, and the pages themselves cannot be opened. Oxford's
catalogue pages and the TESL-EJ reviews of the Starter books were read only in
summary. So claims about the model carry a confidence mark — **[firm]** (the
publisher, a published review, or two independent sources agreeing), **[likely]** (one
source, or inferred from the British parent), **[open]** (not established) — and
nothing in the plan depends on an **[open]** one.

The second pass researched something different: not the model, but the **evidence for
the checks**. Nine strands, in §7, each ending in the checks it licenses. The order
matters and is the point of the brief: the kinds of check were decided from the
research, and only then written down as a register (Appendix D).

### 1.2 The unit

The Teacher's Book states the order as *Starter · presentation of new language ·
practice · skills* **[firm]**:

1. **Starter** — a short warm-up.
2. **Presentation** — the structure in a dialogue, text or listening, with a **Grammar
   Spot**: a guided-discovery box ending in a cross-reference to the **Grammar
   Reference** **[firm]**.
3. **Practice** — controlled exercises.
4. **Vocabulary** — a lexical set tied to the topic.
5. **Skills work** — reading, listening, speaking.
6. **Everyday English** — the functional strand: social expressions and survival
   situations **[firm]**. For Starter: greetings and introductions, the alphabet, on
   the phone, numbers 11–30, days of the week, *How much is it?*, *Can I …?*,
   directions, social expressions **[likely]**.

Back matter: a **Writing** section of one task per unit, collected at the back and
built on a model text to analyze and imitate **[firm]**; a **Grammar Reference** with
exercises; a word list; audio scripts; irregular verbs; phonetic symbols **[firm]**.

### 1.4 Components and assessment

Student Book with an access code to the **Oxford Online Skills Program**, 30 hours a
level **[firm]**. **Workbook** with and without key, plus **iChecker** self-assessment
**[firm]**. **Teacher's Book** with unit notes, **Possible problems** boxes, **EXTRA
IDEA** and **SUGGESTION** boxes, photocopiables, keys, audio scripts **[firm]**.
**Testing program**, downloadable: 14 unit tests in A/B versions, four Stop and Check
quizzes, three Progress tests, a skills test, exit tests, a series placement test
**[firm]**. **iTools** for the classroom **[firm]**; the British fifth edition
replaces it with a Classroom Presentation Tool, a Teacher's Resource Centre, Online
Practice and unit-opener videos **[firm]**.

### 1.5 What to keep and what to change

Keep: the six-page unit with a fixed internal order; guided discovery in a box rather
than an explanation; a functional strand that runs beside the grammar instead of being
folded into it; a deliberately small lexical syllabus; and *Possible problems*, the
most useful heading in ELT publishing, because it names the error before the class
makes it.

Change, and each of these becomes a decision in §4: writing is exiled to the back of
the book; pronunciation is scattered rather than sequenced; the ramp starts above the
floor of a true beginner in a non-Latin script; recycling is implicit, with nothing
guaranteeing that a taught item returns; fourteen units against four Stop-and-Checks
and three Progress tests is arithmetic that does not divide; the video is a wrapper
rather than a strand; and — the finding that drove this second pass — **the images are
mostly decorative**, which the research in §7.7 says is the norm and not an accident.

## 2. The line between modeling and copying

A practical working rule, not legal advice; a publishing lawyer signs off before the
first unit is sold.

### 2.1 What copyright does not reach

- **The grammatical sequence of English for beginners.** That *be* precedes the
  present simple, which precedes the past simple, is a fact about the language and a
  century-old teaching convention.
- **Functional and lexical topics.** Greetings, the alphabet, numbers, family, jobs,
  food, the time, directions, shopping, travel: the obligatory furniture of an A1
  course, *scènes à faire* in the copyright sense.
- **Exercise types.** Gap-fill, matching, multiple choice, transformation, information
  gap, dictation, role-play, listen-and-repeat, odd-one-out.
- **The idea of a fixed unit shape**, and of a functional strand beside the grammar.
- **CEFR descriptors**, published by the Council of Europe for reuse with attribution.
- **Design principles.** Mayer's twelve, the coverage thresholds, the item-writing
  guidelines: published research, cited and applied.

### 2.2 What copyright does reach, and what we therefore originate

Every word of running text — passages, dialogues, audio scripts, rubrics, grammar
explanations, example sentences, headings, captions, keys. Characters and their
worlds, names, biographies, photographs, artwork, maps, realia. The particular
selection and arrangement of a word list: we teach the same high-frequency words
because at A1 there is no other choice, but the list is built from our own source
(the A1 band of a published core list, filtered by our corpus counts and our topics).
Their names and trade dress. Their test items.

### 2.3 The rules we work under

1. **No source text is open while writing.** The §1.3 grid — grammar points and topics,
   which are facts — is the only thing carried across.
2. **Rename everything**: sections, unit titles, box names, back matter. §4.3.
3. **Re-sequence deliberately**: 14 units become 17, four review cycles that divide the
   course, five grammar points moved (§5.3).
4. **Change the shape of the unit, not just its labels.** Writing returns to the unit;
   pronunciation becomes a numbered strand; video becomes a syllabus; every exercise
   gets a visual that has to earn its place.
5. **Every example sentence is ours and is about our world** — the fixed cast and city
   in Appendix E, which is also what makes cross-unit continuity checkable.
6. **A mechanical originality gate before each unit ships** (§11.6): an n-gram sweep
   against a held corpus, a structure diff, a name sweep, an artwork sweep.
7. **Keep the record.** Each unit's data file carries a `provenance` block naming what
   came from research and what is ours, and the block is itself checked.

### 2.4 Why this is safe

We are not reproducing a book. We are teaching a language at a level with a century of
settled practice, in a format our own engine renders, with a visual system and a
verification regime that no A1 course on the market has. What overlap remains after
§2.3 is the overlap any two A1 courses have with each other.

## 4. The fourteen decisions that make our A1 different

The first twelve are from the first pass; 13 and 14 are this pass.

**1. An on-ramp that assumes nothing.** Unit 0, *First Words*: the Latin alphabet by
name and by sound, 0–10, classroom instructions, six fixed phrases, four hours. The
CEFR Companion Volume recognizes Pre-A1; our model starts above it.

**2. Writing lives in the unit**, on page 7, at the point where the language for it
has just been taught — not collected at the back where it gets skipped.

**3. Pronunciation is a numbered syllabus**: sixteen points, one per unit, each
revisited twice, printed in the contents, tested in the Loops, and carried by three
visual types that exist for nothing else (§8.2).

**4. Four blocks of four, closed by a Loop** that reviews, tests, produces and
records. The arithmetic divides: units 4, 8, 12, 16.

**5. Every taught item is guaranteed to come back** — and the threshold is now the
research's, not a guess: **ten encounters** across the level, spaced, with at least
four after the teaching unit (§7.2, check X1).

**6. The video is a strand**: sixteen scripted scenes, each carrying its unit's
function twice — once where it fails, once where it works.

**7. Audio is scripted for listening, not for reading aloud.** Two versions of every
listening: the clean studio take and a natural-speed take with hesitation and overlap.

**8. A spoken-first order inside the unit**: present, notice, drill, *say it*, and only
then read.

**9. The lexical syllabus is budgeted and sourced**: 48 items a unit, hard cap 55, 798
in the level, every reading text ≥98 per cent inside the cumulative list (§7.4).

**10. Checks before a page is printed**, in the manner of the two SAT books in this
repository: §11's 7,509.

**11. Two editions from one source** — international and Arabic-support, one set of
data files, two builds.

**12. A self-study path that is actually complete**: the `S` and `C/S` set, the audio,
the videos and the online bank reach the end of A1 without a classroom.

**13. A visual for every exercise, and none of them decorative.** 932 visuals from 28
types in five functional classes, each declaring what cognitive work it does, each
tied to its rubric by Mayer's spatial-contiguity rule, each with alt text, each
checked (§8, check E6). The research says more than half the images in published ELT
material are decorative; ours fail the build if they are.

**14. Checking that grows with the book.** Five checks per unit against *every* earlier
unit, so the cost of cohesion failures is paid when they are made, not at the end:
Unit 16 is checked 85 times, 80 of them against its predecessors (§11.3).

### 5.3 The five things we moved, and why

1. **Numbers 11–100 move up to Unit 2** (their Unit 5 territory). Phone numbers and
   ages are needed from the second hour of any real class.
2. **Prices move down to Unit 3** from their Unit 7. A beginner buys something before
   they describe a kitchen.
3. **Object pronouns move into Unit 7** with *like*, where they are needed (*I like
   it*), instead of appearing in a grammar box with no work to do.
4. **The past splits into three units, not two**: *was/were*, then regular, then
   irregular with questions and negatives. The single hardest week in an A1 course is
   the one where regular and irregular past arrive together.
5. **Unit 16 is a consolidation unit with new functional work**, not a fourteenth
   dose of new grammar. The level ends by putting the auxiliaries beside each other,
   which is the one thing an A1 student has never seen in one place.

## 7. The evidence the design rests on

Nine strands, researched before the check list was written. Each ends in the checks it
licenses, named as they appear in the register (Appendix D). Sources in §20.

### 7.1 Mayer's twelve principles of multimedia learning

The twelve are multimedia, coherence, signalling, redundancy, spatial contiguity,
temporal contiguity, segmenting, pre-training, modality, personalization, voice and
image. A 2024 review notes that modality has the strongest evidence base, followed by
redundancy, multimedia, signalling and coherence, with pre-training and voice the
thinnest — so the design leans on the strong four and treats the thin two as
preferences, not rules.

**What it licenses.** *Multimedia*: every exercise has a visual (decision 13).
*Spatial contiguity*: a visual sits within one column-width of the rubric it serves,
never on the facing page — **E6**. *Coherence*: no visual element that the task does
not use, which is the non-decorative rule — **E6**. *Signalling*: the form-making
visual types carry callouts, arrows and colour-coded slots — **V17, V18, V25, V26,
V27**. *Redundancy*: the audio script is never printed beside the audio task; it lives
in the Audio File at the back — **E3**. *Pre-training*: the Words slot precedes the
Hear it slot in every unit, so the lexical set is known before the listening needs it
— **U1**. *Segmenting*: listenings run 30–60 seconds, videos 60–150, both in bands —
**U4**.

### 7.2 How many encounters a word needs, and how to space them

The literature gives a range rather than a number: 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, more than 20,
depending on the word, the learner and the test. Nation's own estimate is that for
many words around ten to twelve encounters are needed; Laufer and Nation describe a
rough consensus that ten exposures give some chance of later recognition; Horst et al.
found at least eight; Webb more than ten. Kim and Webb's meta-analysis of spaced
practice in L2 found longer spacing better for retention, with a larger effect on
grammar (g = 0.56) than vocabulary (g = 0.34) and the largest effect for vocabulary
when spacing happened *within* a session (g = 0.79).

**What it licenses.** The recycling threshold moves from the first pass's three
returns to **ten encounters** across the level for every one of the 798 items, of
which at least three are in the teaching unit itself (the within-session effect) and
at least four fall in later units, at least one of them two or more blocks later —
**X1**, and book-level **E family**. The first pass's figure of three was a guess and
is now corrected.

### 7.3 Interleaving is not free

A 2025 *Language Learning* study found interleaved practice *hurt* low-achieving
adolescents when it replaced initial blocked practice: the hybrid group retained
better and the interleaved group forgot faster at two weeks. The authors call
interleaving an undesirable difficulty in that condition. Bjork's own framework puts
the same boundary on it: novices need explicit instruction and worked examples before
mixed practice.

**What it licenses.** The Drill slot is **blocked** — one point at a time, in a fixed
difficulty order — and interleaving is confined to the Loops and to the Workbook's
review slot. A check forbids a unit from mixing two grammar points in one Drill
exercise before its block's Loop — **U3** and **X2**.

### 7.4 The lexical coverage threshold

95 per cent of the running words known is the floor for basic comprehension and about
98 per cent for unassisted reading; the thresholds are soft rather than sharp, and
coverage is necessary but not sufficient. At A1, where a text is sixty words long,
98 per cent means at most one unknown word in the text.

**What it licenses.** Every reading text, audio script and video script must be
**≥98 per cent inside the cumulative Word File** at its unit, with unknown items
capped at three per Model slot and each one glossed by a visual rather than a
translation — **E4**, measured over about 60,000 running words.

### 7.5 Item-writing guidelines

Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez validated 31 guidelines for multiple-choice items,
grouped as content (8), format (2), style (3), the stem (4) and the choices (14); the
1989 taxonomy behind it drew 43 rules from 46 textbooks, with solid consensus on 33.
Later work tested a subset empirically — avoiding *all of the above* and *none of the
above*, avoiding absolute determiners, stating the stem as a question, keeping option
lengths consistent.

**What it licenses.** Eleven of the 31 apply to an A1 exercise and are written into
**E3**: one unambiguously best answer; options homogeneous in content, grammar and
length; no *all/none of the above*; no absolute determiners; no negative stems; no
clueing between items in the same exercise; options in a logical order where one
exists; distractors drawn from our own error list rather than invented; the stem a
question or a complete frame; no repeated words between stem and key; no option
occupying more than 40 per cent of the keys in an exercise.

### 7.6 Materials-development principles

Tomlinson's principles for materials development begin with *impact* — achieved
through novelty, variety, attractive presentation — and continue through achievable
challenge, learner self-investment and personalization. Littlejohn's framework for
analysing materials distinguishes what materials *say* they do from what their tasks
actually ask the learner to do, which is the gap a plan like this one has to close.

**What it licenses.** Variety: no two consecutive exercises of the same type, and all
five visual classes present in every unit — **U3**. Self-investment: at least four
exercises per unit require the student's own information — **U3**. Impact: a new
visual type in every unit, and a unit-opener scene that is never reused — **U3**,
**X3**. And the Littlejohn point becomes the per-exercise claim in **E7**: every
exercise states what the learner actually does, in a verb, and is checked against the
slot's purpose.

### 7.7 Images in coursebooks are mostly decorative

Hill's analysis of three intermediate coursebooks found over half of the photographs
and drawings served decoration alone, and that coursebooks offer few opportunities for
learners to use a picture to generate their own meaning. Romney's six-category study
found most images carried a passive function with no active task attached. A 2019
reanalysis found the majority were neither decorative nor instructional but
*supportive*, and argued the field needs a better classification. Dual coding is the
mechanism that makes a picture work; a picture only works as a mnemonic if the learner
already knows its parts.

**What it licenses.** The whole of §8: a five-class functional typology in which
*decorative* is not a class, a declared class for every one of the 932 visuals, and
**E6**, which fails any visual that no rubric refers to and that carries no task data.
It also licenses the rule that a visual may not depend on words the student does not
yet have — its labels must be in the Word File by that unit.

### 7.8 Accessibility

WCAG 2.2 AA requires 4.5:1 contrast for normal text, 3:1 for large text and for
graphics and interface components, and that colour is never the only means of
conveying information. Images of text carry the same contrast requirement, and images
need text alternatives.

**What it licenses.** **E6**'s accessibility half: 4.5:1 on any text inside a visual,
3:1 on the strokes and fills that carry meaning, a second channel (shape, label,
pattern) wherever colour sorts anything — which matters most in the sound matrices,
where /s/, /z/ and /ɪz/ columns are also labelled and shaped — and alt text of at most
25 words on every visual, written at A1 and recorded in the data file.

### 7.9 The CEFR descriptors

The Companion Volume (2018, 2020) carries the illustrative descriptors, adds Pre-A1
and enriches A1. A1 spoken interaction is conditioned on the interlocutor: the learner
can interact simply provided the other person talks slowly and clearly and is prepared
to help.

**What it licenses.** The 32 descriptors in Appendix C, one named by every exercise
(**E7**), four worked per unit with at least two exercises and one Can I? item each
(**U5**), eight listed per Loop portfolio page. The interlocutor condition licenses
the natural-speed second take: a student who has only heard careful speech has not met
the descriptor's condition, they have met a kinder one.

### 8.5 The eight rules

1. **A visual serves a task.** Every one is referred to by its rubric or holds data the
   task needs. No exceptions, not even the unit opener, which is exercise 2's input.
2. **Adjacency.** Within one column-width of its rubric, on the same page. Mayer's
   spatial contiguity; also the reason no exercise spans a page turn.
3. **No word the student has not met.** Labels inside a visual must be in the Word File
   by that unit, which the lexis check reads from the visual's label list.
4. **No visual replaces the audio script, and no script replaces the visual.** Mayer's
   redundancy principle: the Audio File lives at the back.
5. **Colour is never the only channel.** Every colour-coded distinction is also a
   shape, a label or a position.
6. **Contrast.** 4.5:1 for text inside a visual, 3:1 for meaning-bearing strokes and
   fills.
7. **Alt text that does the same job as the picture**, not a description of it: for a
   sound matrix, the sorted answer is not in the alt text; for a photo scene, the
   objects the task needs are.
8. **The cast is the cast.** The same eight people, drawn consistently, ageing only
   where the text says so (Appendix E, check **X4**).

## 10. What each slot is for

Unchanged from the first pass, with the visual default for each slot added in
brackets. What a slot may *not* do is as important as what it does.

**Open** (2) — recycles the previous unit and surfaces what the class
already knows about this one. Never introduces new language. Always spoken or
non-verbal; never written production.

**Model** (3) — the target structure, in a dialogue of 40–70 words or a text of
40–80, heard before it is read in units 0–8 and read with audio support from 9.
Contains the structure at least four times. Contains at most three unknown words
beyond the unit's lexical set, each glossed in a picture or a cognate.

**Notice** (1, type T13) — the paradigm, incomplete, for the student to finish from
the Model, in no more than four lines, ending in the Grammar File cross-reference.
Never an explanation in prose. A sentence of metalanguage is a failure here.

**Drill** (4) — controlled practice, in a fixed difficulty order: recognition
(T09/T06), then production of the form (T07/T11/T12), then self-monitoring (T10).
Entirely within the taught lexis. Answers are closed.

**Say it** (2) — the unit's pronunciation point, always *hear it, sort it, say it*:
T05 or T14 then T02. Never silent.

**Words** (3) — the lexical set, presented in a group of 8–20, worked twice (T06 or
T16, then T14 or T15), and then used in one personal exchange. The set is a picture
set wherever a picture is possible.

**Hear it** (3) — two listenings: the clean one (T03/T04/T05) and the natural one
(same content, natural speed and hesitation, with an easier task). The task before
the audio is always about the picture, never about the words.

**Use it** (2) — the production slot: an information gap (T18) or a survey (T20),
then a short personal report. Mistakes are expected here and are not corrected on
the spot; the Teacher's Guide says so in those words.

**Real Life** (4) — the function. Always: hear the exchange (T04/T05), notice the
phrases (T06), practice them (T07/T11), use them in a role-play with a goal (T19).
The phrases are listed in a box of six to eight, which is the box students
photograph.

**Read it** (3) — one text, 40 words in Unit 1 rising to 140 in Unit 16, on our own
material, with one pre-task (T06/T16), one comprehension task (T17) and one
personal-response question.

**On Screen** (2) — the video: predict from a still (T05), watch for gist (T17),
watch for the functional language (T04), and a line to perform (T02/T19).

**Write it** (2) — read the model, notice one feature of written English (capitals,
the comma in a date, the greeting line of a message), write 40–60 words to a
checklist. The checklist is five items and the student uses it before the teacher
sees the work.

**Can I?** (1, type T22) — ten items across the unit's grammar, lexis, function and
pronunciation, with a score band and, for each item missed, the page to go back to.

## 11. The check architecture

Three layers. The first two are new in this pass; the third is the first pass's,
raised from 100 to 120.

```
       per exercise              per unit and Loop              per book
       7 checks                  5 intra + 5 per earlier unit   12 families of 10
       × 932 exercises           Σ over 17 units = 765
       = 6,524              +    + 4 Loops × 25 = 100       +   = 120    →  7,509
```

### 11.1 Why seven per exercise and not five

The brief asks for five or more. Seven is the number that covers the five failure
modes an A1 exercise actually has, plus the two this pass added. An exercise can be:
in the wrong place (**E1**); the wrong shape for its slot (**E2**); badly written as
an item (**E3**); made of language the student has not met (**E4**); keyed wrongly or
unanswerably (**E5**); given a picture that does nothing, or that excludes a reader
(**E6**); or pedagogically pointless — answerable without the target language, or
serving no descriptor (**E7**). Five of these were in the first pass; **E6** and
**E7** are this pass's, and they are the two that the research in §7 made unavoidable.

| | Check | What it tests | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **E1** | identity and placement | id, unit, slot, position, global number agree; the exercise appears once | a renumbered unit leaves a stale id |
| **E2** | skeleton conformance | slot, type, path tag and minutes match the skeleton row; only rows 9, 14, 17 may vary, and only within their allowed pair | an author swaps a controlled drill for a free task |
| **E3** | item form | rubric ≤8 words from the type's template; example item present; item count in band; the eleven item-writing rules of §7.5; no audio script printed beside its own task | *all of the above*; options of unequal length; a clue in item 3 that answers item 5 |
| **E4** | lexis and coverage | every word in rubric, items, key, visual labels and audio script is in the cumulative Word File; ≥98% coverage in texts; ≤3 glossed items in a Model | a reading text with four unknown words |
| **E5** | key integrity | key present, shaped for the type, variants listed; no option letter over 40% within the exercise; no identical key sequence elsewhere in the unit | a transformation drill whose key omits the contracted form |
| **E6** | visual integrity | the visual exists, declares type and class, is referred to by the rubric or carries task data, sits within a column-width of it, labels in the Word File, alt text ≤25 words, 4.5:1 and 3:1 contrast, colour never alone | a pretty photograph nobody looks at |
| **E7** | pedagogical warrant | one of the 32 descriptors named; a one-verb statement of what the learner does, consistent with the slot; and the no-guess test: the exercise cannot be completed correctly without the target language | a *there is/are* drill answerable from the picture alone |

**The no-guess test inside E7** is the sharpest of the seven, and the one most likely
to catch a weak exercise. For every closed exercise the check asks whether a student
who knows the lexis but *not* the target structure could score above chance. Where the
answer is yes, the exercise is rebuilt. A beginner's drill that can be passed by
pattern-matching the picture is not teaching the grammar.

### 11.2 The five intra-unit checks

| | Check | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| **U1** | completeness and order | 32 exercises, slots in skeleton order, minutes 270 ± 10, the Words slot before the Hear it slot (Mayer's pre-training), one video, two listenings in two versions each |
| **U2** | internal spacing | each of the unit's 48 new items appears at least three times inside the unit, in at least two different slots — the within-session spacing effect of §7.2 |
| **U3** | variety and investment | no two consecutive exercises of the same type; all five visual classes present; at least one visual type new to the book; at least four exercises requiring the student's own information; the Drill slot blocked, never interleaved |
| **U4** | media integrity | audio durations 30–60 s (clean) and 30–70 s (natural), video 60–150 s, every script in the Audio File matches the recording to the word, the natural take carries at least two hesitation or overlap features |
| **U5** | descriptor closure | the unit's four descriptors each have at least two exercises and one Can I? item; the Can I? items cover grammar, lexis, function and pronunciation |

### 11.3 The five cross-unit checks, run for every earlier unit

This is the layer the brief asks for. For unit *u* and each earlier unit *p*, five
checks run. Unit 1 runs them once (against Unit 0); Unit 16 runs them sixteen times.
They were chosen because cohesion failures in a coursebook are *pairwise* failures —
something taught twice, something that contradicts something, something that got
easier — and a book-level aggregate hides them.

| | Check | What it compares, for every pair (u, p) |
|---|---|---|
| **X1** | lexical | no item taught in *p* is taught again as new in *u*; items of *p* that fall due in *u* under the spacing schedule actually appear; *u*'s cumulative Word File is a superset of *p*'s; the ten-encounter obligation of §7.2 is on track for every item of *p* |
| **X2** | grammatical | *u* teaches no point that *p* taught; *u* uses at least one of *p*'s points productively, not only receptively; *u* uses nothing from a later unit; *u*'s Notice box contradicts none of *p*'s (no rule stated here that was stated differently there) |
| **X3** | duplication and keys | no shared seven-gram between *u* and *p* outside the fixed-phrase allow-list; no exercise in *u* has the same item set as one in *p*; the key-letter sequences of *u* and *p* differ; the phrase boxes share at most two phrases; the unit-opener visual is not a reuse of *p*'s |
| **X4** | world continuity | every fact about every recurring character in *u* agrees with every statement in *p* — job, city, family, age, workplace — or the text accounts for the change; place names and their spellings match; the drawn cast matches |
| **X5** | the ladder | where *u* > *p*: reading length, mean sentence length, new-item load, audio words-per-minute and required output length are all ≥ *p*'s; *u*'s reading coverage is ≥98% against the Word File as it stands at *u*; no text in *u* is easier than the matching text in *p* by more than one band |

Linear in the unit number, as the brief asks: unit *u* carries 5·(u+1) checks, which

Two practical consequences. First, the checks are cheap to run and expensive to
ignore: each pair check is a small deterministic comparison of two data files, so the
whole 765 runs in seconds, and a failure names the pair. Second, **the cost of adding
a unit rises**, which is the right incentive: a unit that does not fit the book is
caught by the unit it does not fit.

### 11.5 Book-level checks: twelve families of ten

| Family | What it holds |
|---|---|

| **I** the visual system | every visual classed; class shares within ±5 points of §8.1; at most 8 photographs a unit; 28 types all used; no type used in more than 12 per cent of exercises; contrast and colour rules |
| **J** the Arabic edition | every Grammar File page, lexical set and *likely trouble* note has its Arabic counterpart; ≥92 per cent Arabic script; no Latin beyond the allow-list |
| **K** the document | page count in band, every unit opening recto, no exercise split across a page, cross-references resolved, the Picture File complete |
| **L** originality | the n-gram sweep clean against the held corpus; no character, place, brand or title collision; every artwork credited; every unit's provenance block complete |

### 11.6 The originality gate

Before a unit ships: an **n-gram sweep** — any run of seven words shared with the held
competitor corpus that is not a fixed phrase of English is rewritten, and the
fixed-phrase allow-list is itself reviewed; a **structure diff** against the §1.3 grid,
where overlap in grammar point and topic is expected and overlap in example sentences,
names, numbers or the order of a lexical set is a flag; a **name sweep**; an **artwork
sweep** against `credits.yaml`; and the **provenance block**, which names what came
from research and what is ours.

**The projects.** Loop 1 *Our class in twelve photos* — a wall of name cards,
countries and jobs. Loop 2 *A map of our street* — ten places, directions between
three, a recorded tour. Loop 3 *A day we remember* — a two-minute story to camera with
five past verbs and two sequencers. Loop 4 *Four minutes about my year in English* —
a profile, a past story, a present description and a plan: the level in one piece.

**The tests.** Four Loop tests of 40 items (grammar 16, lexis 10, function 6,
listening 5, reading 3) in A and B versions; two skills tests, after Loops 2 and 4,
testing reading, listening, writing and speaking separately against printed criteria;
the exit test, Loop 4's at double length; and a six-page placement and on-ramp
screener that decides between Unit 0 and Unit 1 for a new student.

**Checks on a Loop.** A Loop runs the five intra-unit checks adapted — U1
completeness, U2 spacing across the block rather than the unit, U3 variety, U4 media,
U5 descriptor closure for all eight of the block's statements — plus **X1–X5 against
each of its four units**: 5 + 20 = 25 checks a Loop, 100 across the four. These 100
are additional to the 765 of §11.4, which counts units only; the Loops are not
cross-checked against each other, because they share content by design.

### 18.4 The five decisions that are yours

1. **The name.** Wayfare, or *Mileposts* / *Plain Lines* / *Common Hours*, or yours.
2. **Sixteen units or fourteen.** Sixteen gives the 4 × 4 symmetry; fourteen matches

5. **American or British English.** The plan is written American; a British build is a

## 19. What this second pass changed

A verification pass is worth having only if it finds things. Eleven:

1. **The visuals did not exist.** The first pass specified 932 exercises and not one
   picture. They are now specified individually, from a 28-type system, with a class
   each and a check that fails the decorative ones (§8).
2. **The recycling threshold was a guess.** Three returns, chosen because it sounded
   reasonable. The research says 6–20 encounters with a working figure of 10–12, and
   that spacing within a session has the largest effect for vocabulary. The threshold
   is now ten encounters with three inside the teaching unit (§7.2).
3. **Nothing stopped the book from interleaving too early.** The 2025 finding that
   interleaving hurts low achievers without blocked practice first now has a rule
   behind it: Drills are blocked, Loops interleave (§7.3).
4. **Lexical coverage was unspecified.** Texts are now held to ≥98 per cent inside the
   cumulative Word File, which at 60-word A1 texts means at most one unknown word
   (§7.4).
5. **Item quality rested on taste.** Eleven of the 31 validated item-writing
   guidelines are now written into check **E3** (§7.5).
6. **The 42 grammar points were never listed**, so no check could test the claim.
   They are listed, and there are 44 (§5.8).
7. **The functions count was wrong** — 32 claimed, 34 actual with Unit 0 (§5.1, fixed).
8. **The page arithmetic was wrong**: 144 claimed, 176 actual. Fixed in the first pass
   on review; stated here so the record is complete.
9. **Nothing checked a unit against the units before it.** The entire middle layer of
   §11 — 765 passes, 5 per earlier unit — is new, and it is where cohesion failures
   live: a word re-taught, a character who changed jobs, a key pattern repeated, a
   text that got easier.
10. **No exercise had to justify itself.** Check **E7** now requires a descriptor, a
    one-verb statement of what the learner does, and the no-guess test: an exercise
    that can be answered without the target language is rebuilt.
11. **Accessibility was mentioned but not specified.** WCAG 2.2's 4.5:1 and 3:1
    ratios, the colour-is-never-alone rule and 932 alt texts are now in **E6** and in
    family I (§7.8).

Two things the pass *confirmed* rather than changed: the syllabus sequence, which
survived being re-derived against the research; and the 32-exercise skeleton, which the
visual defaults slot into without a seam.
