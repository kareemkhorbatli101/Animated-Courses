# Wayfare 1 — the A1 level

**A plan for the first level of our general-English series, modeled on *American
Headway Starter* (Oxford University Press, 3rd edition) and on its British parent
*New Headway Beginner* / *Headway Beginner* (5th edition), without copying them.**

> **The name is a placeholder.** "Wayfare" is used throughout so the document has a
> noun to hang things on. Alternatives that are clear of existing ELT brands:
> *Mileposts*, *Plain Lines*, *Common Hours*. Avoid "Open English" (a large Latin
> American edtech brand), "Pathways" (Cengage/National Geographic), "Keystone"
> (Pearson), "In Motion" and "English in Motion" (Richmond).

Four things this document contains, in this order: what *American Headway Starter*
actually is, where the legal line falls, what our A1 level looks like against it, and
every exercise in all seventeen units of our book. The last part is the long part.

---

## In one page

**What we are building.** The A1 level of a general-English course: 17 units (an
on-ramp plus 16), four review Loops, 100 teaching hours, 798 lexical items, 42 grammar
points, 16 pronunciation points, 34 functions, 16 videos. 536 Student Book exercises,
340 Workbook exercises, all of them specified in this document.

**What we take from *American Headway Starter*.** Its syllabus skeleton — which
grammar point sits in which unit, which topics carry it, and the idea of a functional
strand running beside the grammar. That is a set of facts about teaching beginners,
and no publisher owns it. §1 reconstructs it in detail; §2 draws the line.

**What we do not take.** One word of text. Not a rubric, not an example sentence, not
a reading passage, not a unit title, not a section name, not a character, not a word
list, not a test item. §2.3 gives seven working rules and §15.6 the mechanical gate
that enforces them before a unit ships.

**What we change on purpose.** Twelve things (§4), of which five matter most:

1. **A Unit 0** that assumes no Latin script and no English at all — the level our
   model starts above.
2. **Writing inside the unit**, on page 7, instead of exiled to the back of the book.
3. **A numbered pronunciation syllabus**: 16 points, each revisited twice, printed in
   the contents and tested in the Loops.
4. **Four blocks of four units**, each closed by a Loop that reviews, tests, produces
   and records — arithmetic that divides the course, unlike 14 units against four
   Stop-and-Checks and three Progress tests.
5. **Video as a strand**, 16 scripted scenes rendered by the engine this repository
   already has, each carrying its unit's function twice: once where it fails, once
   where it works.

**What is already proven here.** The two SAT books in this repository are held to
6,000 per-exercise checks and 120 book-level checks and were finished that way. §15
applies the same machinery: 4,288 per-exercise checks, 100 book-level checks, a
lexical-budget check over 60,000 running words, a recycling check, and the originality
gate.

**What I need from you.** Five decisions, in §16.4: the name, sixteen units or
fourteen, where the video sits commercially, how far the Arabic edition goes, and
American or British English.

## 0. How this was researched, and how good the research is

The container this was written in has no general outbound web access: only a search
tool that returns summaries of pages, not the pages themselves. Oxford's own
catalogue pages, the publisher's sample units and the TESL-EJ reviews could not be
opened directly. Everything below was reconstructed from search summaries of the
publisher's catalogue entries, two TESL-EJ reviews of the Starter Student Book and
Teacher's Book, retailer listings, teacher-shared scope-and-sequence pages, a
Beginner syllabus document, the Oxford Learner's Dictionaries word-list pages, and
the Council of Europe's CEFR Companion Volume. Sources are listed in §17.

Confidence is marked throughout:

- **[firm]** — stated by the publisher, by a published review, or by two independent
  sources that agree.
- **[likely]** — one source, or an inference from the British parent edition, which
  the American edition adapts.
- **[open]** — not established. Treat as a question to settle against a copy of the
  book before the syllabus is frozen.

Nothing in this plan *depends* on an **[open]** item. Where one would have mattered,
the plan makes its own decision and says so.

## 1. What *American Headway Starter* is

### 1.1 The shape of the course

| | |
|---|---|
| Level | CEFR A1 **[firm]** |
| Units | 14, topic-based **[firm]** |
| Student Book length | ~140 pages; six Student Book pages per unit (Unit 1 p.2, Unit 2 p.8, Unit 3 p.14, Unit 4 p.20) **[firm]** |
| Authors | John and Liz Soars; Starter adapted from *New Headway Beginner* **[firm]** |
| Hours | not published per level; A1 is commonly costed at 90–100 guided hours (ALTE/Cambridge estimates; secondary sources give 60–100) **[likely]** |
| Lexical load | unit word lists of a few dozen to about a hundred items; the publisher describes the vocabulary syllabus as high-frequency survival language, deliberately not overloaded **[firm]** |

### 1.2 The unit, section by section

Every unit runs through the same sequence **[firm]**, which the Teacher's Book states
as *Starter · presentation of new language · practice · skills*:

1. **Starter** — a short warm-up that activates what the unit will need.
2. **Presentation of new language** — the target structure in a dialogue, a short
   text or a listening, with a **Grammar Spot**: a small guided-discovery box that
   makes the student notice the form, ending in a cross-reference to the **Grammar
   Reference** at the back **[firm]**.
3. **Practice** — controlled exercises on the structure.
4. **Vocabulary** — a lexical set, usually tied to the unit topic.
5. **Skills work** — reading, listening, speaking, in various combinations.
6. **Everyday English** — the functional, transactional strand: social expressions
   and survival situations **[firm]**. For Starter these include greetings and
   introductions, the alphabet, on the phone, numbers 11–30, days of the week,
   *How much is it?*, *Can I …?*, directions, and social expressions **[likely]**.

Back matter: a **Writing** section with one task per unit, kept out of the units and
collected at the back, built on a model text for the student to analyze and imitate
**[firm]**; a **Grammar Reference** with its own exercises; a **word list**; audio
scripts; irregular verbs; a phonetic-symbols page **[firm]**.

### 1.4 Components and assessment

- **Student Book**, with an access code to the **Oxford Online Skills Program**: 30
  hours per level of reading, writing, listening and speaking practice **[firm]**.
- **Workbook**, with-key and without-key editions, plus **iChecker** online
  self-assessment. The workbook adds grammar and vocabulary extension per unit,
  everyday-English tasks, and review sections **[firm]**.
- **Teacher's Book**: unit-by-unit notes, **Possible problems** boxes naming the
  errors beginners actually make, **EXTRA IDEA** and **SUGGESTION** boxes,
  photocopiable activities at the back, answer keys, audio scripts **[firm]**.
- **Testing program**, downloadable: 14 unit tests in A and B versions, Stop and
  Check quizzes (four), Progress tests (three), a skills test, exit tests, and a
  series placement test **[firm]**.
- **iTools** for the classroom: Student Book and Workbook on screen, audio and video
  with optional scripts, customizable photocopiables **[firm]**. The British fifth
  edition replaces this with a Classroom Presentation Tool, a Teacher's Resource
  Centre, Online Practice, and video introductions on the unit opener pages
  **[firm]**.

### 1.5 What is good about it, and what is not

Worth keeping:

- **The six-page unit with a fixed internal order.** A student knows where they are
  on the page, and a teacher can plan a term from the table of contents.
- **Guided discovery in a box, not a lecture.** The Grammar Spot asks the student to
  complete the paradigm before the reference page explains it.
- **A functional strand that runs beside the grammar** rather than being folded into
  it. *How much is it?* is a lesson, not an aside.
- **A deliberately small lexical syllabus.** Beginners drown in word lists.
- **Pedagogical honesty in the teacher's notes.** *Possible problems* is the most
  useful heading in ELT publishing: it names the error before the class makes it.

Worth changing:

- **Writing is exiled to the back of the book.** One task per unit, physically
  separated from the unit it belongs to, is the part teachers skip.
- **Pronunciation is scattered.** There is no visible, cumulative phonology syllabus
  at A1, which is the level where one matters most.
- **The ramp starts above the floor.** Unit 1 assumes the Latin alphabet and some
  sound-spelling knowledge. The CEFR's own Companion Volume now describes a
  **Pre-A1** level **[firm]**; a true beginner in a non-Latin script needs it.
- **Recycling is implicit.** Items are reused, but nothing in the design guarantees
  that every taught item returns.
- **Fourteen units against four Stop-and-Checks and three Progress tests** is an
  awkward fit. The review cycle does not divide into the course.
- **Video is a wrapper, not a strand.** Added at the unit opener in the latest
  edition, it carries no syllabus of its own.

Every one of those six is a design decision we make differently in §4.

## 2. The line between modeling and copying

This is a practical working rule, not legal advice; a publishing lawyer should sign
off before the first unit is sold.

### 2.1 What copyright does not reach

Copyright protects *expression*, not ideas, systems or facts. In an ELT course that
means these are ours to use freely:

- **The grammatical sequence of English for beginners.** That *be* precedes the
  present simple, which precedes the past simple, is a fact about the language and a
  century-old teaching convention. No publisher owns it.
- **Functional and lexical topics.** Greetings, the alphabet, numbers, family, jobs,
  food, the time, directions, shopping, travel. These are the obligatory furniture of
  an A1 course — *scènes à faire* in the copyright sense.
- **Exercise types.** Gap-fill, matching, multiple choice, transformation,
  information gap, dictation, role-play, listen-and-repeat, odd-one-out.
- **The idea of a fixed unit shape** that moves from warm-up to presentation to
  practice to skills, and the idea of a separate functional strand.
- **CEFR descriptors**, which the Council of Europe publishes for reuse with
  attribution.

### 2.2 What copyright does reach, and what we therefore originate

- **Every word of running text.** Reading passages, dialogues, audio scripts,
  rubrics, grammar explanations, example sentences, headings, captions, answer keys.
  All written from scratch.
- **Characters and their worlds.** Their recurring people, their names, their
  biographies, their photographs, their artwork, their maps and realia.
- **The particular selection and arrangement of a word list.** We may teach the same
  high-frequency words — there is no other choice at A1 — but the list is built from
  our own source (the Oxford 3000's A1 band as a public reference, plus corpus
  frequency), not transcribed from theirs.
- **Their names and trade dress.** No unit titles copied, no section names copied, no
  look-alike covers, no "*Headway*" in any form, no comparative marketing that leans
  on their brand.
- **Their test items.** The testing program is a separate copyright work.

### 2.3 The rules we work under

1. **No source text is open while writing.** The syllabus grid in §5 is the only
   thing carried over, and it is a list of grammar points and topics — facts.
2. **Rename everything.** Section names, unit titles, box names, back-matter names.
   §4.3 gives our set. "Everyday English" becomes **Real Life**; "Grammar Spot"
   becomes **Notice**; "Grammar Reference" becomes the **Grammar File**.
3. **Re-sequence deliberately.** 14 units become 17 (an on-ramp plus 16), four review
   cycles that actually divide the course, and five grammar points moved (§5.3).
4. **Change the shape of the unit, not just its labels.** Writing comes back into the
   unit; pronunciation becomes a numbered strand; video becomes a syllabus.
5. **Every example sentence is ours and is about our world.** Our people, our places,
   our jobs, our 2026.
6. **A plagiarism gate before each unit ships.** Mechanical: every sentence of our
   unit is checked for a long shared n-gram against a corpus of competitor text we
   hold for the purpose. §15.6 specifies it. Any 7-word overlap that is not a fixed
   phrase of English is rewritten.
7. **Keep the record.** Each unit's source note says which decisions came from the
   research and which are ours, so that the provenance of the book can be shown.

### 2.4 The one thing that makes all of this safe

We are not reproducing a book; we are teaching a language at a level with a hundred
years of settled practice, in a format our own engine renders. The overlap that
remains after §2.3 is the overlap any two A1 courses have with each other — and the
twelve things in §4 that no A1 course currently does are why ours is worth building
at all.

## 4. The twelve decisions that make our A1 different

These are the "updating creatively" part of the brief. Each one is a change from the
model, with the reason it is worth the money it costs.

**1. An on-ramp unit that assumes nothing.** Unit 0, *First Words*, teaches the Latin
alphabet by name and by sound, 0–10, classroom instructions, and the first six fixed
phrases, in four hours. The CEFR's Companion Volume recognizes Pre-A1; our model does
not have it, and a true beginner in Arabic, Farsi, Thai or Cyrillic script needs it.

**2. Writing lives in the unit.** One short genre per unit, at the point in the unit
where the language for it has just been taught — not collected at the back where it
gets skipped. The back of the book keeps a **Writing File** of model texts, but the
task itself is on the unit's last page.

**3. Pronunciation is a numbered syllabus, not a sprinkle.** Sixteen points, one per
unit, each revisited twice (§5.5). The strand is printed in the contents, tested in
the Loops, and recorded in the self-check. A1 is where pronunciation is cheapest to
fix and most expensive to leave.

**4. Four blocks of four, with a review cycle that divides the course.** Our model
has 14 units, four Stop-and-Checks and three Progress tests, which do not line up.
Ours: 4 blocks × 4 units, each closed by a **Loop** — review, test, project, portfolio
page. The arithmetic is visible to everyone: unit 4, unit 8, unit 12, unit 16.

**5. Every taught item is guaranteed to come back.** A recycling table (§5.7) and a
machine check (§15.3): no lexical item or grammar point is taught and then abandoned.
Each returns at least three times, at least once in a later block, and at least once
in a skill other than the one that introduced it.

**6. The video is a strand, not a wrapper.** Sixteen animated videos, one per unit,
rendered by the engine this repository already has, each carrying a scripted,
syllabus-bearing scene with before-, during- and after-tasks (§14). The video is
where the functional language is *seen* happening, which is what print cannot do.

**7. Audio is scripted for listening work, not for reading aloud.** Every unit's
listening has two versions: the clean studio version and a *second pass* with natural
speed, hesitation and overlap. Beginners who only ever hear careful speech cannot
hear speech.

**8. A spoken-first order inside the unit.** Our model presents, practices, then
speaks. Ours presents, notices, drills, *says it*, and only then reads. The sequence
puts the mouth before the eye at the level where that is still possible.

**9. The lexical syllabus is budgeted and sourced.** 48 items per unit, hard cap 55,
drawn from the A1 band of a published core list (the Oxford 3000's ~900 A1 words, a
public reference) filtered by our own corpus counts and our topics. A total of about
798 items for the level, which is the right size for A1 and is a number we can
defend, not a word list we inherited.

**10. Checks before a page is printed.** The SAT books in this repository are held to
6,000 per-exercise checks and 120 book-level checks. The A1 book gets the same
treatment, specified in §15: per-exercise checks on rubric length, item count,
answer-key shape, CEFR tagging and lexical compliance, and book-level checks on
balance, recycling, key-letter distribution and the plagiarism gate.

**11. Two editions from one source.** The international edition and the
Arabic-support edition are one set of data files and two builds, as our SAT books
are. No forked content, no drift between editions.

**12. A self-study path that is actually complete.** Every exercise in §11 is tagged
`C` (needs a class), `S` (works alone), or `C/S` (works either way). A student
working alone can reach the end of A1 with the `S` and `C/S` set, the audio, the
videos and the online bank. That is a product our model does not have.

### 5.2 Scope and sequence

`G` grammar · `F` functions (Real Life) · `W` the lexical set · `P` pronunciation ·
`R/L` the reading and listening · `WR` the writing genre · `AH` the unit of *American
Headway Starter* this one answers to.

**Unit 0 · First Words** — no block, 4 hours, 30 items. `AH` none; this is ours.
`G` *I'm …*, *This is …*, yes/no answers · `W` the alphabet, 0–10, twelve classroom
words, six fixed phrases · `P` letter names against letter sounds; the five vowel
letters · `F` greeting someone, asking for repetition (*Again, please*) · `R/L`
listen-and-point, listen-and-write · `WR` printing your name and email address.

**Unit 7 · Food, and What You Like** (`AH` 7, 12 part) · `G` *like / don't like* +

**→ LOOP 2** (after Unit 8): review of the present and of existence; Test 2A/2B;

### 5.3 The five things we moved, and why

1. **Numbers 11–100 move up to Unit 2** (their Unit 5 territory). Phone numbers and
   ages are needed from the second hour of any real class.
2. **Prices move down to Unit 3** from their Unit 7. A beginner buys something before
   they describe a kitchen.
3. **Object pronouns move into Unit 7** with *like*, where they are needed (*I like
   it*), instead of appearing in a grammar box with no work to do.
4. **The past splits into three units, not two**: *was/were*, then regular, then
   irregular with questions and negatives. The single hardest week in an A1 course is
   the one where regular and irregular past arrive together.
5. **Unit 16 is a consolidation unit with new functional work**, not a fourteenth
   dose of new grammar. The level ends by putting the auxiliaries beside each other,
   which is the one thing an A1 student has never seen in one place.

third person, give a number · 3: ask a price, buy one thing · 4: show a photo,
correct politely · 5: tell the time, say when · 6: ask a job, ask to repeat · 7:
order, offer · 8: ask directions, locate · 9: give a date, fill a form · 10: report
yesterday, react · 11: ask about a trip, buy a ticket · 12: request, offer help ·
13: describe a photo, report a call · 14: shop, ask politely · 15: arrange, agree a
time · 16: ask for help, say goodbye.

### 5.6 What A1 deliberately does not touch

Comparatives and superlatives · the present perfect · the past continuous · *will* ·
relative clauses · reported speech · conditionals · passives · phrasal verbs beyond
four fixed items (*get up*, *go out*, *come back*, *look at*) · *used to* · modals
beyond *can*, *would like*, *could* for requests · the full countable/uncountable
system (Unit 14 takes a first pass only) · question tags.

Each of these is the opening syllabus of Wayfare 2. The temptation at A1 is to teach
a little of everything; the cost is that nothing is automatic.

## 6. The unit, page by page

Eight pages. The first six teach; the seventh writes; the eighth checks. The order is
the same in all sixteen units, so that a student on page 3 of Unit 11 knows what page
3 is for.

| Page | Slots | Exercises | Minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Open** (warm-up, recycles the unit before) ×2 · **Model** (target language in context) ×3 | 5 | 35 |
| 2 | **Notice** (guided discovery, cross-referenced to the Grammar File) ×1 · **Drill** (controlled practice) ×4 | 5 | 35 |
| 3 | **Say it** (the unit's pronunciation point) ×2 · **Words** (the lexical set) ×3 | 5 | 35 |
| 4 | **Hear it** (listening, clean then natural) ×3 · **Use it** (semi-controlled to free) ×2 | 5 | 45 |
| 5 | **Real Life** (the function, with a role-play) ×4 | 4 | 35 |
| 6 | **Read it** (the text, with before- and after-tasks) ×3 · **On Screen** (the video) ×2 | 5 | 40 |
| 7 | **Write it** (one genre, modeled then produced) ×2 | 2 | 30 |
| 8 | **Can I?** (ten-item self-check, the Word File page for the unit) ×1 | 1 | 15 |
| | | **32** | **270 + 30 of feedback and Carry it out = 5 hours** |

Two fixed features sit inside those pages rather than taking space of their own:

- **Likely trouble** — a small box in the Teacher's Guide at the point of need, not
  at the top of the unit. It names the error, gives the correction, and gives the
  thirty-second fix. (Modeled on the single best heading in our source's teacher's
  book, written entirely from our own error data.)
- **Carry it out** — one line at the foot of page 8: a task to do in English outside
  the classroom before the next lesson. Sixteen of them, graded.

### 7.1 The rules every exercise obeys

1. **Rubrics are at or below the level.** Eight words or fewer, imperative, in the
   present. A rubric at A1 that needs translating is a failed rubric. The 22 rubric
   templates are fixed per type and listed in the Teacher's Guide appendix.
2. **Every exercise has an example item**, marked `0`, done for the student.
3. **Item counts are fixed by type**: T06 matching is 6–8 pairs; T07/T08 gap-fills
   are 6–10 items; T17 reading tasks are 5–6; T22 is exactly 10.
4. **No exercise introduces a word that is not in the Word File by that unit.** The
   check in §15.2 enforces it over all 510 exercises.
5. **Answer keys carry the accepted variants**, not one answer, wherever English
   allows two (*He isn't / He's not*).
6. **Each exercise names its own CEFR descriptor**, which prints in the Teacher's
   Guide and drives the portfolio pages.
7. **No exercise occupies more than one page.** A page turn in the middle of an
   exercise is a design error, which our build checks (§15.5) the way the SAT books
   check split exercises.

## 8. What each slot is for, and what it may not do

**Open** (2) — recycles the previous unit and surfaces what the class
already knows about this one. Never introduces new language. Always spoken or
non-verbal; never written production.

**Model** (3) — the target structure, in a dialogue of 40–70 words or a text of
40–80, heard before it is read in units 0–8 and read with audio support from 9.
Contains the structure at least four times. Contains at most three unknown words
beyond the unit's lexical set, each glossed in a picture or a cognate.

**Notice** (1, type T13) — the paradigm, incomplete, for the student to finish from
the Model, in no more than four lines, ending in the Grammar File cross-reference.
Never an explanation in prose. A sentence of metalanguage is a failure here.

**Drill** (4) — controlled practice, in a fixed difficulty order: recognition
(T09/T06), then production of the form (T07/T11/T12), then self-monitoring (T10).
Entirely within the taught lexis. Answers are closed.

**Say it** (2) — the unit's pronunciation point, always *hear it, sort it, say it*:
T05 or T14 then T02. Never silent.

**Words** (3) — the lexical set, presented in a group of 8–20, worked twice (T06 or
T16, then T14 or T15), and then used in one personal exchange. The set is a picture
set wherever a picture is possible.

**Hear it** (3) — two listenings: the clean one (T03/T04/T05) and the natural one
(same content, natural speed and hesitation, with an easier task). The task before
the audio is always about the picture, never about the words.

**Use it** (2) — the production slot: an information gap (T18) or a survey (T20),
then a short personal report. Mistakes are expected here and are not corrected on
the spot; the Teacher's Guide says so in those words.

**Real Life** (4) — the function. Always: hear the exchange (T04/T05), notice the
phrases (T06), practice them (T07/T11), use them in a role-play with a goal (T19).
The phrases are listed in a box of six to eight, which is the box students
photograph.

**Read it** (3) — one text, 40 words in Unit 1 rising to 140 in Unit 16, on our own
material, with one pre-task (T06/T16), one comprehension task (T17) and one
personal-response question.

**On Screen** (2) — the video: predict from a still (T05), watch for gist (T17),
watch for the functional language (T04), and a line to perform (T02/T19).

**Write it** (2) — read the model, notice one feature of written English (capitals,
the comma in a date, the greeting line of a message), write 40–60 words to a
checklist. The checklist is five items and the student uses it before the teacher
sees the work.

**Can I?** (1, type T22) — ten items across the unit's grammar, lexis, function and
pronunciation, with a score band and, for each item missed, the page to go back to.

## 10. Unit 1 · Names and Places — every exercise, in full

This is the model unit. Everything specified here — the lengths, the slot order, the
number of items, the way an example item is given, the way the key is shaped — holds
for Units 2–16, which §11 then lists in the same order without repeating the prose.

**Target language.** *am/are* with I and you, affirmative, negative, question and
short answer; *my/your*; the contractions *I'm, you're, what's*.
**Lexis (48).** Twelve countries (*Egypt, Jordan, Morocco, Turkey, Spain, Italy,
Brazil, Mexico, Japan, Korea, Canada, the UK*), eight greeting and parting phrases,
eight question-and-answer frames, six classroom-survival items recycled from Unit 0,
three jobs (*student, teacher, engineer*), eleven high-frequency support words
(*and, in, from, too, here, this, you, I, my, your, name*).
**Pronunciation.** /aɪ/ in *I, I'm, my, hi*; stress on country names (*Jápan,
Brazíl, Móro-cco*).
**Functions.** Introducing yourself; introducing a second person; spelling a name
aloud.
**Writing genre.** A name card and a one-line profile.
**Video 1.** 75 seconds: two new students meet in a corridor; one introduces the
other to a teacher.
**Reading.** 40 words: three name cards with one line each.
**CEFR descriptors.** *Can introduce him/herself and use basic greetings* ·
*Can ask and answer simple questions about personal details* · *Can spell his/her
name and address.*

Key: exact, single answers.

**U1.5 · Model · T02 · C · 10 min.** Say the dialogue in threes, then change the

## 12. The four Loops

A Loop is four Student Book pages and three hours, after units 4, 8, 12 and 16. It
reviews, tests, produces something, and records what the student can now do. Fourteen
exercises each, same skeleton every time.

| # | Part | Type | Path | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L.1 | Review | T06 | S | match 10 items from the block's four lexical sets |
| L.2 | Review | T14 | S | sort 12 words from the block by sound or category |
| L.3 | Review | T09 | S | choose the form, 12 items, one from each grammar point of the block |
| L.4 | Review | T07 | S | gap-fill, 12 items, mixed across the block |
| L.5 | Review | T10 | S | correct the error, 8 items, drawn from the block's own error list |
| L.6 | Review | T11 | S | word order, 6 questions, one per unit plus two mixed |
| L.7 | Review | T04 | C/S | listening: a 90-second exchange that uses all four units |
| L.8 | Review | T17 | S | reading: a 120–160-word text that recycles the block, 6 items |
| L.9 | Review | T19 | C | a role-play that needs two of the block's four functions at once |
| L.10 | Test | — | S | **Test A** (the B version is held for retakes): 40 items, 30 minutes, printed in the Teacher's Guide, not the Student Book |
| L.11 | Project | T20 | C | the block project (below), prepared in class, 45 minutes |
| L.12 | Project | T21 | C/S | the project's written output, 60–100 words |
| L.13 | Project | T19 | C | the project's spoken output: two minutes, to the class or to camera |
| L.14 | Portfolio | T22 | S | the can-do page: 8 statements from the block, each ticked *yes / almost / not yet*, plus the three pages to revisit |

**The projects.** Loop 1: *Our class in twelve photos* — a wall of name cards,
countries and jobs, with one line each. Loop 2: *A map of our street* — ten places,
directions between three of them, a recorded tour. Loop 3: *A day we remember* — a
two-minute story with five past verbs and two sequencers, told to camera. Loop 4:
*Four minutes about my year in English* — the exit project: a personal profile, a
past story, a present description and a plan, which is the level in one piece.

**The tests.** Four Loop tests of 40 items (grammar 16, lexis 10, function 6,
listening 5, reading 3), each in an A and a B version; plus two skills tests (after
Loop 2 and Loop 4) that test reading, listening, writing and speaking separately
against printed criteria; plus the exit test, which is Loop 4's test at double length.
A six-page placement and on-ramp screener decides between Unit 0 and Unit 1 for a new
student, and is the one test a school gives before the course starts.

## 14. The video strand

Sixteen videos, one per unit, 60–150 seconds, rendered from XML by the engine this
repository already has, which is the reason the video can be a syllabus rather than a
decoration: a scene can be re-rendered when the syllabus changes, and the same source
produces the classroom version, the student version and the subtitled version.

Fixed shape: a single location, two or three characters, no narrator, the unit's
function performed twice — once where it goes wrong or nearly does, and once where it
works. The target structure appears at least six times. The script stays inside the
unit's cumulative Word File, which the check in §15.2 enforces on the scripts as well
as on the exercises.

## 15. The checks

The two SAT books in this repository are held to 6,000 per-exercise checks and 120
book-level checks, and the discipline is the reason they are finishable. The A1 course
gets the same treatment. One source of truth, `data/spec.yaml`; the content in
`data/units/U01.yaml` … `U16.yaml`; the checks in `tools/achecks.py`.

### 15.1 Per-exercise checks (eight on each of 536 Student Book exercises = 4,288)

1. **identity** — the id, the unit, the slot and the position agree, and the global
   number is right.
2. **skeleton** — the slot, the type and the path tag match the §11.0 skeleton row for
   that position; only rows 9, 14 and 17 may vary, and only between their two allowed
   types.
3. **rubric** — eight words or fewer, imperative, present tense, drawn from the 22
   fixed rubric templates for the type.
4. **items** — the item count is inside the band the type allows, and item `0` exists
   and is filled in as the example.
5. **lexis** — every word in the exercise is in the cumulative Word File at that unit,
   or is a name, a number or one of the eight declared international words.
6. **key** — the key's shape matches the type (a letter for T05/T09, a pair list for
   T06, exact strings plus accepted variants for T07/T08/T11/T12), and no key is empty.
7. **answer spread** — within an exercise, no option letter holds more than 40 per
   cent of the keys, and no run of three identical letters.
8. **descriptor** — the exercise names one CEFR A1 descriptor, and that descriptor is
   one of the 32 the level claims (four worked per unit, eight listed per Loop).

### 15.2 The lexical budget check

The one that makes the level teachable. A word enters the Word File in the unit that
teaches it. From then on it is free. Before then, it may appear only as a name, a
number, an international word, or a glossed item in the Model slot (at most three per
unit). The check runs over every rubric, every item, every text, every audio script
and every video script — about 60,000 running words — and reports every violation with
its unit and exercise. Budget: 48 new items per unit, hard cap 55, 798 in the level.

### 15.3 The recycling check

For every item in the Word File and every grammar point in §5.2: it appears again at
least three times after the unit that taught it, at least once in a later block, and
at least once in a slot other than the one that taught it. The check prints the
return schedule per item, and fails on any item with fewer than three returns. §5.7 is
the claim; this is its test.

### 15.4 Book-level checks (100)

Ten families of ten, in the manner of the SAT books:

- **A completeness** — 17 units, 4 Loops, 536 exercises, 340 Workbook exercises, 16
  videos, 16 reading texts, 32 listenings, 16 writing tasks, all present.
- **B the grid** — every unit has its 32 exercises in skeleton order; every slot
  appears the declared number of times; the minutes add to 270 ± 10 per unit.
- **C the ladder** — text lengths rise monotonically across the level (reading 40 →
  140, model 60 → 110, writing 10 → 60 words); no unit's reading is shorter than the
  unit before it.
- **D grammar** — the 42 points are each taught once and only once; no unit teaches a
  point from §5.6's exclusion list; every Notice box is four lines or fewer and
  contains no metalanguage beyond the eight permitted terms.
- **E lexis** — the budget and the cap; the 798 total; the A1 banding of every item
  against the published core list; no item taught twice.
- **F functions** — 34 functions, two per unit; every phrase box holds six to eight
  phrases; every function is used in a role-play with a printed goal.
- **G phonology** — 16 points, one per unit, each revisited exactly twice, in the
  units §5.5 names; every Say it slot has audio.
- **H the answer keys** — every exercise has one; the variants are present wherever
  English allows two; no key is identical to another exercise's key; letter balance
  across the level is 25 ± 3 per cent per letter.
- **I the Arabic edition** — every Grammar File page, every lexical set and every
  *likely trouble* note has its Arabic counterpart; the Arabic is at least 92 per cent
  Arabic script; no Latin text in it beyond the declared allow-list.
- **K the document** — the built page count is inside its band, every unit starts on a
  right-hand page, no exercise is split across a page break, 176 pages, the Word File
  and Grammar File cross-references all resolve.

### 15.5 The build

`tools/abuild.py` renders the Student Book, the Workbook, the Teacher's Guide and the
two editions from the same data files, then measures the PDF and writes
`build/doc-stats.json` — page count, units, exercises rendered, split exercises,
cross-references resolved, Arabic blocks — which the K family reads. This is exactly
the arrangement the SAT books use, and it is what lets ten of the hundred checks be
claims about the printed page.

### 15.6 The originality gate

Before a unit ships:

1. **N-gram sweep.** Every sentence of ours is compared against a held corpus of
   competitor material. Any shared run of seven words or more that is not a fixed
   phrase of English (*nice to meet you*, *how much is it*) is rewritten. Fixed
   phrases are kept in a declared allow-list, which is itself reviewed.
2. **Structure diff.** The unit's syllabus row is compared against the research grid
   in §1.3. Overlap in grammar point and topic is expected and fine; overlap in
   example sentences, names, numbers, places or the particular order of a lexical set
   is a flag.
3. **Name sweep.** No character name, place name, brand or title in our material
   matches one in the corpus.
4. **Artwork sweep.** Every illustration is ours or licensed, with the license
   recorded in `data/credits.yaml`; no photograph is reused from any source we do not
   hold a license for.
5. **The record.** Each unit's `provenance` block names what came from research (the
   grammar point, the topic, the function) and what is ours (everything else). The
   block is part of the data file and is checked for completeness.

### 16.3 What a unit costs

Per unit, written to this plan: 32 Student Book exercises, 20 Workbook exercises, 2
audio scripts in two versions, 1 video script, 1 reading text, 1 extra reading text,
1 writing model, 8 phrases, 48 lexical items, 1 Notice box, 1 Grammar File page, 1
Can I? page, 1 teacher's-guide walkthrough with its *likely trouble* boxes, 1 Arabic
page set. Seventeen units and four Loops of that is the level.

### 16.4 The open questions for you

1. **The name.** Wayfare, or one of the three alternatives, or yours.
2. **Sixteen units or fourteen.** Sixteen gives the 4 × 4 symmetry and the four Loops;
   fourteen matches the market's expectation of a 14-unit A1 book and saves about ten
   per cent of the work. The plan is written for sixteen and degrades cleanly to
   fourteen by folding Unit 3 into Unit 2 and Unit 16 into Loop 4.
3. **Where the video sits in the business.** Free on the site as the hook, or inside
   the paid digital package.
4. **The Arabic edition.** Arabic in the Student Book itself, or only in the Workbook
   and Teacher's Guide, which keeps the Student Book sellable in non-Arabic markets.
5. **American or British English.** The research model is American; the plan is
   written American (*apartment, vacation, movie, the fifth of May / May fifth*). A
   British build is a lexical switch file, not a rewrite, if we declare it now.
