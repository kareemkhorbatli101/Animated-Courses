# Unit 6 · The Most Ratified Treaty
%%SUB A hundred and ninety-six parties, and not a hundred and ninety-six identical obligations%%
%%GOALS

A journalist asks you how many countries have signed the Convention on the Rights of the Child. The
short answer is nearly all of them, and it is the kind of true answer that makes a careful person
uneasy, because what a state has promised is written in its reservations and not in the headline
number.

> | | |

### 1 · Relative clauses

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP2-06-06%%

> **Journalist:** How many countries have signed it?
> **Omar:** A hundred and ninety-six states are parties, **which** makes it the most widely ratified

> **Journalist:** And the countries **that** haven't?

> missing is approval by its legislature.
> **Journalist:** So everyone else has accepted all of it.
> **Omar:** Not quite. Parties **that** have entered reservations have not accepted those provisions
> in the same terms. The reservations, **which** are published, are where the real answer is.
> **Journalist:** That's a longer answer than I wanted.

*Look at the relative pronouns. Which clauses could you delete without changing the main fact?*

**1B  Underline every relative clause in 1A. Mark with a star the ones inside commas.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **who** for people | the officer **who** took the call |
| **which** for things | the reservations, **which** are published |
| **that** for people or things, defining | the step **that** is missing |
| **whose** | a state **whose** reservation was withdrawn |
| **where** | the office **where** the instrument is deposited |
| defining, **no commas** | Parties **that** have entered reservations have not accepted it. |
| non-defining, **commas** | The Convention, **which** was adopted in 1989, has 196 parties. |

> **Grammar Note** — The commas change the meaning, not just the punctuation. *Parties that have
> entered reservations* means only some of them. *Parties, which have entered reservations* would
> mean all of them, which is false. In this field the two commas are a factual error, not a style
> choice.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Questions about universality are among the most politically loaded a ministry answers, and the
temptation runs in both directions. A spokesperson who wants to sound committed says *universally
accepted*. A spokesperson who wants to sound sceptical says *riddled with reservations*. Both
sentences are built from the same published facts and both are misleading, and a careful officer
will be accused of each by the people who prefer the other. What protects you is sequence: give the
membership figure first, flatly, and the content caveat second, shorter. Put them the other way round
and you sound as though you are undermining your own instrument; leave the second out and you will be
corrected by somebody with a copy of the reservations in front of them, in public, and the correction
will be the story.

**Discuss:** In your own working language, is there a word for *universal* that carries both meanings
at once? How do you avoid it?

**Give two numbered sentences, the figure first and the caveat second and shorter. Add what to say if
asked about the exception, and tell the speaker not to characterize another state's reasons. Add what
to say about the small print and where the legal question goes. End with a *do not say* line and a
source line.**

**Read your note aloud to your partner first.** Reading it out is how you hear a caveat that has
outgrown its fact. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you explain the non-party state's
