### 1 · *Although*, *despite*, *however*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP2-11-06%%

> **Caller:** I won. Two years ago. I have the award in my hand.
> **Omar:** **Although** you have an award, the award by itself does not move money. **Despite**
> winning, you are at step one of four.
> **Caller:** The convention has a hundred and seventy-two parties. Everybody is in it.
> **Omar:** Almost everybody, yes. **However**, a convention that nearly everybody has joined still
> only tells courts what to do when somebody asks them. **In spite of** the membership, somebody has
> to go to a court.
> **Caller:** And if the court agrees?
> **Omar:** Then you have recognition, and **although** that is real progress, enforcement is a
> separate application. **Even so**, people stop at that point and wait.
> **Caller:** Nobody has explained it in this order before.
> **Omar:** It is a short explanation and it is nobody's favorite conversation, which is probably
> why.

*Look at the five linking expressions. Which are followed by a clause and which by a noun?*

**1B  Underline the five linking expressions in 1A and write *clause* or *noun* beside each.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Followed by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **although** / **though** | a clause | **Although** he has an award, he has no money. |
| **even though** *(stronger)* | a clause | **Even though** he won, nothing was paid. |
| **despite** / **in spite of** | a noun or *-ing* | **Despite** the award &nbsp;·&nbsp; **In spite of** winning |
| **however** | a new sentence, then a comma | He won. **However**, nothing was paid. |
| **nevertheless** / **even so** | a new sentence | He won. **Nevertheless**, he is at step one. |
| **but** | joins two clauses | He won, **but** nothing was paid. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Despite* and *in spite of* take a noun or an *-ing* form, never a clause:
> *despite he won* is wrong; *despite winning* and *despite the fact that he won* are right. And
> *however* starts a new sentence with a comma after it, not a comma before it joining two clauses.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Commercial callers are the group a consular section is least prepared for and most often blamed by.
They arrive with a document that a tribunal has signed, and they have usually been told by somebody
that an international convention exists, and the combination of those two facts produces a
reasonable belief that an official somewhere can act. The frustration is not unreasonable either:
they have won, and the system that let them win does not deliver. What makes the difference is
sequencing and arithmetic. Number the steps before you explain them, so the caller knows how much
bad news there is rather than discovering it piece by piece. And when a figure comes up, say its
base, because a caller who has looked something up and been told a different number stops believing
the rest of the conversation. Neither habit changes the outcome. Both change whether the caller
spends the next year pursuing the right thing.

**Discuss:** Which group of callers does your own office handle worst? What do they arrive believing?

>
> **What this ministry does not do.** We do not recognize awards. We do not enforce them. We do not
> hold or attach assets. **We cannot instruct a foreign court** — the same rule prevents their
> ministry from instructing ours.
>
> **What I can do, and have done.**
> 1. Set out the four steps above, so that you are not paying anybody to explain step 1.
> 2. State plainly that **enforcement is a separate application from recognition**.
> 3. Note that if the debtor has no assets in that country, enforcement **there** will not produce
>    money, **although assets elsewhere may be a separate question** for your lawyer.

> Yours sincerely,
> O. Sabbagh, Consular Affairs, ext. 219
