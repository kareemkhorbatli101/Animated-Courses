%%SUB The Convention decides where the case is heard, and a parent hears it deciding who wins%%
%%GOALS

A mother telephones from Damascus. Her daughter was taken to Perenne in January and she wants her
back. There is a convention for this, it has a hundred and three Contracting Parties, and its own
Article 19 says in one line that a return decision is not a custody decision. That line is the
hardest sentence in this book to say to a parent, and it has to be said early.

> **Reader's Tip** — When a convention has one article that stops a common misunderstanding, learn
> that article by its number. You will say the number more often than you say anything else in the
> instrument.

%%FIG h03-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line in bold with a convention date and a number of Contracting Parties, a boxed quotation of one article in smaller type, a short table with two columns headed the return question and the custody question, a paragraph headed the twelve months, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · consular note 3
> *Consular Affairs · child abduction applications · page 1 of 1*
>
> The **Convention of 25 October 1980** on the civil aspects of international child abduction has
> **103 Contracting Parties** on the depositary organization's status table. It entered into force
> on **1 December 1983**.
>
> The sentence to learn is **Article 19**.
>
> > *A decision under this Convention concerning the return of the child shall not be taken to be a
> > determination on the merits of any custody issue.*
>
> | The return question | The custody question |
> |---|---|
> | Was the child wrongfully removed from, or retained outside, its country of **habitual residence**? | Who should the child live with? |
> | Decided by a court **where the child now is** | Decided by the courts of the **habitual residence**, afterwards |
> | Fast, and narrow | Slow, and about the whole family |
>
> **The twelve months.** Where proceedings are begun **within one year** of the removal, the court
> is required to order return. **After** one year, the court may refuse if the child **has settled**
> in its new environment. So the year does not close the door, but it changes who has to persuade
> whom.
>
> There are narrow exceptions — a **grave risk** to the child, and, where the child is old enough,
> the child's own **objection**. They are exceptions, and they are argued, and they are not the
> ordinary case.
>
> *Source: the Convention of 25 October 1980 and the depositary organization's status table. As on
> the other tables, "Contracting Party" can include a state for which the Convention is not yet in
> force, so the per-state line has to be read as well as the headline figure.*

<sub>**habitual residence** — where the child genuinely lived before · **the merits** — who should
win a case · **to settle** — to become established in a new place</sub>

### 1 · *Must*, *have to*, *be required to*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP2-03-06%%

> **Mother:** What do I have to do?
> **Omar:** You **have to** fill in this application and sign it. You **must** give the address where
> the child was living before January, because that is the question the court will start from.
> **Mother:** And then they **have to** send her back.
> **Omar:** If the court is satisfied that she was wrongfully removed, and the application was made
> within a year, the court **is required to** order her return. I **have to** tell you what that
> does and does not mean.
> **Mother:** It means she comes home.
> **Omar:** It means the case comes home. The return order **is not allowed to** decide custody. The
> Convention says that itself.

*Omar uses three different ways of saying an obligation. Who is obliged in each one?*

**1B  Underline every obligation in 1A and write beside each one who is obliged.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | Who is behind it |
|---|---|---|
| **must** | You **must** give the address. | the speaker, or the rule itself |
| **have to** | You **have to** sign it. | a rule outside the speaker |
| **be required to** | The court **is required to** order return. | an instrument, named |
| **don't have to** | You **don't have to** apply within a year. | not necessary |
| **mustn't** | You **mustn't** sign a blank form. | forbidden |
| **be allowed to** | The court **is allowed to** refuse after a year. | permitted |
| **is not allowed to** | It **is not allowed to** decide custody. | forbidden by the instrument |

> **Grammar Note** — *Don't have to* and *mustn't* are opposites, not alternatives. *You don't have
> to come on Thursday* is an offer. *You mustn't come on Thursday* is an instruction. In a consular
> interview the difference is the difference between a kindness and a refusal.

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **Under** + instrument | **Under the 1980 Convention**, the question is return. |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Family cases are where consular work is least like the rest of diplomacy, because the person in
front of you is not representing anybody. They are a parent, they have usually not slept, and they
have come to the ministry because every other door was locked. Two habits make the difference. The
first is to say the limit early: the sentence *this does not decide custody* costs you five minutes
in the first meeting and saves a betrayal in the sixth. The second is to report silence. Officers
naturally telephone when there is news, which means that the absence of a call comes to mean bad
news, and a parent sits by a telephone building a case against you out of nothing. A call that says
*nothing has happened this week and I have checked* is worth more than most of the calls that carry
information.

**Discuss:** In your own work, who waits for your calls? What do they assume when you do not
telephone?

> ### Letter to an applicant
> *Consular Affairs · ref. CA/2026/12 · 4 March*
>
> Dear Mrs Haddad,
>
> **This letter is about your application for the return of your daughter.** I am setting out what
> the application does, what it does not do, and what I need from you.
>
> **What it does.** Under the **Convention of 25 October 1980**, your application asks a court in
> the country where your daughter now is to order her **return** to the country where she was living
> before January. If that court is satisfied that she was **wrongfully removed**, and your
> application is made **within one year** of the removal, the court **is required to** order her
> return.
>
> **What it does not do. Article 19 of the Convention says that a decision about return is not a
> decision about custody.** Custody is a separate case, and it is heard **after** she is back, by the
> courts of the country she lived in. I am telling you this in my first letter so that it is never a
> surprise.
>
> **What I need from you, by 11 March.**
> 1. The address where she lived before January, **with the dates** you lived there.
> 2. Her birth certificate and the details of her passport.
> 3. Any existing court order about her.
> 4. The names and any contact details of people who know where she is now.
>
> **Please do not post original documents.** Bring them to the counter and we will copy them here.
>
> **Timing.** I am not able to tell you how long a foreign court will take, and I will not guess.
> **I will telephone you every Thursday**, including the Thursdays when there is nothing to report,
> so that silence never has to mean anything.
