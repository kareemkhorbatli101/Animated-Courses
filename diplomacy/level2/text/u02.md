### 1 · The present perfect with *for* and *since*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP2-02-06%%

> **Astrand:** We sent the papers in February. Have they been served?
> **Omar:** They **have been** with our central authority **since the eleventh of February**. They
> **have not been served** yet.
> **Astrand:** Six weeks.
> **Omar:** Six weeks, yes. They **have been waiting for** a translation **for** most of that time.
> **Astrand:** Nobody **has told** us that.
> **Omar:** No, and that is our side of it. I **have already** asked for the file. I **have not**
> spoken to the translator **yet**, and I will today.

*Which two sentences are about a period of time, and which two are about a result that is still
true?*

**1B  Underline every present perfect in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **have / has + past participle** | They **have been** with the authority since February. |
| **for** + a length of time | **for** six weeks · **for** most of that time |
| **since** + a point in time | **since** the eleventh of February · **since** it arrived |
| **already** (before the end) | I **have already** asked for the file. |
| **yet** (at the end, in questions and negatives) | They **have not been served yet**. |
| **still not** | The certificate **still has not** come back. |
| past simple with a finished time | We **sent** the papers **in February**. |

> **Grammar Note** — *For* takes a length (*for six weeks*); *since* takes a starting point (*since
> February*). And the moment you name a finished time — *in February*, *last week*, *on the
> eleventh* — you must use the past simple: *we have sent it last week* is the error that lasts
> longest at this level.

### 2 · *Already*, *still* and *yet*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| **already** | I have **already** chased it twice. | sooner than expected |
| **yet** | It has not come back **yet**. | expected, has not happened |
| **still** + negative | It **still** has not come back. | and I am surprised |
| **just** | The certificate has **just** arrived. | a moment ago |
| **ever / never** | Have you **ever** had one refused? | at any time |

> **Grammar Note** — *Already* goes in the middle. *Yet* goes at the end. *Still* goes in front of
> the negative verb and carries a small complaint with it, which is sometimes exactly what you want
> and is never neutral, so choose it on purpose.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Treaty status pages look like the most neutral documents in the world, and they are full of words
that mean something narrower than they appear to. *Contracting Party* can include a state the treaty
does not yet bind. *Signature* can mean a promise to consider rather than a promise to perform.
*Entry into force* happens on a different date for every state, so a convention can be in force for
your office and not in force for the office you are writing to. None of this is hidden; all of it is
printed in small type above the table that nobody reads. The habit worth building is simple and
slightly humbling: read the definitions before the numbers, every time, even on a page you have
opened a hundred times, because the definitions are where the trap is kept.

**Discuss:** Which word in your own working life looks precise and turns out to be broad? Who gets
caught by it?

### 3 · Write

Write **100 to 130 words**: a letter about an outstanding request.

**Give the reference and the date it arrived. Say how long, as a length. Name the cause and say whose
delay it is. Say what you have already done and what you have not done yet. Give a named day. End
with what you will do if you miss it.**

**Read your letter aloud to your partner first.** Reading it out is how you hear *in hand* and
*shortly*. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's letter as a judge who has waited six weeks. Are you
reassured, and by which sentence?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you avoid the words *our delay*? Why is
