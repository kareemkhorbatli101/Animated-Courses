**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| present continuous | a fixed arrangement | I **am sending** it this afternoon. |
| **going to** | a plan already made | I **am going to** telephone them. |
| **will** | a decision as you speak, an offer, a promise | Then I **will** attach it. |
| **is / are due to** | scheduled, expected | It **is due to arrive** on the 24th. |
| **is / are to** | an instruction, formal | The note **is to** be attached. |
| present simple | a timetable | The post **leaves** at four. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Is to* is the formal instruction form and it appears constantly in official
> writing: *the form is to be signed in black ink*, *copies are to be retained*. It is not the same
> as *is going to*. One is an order; the other is a prediction, and confusing them in a minute makes
> an instruction sound like a forecast.

### 2 · *Due to* and *is to*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **is due to** + verb | The consignment **is due to** arrive on the 24th. |
| **is due** + noun phrase | The reply **is due** on Thursday. |
| **due to** + noun *(= because of)* | The delay is **due to** the system outage. |
| **is to** + verb *(instruction)* | The receipt **is to** be filed with the case papers. |
| **are not to** + verb *(prohibition)* | Originals **are not to** be sent by post. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Due to* has two completely different jobs: *due to arrive* is about time, and
> *due to the outage* means *because of*. Both are correct and the sentence tells you which. The
> error worth avoiding is *due to* at the start of a sentence where you mean *because of*: in formal
> drafting, write *because of the outage* or *owing to the outage* and keep *due to* for schedules.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Choosing a channel looks like administration and is actually a small diplomatic decision, because
every channel carries a meaning as well as a document. A diplomatic bag says *this is between
states*. A courier says *this is urgent and commercial*. Registered post says *this is formal and we
have proof*. An electronic message says *this is routine*. Using the bag for something that is not
mission correspondence is the commonest misuse, and it is almost always done for a good reason: the
bag is leaving, the deadline is tomorrow, the alternative is slow. It is still worth refusing,
because a channel reserved for one purpose only keeps its protection while it is used only for that
purpose, and the first time it is opened for a certified copy to a private address is the first
argument about what else it may carry.

**Discuss:** In your office, which channel is misused most often? Who decides, and does anybody ever
say no?
