# Unit 9 · Signed Is Not Ratified
%%SUB Signature does less than people claim and more than people admit%%

A newspaper writes that your state has agreed to a convention. Your state has signed it. Those are
not the same sentence, and the difference is not a technicality: there is an article about exactly
this, in a convention about conventions, and that convention has a hundred and sixteen parties out
of rather more states than that.

> > A state is obliged **to refrain from acts which would defeat the object and purpose** of a

> > a party.

> |---|---|
> | **Signature** | authenticates the text and triggers **Article 18**. It does **not** make the state bound by the treaty's substantive obligations |

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP2-09-06%%

*After which verbs does the next verb take *-ing*, and after which does it take *to*?*

**1B  Underline every *-ing* form and circle every *to* + verb in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| verb + **to** | agree, decide, refuse, undertake, hope, promise, offer, fail, manage, intend | They agreed **to sign**. |
| verb + **-ing** | avoid, consider, involve, risk, suggest, mind, enjoy, finish, stop, keep | We are considering **ratifying**. |
| verb + object + **to** | ask, tell, advise, allow, require, remind | They advised us **to wait**. |
| **-ing** after a preposition | without, before, after, by, instead of | **without signing** · **before ratifying** |
| both, different meanings | stop, remember, forget | stop **minding** *(no longer mind)* · stop **to mind** *(pause in order to)* |

> **Grammar Note** — There is no rule to deduce these from; they are learned by verb. The three that
> cost most in drafting are *avoid*, *consider* and *risk*, which all take *-ing*: *we avoid to
> write*, *we are considering to ratify* and *we risk to defeat* are all wrong and all common.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The gap between signature and ratification is one of the most misreported facts in international
affairs, and both misreadings are politically useful to somebody. A government that wants credit for
a treaty will let *signed* be read as *joined*. A critic who wants to deny it any credit will say
that a signature is worthless. An officer who insists on the accurate middle will be accused in turn
of inflating and of minimizing, usually in the same week. The defense is not tone but structure: two
clauses, always in the same order, *we have signed; ratification is outstanding*, followed if there
is room by the obligation of restraint. Said that way it is quotable, it is complete, and it cannot
be cut in half without the cut being visible.

**Discuss:** In your own system, who has to approve a treaty after signature? How long does that
usually take, and who explains the delay?
