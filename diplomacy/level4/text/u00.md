# Starting Point · What Protocol Is For
%%SUB Before Unit 1: the formula is not decoration%%

People outside a ministry believe protocol is about seating plans and flags. People inside it know
that protocol is a system for allowing two states that disagree about almost everything to exchange
a document, a visit and a handshake without creating a new disagreement in the process.

### 1 · What you already have

%%FIG j00-f1 | A single review sheet with six boxes. Each box has a heading — the past tenses, the perfect aspect, modals, the passive, conditionals, and hedging — and one short example sentence from a protocol officer's working day.%%

Three books have given you the tenses, the modals, the passive, reported speech, conditionals and
hedging. This book adds the one thing none of them needed: **register**. Not politeness, which is a
tone, but register, which is a set of forms that carry meaning precisely because they are fixed.

**1A  Say one sentence from each box to your partner.** Then say which box your own drafts use least.

### 2 · Why formula is useful

%%FIG j00-f2 | Two columns headed the same message, twice. The left column has three plainly worded sentences. The right column has the same three in formulaic register, each one marked with a small label reading what the formula protects.%%

| Plain | Formulaic | What the formula protects |
|---|---|---|
| We want to send a delegation next month. | The Ministry **has the honour to request the consent** of the Government of Astrand to a special mission. | that consent is being **asked for**, not assumed |
| You have not replied. | The Ministry **avails itself of the opportunity to recall** its note of 2 March. | that the fact is **recorded**, without an accusation |
| We will not accept that. | The Ministry **is not in a position to** agree. | that a refusal is **not a quarrel** |

> **The formula is a shared surface.** Both ministries know exactly what each phrase means and
> exactly what it does not mean. A plainly worded version of the same message carries whatever tone
> the reader is in the mood to hear.

### 3 · Four sentences a protocol officer writes every week

%%FIG j00-f3 | A card taped inside a folder, printed with four sentences in bold, each with one line of smaller type under it explaining when to use it.%%

> **1  Consent is required and has not yet been obtained.**
> *Said before anybody books an aircraft.*
>
> **2  The Convention has not entered into force.**
> *Said before anybody cites it.*
>
> **3  The figure is on the depositary's page and carries the date of that page.**
> *Said instead of the figure on its own.*
>
> **4  We do not print a count here.**
> *Said when the figure is not to hand, which is more often than anybody admits.*

**3B  Cover the explanations.** Your partner reads a situation; you say which sentence fits.

### 4 · The three questions before any instrument is cited

%%FIG j00-f4 | Three numbered boxes in a column, each with a question in bold and two short lines under it, and an arrow at the foot pointing to a fourth box reading only now may you cite it.%%

> **1  Is it in force at all?**
> Adopted is not in force. Signed is not ratified. Read the entry-into-force clause, not the date on
> the cover.
>
> **2  Is it in force for **both** states?**
> A convention can bind us and not them. The status page is read line by line, not totalled.
>
> **3  What exactly has each state accepted?**
> Protocols, annexes, reservations and declarations. A party to a framework may have accepted none
> of its protocols.

**4B  Think about it.** *Talk with a partner.*

1. Which of the three questions does your own office skip most often?
2. What would it cost to ask it every time?

> **One thing before Unit 1.** Every instrument in this book is real and most of them are weaker than
> they look. That is not a complaint about international law; it is the working condition of the
> profession. The officers who are trusted with difficult files are the ones who know, for each
> instrument on their desk, exactly how much of it is in force, for whom, and since when.
