# Unit 10 · The Nineteenth of March, 1815
%%SUB Precedence by the date of notification — a rule invented to stop people quarrelling about doorways%%
%%GOALS

Who goes through the door first was, for two centuries, a question that produced genuine disputes
between states. In March 1815 a congress annexed a short regulation to its final act: heads of
mission would be divided into classes, and within each class precedence would run by the date on
which arrival was officially notified. Not by the size of the state. Not by its antiquity. By the
date on a piece of paper.

> *Protocol Department · precedence · page 1 of 1*
>
> **On 19 March 1815**, a regulation on the relative rank of diplomatic agents was **annexed to the
> Final Act of the Congress of Vienna**. It did two things.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **1** | it divided heads of mission into **classes** |
> | **2** | it provided that, **within each class**, precedence should run **by the date of the official notification of arrival** |

> power of the state represented, and the arguments were real: they delayed negotiations, produced
> incidents at ceremonies and, on occasion, worse. **It was the arbitrariness of the new rule that
> made it work.** A date cannot be argued about by two ministries that disagree about everything
> else.
>
> **What is in force today.** The 1815 regulation **is not the instrument in force**. Modern practice
> rests on the law of diplomatic relations as it now stands, and on each state's own rules. **What has
> survived is the principle**: classes, and within a class, order by the date on which arrival was
> officially notified. **Rarely has a short annex outlived its congress so completely.**
>

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP4-10-06%%

**1B  Underline the three fronted structures in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Device | Example |
|---|---|
| **It is … that** | **It is the arbitrariness that** makes it work. |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Precedence looks like the most trivial thing a ministry does and is one of the few parts of its work
that has never gone wrong in two centuries, which is worth thinking about. The reason is that the
rule contains no judgment: it takes one fact, the date on which arrival was notified, and produces
every seating decision mechanically from it. Protocol officers are offered better criteria every week
— the size of a delegation, the warmth of a relationship, the seniority of an individual, the
importance of a visit — and all of them are offered kindly and all of them would destroy the system
within a year, because each one is arguable and a date is not. The lesson generalizes well beyond
seating plans. A rule that nobody can argue with is worth more than a rule that is wise, and
officers who understand that are the ones nobody has to telephone about a dinner.

**Discuss:** Which arbitrary rule in your own work would you defend hardest? Who tries to improve
it?
