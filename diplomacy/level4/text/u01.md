A delegation of eleven people wants to arrive on the fourteenth. Nobody has asked the other state
whether it may. There is a convention about exactly this, it was adopted in 1969, it did not enter
into force until 1985, and about thirty-eight states are parties to it. None of that changes the
answer, and all of it changes your draft.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Two dates, always. The date a convention was adopted tells you what people were
> thinking. The date it entered into force tells you whether it is law. The gap between them is the
> most informative number on the page.

%%FIG j01-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a boxed quotation of one article, a short table with three dated rows, a paragraph headed what follows for our drafting, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · protocol note 1
> *Protocol Department · visiting delegations · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The rule first, in the Convention's own words. Article 2 of the Convention on Special
> Missions:**
>
> > A State may send a special mission to another State **with the consent of the latter, previously
> > obtained** through the diplomatic or another agreed or mutually acceptable channel.
>
> Article 2 adds that a state wishing to send the **same** special mission to two or more states
> **shall so inform** each of the receiving states when seeking their consent.
>
> **Now the three dates.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Adopted** | **8 December 1969** |
> | **Entered into force** | **21 June 1985** — **sixteen years later** |
> | **Parties** | **38**, on the depositary's table **as at April 2012** |
>
> **What follows for our drafting.** Two things, and they pull in opposite directions.
>
> First: **the Convention probably does not bind us or the state we are writing to.** With
> thirty-eight parties, most pairs of states in the world are not both parties to it, and a draft
> that says *under the Special Missions Convention* is very likely to be wrong about which law
> applies.
>
> Second: **the requirement of consent does not depend on the Convention.** No state may send an
> official mission into another state's territory without that state's agreement, obtained in
> advance. The Convention **codifies** the requirement; it is not the source of it. So the practical
> rule survives entirely intact, and only the citation disappears.
>
> **What to write instead.** *Consent is required and has not yet been obtained.* Eight words, no
> instrument cited, nothing to be corrected on.
>
> *Source: the Convention on Special Missions, adopted 8 December 1969, Article 2; its entry into
> force on 21 June 1985; and the depositary's table, which recorded 38 parties as at April 2012. A
> current figure should be taken from that table on the day it is needed.*

### 1 · Inversion after a negative adverbial

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP4-01-06%%

> **Hala:** Who has booked the aircraft?
> **Omar:** Transport booked it yesterday.
> **Hala:** **Not until consent has been obtained is anything booked.** That is the rule and it is
> the only rule in this office that I will not discuss.
> **Omar:** They told us informally that it would be fine.
> **Hala:** **At no point does an informal indication amount to consent.** **Nor does a conversation
> at a conference.**
> **Omar:** So I write to Transport and cancel it.
> **Hala:** You write to Transport, and you write the note requesting consent, in that order.
> **Only when the reply is on this desk will I sign a program.**

*What has happened to the word order in each of Hala's sentences?*

**1B  Underline the four inverted structures in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Adverbial at the front | Inversion | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Not until** | yes | **Not until** consent has been obtained **is** anything booked. |
| **At no point / Under no circumstances** | yes | **At no point does** an informal indication amount to consent. |
| **Nor / Neither** | yes | **Nor does** a conversation at a conference. |
| **Only when / Only after / Only then** | yes | **Only when** the reply is received **will** I sign. |
| **Never** | yes | **Never has** this office booked before consent. |
| **No sooner … than** | yes | **No sooner had** the note gone **than** the reply arrived. |

> **Grammar Note** — The inversion is the same as in a question: the auxiliary moves in front of the
> subject. *Only when the reply is received I will sign* is wrong; *only when the reply is received
> will I sign* is right. And the structure is emphatic by nature, which is why it belongs in a
> standing instruction and not in every paragraph.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Consent for a visit is the most routine thing in protocol and the one most often skipped, because
skipping it almost always works. A state that has been told informally that a visit would be welcome
will usually be delighted when the delegation lands. The reason to do it properly is not that the
visit will be refused; it is that the small number of occasions on which a state does want to say no
are exactly the occasions when it matters most that a refusal can be given quietly, in a note, as an
answer to a request. A state that cannot refuse without creating an incident has not been shown
respect; it has been cornered. Protocol officers who understand that tend to be the ones other
ministries deal with gladly, and the reputation is worth more than the ten days Transport thinks it
needs.

**Discuss:** When did your office last say no to something? Was it possible to do it quietly?

### 3 · Write

Write **130 to 160 words**: a note verbale requesting consent.

**Use the opening and closing formulas. Request consent rather than announcing a visit. Give the
purpose in about six words and a maximum number for the composition. Say *on dates to be agreed*.
Offer a channel for the reply. Then add an internal note, ruled off, saying what has not been
arranged and what instrument you have not cited, and why.**

**Read your note aloud to your partner first.** Reading it out is how you hear an announcement
pretending to be a request. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you put a date in the request? What would
the reply have answered?
