# Foreign Service English 3
%%SUB English for Foreign Ministry Staff · Syrian Edition · CEFR B1+%%

## How to use this book

This is the fourth of six books. Book Foundation was the building, Book 1 the consular section and
Book 2 the desk officer's chair. This one is **protocol and the mission**: the visiting delegation
that needs consent before it travels, the status page that decides whether an instrument applies at
all, the order of precedence that was settled in 1815 to stop people duelling, and the motorcade
that is nine minutes late.

**Twelve units**, sixteen pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | A real instrument or published record, with its figures, its dates and its caveats |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures of formal register, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a long dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `DIP4-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**One subject runs through all twelve units: the status page.** Not the glamorous part of protocol —
the page that tells you how many states are parties, on what date, under what entry-into-force
clause, and which protocols a particular state has actually accepted. Every unit in this book turns
on something that page says and that nobody reads.

**Twelve instruments, and what each one is really worth.** A convention adopted in 1969 that took
sixteen years to enter into force and has thirty-eight parties. A convention from 1975 that is one
ratification short of entering into force and has been for fifty years. A convention on United
Nations privileges and immunities with a hundred and sixty-two parties out of a hundred and
ninety-three member states. A framework convention whose six protocols have memberships that differ
by forty. A treaty with a hundred and seventy-eight ratifications that is not in force, because of
how one clause is written. A count that its own publisher's commentators say cannot officially be
stated.

**The grammar is the grammar of formal register.** Inversion after a negative adverbial, participle
clauses, formulaic requests, modal perfects, the future in the past, complex noun phrases,
conditional inversion, impersonal structures, qualifying markers, fronting for emphasis, passive
causatives, and finally the note verbale itself. Protocol writing is the most formulaic register in
the service, and formula is not decoration: it is what lets two ministries that disagree about
everything else exchange a document without creating a new dispute.

**Four sentences you will write until they are automatic.** *Consent is required and has not yet been
obtained.* *The Convention has not entered into force.* *The figure is on the depositary's page and
carries the date of that page.* And *we do not print a count here*, which is the hardest sentence in
this book and the one that has saved every officer who has learned to write it.

## The people

The same ministry, now seen from the fourth floor. **Omar Sabbagh** has been moved to Protocol for a
year, which everybody tells him is a reward and which involves more arithmetic than any job he has
had. **Hala Mounzer** runs Protocol and has done for eleven years; she has a card index of
precedence that she does not trust to a computer. **Layla Bahri** in Legal Affairs reads status pages
for pleasure. **Nadia Shaheen** is still on the Europe desk and still marks drafts in pencil.
**Fadi Antoun** has been moved to the Treaty Section, where he is happier. **Samira Barakat**,
**Hana Darwish**, **Ahmad Rifai** and **Yusuf Mansour** are where they were.

New in this book: **Tarek Ayyoub**, the ministry's transport officer, whose entire professional life
is the nine minutes between a motorcade's scheduled arrival and its actual one.

Outside the building: **Karin Lindholm** and **Oskar Rehn** at the Embassy of Astrand, **Lucia
Moretti** at the Consulate of Perenne, **Linh Tran** at the Embassy of Marovia, **Ida Pedersen**, and
**Marco Caruso**, who in Unit 7 is finally told something useful.

Every one of them is invented. Astrand, Perenne, Marovia, Valmora, Kesteland and the Sable Isles are
invented countries, and they are invented on purpose: in a role-play about consent, precedence or
accession, nobody should have to put a real government's name to a position it has never taken.

## Nothing here is official

This is an independent teaching draft. It is not an official publication of any ministry and it
carries no official status. Every person, office, note, mission, motorcade and seating plan in it was
written for this book. The instruments and published records quoted in the reading texts are real,
and each text gives the instrument, its date, the figure, the page the figure came from and the date
of that page — because in this field a number without a page and a date is not information.
