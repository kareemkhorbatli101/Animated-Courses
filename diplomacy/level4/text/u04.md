# Unit 4 · Universal, and Its Protocols
%%SUB Four instruments, four memberships, and a figure that two publishers give differently by one%%

The Conventions of 1949 are the instruments most often described as universally ratified, and the
description is very nearly right. The Protocols are three further instruments with three smaller
memberships. A draft that says *the Geneva Conventions and their Protocols* as though it were one
thing should have said four.

*Which of Hala's modal perfects is about something that did happen?*

**1B  Underline the five modal perfects in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **should have** | You **should have** named it. | the right thing, not done |
| **shouldn't have** | You **shouldn't have** used *universal*. | the wrong thing, done |
| **need not have** | You **need not have** mentioned the Protocols. | done, and unnecessary |
| **didn't need to** | You **didn't need to** check — I had. | not necessary, so not done |
| **might / may have** | Somebody **might have** checked. | possible; unknown |
| **could have** | You **could have** written one figure. | possible; not done |
| **would have been** | The sentence **would have been** unanswerable. | the result of the thing not done |

> **Grammar Note** — *Need not have done* and *did not need to do* are not the same. *You need not
> have mentioned the Protocols* means you did mention them and it was unnecessary. *You did not need
> to mention them* means it was unnecessary, and leaves open whether you did. In a note about what
> went wrong, that difference is the difference between a criticism and an observation.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The law of armed conflict is the field where a ministry's figures are most likely to be read by
people who know them better than the drafter does, and where the cost of a loose phrase is highest.
Two habits are worth building. The first is naming the instrument: *the Conventions of 1949*, *the
first Additional Protocol*, never *the Geneva law*. The second is treating *universal* as a technical
claim rather than a compliment: the Conventions have earned it to within a state or two, and
extending it to instruments with two thirds or a third of that membership is not generous, it is
simply inaccurate. There is also a point about tone. Officers who work in this field daily, in
national societies and in armed forces, are usually the most precise people a ministry deals with.
A loose paragraph does not offend them; it just tells them, quietly, how much weight to give
everything else in the document.

**Discuss:** Which phrase does your own ministry use that merges several instruments? Who would
notice?
