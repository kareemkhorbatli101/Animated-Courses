# Unit 12 · The Depositary's Own Notes
%%SUB The last reading in this book is the publisher explaining its own table%%
%%GOALS
Read a publisher's notes on its own figures before using the figures
Draft a note verbale in the third person
Review the structures of Units 1 to 11
%%

Eleven units of this book have taken a published figure and asked what it does not show. This one
ends the book by reading the other half of the page: the notes in which the publisher explains, in
its own words, what its table records, as at which date, and what a reader must not conclude from it.
After that, the note verbale — the form in which a ministry says any of this to an embassy.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — The last and best trick of this trade is to read the publisher's own notes as a
> text in their own right. They are usually written by people who have been asked the same wrong
> question for forty years.

%%FIG j12-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a boxed block of small-type extracts headed what the publisher says, five short numbered observations, a line in bold set off alone, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · reference note 12
> *Legal Affairs · reading a depositary's status tables · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The source.** The United Nations publishes, through its Treaty Section, the status of the
> multilateral treaties deposited with the Secretary-General, together with **notes on how treaty
> actions are recorded**. The notes, not the tables, are the subject of this reference note.
>
> **1 The table is current only as at a stated date.** The published status carries **the date to
> which it is current**. A figure taken from it is a figure as at that date and must be quoted with
> it.
>
> **2 A recorded action carries its own date.** Ratification, acceptance, approval and accession are
> **recorded with the date of deposit** of the instrument, and the date of deposit is not the date of
> entry into force for that state, which the notes explain separately.
>
> **3 Accession and ratification have the same legal effect.** The notes make clear that a state which
> accedes becomes a party as fully as one which ratifies. **A table that lists both is not listing two
> classes of party.**
>
> **4 Some columns include signatories.** Where a column is headed **participant**, it may include
> states that have signed without becoming parties. **Counting a participant column gives a figure
> that is not the number of parties** — and this single confusion has produced more wrong figures than
> any other feature of these tables.
>
> **5 Reservations, declarations and objections are reproduced.** The notes record them **with their
> dates**, and the text of each. A state's name in a table therefore tells you less than the entry
> under its name.
>
> **The disclaimer.** The published status also carries language to the effect that **the inclusion
> of an entity in these tables does not imply any position on questions of status or recognition**.
> A drafter who quotes a table has quoted that too.
>
> **Why this is the last reading in this book. Every other unit asked what a figure did not show.
> This one shows you that the publisher said so first**, in smaller type, above the table, in notes
> that most readers walk past. The whole method of this book is in that habit: read the notes, quote
> the date, count the right column, and say what you checked.
>
> *Source: the United Nations Treaty Collection's published status of multilateral treaties deposited
> with the Secretary-General, and the Treaty Section's published notes on how treaty actions are
> recorded. No figure is quoted here: the practice described, not any count, is the subject. Readers
> should consult the published status for any figure, with the date to which it is current.*

date to which information is current · **a disclaimer** — a paragraph saying what a listing does not
imply</sub>

### 1 · The note verbale

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP4-12-06%%

> **Hala:** Your draft note verbale says *we would like to inform you*.
> **Omar:** That is what we want to do.
> **Hala:** It is, and the form does not permit *we*. **The Ministry presents its compliments to the
> Embassy and has the honour to inform it** — third person throughout, no *we*, no *you*, no
> signature.
> **Omar:** It feels like a costume.
> **Hala:** It is a costume, and the costume does something. An institution is writing to an
> institution, nobody's personality is in the room, and the same form carries good news and a protest
> without changing its voice. **Only by keeping the voice identical** can a ministry make a protest
> that is unmistakably a protest and still unmistakably correct.
> **Omar:** And the facts?
> **Hala:** Enclosure. The body carries the act — informing, requesting, protesting, transmitting —
> and the enclosure carries the dates and the figures, where they can be checked and corrected without
> rewriting the note.
> **Omar:** And the ending?
> **Hala:** **The Ministry avails itself of this opportunity to renew to the Embassy the assurances of
> its highest consideration.** Every time. The day you vary the ending is the day the Embassy spends
> an afternoon wondering why.

*Why does Hala say the identical voice is useful rather than merely traditional?*

**1B  Underline the third-person formulas in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Part | Formula |
|---|---|
| opening | The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates **presents its compliments to** the Embassy of … |
| the act | **and has the honour to** inform / request / transmit / draw attention to |
| reference | **With reference to** the Embassy's note No. … of … |
| enclosure | **Enclosed herewith** is … |
| closing | The Ministry **avails itself of this opportunity to renew to** the Embassy **the assurances of its highest consideration.** |
| foot | place, date, seal — **no signature** |

> **Grammar Note** — The note verbale has one tense problem worth naming: *has the honour to inform*
> is present, and everything it informs about is usually past. Keep the formula present and put the
> past where it belongs, in the clause and the enclosure: *has the honour to inform the Embassy that
> the instrument was deposited on 12 November*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The habit this book has been teaching is unglamorous and it is the one thing a ministry cannot buy.
Anybody can be fluent; fluency is three years of practice. What takes a career is the reflex that
stops a hand halfway to a number — the half-second in which an officer thinks *which column was that*
— and the willingness to be the person who says the longer sentence at five o'clock on a Thursday
when everybody wants the shorter one. Nobody will thank you for it, because the thing that does not
happen leaves no trace: no correction, no awkward question in a committee, no minister using a figure
that counts documents when the paragraph says states. The officers who have this reflex are known in
every ministry, and they are not known for it. They are known for being the person you ask.

**Discuss:** Who in your ministry is the person people ask? What do they do that is visible, and what
do they do that is not?

### 3 · Write

Write a **note verbale of six to eight lines with a five-row enclosure**, transmitting or correcting a
figure in your own area.

**Use the third person throughout. Perform one act in one line. Refer to the earlier note by number
and date. Put the figure, the column or basis, the as-at date, the source and who checked it in the
enclosure. Keep the opening and closing formulas unvaried. Date and seal; do not sign.**

**Read your note aloud to your partner first.** Any sentence that sounds like you personally is a
sentence to move into the enclosure or delete. Then check your own against the list, and swap.
