Adopted in March 1975 by fifty-seven votes to one. Needing thirty-five instruments of ratification to
enter into force. Having thirty-four. There is no sentence in this book that teaches the difference
between a treaty and the law better than that one, and nothing in the text obliges anybody to deposit
the thirty-fifth.

**1B  Underline the three participle clauses in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Example | Means |
|---|---|---|
| **-ing** (present participle) | **Having** thirty-four parties, it is not in force. | because / since |
| **having been + participle** | **Having been adopted** in 1975, it… | after / because, in the past |
| **-ed** (past participle) | **Adopted** in 1975, the Convention… | which was adopted |
| **being + participle** | **Being open** for accession, it may be joined. | because it is |
| formulaic equivalents | **pending** agreement · **subject to** ratification · **failing** a reply |

> **Grammar Note** — The subject of a participle clause must be the subject of the main clause.
> *Having thirty-four parties, the Ministry considers it not to be in force* says that the Ministry
> has thirty-four parties. That error — the dangling participle — is the one thing that makes formal
> drafting look careless rather than careful, and it survives every proofread because every word in
> it is spelled correctly.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The number of adopted-but-not-in-force instruments in international law surprises everybody who
learns it, and it is not a scandal. A conference produces a text that a majority can accept; states
then decide one by one whether the text is worth the domestic effort of ratification, and some texts
are simply not. The professional consequence is a habit: read the final clauses first, every time,
including for instruments you have cited for years. There is also a point about how to describe such
an instrument to a counterpart. Some of the states you write to have ratified the convention you are
calling not in force, and they are proud of having done so. *Has not entered into force* is a fact
about the collective; *dead letter* is a verdict on their decision. One of those sentences you can
use in a note to any ministry in the world, and the other you cannot use in a note to about
thirty-four of them.

> *Protocol Department with Legal Affairs · 4 March*
>
> **Conclusion first. The Convention has not entered into force and is not available to be cited as

>
> **Why.** The Convention, **having been adopted on 14 March 1975** by 57 votes to 1 with 15
> abstentions, provides in **Article 89** that it enters into force **on the thirtieth day following
> the deposit of the thirty-fifth instrument of ratification or accession**. **The depositary's page

> fifty years. **Nothing in the text obliges any state to deposit the thirty-fifth**, and no

>

> relation to an organization only once **the host State has accepted** it and the organization has
> completed the procedure the Convention lays down. **Being a party would therefore not be sufficient
> on its own.**
>
> **What governs instead, and what we cite.**
> 1. the **headquarters agreement** between the organization and its host state;
> 2. any **bilateral arrangements** to which we are party;

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the conclusion in the first line and the reasoning underneath. A legal note
