# Unit 7 · A Hundred and Ninety-Three of a Hundred and Ninety-Seven
%%SUB Near-universal is not universal, and the denominator is almost never the UN membership%%
%%GOALS

A hundred and ninety-three states parties. Everybody hears a hundred and ninety-three and thinks of
the United Nations membership, which is also a hundred and ninety-three, and concludes that the
instrument is universal. The denominator here is a hundred and ninety-seven, and the coincidence of
the numerator with a famous figure is the trap.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When a numerator looks like a famous figure, that is the moment to find the
> denominator. Coincidences of this kind are the commonest source of confident errors in this field.

%%FIG j07-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, two figures printed one above the other as a fraction, a short table explaining the denominator, a boxed paragraph headed the coincidence, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · protocol note 7
> *Protocol Department and Legal Affairs · a near-universal convention · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The figure, written properly.**
>
> > **193 out of 197.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **States Parties** | **193** |
> | **Entities eligible to become parties** | **197** |
> | Of which | the **193 United Nations member states** and **four further entities** that are not UN member states |
>
> **The coincidence.** The numerator, 193, is the same number as the current United Nations
> membership. A reader who knows that figure — which is almost every reader in this building — will
> assume that the denominator is also 193 and that the convention is **universal**. **It is
> near-universal**, which is a different and accurate word, and the four states that are not parties
> are **not** the four non-UN entities. **Two separate facts are being merged by one coincidence.**
>
> **Why the denominator is larger than the UN membership.** The convention is open to entities beyond
> the United Nations membership, which is common in this field and almost never reflected in a
> briefing. **In most published counts in this book the denominator is not the UN membership**, and in
> several of them it is not a round number at all.
>
> **What to write.** Two sentences. *The Convention has 193 States Parties out of 197 eligible
> entities.* *It is therefore near-universal, and four eligible entities are not parties.*
>
> **What not to write.** *All states are parties.* *Universal.* *193 — that is to say, every member
> of the United Nations.* The third is the most dangerous, because it is nearly true and invites no
> checking.
>
> *Source: published figures giving 193 States Parties to the Convention and 197 entities eligible to
> become parties, the latter comprising the 193 United Nations member states and four further
> entities. Figures are dated; take current ones from the implementing organization's own page.*

### 1 · Conditional inversion

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP4-07-06%%

> **Hala:** Your note says *if you want the current figures, let us know*.
> **Omar:** That is a normal sentence.
> **Hala:** It is a normal sentence in a letter to a colleague. In a note to a ministry: **should you
> wish to receive the current figures, they can be transmitted at once**.
> **Omar:** Because *should you wish* is more polite?
> **Hala:** Because it is more formal, which is not the same thing. Formal register here is a signal
> that the document is official and that its words have been chosen. **Were it not for** that signal,
> every note would read as a personal opinion from whoever typed it.
> **Omar:** And *had we known*?
> **Hala:** Is for explaining a mistake without apologizing four times. **Had we known the
> denominator, the brief would have said near-universal.** One clause, and the error is accounted
> for.

*Which of Hala's inverted forms is about the past?*

**1B  Underline the three inverted conditionals in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Inverted form | Equivalent | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Should you wish** to… | if you wish to | a formal offer |
| **Should any difficulty arise** | if any difficulty arises | a formal contingency |
| **Were it not for** X | if it were not for X | present counterfactual |
| **Were the Ministry to** agree | if the Ministry agreed | a formal hypothetical |
| **Had we known** | if we had known | past counterfactual |
| **Had it not been for** X | if it had not been for X | past counterfactual |

> **Grammar Note** — Only three verbs invert this way in modern English: *should*, *were* and *had*.
> There is no inverted form of *if we agree* or *if it rains*. And an inverted conditional never takes
> *if* as well: *should you wish* and *if you should wish* are both correct, but *if should you wish*
> is not English at all.

**1D  Ask and answer with a partner. Three each.**

2. What would be different, beginning *were it not for*?
3. What would have been different, beginning *had we known*?

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Universality is the most politically charged adjective in this field, and the arithmetic around it is
more interesting than the politics. In almost every instrument the denominator is not the United
Nations membership: some conventions are open to entities that are not UN member states, some are
open only to members of a particular organization, some are regional. A ministry that writes
*universal* about a near-universal instrument is usually not making a political claim; it has
assumed a denominator. The repair is not skepticism but arithmetic: read the eligibility provision in
the final clauses, write the fraction with both numbers, and use *near-universal*, which is accurate
and sounds like a compliment to anybody who understands it. The officers who do this are also the
ones who notice when a genuinely universal instrument comes along, and there are very few of them.

**Discuss:** Which instrument in your field is genuinely universal? How would you prove it?
