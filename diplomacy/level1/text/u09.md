### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A document symbol is not a title. It tells you **where a document came from**
> and **what kind of document it is**. It does not tell you what it says, and it does not tell you
> which edition you have.

%%FIG g09-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a symbol written large and broken into four parts with a label under each part, a short table of five symbol beginnings with their meanings, a boxed paragraph about additions and corrections, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · induction note 9
> *For new entrants · Documents Unit · page 1 of 1*
>
> Every official United Nations document has a **symbol**. Learn to read it. It saves hours.
>
> **A / RES / 79 / 249**
>
> | Part | What it means |
> |---|---|
> | **A/** | the **General Assembly** |
> | **RES/** | it is a **resolution** |
> | **79** | the **session** number |
> | **249** | the number of that resolution in that session |
>
> Some beginnings you will see every week:
>
> | Symbol begins | The organ or the kind |
> |---|---|
> | **A/** | General Assembly |
> | **S/** | Security Council |
> | **A/RES/** | a General Assembly resolution |
> | **S/RES/** | a Security Council resolution |
> | **/INF/** | information |
>
> **Additions and corrections.** A symbol ending in **/Add.1** is an **addendum** — something added
> to the document. A symbol ending in **/Corr.1** is a **corrigendum** — a **correction** to it.
>
> So **A/80/11** and **A/80/11/Add.1** are **not** the same document, and **A/80/11/Corr.1** means
> part of A/80/11 was wrong. **A symbol can be cited perfectly and still be the wrong edition.**
> Always check for an Add. or a Corr.
>
> *Source: United Nations document symbol system, as described by the Dag Hammarskjöld Library.*

### 1 · The present perfect

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP1-09-06%%

> **Nadia:** Have you sent the list?
> **Omar:** Yes, I've sent it.
> **Nadia:** When?
> **Omar:** At half past nine.
> **Nadia:** Then say that. *I sent it at half past nine.* If you tell me when, use the past. If you
> only tell me that it is done, use *have sent*.
> **Omar:** And if it isn't done?
> **Nadia:** *I haven't sent it yet.* Not *I didn't send it*. *Yet* means you are going to.

*Nadia says both sentences are correct. What is the difference between them?*

**1B  Underline the present perfect forms in 1A and the one past simple.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **have / has + past participle** | I **have sent** it. She **has replied**. | it is done; the time is not important |
| **with *already*** | I**'ve already** copied them. | sooner than expected |
| **with *yet*** (negative) | I **haven't sent** it **yet**. | not done, and expected |
| **with *just*** | She **has just** telephoned. | a few minutes ago |
| **Question** | **Have** you **sent** it? | |
| **With a time → past simple** | I **sent** it at half past nine. | the time is given |

> **Grammar Note** — The present perfect does not take a finished time. *I have sent it yesterday*
> is wrong; say *I sent it yesterday*. The rule is simple and it is broken constantly: as soon as you
> say **when**, the verb goes into the past simple.

**1D  Ask and answer with a partner. Three each.**

3. What haven't you done yet?

### 2 · *Already*, *yet* and *just*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Word | Where it goes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **already** | before the main verb | I've **already** sent it. |
| **yet** | at the end, in negatives and questions | I haven't sent it **yet**. · Have you sent it **yet**? |
| **just** | before the main verb | She's **just** telephoned. |
| **still** | before the main verb, for something continuing | I'm **still** waiting for the reply. |
| **never** | before the main verb | I've **never** seen that symbol. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Yet* belongs in negatives and questions and goes at the end. *Already* belongs
> in positives and goes in the middle. Learners often swap them, and the result — *I have yet sent
> it* — is one of the few mistakes at this level that stops the sentence working at all.

**2B  Listen and repeat.** %%AUD DIP1-09-07%%

**2C  Put the word in the right place.**

1. I have sent the list. *(already)*
2. She hasn't replied. *(yet)*
3. The embassy has telephoned. *(just)*

**3A  Study the chart.**

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| *Question* | **Visit 14 March — room for 12 — for action by Tue 11th** |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Written messages carry different expectations in different services. In some ministries an email is
informal and the real communication is a signed paper note, so an instruction by email is not an
instruction; in others email is the record and paper is a courtesy. The use of the copy field is a
cultural fact too: in some organizations copying a superior is normal and polite, and in others it
is read as a threat. Length differs — a message of one line is efficient in some capitals and curt
in others, where a greeting and a closing are expected before any content. And the word *urgent* is
used so differently that experienced officers stop using it and write a date instead.

**Discuss:** In your office, is an email the record? Who do you copy, and why?

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD DIP1-09-16%%

%%FIG g09-f16 | A screen eleven months later showing a search box with two words typed into it and a results list of forty-one messages, all with the same forwarded subject line. Beside the screen, a printed message with an empty subject line and a note clipped to it. On the wall, a printed card listing the three parts of a subject line.%%

> Eleven months later a question came from another department. They needed to know what the Ministry
> had told the Embassy of Astrand about the room on the 14th.
>
> Omar searched his sent messages. Forty-one of them had the same subject line, because the thread
> had been forwarded through four departments and nobody had rewritten it. He found the right one in
> twenty minutes by opening them in order.
>
> The two messages with no subject line at all were never found. One of them was the two-line message
> he had defended to Nadia. He does not know what was in it. Neither does anybody else, and nothing
> appears to have gone wrong, which is the reason the habit is hard to change.

1. What did the twenty minutes cost?
2. Why is it difficult to learn from the two lost messages?
3. What rule would you write on the card on the wall?
