### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When somebody quotes a rule, ask one question: **which instrument?** Most
> wrong rules are not invented. They are real rules from the wrong document, or real numbers from
> the wrong place.

%%FIG g03-f5 | A one-page counter note: a heading, a boxed line in bold giving one date, a short table with three rows naming three instruments and what each one does, a short paragraph headed what is not in any of them, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · counter note 3
> *Consular Affairs · for staff at the counter · page 1 of 1*
>
> People at the counter quote two rules about passports. One is real. One is not where they think it
> is.
>
> **The real date: 24 November 2015.**
>
> | Instrument | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | **Annex 9** to the Convention on International Civil Aviation, **Standard 3.10.1** | It says that passports issued after **24 November 2005** that are **not machine readable** had to have an expiry date **before 24 November 2015**. |
> | **Doc 9303** | It is the **technical specification**. It says what a machine-readable travel document must look like. |
> | National law | It says **how long** a passport is valid for in each country. |
>
> **What is not in any of them.** There is **no international standard that says a passport is valid
> for ten years**. Ten years is in **national law**, country by country. The ten years appear in the
> international record only as a reason: the date of 2015 was chosen because some states were still
> issuing ten-year passports in 2005.
>
> So at the counter:
>
> 1. If somebody asks about **machine readable**, the answer is **Annex 9**, and the date is 2015.
> 2. If somebody asks **how many years**, the answer is **national law**, and it is not the same
>    everywhere.
> 3. If somebody says *it is an international rule*, they are quoting the right number from the
>    wrong place.
>
> *Sources: Convention on International Civil Aviation, Annex 9 (Facilitation), Standard 3.10.1;
> ICAO Doc 9303, Machine Readable Travel Documents. Validity periods are set by national law.*

agreement · **a specification** — the document that says exactly how to make something</sub>

### 1 · *Can* and *could* for requests

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP1-03-06%%

> **Samira:** Good morning. Could I see your passport, please?
> **Visitor:** Here you are. Can you tell me if it's all right?
> **Samira:** One moment. — It's valid until the fourth of July. That's four months.
> **Visitor:** So it's fine.
> **Samira:** It's valid. The country you are going to asks for six months, so for that trip it is
> not enough.
> **Visitor:** But ten years is the rule.
> **Samira:** Ten years is how long it was issued for. Could I write this down for you?

*Samira says the passport is valid and also not enough. How can both be true?*

**1B  Underline every *can* and *could* in 1A. Which one is more polite?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Could I …?** | **Could I** see your passport, please? | the politest request |
| **Can I …?** | **Can I** take a copy? | neutral, friendly |
| **Could you …?** | **Could you** spell that, please? | asking the other person |
| **Can you …?** | **Can you** come back on Thursday? | neutral |
| **Would you mind …?** | **Would you mind** waiting two minutes? | very polite, + *-ing* |
| **Short answers** | Of course. · Certainly. · I'm afraid not. | |

> **Grammar Note** — *Could* is not the past of *can* here. It is politeness. At a counter, *could*
> is the safe choice with a stranger and *can* is normal with a colleague. Both take the bare verb:
> *could I **see**…*, never *could I to see*.

**1D  Ask and answer with a partner. Three each.**

3. Ask somebody to wait.

### 2 · *Have to* and *don't have to*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **have to** = it is necessary | You **have to** renew it before you travel. |
| **has to** | He **has to** sign both copies. |
| **don't have to** = it is not necessary | You **don't have to** come back here. |
| **doesn't have to** | She **doesn't have to** pay today. |
| **Question** | **Do** I **have to** come back? |
| **Short answer** | Yes, you do. · No, you don't. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Don't have to* means it is **not necessary**. It does not mean *you must not*.
> *You don't have to come back* is an offer. *You must not come back* is something else entirely, and
> saying the wrong one at a counter causes complaints.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What a counter is for differs from country to country, and so does how a refusal sounds. In some
systems the officer at the counter has real authority and can decide a case; in others the counter
only receives papers and every decision is taken elsewhere, which means *I cannot decide this* is
the truth rather than an excuse. There are differences in politeness, too: in some languages a
direct *you have to* is normal and friendly, and translated into English it can sound like an order,
while a very polite English request can sound weak and be ignored. And the word *rule* travels
badly — in some places a rule is a rule, and in others the first question is always whose rule, and
that question is not rudeness but good practice.

**Discuss:** At your counter, who can decide? What do you say when the answer is somebody else's
rule?
