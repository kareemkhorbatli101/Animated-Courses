### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Three different things get called *a visa*: the permission to travel, the
> permission to stay, and the decision at the border. Keep them apart and most visa questions answer
> themselves.

%%FIG g11-f5 | A one-page counter note: a heading, a line in bold giving the rule, a diagram of a sliding window over a calendar strip, a short table with three rows separating the visa, the stay and the border decision, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · counter note 11
> *Consular Affairs · short stays · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The rule: no more than 90 days in any 180-day period.**
>
> The important word is **any**. You do not count from the first of January. You count **backwards
> from each day of the stay**: look back 180 days and add up the days spent inside.
>
> So the window **rolls**. Tomorrow the oldest day drops out and today comes in. **Leaving does not
> reset the count.** Days from an earlier trip in the same window still count.
>
> Now three things that are all called *the visa*.
>
> | | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | **The visa** | permission to **present yourself** at the border between two dates. |
> | **The 90 days** | the **maximum stay** inside the 180-day window. |
> | **The decision at the border** | taken by an officer, on the day. |
>
> **A visa authorizes the holder to apply for entry. It is not a guarantee of entry, and it is not an
> entitlement to 90 days.** A visa valid for six months with a 90-day limit is completely normal and
> is not a contradiction.
>
> *Source: Schengen Borders Code, Article 6, and the European Commission's short-stay calculator.
> Other countries have their own rules; this note is about this one.*

### 1 · Comparatives

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP1-11-06%%

*Samira says it is "more complicated than it looks and simpler than it sounds". What does she mean?*

**1B  Underline every comparative in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Adjective | Comparative | Example |
|---|---|---|
| long | long**er** | The visa is **longer than** the stay. |
| short | short**er** | Ninety days is **shorter than** six months. |
| easy | eas**ier** | Counting forward is **easier than** counting back. |
| simple | simpl**er** / **more** simple | This is **simpler than** it sounds. |
| complicated | **more** complicated | It is **more complicated than** it looks. |
| good / bad | **better** / **worse** | A date is **better than** a word. |

> **Grammar Note** — Short adjectives add *-er*; long ones take *more*. Two or more syllables
> usually means *more*, except for words ending in *-y*, which change to *-ier*. And the second half
> is *than*, not *that*: *longer than*, never *longer that*.

### 2 · *Too* and *enough*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **too** + adjective = more than you want | The passport is **too** old. |
| **too many** + countable | You have used **too many** days. |
| **too much** + uncountable | There is **too much** time between the two stamps. |
| **enough** + noun | You don't have **enough** days left. |
| adjective + **enough** | The passport isn't valid **long enough**. |
| **not … enough to** | You don't have **enough** days **to** stay three weeks. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Enough* goes **after** an adjective and **before** a noun: *long enough*, but
> *enough days*. *Too* always goes before the adjective. A sentence like *you have days enough* is
> old-fashioned English and will be understood; *you have enough days* is what to say.

### 3 · Explaining a limit without promising an outcome

**3A  Study the chart.**

| Risky | Safe |
|---|---|
| You can stay ninety days. | The **maximum** is ninety days in any one hundred and eighty. |
| Your visa lets you in. | Your visa lets you **apply** to enter. The officer decides on the day. |
| You'll be fine. | On these dates you have **forty days left**. I cannot say what an officer will do. |
| They never check. | I cannot advise you on that. |

> **Grammar Note** — Say *maximum* and *apply*. Those two words are the difference between
> explaining a rule and making a promise you cannot keep. A visitor who is refused entry after being
> told *you'll be fine* will remember who said it.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Entry rules are among the most sensitive subjects a consular officer handles, because they are
decided entirely by the other country and felt entirely by the person in front of you. Some
countries count a short stay in days and others in months, which is not the same thing in February.
Some reset the count on each entry and some do not. Some require a minimum passport validity beyond
the date of departure, and the number is different from country to country. And the person who makes
the final decision is never the officer at the counter, which is an explanation that sounds like an
excuse in every language and is nonetheless the truth.

**Discuss:** Which entry rule do you explain most often? What do people most often misunderstand?

> In September the visitor came back with the slip from March. It said forty days. He had booked
> three weeks.
>
> The slip was right in March and wrong in September, and it did say *as at 6 March* in the line
> underneath, in the same size as everything else. He had not read that line. He had read the number.
>
> As it happened, he had more days in September than in March, because the March trip had dropped
> out of the window. He traveled, and nothing went wrong. Samira's note on the file says that the
> outcome was luck and the slip was still badly designed, and that *as at* should be printed next to

1. Was the slip good or bad?
2. What is the difference between being right and being read?
3. Where should *as at* be printed?
