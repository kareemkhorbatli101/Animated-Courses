# Foreign Service English Foundation
%%SUB English for Foreign Ministry Staff · Syrian Edition · CEFR A2%%

## How to use this book

This is the first of six books. It is about **the ministry itself**: the building, the desk, the
visitor at the door, the form, the telephone, the file, the diary, the stamp. Before anybody drafts
a note verbale or sits at a negotiating table, somebody has to answer the phone correctly, spell a
name correctly, and say which floor the Treaty Section is on.

**Twelve units**, fourteen pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time. Once
you have done Unit 1 you know where everything is.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | A real document, and one real fact from a real instrument |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a long dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `DIP1-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Every reading text quotes something real, and names it.** A treaty article. A party count from the
depositary's own record. A resolution number and the date it was adopted. An international standard
and the instrument it actually sits in. At this level the sentences are short, and the facts are not
simplified — a figure is either right or it is not in the book.

**Every text also says what the fact does not tell you.** A hundred and thirty states are party to
the Apostille Convention, and one of them cannot use it until February next year. Ninety days is
the ceiling, not the entitlement. The ten-year passport rule is national law, not an international
standard. Six units in this book exist because the short version of a true sentence is the version
that gets an officer into trouble.

**You will practice the four things that actually fill a working day.** Saying a name so the other
person can write it. Saying a number so the other person can write it. Writing down who asked, what
they asked for, and when. And saying *I do not know yet — I will find out and call you back*, which
is the single most professional sentence in this book.

**Nothing here is official.** Every person, office, document and case is invented. Where a unit
needs another country for a role-play, that country is invented too, so that no real government is
given a position it has not taken.

## The people

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates, Damascus. Nine floors, one lift that works, and a
Registry that closes at three.

**Omar Sabbagh** is new. He arrived three weeks ago as a third secretary and he is the person this
book follows. **Samira Barakat** runs the consular front office and has worked here for nineteen
years. **Ahmad Rifai** is on the reception and accreditation desk, which means he is the first
person every visitor meets. **Hana Darwish** keeps the Registry and knows where everything is.
**Nadia Shaheen** is a desk officer in the Europe Department. **Yusuf Mansour** is on the
switchboard. **Layla Bahri** is in the Legal Affairs Department and answers the questions nobody
else can.

Outside the building: **Karin Lindholm** and **Oskar Rehn** at the Embassy of Astrand, **Lucia
Moretti** at the Consulate of Perenne, **Linh Tran** at the Embassy of Marovia, **Ida Pedersen** and
**Marco Caruso**, who both arrive in Unit 12 with the wrong documents.

Every one of them is invented. Astrand, Perenne, Marovia, Valmora, Kesteland and the Sable Isles
are invented countries. Every treaty, resolution, standard and party count quoted in a reading text
is real, and the text says where it comes from and what it does not show.
