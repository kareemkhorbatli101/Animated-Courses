### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A treaty can be completely clear and still be argued about. Read what it says
> first. Then read who may ask a court to enforce it, which is a different question.

%%FIG g10-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving a treaty name and a date, a short table with three rows about the article, a boxed paragraph headed what is argued about, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · induction note 10
> *For new entrants · Consular Affairs · page 1 of 1*
>
> The treaty for consular work is the **Vienna Convention on Consular Relations**, signed at Vienna
> on **24 April 1963**. There are **182 states parties** on the records available to us.
>
> The article you will use is **Article 36**.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | What it is about | communication between a consulate and the nationals of its own state |
> | What the receiving state must do | **on the request** of the person detained, inform the consulate of the sending state — **without delay** |
> | What it gives the consulate | the right to communicate with and visit the person |
>
> Two words matter. **On request** — the person has to ask. **Without delay** — once they ask, the
> authorities must not wait.
>
> **What is argued about.** Courts do not agree on one question: does Article 36 give **rights to
> the person**, or only **obligations between states**? The International Court of Justice has found
> that a state breached the article. Some national courts have found that the person themselves could
> not rely on it in their own case. Both decisions exist.
>
> So in a note, write what the article **requires**. Do not write what a court will do.
>
> *Source: Vienna Convention on Consular Relations, 24 April 1963, Article 36. The number of states
> parties is from the records available to us and is given as at the date those records were
> published.*

<sub>**the sending state** — the country the person belongs to · **the receiving state** — the
country the person is in · **to breach** — to fail to do what a treaty requires</sub>

### 1 · *Some* and *any*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP1-10-06%%

> **Samira:** Do you have any information about where he is?
> **Caller:** No, I don't have any. I have some papers, but they're old.
> **Samira:** That's all right. Is there anybody there with you?
> **Caller:** My mother.
> **Samira:** Good. I need some time — about an hour. I'm going to telephone the consulate and I
> will have some news by six.
> **Caller:** Any news. Even if it's nothing.
> **Samira:** Even if it is nothing. I will telephone at six either way.

*The caller says "any news. Even if it's nothing." What is she really asking for?*

**1B  Underline every *some* and *any* in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **some** in positives | I have **some** papers. I need **some** time. |
| **any** in negatives | I don't have **any** information. |
| **any** in questions | Do you have **any** news? |
| **some** in offers and requests | Would you like **some** water? Could I have **some** help? |
| **anybody / anything / anywhere** | Is there **anybody** with you? |
| **somebody / something** | There is **somebody** at the consulate now. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Some* in positives, *any* in negatives and questions. The exception matters at
> a consular desk: use *some* in an offer or a request, because *would you like any water* sounds
> as if you expect the answer no.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Consular assistance is understood differently by families and by officials, and the gap causes most
of the difficulty. In some countries people expect a consulate to be able to intervene in a court
case, and explaining that it cannot is the hardest conversation in the job. Rules differ too: some
states require notification of a detention whether or not the person asks, by their own law or by a
bilateral agreement, which is more than the Convention requires. Whether a consular officer may be
present at a hearing varies. And families may not know that a person can refuse consular contact —
which happens, and which the officer must respect even when the family is on the telephone every
day.

**Discuss:** What do families expect in your experience? What is the hardest thing to explain?
