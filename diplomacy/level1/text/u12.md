short answer is *since the beginning*. The honest answer takes one more sentence, and the one more
sentence is this unit.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When a history has a complication in it, put the complication in the second
> sentence, not the fifth. A reader who finds it late thinks it was being hidden.

%%FIG g12-f5 | A one-page briefing note: a heading, a line in bold giving the founding date, a short timeline strip with three marked points in 1945, 1958 and 1961, a two-row table separating membership from the seat, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · briefing note 12
> *Information Department · for use at public events · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Syria has been a member of the United Nations since 24 October 1945.** Syria was an **original
> member** and one of the **51 states** that signed the Charter.
>
> There is one complication, and it belongs here rather than at the end. From **1958 to 1961** Syria
> and Egypt were one state, the **United Arab Republic**, and the Organization recorded that state
> as **a single member holding one seat**. On **13 October 1961** Syria **resumed its separate
> membership**.
>
> So two true sentences:
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **The membership** | continuous since 1945. |
> | **The seat** | shared for three years, separate again from 1961. |
>
> **An unbroken membership and a continuous separate seat are not the same thing.** Say the first
> and you are right. Say the second and somebody with the records in front of them will correct you
> in public.
>
> *Source: the United Nations member state record at un.org, and the Charter of the United Nations.
> The record gives dates of admission; it does not explain them, and a date of admission is not a
> history.*

<sub>**an original member** — a state present at the founding · **to resume** — to take up again ·
**a single seat** — one place, held by one state</sub>

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP1-12-06%%

*Omar says "we didn't join". What is he correcting?*

**1B  Underline every past simple verb in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| Regular: + **-ed** | We **signed** the Charter. |
| Irregular | We **were** there. The state **had** one seat. |
| Negative: **didn't** + base | We **didn't join** — we founded it. |
| Question: **did** + base | **Did** your country **sign** in 1945? |
| With a past time | We resumed our membership **in 1961**. |

> **Grammar Note** — In a negative or a question, *did* carries the past and the main verb goes back
> to its base form: *didn't join*, not *didn't joined*. The one exception is *be*: *we weren't*,
> *were you*.

### 2 · *Would like*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **I would like** + noun | **I would like** a copy of the briefing note. |
| **I would like to** + verb | **I would like to** check the date first. |
| **Would you like** …? | **Would you like** me to send it? |
| **I'd like** *(spoken)* | **I'd like** to give you the full answer. |
| softer than *I want* | *I want the file* → **I would like the file, please.** |

> **Grammar Note** — *Would like* is followed by **to** before a verb: *I would like to check*, never
> *I would like check*. And it is not the same as *I like*: *I like coffee* is always true; *I would
> like a coffee* is now.

### 3 · Putting a date on a claim

**3A  Study the chart.**

| Risky | Safe |
|---|---|
| We have always had our own seat. | Our membership is continuous since 1945. From 1958 to 1961 the seat was shared. |
| About fifty states signed. | Fifty-one states signed, on 24 October 1945. |
| I think it was in the sixties. | I would like to check the date before I answer. |

> **Grammar Note** — *I would like to check before I answer* is a complete, professional answer. It

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Every foreign ministry has a history with a complication in it, and every one of them has somebody
who tells the smooth version because the smooth version is shorter. The smooth version survives in a
school hall and dies in a press room. What makes the difference is not patriotism, which both
speakers have, but whether the complication is said by you or found by somebody else. A state that
explains its own history is describing itself. A state that is corrected about its own history is
being described. In many countries the word for the first is *dignity* and the word for the second is
not.

**Discuss:** What is the complication in a history you know well? Who usually tells it?

> *Information Department · for speakers · revised 4 March*

> **Use these three, in this order.**
> 1. Syria has been a member of the United Nations **since 24 October 1945**.
> 2. Syria was an **original member** and one of the **51** states that signed the Charter.
> 3. From **1958 to 1961**, Syria and Egypt formed one state, the **United Arab Republic**, which

> will send it to you today.* Then send it today.
>

> gives dates; it does not give explanations.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put *do not say* on the card. A card that only tells a speaker what is true
> leaves them to invent the phrasing under pressure, and under pressure people reach for the
> smoothest sentence available.
