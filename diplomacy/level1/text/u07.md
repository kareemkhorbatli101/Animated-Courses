> **Reader's Tip** — Two things that look like one thing: **where people sit** and **when people
> speak**. In the General Assembly they are decided in two completely different ways.

%%FIG g07-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a simple plan of a curved hall with numbered rows and the first seat marked, a short table with two rows comparing seating and speaking, a boxed paragraph about the draw, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> *For new entrants · Multilateral Affairs · page 1 of 1*
>
> The General Assembly has **193 member states** and a hall with 193 places.
>
> | | How it is decided |
> |---|---|
> | **Where you sit** | **English alphabetical order** by country name — and the **Secretary-General draws lots** each session to decide which delegation takes the **first seat**. Everybody else follows in order from there. |
> | **When you speak** in the general debate | **Separately**. It is not the seating order, and it is settled by the Secretariat from the requests delegations send in. |

**1B  Underline every *going to* and every *will* in 1A. What is the difference?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **going to** | a plan you already have | I**'m going to** move the briefing to Thursday. |
| **going to** | something you can see coming | Room 204 **is going to** be a problem. |
| **will / 'll** | a decision made now | **I'll** do it. |
| **will / 'll** | an offer or a promise | **I'll** send you a note. · **I'll** be here. |
| **Shall I …?** | offering to do something | **Shall I** telephone the embassy? |
| **Negative** | | I**'m not going to** cancel it. · I **won't** be late. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Going to* is for a plan you had before you opened your mouth. *Will* is for a
> decision you make while you are speaking. Both are correct about the future; they say different
> things about when you decided. In a diary conversation you need both in the same minute.

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **on** + day | **on** Tuesday · **on** the 14th |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Time is not neutral. In some working cultures a meeting that starts at ten starts at ten and the
door closes; in others ten means the beginning of a period in which people arrive, and arriving at
ten exactly can mean waiting alone. How far ahead you may book differs too: in some ministries a
month is normal and a week is rude, and in others anything more than a few days will be moved
anyway. The language of changing an arrangement carries weight: in some places *we will try to find
another time* is a polite cancellation, and in others it is a real promise to find one. And the
weekend falls on different days, so *the following week* can begin on three different mornings.

**Discuss:** In your office, what does *ten o'clock* mean? How far ahead do you book?
