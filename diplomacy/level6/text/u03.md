# Unit 3 · Commended to the Attention of Governments
%%SUB The most cited text in the field binds nobody — and says the state answers for conduct it forbade%%
%%GOALS
Use the language of attribution accurately
State the status of a text that is not binding
Distinguish attribution from responsibility
%%

In December 2001 the General Assembly took note of a set of articles on the responsibility of
states, annexed them to a resolution, and **commended them to the attention of Governments**. They
are not a treaty. Nobody is bound by them as such. They are quoted in almost every serious argument
about state responsibility, by ministries, counsel and tribunals, and one of them says that a state
answers for the conduct of its own organs **even where they exceeded their authority or contravened
instructions**.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When you quote a text that binds nobody, state its status in the same sentence
> as the quotation. If you do not, your reader will assume you have quoted a treaty, and nothing you
> write later will undo that.

%%FIG l03-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a boxed paragraph on status quoting a resolution's operative words, a boxed quotation of two articles with one phrase underlined, a two-row box separating attribution from breach, a paragraph headed why the rule is drafted this way, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · legal note 3
> *Legal Affairs with Dr S. Mallah · attribution of conduct · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The status of the text, first and not last.**
>
> > The **International Law Commission** adopted the **Articles on Responsibility of States for
> > Internationally Wrongful Acts** in **2001**. By resolution **56/83 of 12 December 2001** the
> > General Assembly **took note of** the articles, **annexed them to the resolution**, and
> > **commended them to the attention of Governments without prejudice to the question of their
> > future adoption or other appropriate action**.
> >
> > **They are therefore not a treaty.** No State is a party to them. **They bind nobody as such**,
> > and they are among the most frequently cited texts in international law. A ministry that quotes
> > them without saying what they are has misled its reader; a ministry that declines to quote them
> > has handicapped itself.
>
> **Attribution: the basic rule.**
>
> > The conduct of **any State organ** shall be considered **an act of that State**, whether the
> > organ exercises **legislative, executive, judicial or any other** functions, **whatever position
> > it holds** in the organization of the State, and **whatever its character** as an organ of the
> > central Government or of a **territorial unit**.
>
> **Attribution: the rule that surprises everybody.**
>
> > The conduct of an organ of a State, or of a person or entity empowered to exercise elements of
> > governmental authority, **shall be considered an act of the State** under international law if
> > the organ, person or entity **acts in that capacity**, <u>**even if it exceeds its authority or
> > contravenes instructions**</u>.
>
> **Why the rule is drafted that way.** Not out of severity. Consider the alternative. If
> contravening instructions broke attribution, **any State could escape responsibility by issuing
> instructions and tolerating their breach**, and the injured State would have to prove the content
> of another State's internal orders — which it cannot do and should not have to. **The rule puts the
> risk of an organ's misconduct on the State that organized it**, which is the only party able to
> control it.
>
> **What instructions are still worth.** A great deal, and in four directions: internally, in
> discipline; in showing that the State did not authorize the conduct, which can matter to
> reparation and to the gravity of the breach; in the separate question of whether the person was
> acting **in that capacity** at all; and in good faith, which is not a legal category and is
> noticed.
>
> **Two questions, not one.**
>
> | | The question |
> |---|---|
> | **1 Attribution** | Is the conduct attributable to the State? |
> | **2 Breach** | Does that conduct **constitute a breach of an international obligation** of the State? |
>
> **Both are required.** Attribution alone establishes nothing: there is no responsibility without a
> breach, and an attributable act that breaches no obligation is simply an act of the State.
>
> **What to write.** *The conduct is attributable to the State, the officer having acted in that
> capacity, notwithstanding that it contravened instructions. Whether it constitutes a breach of an
> obligation owed to Astrand is a separate question, addressed below.* **What not to write:** *the
> officer acted without authority, so the State is not responsible.*
>
> *Source: the Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts, adopted by the
> International Law Commission in 2001 and annexed to General Assembly resolution 56/83 of 12
> December 2001, which took note of them and commended them to the attention of Governments without
> prejudice to the question of their future adoption; Articles 4, 5 and 7. The Articles are not a
> treaty and are not binding as such.*

### 1 · Attribution

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP6-03-06%%

> **Suhair:** Your draft says the State is not responsible because the officer acted without
> authority.
> **Jamil:** He was expressly instructed not to.
> **Suhair:** Which is true, and which is not an answer to the question that has been put to us.

> **Jamil:** It feels unjust.
> **Suhair:** It feels unjust for about a week, and then you imagine being on the other side. You are
> the injured State. You are told that the official who did this had been instructed not to. You
> cannot see the instruction, you cannot test it, and you cannot know whether it was issued
> yesterday. **A rule that turned on another State's internal orders would be a rule that could never
> be applied.**
> **Jamil:** So what do I write?
> **Suhair:** Attribution, in the Articles' own words, and then the second gate as a separate
> paragraph: **whether the conduct constitutes a breach of an obligation owed to Astrand**. That is
> where our arguments are, and they are good ones. You have spent three paragraphs on the gate we
> cannot win and none on the gate we can.
> **Jamil:** And the instructions?
> **Suhair:** Go in a third paragraph, as what they are: evidence that the State did not authorize
> the conduct, which bears on reparation and on gravity, and which we should put forward for exactly

**1B  Underline the three attribution structures in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Example | The mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **is attributable to** | the conduct **is attributable to** the State | arguing attribution from authority |
| **acting in that capacity** | the officer was **acting in that capacity** | forgetting the one condition that matters |
| **notwithstanding that** | **notwithstanding that** it contravened instructions | treating instructions as decisive |
| **does not of itself establish responsibility** | attribution alone | collapsing two questions into one |
| **a separate question, addressed below** | the second gate | arguing the wrong gate at length |
| **bears on reparation and on gravity** | what instructions are worth | throwing away a real argument |

> **Grammar Note** — *Notwithstanding that* is the connector this subject was built for. It concedes
> a true fact and denies it the consequence the reader expects, in two words, without argument.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Officers meet the ultra vires rule with a sense of injustice that is worth taking seriously rather
than arguing away. A state is made to answer for an act it forbade, by an official it is about to
dismiss, and the sentence that does it is nine words long. But look at the alternative from the other
chair. You are the ministry whose national was harmed. You are told that the official had been
instructed not to do it. You cannot read the instruction, you cannot date it, you cannot test it, and
you certainly cannot cross-examine the person who signed it. Every state in the world would own a
drawer full of instructions, and nobody would ever be responsible for anything. The rule puts the
risk on the only party able to control the conduct, which is the state that organized the office,
appointed the officer and wrote the order it failed to enforce. Most of the hard rules in this field
have that shape: they look harsh from inside one ministry and obvious from inside two.

**Discuss:** Can you think of another rule in your work that looks unjust from inside and obvious
from outside?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Reply · complaint of the Ministry of Astrand of 3 March
> *Legal Affairs · draft for clearance · 19 March*
>
> **1 The text relied on.** Both Ministries have referred to the **Articles on Responsibility of
> States for Internationally Wrongful Acts**, adopted by the **International Law Commission in
> 2001** and **annexed to General Assembly resolution 56/83 of 12 December 2001**, which **took note
> of them and commended them to the attention of Governments**. **They are not a treaty.** This
> Ministry refers to them, as the Ministry of Astrand does, as the standard statement of the
> subject.
>
> **2 Attribution, which this Ministry does not contest.** The officer concerned was an organ of this
> State and was **acting in that capacity**. His conduct is accordingly **attributable to this
> State**, **notwithstanding that it exceeded his authority and contravened a standing order of 11
> January**.
>
> **3 Breach, which this Ministry does contest.** Attribution **does not of itself establish
> responsibility**. The Ministry does not accept that the conduct constituted a breach of any
> obligation owed to the Ministry of Astrand, for four reasons, which are set out at Annex A: **the
> obligation invoked**, **to whom it is owed**, **its scope**, and **the nationality of the person
> concerned**.
>
> **4 Non-authorization, and conduct since. The conduct was not authorized**, as the standing order
> of 11 January shows. Since 3 March the officer has been **relieved of his duties pending
> proceedings**, the standing order has been **reissued to all posts**, and a copy is **attached**.
> These matters bear on the characterization of the conduct and on any question of reparation.
>
> **5 What this reply does not do.** It does not accept that a breach has occurred. It does not state
> the position of this Ministry on the quantum of any reparation. And it does not characterize the
> conduct of any official of the Ministry of Astrand.
>
> *Annex A: the four arguments on breach. Attachment: the standing order of 11 January, as reissued.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Paragraph 2 is the paragraph junior officers fight hardest against writing and
> the one that makes the rest of the reply credible. A ministry that concedes what it must, early and
> in the right words, is read carefully when it refuses something.
