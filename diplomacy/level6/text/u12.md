# Unit 12 · Three Signatures
%%SUB The last page of six books is one sheet of paper and whose name is at the foot of it%%

Six books have been about status, dates, columns, thresholds, procedures and capacity. They end
where the whole apparatus touches paper: a single sheet by which a state declares that it is bound,
signed by one of **three** people, containing **five** things, and accepted or refused by a
depositary that will not telephone to ask what you meant. This is the last reading text in the
series, and it is a practice note about a signature block.

>
> | | What the instrument must contain |
> |---|---|
> | **1** | the **title, date and place of conclusion** of the treaty concerned |
> | **2** | the **full name and title** of the person signing |
> | **3** | an **unambiguous expression of the intent** of the Government to be bound by the treaty and to **undertake faithfully to observe and implement** its provisions |
> | **4** | the **date and place** where the instrument was issued |
> | **5** | the **signature** of the Head of State, the Head of Government or the Minister for Foreign Affairs — **or** of another person holding **full powers** issued by one of those three |
>
> **The three authorities.**
>
> > The depositary accepts an instrument signed by the **Head of State**, the **Head of Government**
> > or the **Minister for Foreign Affairs**. **A signature by any other authority — including another
> > minister, however senior, and an ambassador — requires full powers**, which must be issued by one
> > of those three and must be deposited with the instrument.
>
> **These are the same three offices as in Unit 1 of this book, and the overlap is not a
> coincidence.** The persons who may bind the State by signing a treaty in virtue of their functions
> are the persons whose signature a depositary will accept on an instrument without inquiry. **The
> rule exists so that a depositary in another city, with no knowledge of our constitution, can verify
> capacity from the face of one page.**
>
> **What a depositary will not accept.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | a **copy**, a facsimile or a scan | the **original instrument** is required |
> | a signature by a person outside the three **without full powers** | the instrument is **refused** |
> | an instrument that does **not identify the treaty** unambiguously | it cannot be recorded |
> | a **reservation or declaration** intended to be made but **not included in the instrument** | it must be in the instrument itself |
>
> **And the consequence of a refusal, which is the part nobody budgets for. The instrument comes

### 3 · Review: the habit of six books

**3A  Study the model.**

> **In virtue of their functions**, the Minister for Foreign Affairs may sign. The instrument,
> **however named**, must contain the five elements. Conduct of our officials in preparing it is

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

It is worth asking why a series about international law ends with a formality. The answer is that the
formality is where all the rest of it becomes real. Every unit in these six books has been about
establishing something that could not be taken on trust: what a figure counts, as at what date, under
what rule, with whose consent, on whose authority, in whose words. A depositary's three-name rule is
the same discipline applied to the last step, and applied by somebody who has never met you. They
cannot know your constitution, your portfolios, your internal clearances or your good faith. They can
know one thing: whether the name under the line is one of three. That is not bureaucracy. It is the
only mechanism by which a hundred and ninety-odd states, with nothing in common, can read each
other's documents and believe them — and it is the same reason the ribbon on a set of full powers has
survived two centuries. The whole system runs on things a stranger can check.

**Discuss:** What in your own work could a stranger check, and what has to be taken on trust? Which
would you rather rely on?

### 🗣 Group ranking

*In fours.* An instrument signed by the wrong minister has been found four weeks before deposit is
due. Rank these, best to worst. You must agree.

a. Put both routes and both timings to the Director today
b. Have the Foreign Minister re-sign, without troubling the Director
c. Prepare full powers and deposit as it stands
d. Deposit it and deal with any refusal when it comes
e. Ask the depositary informally whether it would be accepted

Report your order, and say which one would be worst if the Director had a domestic reason for the
original signature.

### 3 · Write

Write **an instrument of ratification and its seven-box checklist** for a treaty in your own area.

**Recite the treaty by title, place and date, and the signature on behalf of your State. Use the
operative verbs: ratifies, and undertakes faithfully to observe and implement. Put any declaration in
its own paragraph, and say in terms that it is made in the instrument. Give the date and place of
issue. Put one of the three authorities beneath the line, or note the full powers enclosed. Then tick
your own seven boxes.**

**Read your instrument aloud to your partner first.** Formulaic language is hard to check silently and
easy to check aloud: you will hear the missing date. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's instrument as a depositary's officer with four hundred on the
desk. Would you accept it, and if not, which box fails?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Which box did you both nearly leave unticked? Why that
one?
