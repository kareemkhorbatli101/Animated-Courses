# Starting Point · What Capacity Means
%%SUB Four questions about any sentence that leaves the building%%
%%GOALS
Review the structures of Books Foundation to 4
Distinguish capacity from authority and from instructions
Ask the four questions before a sentence goes out
%%

Everything in the first five books was about documents: what they say, when they operate, who is
party to them, how they are decided. This book is about people — which of them can commit a state,
whose conduct it answers for, and what its own words do to it afterwards. The technical word for the
subject is **capacity**, and it is not the same as authority, and neither is the same as having
instructions.

### 1 · What you already have

%%FIG l00-f1 | A single review sheet with six boxes. Each box has a heading — deeming, the perfect passive, exceptives, two readings, apposition and reporting absence — and one short example sentence from a legal adviser's working day.%%

**1A  Say one sentence from each box to your partner.** Then say which box your own drafts use least.

### 2 · Three things that are not the same

%%FIG l00-f2 | Three nested boxes drawn one inside another. The outer box is labeled capacity, the middle one authority, and the inner one instructions, with a short line of explanation beside each and a note at the foot reading all three, or the sentence does not commit anybody.%%

| | What it is | What happens without it |
|---|---|---|
| **Capacity** | the power, in law, to commit the State by this act | the act **does not bind the State** |
| **Authority** | permission, within the ministry, to do it | the act may bind the State and end a career |
| **Instructions** | what you have been told to say | the act binds, and is wrong |

**2A  Listen.** %%AUD DIP6-00-01%%

**2B  Which of the three is hardest to check in a hurry?** *Talk with a partner, out loud.*

### 3 · Four sentences a legal adviser writes every week

%%FIG l00-f3 | A card taped inside a folder, printed with four sentences in bold, each with one line of smaller type under it explaining when to use it.%%

> **1  The signature of a person without full powers does not bind the State.**
> *When somebody has signed something at a conference.*
>
> **2  The conduct of an organ is attributable to the State even if it exceeded its authority.**
> *When a ministry wants to say that somebody acted without permission.*
>
> **3  The character of a statement is determined by the effect it purports to produce, and not by
> its name.**
> *When a document is called something convenient.*
>
> **4  The claim is the State's, and the State is not obliged to bring it.**
> *The hardest of the four to say out loud, and the first thing a consular officer must
> understand.*

**3A  Listen and repeat.** %%AUD DIP6-00-02%%

**3B  Cover the explanations.** Your partner reads a situation; you say which sentence fits. Take
turns.

### 4 · The four questions before a sentence leaves the building

%%FIG l00-f4 | Four numbered boxes in a column, each with a question in bold and two short lines under it, and an arrow at the foot pointing to a fifth box reading only now may it go out.%%

> **1  Who is saying it, and in what capacity?**
> A person, an organ, or the State. The three are different and only one of them is on the paper.
>
> **2  What status has the text we are relying on?**
> Binding, commended, noted, or published as a guide to practice.
>
> **3  What will this sentence be evidence of, later?**
> Your own conduct is read back to you as evidence of what a treaty means.
>
> **4  Whose is the right, and whose is the discretion?**
> Rarely the same person, and almost never the person asking you.

**4A  Listen.** %%AUD DIP6-00-03%%

**4B  Think about it.** *Talk with a partner.*

1. Which of the four questions does a hurried draft skip?
2. Which one would you add to the four?
3. Which one did nobody teach you?
