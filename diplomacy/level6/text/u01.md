# Unit 1 · Who May Bind the State
%%SUB Full powers, three people who need none, and the act that is without legal effect%%
%%GOALS
Distinguish capacity from authority and from instructions
Use *in virtue of*, *for the purpose of* and *is considered as representing*
Say what a signature without capacity does and does not do
%%

Three people in a state can perform any act relating to the conclusion of a treaty without producing
a document to prove it. Everybody else needs full powers, and the powers are specific to the act.
An act performed by somebody who had neither is **without legal effect** — unless the state afterwards
confirms it, which is a choice and not a formality. This unit is about the narrowest and most
consequential question in the field: whose signature commits a country.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When an article lists categories of person, read the purpose clause attached to
> each one. The list is not the rule; the list paired with its purposes is the rule, and officers
> remember the list.

%%FIG l01-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the instrument with its dates and status, a boxed quotation of one article in two numbered paragraphs, a three-row table of the functional categories with the purpose attached to each, a boxed paragraph on the act without legal effect, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · legal note 1
> *Legal Affairs · capacity to conclude treaties · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The instrument.** The **Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties**, done at Vienna on **23 May
> 1969**, entered into force on **27 January 1980**. It has about **116 parties**, on the
> depositary's record, which carries the date of that page. **Many of its provisions are widely
> regarded as reflecting customary international law**; the extent to which any particular article
> does so is a separate question, and Unit 11 of this book deals with how to say so.
>
> **The rule, in the Convention's own words.**
>
> > **1.** A person is considered as representing a State for the purpose of adopting or
> > authenticating the text of a treaty or for the purpose of expressing the consent of the State to
> > be bound by a treaty if **(a)** he **produces appropriate full powers**; or **(b)** it appears
> > from the **practice** of the States concerned or from **other circumstances** that they intended
> > that person to represent the State for such purposes and **to dispense with full powers**.
> >
> > **2. In virtue of their functions and without having to produce full powers**, the following are
> > considered as representing their State …
>
> **The three functional categories, each with its own purpose.**
>
> | Who | For what purpose |
> |---|---|
> | **Heads of State, Heads of Government and Ministers for Foreign Affairs** | **all acts** relating to the conclusion of a treaty |
> | **Heads of diplomatic missions** | **adopting the text** of a treaty **between the accrediting State and the State to which they are accredited** |
> | **Representatives accredited to an international conference or organization** | **adopting the text** of a treaty **in that conference or organization** |
>
> **Read the second and third rows again.** A head of mission may adopt a bilateral text. **A head of
> mission may not, on that basis, express the State's consent to be bound**, and may not adopt a
> multilateral text at a conference. A representative accredited to a conference may adopt the text
> **in that conference** and nothing else. **The categories are narrow and the purposes are narrower.**
>
> **The act performed without capacity.**
>
> > An act relating to the conclusion of a treaty performed by a person who **cannot be considered**
> > as authorized to represent a State for that purpose **is without legal effect unless afterwards
> > confirmed by that State**.
>
> **What that means for us.** Three things. The act does not bind us. **It does not bind the other
> State either, which is the half officers forget.** And confirmation is available, which means the
> question *was the signature valid?* is in practice the question *do we wish to be bound?* — a
> question for this ministry and not for the officer who signed.
>
> **A fourth possibility, worth knowing.** A treaty may provide, or the negotiating States may agree,
> that **signature is subject to confirmation**. That is a different thing from confirming an
> unauthorized act: it is a signature that was authorized and that does not yet commit.
>
> **What to write.** *The signature of a person without appropriate full powers does not bind the
> State, and the act is without legal effect unless the State afterwards confirms it.* **What not to
> write:** *the signature was invalid*, which sounds like a defect and conceals a decision we have
> not yet taken.
>
> *Source: the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties of 23 May 1969, in force 27 January 1980,
> Articles 7, 8 and 12, and the depositary's published figure of about 116 parties, which carries the
> date of that page. The customary status of individual provisions is not stated here.*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP6-01-06%%

> **Layla:** Your note says the ambassador was not authorized to sign.
> **Jamil:** The ministry had not cleared it.
> **Layla:** Then you have described an internal failure and left out the question the other State
> will ask. Two different sentences are available and they have different consequences. **The

> it in the Convention's own terms: a head of mission is considered as representing the State, **in

> was a multilateral text at a conference to which the ambassador was not accredited. **No capacity.

> **Jamil:** May I say the signature was invalid?
> **Layla:** No, and not because it is impolite. *Invalid* says the thing failed. The Convention says
> the act **is without legal effect unless afterwards confirmed**, which means it is not a failure

> door stays visible.

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Example | The mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **in virtue of their functions** | capacity that needs no document | treating every signature as needing powers |
| **for the purpose of** | the act covered | treating one capacity as covering all acts |
| **produces appropriate full powers** | the Convention's own phrase | *has permission*, which is authority |
| **is without legal effect unless afterwards confirmed** | the consequence and the door | *invalid*, which closes a door we hold |
| **had no authority from this Ministry** | an internal fact | saying it to another State |
| **the act does not bind either State** | the half that is forgotten | thinking only about our own position |

> **Grammar Note** — *Capacity* is about the law, *authority* is about your ministry, and
> *instructions* are about the file. The three fail in different directions and only the first is
> ever an answer to the other State.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Full powers look like the most archaic survival in diplomatic practice — a sealed paper with a
ribbon, authorizing a named person to sign a named document — and they are the most quietly modern
thing in this book. What the system does is make capacity checkable by a stranger in thirty seconds,
without anybody having to telephone a capital or trust an assurance. It converts a question about
internal authority, which no foreign ministry can verify, into a question about a document, which
anybody can read. That is why the three functional exceptions are so narrow: a head of state, a head
of government and a foreign minister are recognizable offices, and everything below them is a matter
of internal arrangement that the Convention sensibly declines to inquire into. Officers who find the
ribbon faintly ridiculous usually come round the first time somebody at a desk asks them to sign a
page they have no powers for, and discovers that the ribbon is the only thing standing between a
ministry and a decision it was not ready to take.

**Discuss:** Is a document better than an assurance? What does each one cost the person relying on
it?

### 1 · Read a model

**1A  Look at the two notes. Answer with a partner.**

The second separates capacity from authority, quotes the consequence provision, says the act binds neither State, and reserves the question of confirmation to the ministry.%%

2. Which one separates capacity from authority?
3. Which one reserves a decision to the ministry?

**1B  Listen. What did the other ministry ask after receiving the first note?** %%AUD DIP6-01-14%%

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Legal note · signature of the Protocol at the Geneva Conference
> *Legal Affairs · for the Director and the Treaty Section · 16 April*
>
> **1 What happened.** On **14 April** the Head of our Delegation signed, at the Conference
> secretariat's request, a page headed *definitive signature* in respect of the Protocol. **Our
> delegate's full powers of 3 April cover the adoption of the text and are silent as to the
> expression of consent to be bound.**
>
> **2 Capacity, which is the question the other States will ask.** Under **Article 7** of the Vienna
> Convention on the Law of Treaties, a person is considered as representing a State for the purpose
> of expressing consent to be bound if he **produces appropriate full powers** or if the States
> concerned intended **to dispense with** them. **Neither applies here.** The Head of Delegation was
> accredited to the Conference and is therefore considered as representing the State **for the
> purpose of adopting the text in that Conference** — and for no other purpose.
>
> **3 The consequence, in the Convention's words. The act is without legal effect unless afterwards
> confirmed by this State.** It **binds neither State**. **It is not described as invalid** in any
> communication from this Ministry.
>
> **4 Authority, which is an internal question.** Our delegate had no instruction to sign that page.
> **This paragraph is for internal use only** and does not appear in any note to another Ministry or
> to the Conference secretariat.
>
> **5 The decision, and where it belongs. Whether to confirm the act is a decision for this
> Ministry**, to be taken on the merits of the Protocol and not under the pressure of a signature
> already given. We recommend that no confirmation be given before the Treaty Section has reported
> on the final text, and that the Conference secretariat be informed in the terms at paragraph 3.
>
> *Attached: the full powers of 3 April; the page as signed; the relevant articles.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Paragraph 4 is the one with a boundary drawn round it. In this field you will
> often know two true things, one of which belongs only inside the building, and a note that marks
> which is which is a note a director can forward without reading twice.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. The powers covered (adoption / consent to be bound).
2. The act is (invalid / without legal effect unless confirmed).
3. It binds (us only / neither State).
4. Paragraph 4 is for (external / internal) use.

**1E  Listen to a second note. Circle the details.** %%AUD DIP6-01-15%%

1. It (separates / merges) capacity and authority.
2. It (uses / avoids) the word *invalid*.
3. It (reserves / takes) the decision on confirmation.

### 3 · Write
