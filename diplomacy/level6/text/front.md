# Foreign Service English 5
%%SUB English for Foreign Ministry Staff · Syrian Edition · CEFR B2+ → C1%%

## How to use this book

This is the last of six books. Book Foundation was the building, Book 1 the consular section, Book 2
the desk officer's chair, Book 3 protocol and the mission, and Book 4 the conference room. This one
is **representing the state**: who may bind it, whose conduct it answers for, what its own words do
to it afterwards, and what happens to a sentence that goes out under somebody else's name.

**Twelve units**, sixteen pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | A real instrument or published text, with its status, its dates and its caveats |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures of legal and official register, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a long dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `DIP6-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**One subject runs through all twelve units: who speaks for the state, and what their words do.**
Not policy, and not procedure — capacity. Which three people can sign an instrument that binds a
country of twenty million. Whose conduct the state answers for, including conduct it forbade. When a
public statement creates an obligation nobody intended to create. What a document is, whatever it is
called. And whose claim it is when a national of your state is harmed abroad.

**Twelve texts, and the status of each one stated plainly.** Four of the twelve are not binding on
anybody and are among the most cited texts in the field — texts *commended to the attention of
Governments*, or *noted*, or published as a guide to practice. Three are articles of conventions this
series has already used for other purposes, read here for a different rule. One is a convention with
about twenty-three parties whose rules are argued to be customary anyway. And one is a depositary's
published practice note about which three signatures it will accept.

**The grammar is the grammar of capacity and consequence.** Authority and capacity; definition and
characterization; attribution of conduct; manifested intention and restrictive interpretation; rights
held at discretion; express terms and what may not be implied; conditions precedent; immediacy;
evidential weight; hedged statements about custom; and, at the end, the instrument of ratification
itself.

**Four sentences you will write until they are automatic.** *The signature of a person without full
powers does not bind the State.* *The conduct of an organ is attributable to the State even if it
exceeded its authority.* *The character of a statement is determined by the effect it purports to
produce, and not by its name.* And *the claim is the State's, and the State is not obliged to bring
it* — the sentence that takes the longest to say to a family and is the first thing a consular
officer must understand.

## The people

The same ministry, at the end of a decade. **Omar Sabbagh** is now a section head and marks other
people's drafts in pencil, which he notices with some discomfort. **Nadia Shaheen** is a director.
**Layla Bahri** is the ministry's senior legal adviser and the person everybody telephones at twenty
to six. **Hala Mounzer** has retired from Protocol and appears once, by telephone, about a seating
plan. **Fadi Antoun** runs the Treaty Section. **Samira Barakat**, **Hana Darwish**, **Ahmad Rifai**,
**Yusuf Mansour** and **Rania Haddad** are where they were, mostly.

New in this book: **Jamil Sarraj**, two months into the service, who asks in Unit 1 the question this
whole book answers, and **Dr Suhair Mallah**, the ministry's consultant on state responsibility, who
has never worked anywhere else and has read everything.

Outside the building: **Karin Lindholm** and **Oskar Rehn** of Astrand, **Lucia Moretti** of Perenne,
**Linh Tran** of Marovia, **Ida Pedersen**, and **Marco Caruso**, who in Unit 10 quotes the ministry
back to itself.

Every one of them is invented. Astrand, Perenne, Marovia, Valmora, Kesteland and the Sable Isles are
invented countries, and they are invented on purpose: in a role-play about capacity, attribution or
a claim, nobody should have to put a real government's name to a position it has never taken.

## Nothing here is official

This is an independent teaching draft. It is not an official publication of any ministry and it
carries no official status. Every person, office, instrument of ratification, statement, claim and
telephone call in it was written for this book. The instruments and published texts quoted in the
reading texts are real, and each text gives the instrument, its date, **its status**, and what its
publisher says it does and does not do — because in this field a rule without its status is not
information.
