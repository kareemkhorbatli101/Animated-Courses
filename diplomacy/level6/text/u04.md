# Unit 4 · A Statement That Binds
%%SUB A public word, clearly meant, by somebody competent — and it cannot be taken back at will%%
%%GOALS
Use the language of manifested intention
Apply restrictive interpretation to a doubtful commitment
Say who can commit the state by a public statement
%%

A state can bind itself without a treaty, without a signature and without anybody on the other side
agreeing to anything. A declaration **publicly made and manifesting the will to be bound** may create
legal obligations; other states may rely on it; and once it has created an obligation it **cannot be
revoked arbitrarily**. Three conditions keep this from swallowing every speech ever made: the terms
must be clear and specific, doubt is resolved restrictively, and the person must have been competent
to make it.

> **Reader's Tip** — Guiding principles are drafted as a sequence of conditions. Read them as a
> checklist with the brakes marked, because the first principle on its own always sounds alarming.

%%FIG l04-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a boxed paragraph on status, a boxed quotation of a first principle, a three-row box of brakes with the principle number beside each, a boxed quotation on revocation, a paragraph headed what this means for our press office, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · legal note 4
> *Legal Affairs with the Press Office · unilateral declarations · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The status of the text, first.**
>
> > The **International Law Commission** adopted in **2006** the **Guiding Principles applicable to
> > unilateral declarations of States capable of creating legal obligations**, with commentaries. The
> > General Assembly **took note of them** and **commended them to the attention of Governments**.
> > **They are not a treaty.** They are the standard statement of a rule that exists independently of
> > them, which is why they are worth reading and worth citing with their status attached.
>
> **The gate.**
>
> > Declarations **publicly made** and **manifesting the will to be bound** may have the effect of
> > creating legal obligations. When the conditions for this are met, the **binding character** of
> > such declarations is based on **good faith**; States concerned may then **take them into
> > consideration and rely on them**; such States are **entitled to require** that such obligations
> > be respected.
>
> **Read what that does not require.** No treaty. No signature. **No acceptance by anybody.** A
> declaration of this kind is binding because the State said it meant to be bound and others were
> entitled to proceed on that basis.
>
> **The three brakes.**
>
> | | What it requires |
> |---|---|
> | **Clear and specific terms** | a declaration entails obligations **only if** it is stated in **clear and specific terms** |
> | **Restrictive interpretation** | in case of **doubt as to the scope** of the obligations, they **must be interpreted in a restrictive manner** |
> | **Competence** | a declaration binds the State **only if made by an authority vested with the power to do so** — **in virtue of their functions**, Heads of State, Heads of Government and Ministers for Foreign Affairs; others only **in areas falling within their competence** |
>
> **And what is taken into account.** In determining the legal effect of such a declaration, account
> must be taken of **its content**, of **all the factual circumstances** in which it was made, and of
> **the reactions to which it gave rise**. **The reactions of other States are part of the test**,
> which is why a statement that nobody acted on is in a weaker position than one that was relied on
> the following week.
>
> **Revocation.**
>
> > A unilateral declaration that has created legal obligations for the State **cannot be revoked
> > arbitrarily**. In assessing whether a revocation would be arbitrary, consideration should be
> > given to **the terms of the declaration**, **the extent to which those to whom the obligations
> > are owed have relied on them**, and **the extent to which there has been a fundamental change of
> > circumstances**.
>
> **What this means for our press office.** Three working rules. **Avoid clear and specific terms
> unless a commitment is intended** — *will consider* is not *will*, and *intends to* is not
> *undertakes to*. **Know who is speaking**: the three offices commit the State generally, and
> everybody else only within their own area of competence. And **read the reactions**: if another
> ministry has written to thank us for an undertaking, we have an undertaking, whatever we thought we
> were saying.
>
> **What to write.** *The statement of 9 March was publicly made. Whether it manifests the will to be
> bound turns on its terms, which are set out below; any doubt as to scope falls to be interpreted
> restrictively.* **What not to write:** *the statement was political and has no legal effect*, which
> is a conclusion dressed as a description.
>
> *Source: the Guiding Principles applicable to unilateral declarations of States capable of creating
> legal obligations, adopted by the International Law Commission in 2006 with commentaries, of which
> the General Assembly took note and which it commended to the attention of Governments; Principles 1,
> 3, 4, 7 and 10. The Guiding Principles are not a treaty.*

### 1 · Manifested intention

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP6-04-06%%

> **Layla:** The press office wants the Minister to say *we will complete the review by the end of
> the year*.
> **Jamil:** It is only a review.
> **Layla:** It is a declaration publicly made, in clear and specific terms, by a person who in
> virtue of their functions is competent to commit the State. Every element of the gate is satisfied
> by that sentence, and the only question left is whether it **manifests the will to be bound**,
> which a plain future with a date usually does.
> **Jamil:** And if we want the sentence anyway?
> **Layla:** Then we are making a commitment deliberately, which is a perfectly respectable thing to
> do, and the note says so, and the Minister knows it before the podium. What I object to is making
> one by accident.
> **Jamil:** Could we soften it?
> **Layla:** We could, and the vocabulary exists for exactly this purpose. *We will complete* is a
> commitment. *We intend to complete* manifests an intention and not a will to be bound. *We will
> consider* commits us to considering, which is almost nothing, and officers should know that it is
> not quite nothing. **Each of those is a different sentence with a different legal weight, and the
> drafting is the whole of the choice.**
> **Jamil:** And if there is doubt?
> **Layla:** Then **the obligation must be interpreted in a restrictive manner** — and that is a
> brake, not an escape. It narrows an obligation; it does not remove one.

*What distinction does Layla draw between the three drafts?*

**1B  Underline the four intention expressions in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Weight |
|---|---|
| **we undertake to** | a commitment, expressly |
| **we will** + date | a commitment, in ordinary language |
| **we intend to** | an intention, which is not a will to be bound |
| **we will consider** | a commitment to consider, which is not nothing |
| **we are examining the possibility of** | the weakest form that is still honest |
| **it is our policy to** | a statement of policy, changeable by announcement |

> **Grammar Note** — This scale is the most practical thing in the book. Six forms, six weights, and
> the difference between the first and the last is the difference between an obligation and a
> sentence. Learn it, and never let a press office choose between them without you.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Nothing in this book surprises officers more than the idea that a state can bind itself by speaking.
The instinct is that obligations come from negotiation — two parties, a text, a signature — and a
sentence at a podium is a different kind of thing. But consider what the alternative would do to
diplomacy. If a public undertaking by a foreign minister could be disavowed as mere politics, no
state could ever rely on another's word without extracting a treaty for it, and the ordinary currency
of international relations — the assurance, the undertaking, the public commitment — would be worth
nothing. The rule exists so that states can be taken at their word, and it is founded, in terms, on
good faith. That is why the brakes are drafted so tightly: a system that lets states commit by
speaking must also protect them from committing by accident, which is what clear and specific terms,
restrictive interpretation and competence are for. The practical upshot is unglamorous. Read the
verbs. Know who is speaking. And answer a note that calls your statement an undertaking.

**Discuss:** Would you rather live in a system where public words bind, or one where only treaties
do? What does each cost?

### 🗣 Group ranking

*In fours.* The Minister's sentence has been welcomed as an undertaking and the review will not be
finished. Rank these, best to worst. You must agree.

a. Publish what is complete, on time, with a reasoned note on the remainder
b. Publish a short interim document on time and say so
c. Write now, stating reasons, and propose a revised date
d. Say nothing until January
e. Say the statement was never an undertaking

Report your order, and say which one a ministry would be forgiven for.

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Clearance note · proposed sentence for the press conference of 9 March
> *Legal Affairs · for the Press Office and the Minister's Office · 8 March*
>
> **1 The sentence proposed.** *We will complete the review and publish our findings before the end
> of the year.*
>
> **2 What it would do. As drafted, this sentence would commit the State.** It would be **publicly
> made**; it is in **clear and specific terms**, with an object and a date; and the Minister for
> Foreign Affairs is, **in virtue of their functions**, competent to commit the State.
>
> **3 The commitment, stated exactly. To complete the review and to publish findings before 31
> December.** It would **not** be a commitment as to the content of the findings, their length, or
> any action following them. **In case of doubt as to scope, such an obligation falls to be
> interpreted in a restrictive manner** — but the two obligations above are not doubtful.
>
> **4 Three alternative drafts, at different weights.**
>
> | Draft | What it commits us to |
> |---|---|
> | *We will complete the review and publish our findings before the end of the year.* | **both acts, by a date** |
> | *We intend to complete the review and publish our findings this year.* | **nothing**: an intention, not a will to be bound |
> | *We will consider how best to publish the findings of the review.* | **to consider**, which is not nothing |
>
> **5 What this note records. If the first draft is used, the Ministry will have given an
> undertaking, deliberately**, and this note is the record that it was given with advice. **The
> Minister's Office is asked to confirm which draft is to be used.**
>
> **6 One further step, whichever draft is used.** Reactions form part of the legal effect of a
> public statement. **Any note from another Ministry characterizing the statement as an undertaking
> is to be referred to this Department within three working days**, so that any necessary
> clarification is made at once and not after seven weeks.
>
> *The Guiding Principles referred to were adopted by the International Law Commission in 2006 and
> commended to the attention of Governments; they are not a treaty.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Paragraph 6 is the paragraph that would have saved the ministry in the
> conversation you have just read. A clearance note that only clears words is half a note; the other
> half is what to do about what comes back.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. As drafted, the sentence (would / would not) commit the State.
2. The commitment (would / would not) cover the content of the findings.
3. *We intend to* commits us to (an intention / both acts).
4. Reactions (form / do not form) part of the legal effect.
