# Unit 11 · The Clean Slate
%%SUB Twenty-three parties, two opposite presumptions, and how to write about custom honestly%%
%%GOALS
Hedge a statement about customary law accurately
Distinguish a convention's rule from the rule in force
Say what your ministry does not state a view on
%%

A convention of 1978 sets out what happens to treaties when states succeed one another. It took
eighteen years to enter into force and has about twenty-three parties. Its rules are nonetheless the
starting point of every discussion of the subject, because parts of them are widely regarded as
reflecting customary law, parts are contested, and state practice has varied a great deal. This unit
is about the sentences a ministry uses when the law is genuinely unsettled — and about why those
sentences are a strength and not an evasion.

> **Reader's Tip** — A convention with twenty-three parties is not a weak text; it is a text whose
> authority has to be established sentence by sentence rather than claimed in a heading. Read it with
> two pencils: one for what it provides, one for what is said about its customary status.

%%FIG l11-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the instrument with its dates and parties, two boxed quotations far apart in the text with their presumptions marked, a two-column box comparing the situations, a boxed paragraph headed what may and may not be said about custom, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · legal note 11
> *Legal Affairs with Dr S. Mallah · succession in respect of treaties · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The instrument, and its reach.** The **Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of
> Treaties**, done at Vienna on **23 August 1978**, entered into force on **6 November 1996** —
> **eighteen years** later. It has about **twenty-three parties** on the depositary's record, which
> carries the date of that page. **In any particular case the first question is whether the States
> concerned are parties to it. Usually they are not.**
>
> **The first presumption: the clean slate, for a newly independent State.**
>
> > A **newly independent State** — defined as a successor State the territory of which, immediately
> > before the date of succession, was a **dependent territory** for the international relations of
> > which the predecessor State was responsible — **is not bound to maintain in force, or to become a
> > party to, any treaty by reason only of the fact that at the date of succession the treaty was in
> > force in respect of that territory.**
> >
> > Such a State **may**, by a **notification of succession**, **establish its status as a party** to
> > a multilateral treaty which at the date of succession was in force in respect of the territory.
>
> **The second presumption: continuity, where a State separates.**
>
> > Where a part or parts of a State separate to form one or more States, **any treaty in force at
> > the date of the succession in respect of the entire territory of the predecessor State continues
> > in force in respect of each successor State so formed** — subject to the exceptions the
> > Convention itself provides.
>
> **Two situations, two opposite starting points.**
>
> | | The presumption | What the successor State does |
> |---|---|---|
> | **a newly independent State** | **clean slate**: not bound by reason only of the treaty's prior application | **may** notify succession, treaty by treaty |
> | **separation of part of a State** | **continuity**: treaties continue in respect of each successor | takes the treaties as it finds them, subject to the exceptions |
>
> **What may and may not be said about custom.**
>
> > **It is widely accepted** that the clean slate rule for newly independent States reflects a rule
> > of customary international law. **The extent to which the Convention's presumption of continuity
> > in cases of separation reflects customary international law is unsettled**, and **State practice
> > has varied considerably**, including in cases where the States concerned reached their own
> > agreements. **This Ministry states no view on the position of any particular State**, and
> > nothing in this note is to be cited as doing so.
>
> **Why the hedging is the point, and not a failure of nerve.** A ministry has three honest positions
> available on any unsettled question: that something is generally accepted, that something is
> contested, and that we state no view. **Each of the three is useful, and the third is the most
> useful of all**, because the alternative is to be quoted as having taken a side in somebody else's
> dispute. **A note that distinguishes the three cannot be used against us; a note that asserts a
> single clean answer will be.**
>
> **What to write.** *The Convention provides [X]. The States concerned are not parties to it. It is
> widely accepted that [the clean slate rule] reflects customary international law; the extent to
> which [the continuity presumption] does so is unsettled and practice has varied. This Ministry
> states no view on the position of any particular State.* **What not to write:** *under
> international law, the treaties continue in force*, which asserts the contested half as though it
> were the accepted half.
>
> *Source: the Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of Treaties, done at Vienna on 23
> August 1978, in force 6 November 1996, Articles 2, 16, 17 and 34; and the depositary's published
> figure of about twenty-three parties, which carries the date of that page. The customary status of
> individual provisions is a matter of debate and is not resolved in this note.*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Example | The mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **the Convention provides** | what the text says | *international law provides* |
| **the States concerned are not parties to it** | reach, stated early | applying a text to non-parties |
| **it is widely accepted that** | the accepted half | treating everything as equally accepted |
| **the extent to which … is unsettled** | the contested half | asserting the contested half |
| **State practice has varied** | the factual observation | an argument about theory |
| **this Ministry states no view on** | the reservation | being quoted in somebody else's dispute |

> **Grammar Note** — Each of these forms is a different claim about the state of the law, and a note
> that uses all of them in the right places is more authoritative than one that asserts a single
> clean answer. Confidence in this field is shown by precision, not by volume.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Officers trained to be useful find *the position is unsettled* an uncomfortable sentence, and the
discomfort is worth examining. In most of a ministry's work, uncertainty is a problem to be removed:
you find the provision, you check the status page, you give the answer. In this corner of the subject
the uncertainty is the answer, and it is a real finding rather than a failure of research. A
convention drafted over years by able people attracted twenty-three parties, which tells you
something; the commentaries disagree, which tells you something; and states in this situation have
very often sat down and agreed matters between themselves, which tells you most of all. A ministry
whose note reflects all three is more valuable than one that produces a confident sentence, because
the confident sentence will be attributed to it for twenty years in disputes it has no interest in.
The skill being taught here is not caution. It is accuracy about how much is known, which is the last
thing this series has to teach and the hardest to practice under pressure.

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Legal note · treaty position of a successor State
> *Legal Affairs · for the Director · 7 May*
>
> **1 What the Convention provides.** The **Vienna Convention on Succession of States in respect of
> Treaties** of **23 August 1978**, in force **6 November 1996**, provides for **continuity** where a
> part of a State separates to form a new State, and for a **clean slate** in the case of a **newly
> independent State** as defined in that Convention.
>
> **2 Its reach. The Convention has about twenty-three parties**, on the depositary's record. **The
> States concerned in this case are not among them**, and the Convention therefore does not apply as
> a treaty to the question asked.
>
> **3 The state of the law. It is widely accepted** that the clean slate rule for newly independent
> States reflects a rule of customary international law. **The extent to which the presumption of
> continuity in cases of separation reflects customary international law is unsettled**, and **State
> practice has varied considerably**, including in cases where the States concerned have reached
> their own agreements.
>
> **4 This Ministry's position. This Ministry states no view on the treaty position of any
> particular State.** **Nothing in this note is to be cited as doing so.** The Ministry's advice to
> any party asking is that the position depends in the first place on **any agreement between the
> States concerned**, which should be sought before any question of custom is reached.
>
> **5 Internal only — not to be transmitted outside this Ministry.** Were this Ministry obliged to
> take a position, the argument we would advance is set out at **Annex A**, together with the two
> authorities against it and an assessment of their weight. **We are not obliged to take a position
> today, and in the Department's view should not.** The reasons are at Annex A, paragraph 6.
>
> *Figures for parties carry the date of the depositary's page. The customary status of individual
> provisions is a matter of debate which this note does not resolve.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Paragraph 5 is why a note like this is worth writing. Its existence lets
> paragraphs 1 to 4 be as cautious as the law requires, because the thinking is not lost — it is
> recorded where it belongs, with the arguments against it, for the day somebody has to decide.
