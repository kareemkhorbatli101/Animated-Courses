# Unit 6 · The Claim Belongs to the State
%%SUB A right the state holds, exercises at its discretion — and should, not shall%%
%%GOALS
Distinguish a right held at discretion from an obligation
Use *should* and *shall* with precision
Explain to a family whose claim it is
%%

When a national of your state is injured abroad by another state's wrongful act, the claim that
arises is not the injured person's. It is **the state's own right**, exercised at the state's
discretion, through diplomatic action. The person may be the reason for the claim and is not its
owner. A set of articles drafted by the International Law Commission recommends that a state
**should** give due consideration to exercising the right, **should** take the injured person's views
into account, and **should** pass on what it recovers. *Should*, in all three cases, and the word is
the subject of this unit.

> **A:** The family want us to bring their claim. It is their claim.
> **B:** It is not, and this is the hardest sentence in consular work. The claim is the State's own.
> Diplomatic protection is a right of **this State**, exercised by it, in its own name, at its own

> **A:** That cannot be right. The injury was to their son.
> **B:** The injury was to their son and the right is ours. That is not a technicality invented to

> States, so the law gives the State of nationality a right of its own, arising out of the injury to
> its national.

> subject to reasonable deductions.

> **B:** *Should*, three times, and the difference between those two words is what you will be

> **Reader's Tip** — In any text of this kind, mark every *shall* and every *should* on the first
> reading and count them. The proportion tells you what kind of text you are holding.

%%FIG l06-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a boxed paragraph on status, a boxed quotation defining diplomatic protection, a short box headed whose right it is, a two-row box of preconditions, a boxed quotation of a recommended practice with the word should underlined three times, a paragraph headed what we say to a family, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · legal note 6
> *Legal Affairs with the Consular Department · diplomatic protection · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The status of the text.**
>
> > The **International Law Commission** adopted the **Articles on Diplomatic Protection** in
> > **2006**. The General Assembly **took note of them** and **commended them to the attention of
> > Governments**, deferring the question of a convention. **They are not a treaty.** They state a
> > long-established rule and a modern recommended practice, and the distinction between those two
> > things is the whole of this note.
>
> **What diplomatic protection is.**
>
> > The **invocation by a State**, through diplomatic action or other means of peaceful settlement,
> > of the **responsibility of another State** for an injury caused by an internationally wrongful
> > act of that State **to a natural or legal person that is a national** of the former State, with
> > a view to the implementation of such responsibility.
>
> **Whose right it is.**
>
> > **A State has the right to exercise diplomatic protection.** The State entitled to exercise it is
> > **the State of nationality**. **The right is the State's own.** The injured person is the
> > occasion of the claim and not its owner, and cannot require the State to bring it.
>
> **Two preconditions.**
>
> | | What it requires |
> |---|---|
> | **Nationality of claims** | the injured person must be **a national of the claiming State**, established from documents |
> | **Exhaustion of local remedies** | the injured person must, **subject to the recognized exceptions**, have **exhausted all local remedies** in the responsible State |
>
> **The recommended practice, and its verbs.**
>
> > A State entitled to exercise diplomatic protection **should**:
> > **(a)** give **due consideration** to the possibility of exercising diplomatic protection,
> > **especially when a significant injury has occurred**;
> > **(b)** take into account, **wherever feasible**, the **views of injured persons** with regard to
> > resort to diplomatic protection and to the reparation to be sought; and
> > **(c)** **transfer to the injured person any compensation obtained** for the injury from the
> > responsible State, **subject to any reasonable deductions**.
>
> **Three *shoulds*, and not one *shall*.** The article is headed **recommended practice**, and the
> drafters chose the word deliberately. **A State that gives no consideration at all, consults
> nobody and keeps what it recovers has not breached an obligation** — and has done something this
> Ministry would not defend, which is a different kind of statement and belongs in a different part
> of the note.
>
> **What we say to a family, and what we do not.** We say that the claim is the State's and that the
> decision is the Ministry's. We say that their views will be taken into account and that we will
> ask for them in writing. We say that local remedies must ordinarily be exhausted first and we
> explain what that means in their case, with the stages and an estimate of time. **We do not say
> *there is nothing we can do*, which is untrue, and we do not say *we will bring your claim*, which
> is not ours to promise.**
>
> **What to write.** *The right to exercise diplomatic protection is a right of this State. The
> Ministry is giving due consideration to its exercise, as recommended practice provides, and the
> views of the family have been sought in writing.* **What not to write:** *the family's claim has
> been submitted*, which misdescribes whose claim it is and who may withdraw it.
>
> *Source: the Articles on Diplomatic Protection, adopted by the International Law Commission in
> 2006, of which the General Assembly took note and which it commended to the attention of
> Governments; Articles 1, 2, 3, 14 and 19. The Articles are not a treaty. Article 19 is headed
> *Recommended practice* and uses *should*.*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP6-06-06%%

> **Layla:** Your draft letter tells the family that the Ministry will submit their claim.
> **Jamil:** That is what we are going to do.
> **Layla:** It may be what we do, and the sentence contains two errors that will cost us the family's

> **Jamil:** It feels unkind to tell them it is not their claim.

> right to make this claim is a right of the State. The Ministry is giving due consideration to
> exercising it.** Then everything we are actually doing for them: their views, sought in writing;

> **Jamil:** And the *should*?
> **Layla:** Use it where the text uses it and do not pretend it is more. **Recommended practice says
> the State should give due consideration, should take their views into account wherever feasible,
> and should transfer any compensation obtained, subject to reasonable deductions.** Those are three
> *shoulds*, and this Ministry intends to honor all three, which is a promise about our own conduct
> and not a statement about the law.
> **Jamil:** So I may say we intend to?
> **Layla:** You may say what we intend, in our own name, and you should. **The thing that destroys a
> family's trust is not bad news in March. It is good news in March that turns out in December to

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Example | The mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| **a right of the State** | the ownership, stated plainly | *their claim* |
| **is giving due consideration to** | what we are doing now | promising an outcome |
| **should**, as the text has it | recommended practice, reported | reading *should* as *shall* |
| **this Ministry intends to** | a promise about our conduct | dressing an intention as law |
| **a decision for the Director** | where the discretion sits | implying the desk decides |
| **we will write again by [date]** | the thing a family can hold | *in due course* |

> **Grammar Note** — In a letter to a family, every sentence should be checkable by them. *A right of
> the State* is checkable. *We are giving due consideration* is checkable against the date you

> is meant.

### 2 · *Should* and *shall*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **shall** | the claimant **shall** be a national of the claiming State — an obligation |
| **should** | the State **should** give due consideration — a recommendation |
| **may** | the State **may** present the claim — a power |
| **is not obliged to** | and **is not obliged to** do so — the express negative |
| **intends to** | this Ministry **intends to** transfer any sum recovered — our own undertaking |

> **Grammar Note** — Never upgrade *should* to *shall* in a quotation, and never downgrade it in a
> letter. Officers do the first out of kindness and the second out of caution, and both are
> inaccurate reports of what a text says.

> **Layla:** They arrive in ten minutes. What is your first sentence?

> **Layla:** Kind, and it promises something you cannot measure and they cannot check. Start with the
> thing that is true and difficult, because you will only be able to say it once.
> **Jamil:** That the claim is not theirs.
> **Layla:** In those words it sounds like a door closing. Say it in the form it actually takes: **the
> right to make this claim is a right of the State, and the decision whether to exercise it is the
> Ministry's.** Both halves. The second half tells them who to talk to, which is the useful part.
> **Jamil:** And then they ask why.
> **Layla:** Then you explain it, briefly and without apology, because the reason is a good one. An
> individual cannot bring a claim against a State in the way a State can. So the law gives the State
> of nationality a right of its own, arising out of the injury to its national. **The structure is
> there because it is the only structure that produces anybody who can act at all.**
> **Jamil:** They will hear it as a refusal.
> **Layla:** They will hear it as a refusal if you stop there. You do not stop there. You tell them
> what is happening: the Ministry is **giving due consideration** to exercising the right; their views
> **will be taken into account** and you are going to ask for them in writing, today, on that sheet;
> the local remedies position is being examined, and you explain the three stages in that file with
> an estimate of time; and the file goes to the Director on the ninth. Four facts, all checkable.

> **Layla:** No. And when they ask you — and they will, at the door, standing up — you say that you
> do not know and that you will write by the fourteenth either way. **Everything you say in this room

> **Layla:** If a sum is ever recovered, recommended practice says the State **should** transfer it to

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

This is the unit officers resist, and the resistance is honorable. A family sits in front of you,
their son has been injured by the act of a foreign state, and you have to explain that the claim
arising from it is not theirs. It sounds like the law protecting institutions from people. Look at
the alternative and the shape changes. If the right were the individual's, it would have to be
exercised against a state by an individual, and in most systems there is no forum in which that can
be done and no means of enforcing anything. The rule gives the injury to somebody who can actually
act: a state, with a treaty relationship, an embassy, a note-verbale channel and the standing to
invoke another state's responsibility. What the modern recommended practice adds is the decency the
old rule lacked — consider it properly, ask the person what they want, and hand over what you
recover. Three *shoulds* instead of three *shalls*, which is less than one would like and is also the
most that states would accept, and it is written down, which is the part that matters.

**Discuss:** Should the three recommended steps be obligations? What would states lose by accepting
that?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Letter · to the family of a national injured in Perenne
> *Consular Department, cleared by Legal Affairs · 2 October*
>
> Dear Mr and Mrs Haddad,
>
> **1 Whose right this is.** I must explain one thing clearly, because it affects everything that
> follows. **The right to make a claim against the Government of Perenne in respect of the injury to
> your son is a right of our State, and not of your family.** **The decision whether to exercise it
> is the Ministry's**, and I will tell you below who takes it and when.
>
> **2 What is happening now. The Ministry is giving due consideration to exercising that right**, as
> the recommended practice of the International Law Commission provides for a case where a
> significant injury has occurred. **The file will go to the Director of Legal Affairs on 9
> October.**
>
> **3 What we ask of you. Your views will be taken into account.** I enclose a sheet headed *Views of
> the injured person and the family*. Please return it, in your own words and at any length, **by 7
> October**, so that it is on the file before the Director sees it.
>
> **4 The position in the courts of Perenne.** As a general rule a claim of this kind cannot be
> presented until **all local remedies have been exhausted** — that is, until the courts of Perenne
> have been through their stages. **In your son's case that means three stages**, which in two
> comparable cases took **between eleven and nineteen months**. We are also examining whether any of
> the recognized exceptions applies. **This rule is not an obstacle we have invented: it exists so
> that Perenne has the opportunity to put the matter right in its own courts.**
>
> **5 What I will do, whatever is decided. I will write to you again by 14 November**, whether or not
> a decision has been taken, and I will tell you what it is and the reasons we are able to give.
> **If compensation is ever recovered, it is this Ministry's practice to transfer it to the injured
> person, subject to any reasonable deductions.** That is our practice and I am glad to state it.
>
> Yours sincerely,
>
> *[Consular Officer]*

> **Writer's Tip** — Paragraph 5 is why this letter works. Two dates, one of them unconditional, and
> one sentence about the ministry's own practice stated as practice. Every other paragraph tells the
> family something about the law; this one tells them something about us, and it is the one they
> will keep.

### 3 · Write

Write **170 to 200 words**: a letter to somebody affected by a decision that is not theirs to take.

**Say whose right it is, in both halves, once. Say what is happening now and when the decision will
be taken. Say what you are asking of them, with a date. Explain any precondition in their case, with
stages and an estimate of time, and say what the precondition is for. End with what you will do
whatever is decided, and with any practice of your own institution that you are glad to state.**
