# Foreign Service English · sources

**English for Foreign Ministry Staff · Syrian Edition.** Six books, seventy-two units, seventy-two
separate source bodies. Nothing in this series reuses a figure from Wayfinder English or from the
Clearline business series.

## The rule this series is built on

Every reading text in every book quotes something real: a treaty article, a depositary record, a
published standard, an official count, a resolution number. Three rules apply without exception.

1. **The text names its source, its date and its publisher.** Not "the UN says" but "the General
   Assembly, in resolution 79/249, adopted 31 December 2024".
2. **The text prints what the source does not show.** A party count is accurate on the day the
   depositary published it and not a day later. A standard that is not in the instrument people
   think it is in gets said so.
3. **Every person, office, document and case is invented.** The Ministry in these books, its staff,
   its files and its visitors do not exist. Where a unit needs a counterpart state for a role-play,
   that state is invented too — Valmora, Kesteland, Marovia, Astrand, Perenne, the Sable Isles — so
   that no real government is given a position it has not taken.

**This is an independent teaching draft.** It is not an official publication of any ministry and
carries no official status.

Twelve units, twelve source bodies. At this level the sources are deliberately **institutional

short enough to say in one A2 sentence and real enough to be worth learning.

## Notes on honesty

- **Unit 3 corrects a rule most people get wrong.** The ten-year passport maximum is written in
  national law, not in an ICAO standard, and the deadline everybody half-remembers is in Annex 9
  rather than in Doc 9303. A consular officer who cites the wrong instrument loses the argument
  even when the practical answer is right.
- **Unit 4 is the difference between acceding and being bound.** Thailand deposited its instrument
  on 30 June 2026 and the Convention does not enter into force for it until 28 February 2027. For
  eight months the honest answer to "do I still need full legalisation?" is **yes**, and the list of
  130 parties does not tell you that.
- **Unit 5 prints two counts that are both right.** 194 ITU member states and 193 UN member states.
  Nothing is wrong with either figure; they count different memberships, and an officer who treats
  them as interchangeable will eventually be corrected in public.
- **Unit 6 separates a treaty from a treaty action.** More than 158,000 treaties have been
  registered since 1946, and the number of *actions* recorded against them is a larger and different
  figure. Both get quoted as "the number of treaties".
- **Unit 8 prints two scales that must not be mixed.** The regular budget scale runs from 0.001 to
  22 per cent; the peacekeeping scale applies further adjustments on top. Quoting a regular-budget
  percentage in a peacekeeping discussion is the commonest arithmetic error in multilateral work.
- **Unit 10 prints an article whose legal effect is contested.** Article 36 of the 1963 Convention
  obliges the receiving state to notify the consular post without delay when the detained person
  asks. Whether the detained person can enforce that themselves has been answered one way by the
  International Court of Justice and another way by some domestic courts, and the unit says so
  rather than choosing.
- **Unit 11 is a permission, not an entitlement.** A short-stay visa authorizes its holder to
  present themselves at a border. It does not guarantee entry and it does not grant ninety days;
  the ninety days are the ceiling of a rolling window that the traveler shares with their own
  earlier trips.
- **Unit 12 is a membership record read carefully.** Syria was an original member from 24 October
  1945 and has been a member ever since; between 1958 and 1961 that membership was exercised as part
  of a single member with Egypt, and the separate seat resumed on 13 October 1961. The unit prints
  all three facts, because the short version — "member since 1945" — is true and leaves out the part
  a counterpart may raise.

## Book 2 · Foreign Service English 1 (A2+ to B1) · *The consular section*

Twelve source bodies, none of them used in Book 1 and none used anywhere in the two sister series.
Every count below is a count on a published status page on a stated date, and every unit prints that
date, because these numbers move.

| Unit | Source | What the text prints |
|---|---|---|
| 1 The Notice and the Warrant | INTERPOL, own published pages | **196 member countries**, reached in 2023 when Palau was admitted as the 196th. **A Red Notice is not an arrest warrant.** It rests on a warrant or order issued by a national judicial authority, and each member country decides for itself what effect to give it |
| 2 Serving the Papers | HCCH, Service Convention of 15 November 1965, status table | **84 Contracting Parties**, table last updated **21 March 2024**. The HCCH's own note: **"Contracting Party" covers both cases in which the Convention has, and cases in which it has not yet, entered into force** for that party |
| 3 A Child in Two Countries | HCCH, Convention of 25 October 1980 on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, status table | **103 Contracting Parties**; in force since **1 December 1983**. The Convention decides **return**, not **custody** |
| 4 What the Caller Said | UNHCR, on the 1951 Convention and its 1967 Protocol | **149 states are parties to the 1951 Convention and/or its 1967 Protocol** — a combined count. Per instrument, UNHCR has published **146** for the Convention and **147** for the Protocol. The pre-1951 time limitation still applies for **Saint Kitts and Nevis** and **Madagascar** |
| 5 Nobody's National | The 1954 Convention relating to the Status of Stateless Persons; the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness | **1961 Convention: 82 parties as at 10 September 2025**, against **78 as at 1 February 2023**. The most widely quoted figure for the **1954** Convention, **83**, is a **UNHCR count from November 2014**. Two conventions, two party lists, two different jobs |
| 6 The Most Ratified Treaty | Convention on the Rights of the Child, 1989 | **196 states parties** — every eligible state but one. The one signed on **16 February 1995** and has not ratified. **A party count is not a count of identical obligations:** parties have entered reservations and interpretive declarations |
| 7 Twenty-Four Hours | WHO, International Health Regulations (2005), Article 6, as amended in 2024 | Assess within **48 hours**; notify within **24 hours** of determining that the event is notifiable, through the National IHR Focal Point. Published material gives both **196** and **194** States Parties, and the larger figure includes States Parties that are not WHO Member States |
| 8 Asking Another State | UN Convention against Corruption; UNODC ratification status page | **191 parties**; **last ratification 7 August 2024**. **Article 44 is extradition. Article 46 is mutual legal assistance.** Two articles, two requests, two answers |
| 9 Signed Is Not Ratified | Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 | **116 parties** and **45 signatories** on the depositary's page; in force **27 January 1980**. **Article 18:** a state that has signed is obliged not to defeat the object and purpose of the treaty before it enters into force. That is not ratification, and it is not nothing |
| 10 The Slow Route | Universal Postal Union | **192 member countries**; **Treaty of Bern, 9 October 1874**; renamed the Universal Postal Union in **1878**; a UN specialized agency since **1948**. The UPU's own anniversary release says **22** founding countries; other published accounts say **21** |
| 11 The Award and the Court | Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, New York, 1958 | **172 parties** and **24 signatories**. The 172 is **169 UN member states plus three non-members** — the Cook Islands, the Holy See and the State of Palestine — which is why a second figure, **169**, is also published and also right |
| 12 Permission to Land | Convention on International Civil Aviation, Chicago, 7 December 1944 | **52 signatory states** on the day; in force **April 1947**; **193 parties**. **Article 1:** every state has complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory. One state is covered through another state's ratification, and the Convention's party count and ICAO's membership count are two different measures |

**Twelve honesty notes, printed in the units as well as here.**

- **Unit 1 is the difference between an alert and an order.** A Red Notice asks; it does not compel.
  Published analysis says roughly a third of member countries will treat one as a basis for
  provisional arrest and the rest require a formal request first, so the honest answer to "will they
  arrest him?" is a question about which country.
- **Unit 2 is a word with a definition that surprises people.** On the HCCH tables, *Contracting
  Party* includes states for which the Convention is not yet in force. So the count on the page is
  not the count of states you can actually serve documents in, and the page says so in small type.
- **Unit 3 separates two questions a parent hears as one.** The Convention is about where the case
  is decided, not who wins it. A return order is not a custody order, and saying so early is kinder
  than saying it late.
- **Unit 4 is a combined number.** UNHCR's own headline figure, 149, is parties to the Convention
  *and/or* the Protocol. Quoting it as the number of parties to the Convention is wrong by three,
  and the two instruments do not have the same party list.
- **Unit 5 is a figure with a date on it.** The 1961 count moved from 78 to 82 in under three years.
  The commonly quoted 1954 figure is more than a decade old. The unit teaches the words *as at*
  before it teaches either number.
- **Unit 6 is what a party count hides.** 196 parties is the widest ratification of any human rights
  treaty, and it does not mean 196 identical sets of obligations, because reservations and
  interpretive declarations sit on top of it.
- **Unit 7 is two clocks, not one.** Everybody remembers 24 hours. The 24 hours start after the
  assessment, and the assessment has its own 48. A text that quotes only the 24 has described half
  the obligation.
- **Unit 8 is two articles that get asked for interchangeably.** A request for evidence and a
  request for a person are different instruments with different grounds of refusal. The unit makes
  the officer name which one is being asked for before answering.
- **Unit 9 is a signature that does something smaller than people think and larger than they say.**
  Signature is not consent to be bound. It does carry Article 18. Both halves of that sentence are
  needed, and most speakers drop one of them.
- **Unit 10 is two reputable sources with different numbers.** The UPU says 22 founding countries;
  other published accounts say 21. Neither is lying. The unit teaches the learner to name the source
  rather than pick the number they prefer.
- **Unit 11 is why two right answers differ by three.** 172 counts three parties that are not UN
  member states. 169 counts only those that are. A reader given one figure and shown the other will
  suspect the first unless the text explains the base.
- **Unit 12 is a count that is not quite a count of states.** One state is a party because another
  state's ratification extends to it, and the Convention's parties and ICAO's members are tallied
  differently. The unit prints the sovereignty rule in Article 1 and then prints the arithmetic that
  sits underneath the headline number.

## Book 3 · Foreign Service English 2 (B1) · *The desk officer*

Twelve source bodies, none used in Books Foundation or 1, and none used anywhere in the two sister
series. Every count is a count on a published page on a stated date, and every unit prints the date
and the base.

| Unit | Source | What the text prints |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Nine Votes | Charter of the United Nations, Article 27; Security Council composition | **15 members, 5 of them permanent**; a substantive decision needs **9 affirmative votes** and no permanent member voting against. **An abstention by a permanent member has not been treated as a veto in practice** — a practice built on conduct, not on an amended text |
| 2 Present and Voting | Charter of the United Nations, **Article 18** | Important questions are decided by a **two-thirds majority of the members present and voting**. **An abstention is not a vote**, so the denominator moves while the vote is happening |
| 3 Credentials Are Not Recognition | General Assembly practice; the **Credentials Committee** | The Committee has **nine members**, appointed at the start of each session, and reports on the credentials of representatives. **Credentials concern who may speak for a delegation in the Assembly. They are not an act of recognition of a government by other states** |
| 4 It Is Reported That | Statute of the International Court of Justice, **Articles 36 and 59** | **15 judges**, elected for **nine-year** terms. Jurisdiction rests on **consent**. **Article 59: a decision has no binding force except between the parties and in respect of that particular case** |
| 5 Forty-Seven Seats | General Assembly resolution **60/251 of 15 March 2006** | The Human Rights Council has **47 member states**, elected **directly and individually by secret ballot by a majority of the members of the General Assembly**. The resolution also established the **Universal Periodic Review**, whose first cycle ran over **four years, 48 states a year** |
| 6 Concluding Observations | The Human Rights Committee under the 1966 Covenant | **18 members**, serving as **independent experts in their personal capacity**, not as representatives of states. **Its concluding observations are not judgments**, and a treaty body is not a court |
| 7 Nationally Determined | Paris Agreement, 2015; UNFCCC secretariat synthesis reports | **Adopted by 195 Parties on 12 December 2015.** The secretariat's own page gives **194 Parties as at 27 January 2026** — a count that went **down**. A 2024 synthesis covered **168 NDCs communicated by 195 Parties**, because the European Union and its member states file **one joint NDC counted as one** for 28 Parties |
| 8 A Table That Says It Is Unofficial | United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea; the ocean affairs division's status page | Published counts give **169** parties and **170**, depending on whether the **European Union** is counted alongside the states. The division's own table shows a totals row of **168 ratifications** and is dated **2019**, and the page says it is **unofficial, quick-reference information** |
| 9 A Bundle of National Rights | WIPO, the Madrid System for the international registration of marks | **116 members covering 132 countries**, on WIPO's own members page; other published accounts give **115 covering 131**. An international registration is **a bundle of national or regional rights**, in WIPO's own words, and the scope of protection in each designated member is decided by **that member's own law** |
| 10 The Fifth Committee | General Assembly Main Committees | **Six Main Committees**, each with every member state entitled to representation. **A Main Committee recommends; the plenary decides.** A draft agreed in committee is a draft until the Assembly adopts it |
| 11 Inscription Is Not Protection | UNESCO, 1972 World Heritage Convention; the World Heritage List | **1,248 properties** on the List — **972 cultural, 235 natural, 41 mixed** — in **170 States Parties**, out of **196 States Parties** to the Convention. **53 properties are on the List of World Heritage in Danger.** Inscription does not transfer sovereignty and does not by itself protect anything |
| 12 The Flag and the Port | International Maritime Organization | **176 Member States and three Associate Members** — the Faroe Islands, Hong Kong and Macao — so a membership list is not a list of states. **Ten port State control regimes**: eight regional memoranda of understanding and one agreement. **Flag State, port State and coastal State are three different roles**, and one state wears all three on different days |

**Twelve honesty notes, printed in the units as well as here.**

- **Unit 1 is a rule that is not in the text it is said to be in.** Article 27 requires that a
  substantive decision carry nine affirmative votes without a negative vote by a permanent member.
  The treatment of an abstention as not a negative vote is a long practice, accepted in conduct and
  by the Court's advisory reasoning, and it is not an amendment of the Article. The unit says so,
  because an officer who quotes it as the text will be corrected.
- **Unit 2 is a denominator that moves.** *Present and voting* excludes abstentions, so the
  two-thirds is two-thirds of a smaller number than the number of states in the room. A
  prediction made on the membership figure is a prediction about the wrong arithmetic.
- **Unit 3 is a word that carries more weight outside the room than inside it.** A credentials
  decision concerns participation in the Assembly's work. Reporting it as recognition, or as a
  decision about who governs a country, is the commonest error in this field, and the unit teaches
  the sentence that avoids it.
- **Unit 4 prints Article 59 in full.** A judgment binds the parties, in that case. It is not
  legislation, it is not a precedent in the common-law sense, and a desk officer who summarizes it
  as *the Court has ruled that states must…* has written something the Court did not say.
- **Unit 5 separates two things that share a word.** The Council's members are **states**, elected
  by the Assembly. Its review mechanism is **periodic** but the length of the cycle has been set and
  reset by its own decisions, so a confident *every four years* is not safe. The unit prints only
  the published first-cycle figure.
- **Unit 6 separates experts from representatives.** The Committee's eighteen members sit in their
  personal capacity. Their concluding observations are authoritative and they are not binding
  judgments, and both halves of that sentence have to be said in the same breath.
- **Unit 7 is a party count that went down, and a figure that is not what it looks like.** 195 at
  adoption, 194 on the secretariat's page in January 2026. And 168 NDCs for 195 Parties is not a
  gap in compliance: one of those NDCs is filed jointly for 28 Parties. The unit prints both
  arithmetics side by side.
- **Unit 8 is a publisher saying so itself.** The status page the whole field uses calls itself
  unofficial quick reference, carries a totals row dated 2019, and sits beside two different
  published party counts depending on how a regional organization is tallied. The unit teaches the
  learner to quote the page's own disclaimer along with its number.
- **Unit 9 is a registration that is not a right.** One filing, one fee, one number — and then as
  many separate national or regional rights as there are designations, each governed by its own
  law. The phrase *international trademark* is in common use and is wrong in a way that costs
  companies money.
- **Unit 10 is a draft that is not a decision.** Agreement in a Main Committee is reported as a
  decision of the Assembly roughly every session, and it is not one until the plenary adopts it.
  The unit teaches the two-sentence correction.
- **Unit 11 is three figures that are usually quoted as one.** Properties on the List, States
  Parties with any property inscribed, and States Parties to the Convention are 1,248, 170 and 196,
  and they answer three different questions. And fifty-three of the properties are on the Danger
  List, which is the most important sentence in the unit.
- **Unit 12 is a list with three members that are not states.** A membership figure that includes
  associate members cannot be quoted as a number of countries, and the three roles a maritime state
  plays — flag, port, coastal — attract different obligations on the same day.

## Book 4 · Foreign Service English 3 (B1+) · *Protocol and the mission*

Twelve source bodies, none used in Books Foundation, 1 or 2, and none used anywhere in the two
sister series. This book's running subject is the **status page**: what a published count counts, on
what date, under what entry-into-force clause, and what a state has actually accepted.

| Unit | Source | What the text prints |
|---|---|---|
| 1 The Special Mission | Convention on Special Missions, **8 December 1969** | **Article 2:** a state may send a special mission with the consent of the receiving state, **previously obtained** through the diplomatic or another agreed channel. Adopted **1969**; entered into force **21 June 1985** — sixteen years later; **38 parties as at April 2012** on the depositary's table. **Most special missions in the world are therefore conducted outside this Convention, on consent and practice** |
| 2 One Ratification Short | Vienna Convention on the Representation of States in their Relations with International Organizations of a Universal Character, **14 March 1975** | Adopted by **57 votes to 1, with 15 abstentions**. **Article 89:** entry into force thirty days after the deposit of the **thirty-fifth** instrument. The depositary's page records **34 parties and 20 signatories**. **Fifty years on, the Convention is one ratification short and has never entered into force** |
| 3 A Hundred and Sixty-Two of a Hundred and Ninety-Three | Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the United Nations, **1946**; Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the Specialized Agencies, **21 November 1947** | The 1946 Convention entered into force **17 September 1946** and has **162 parties**, with **23 states** having accepted it subject to reservations. So **31 UN member states are not parties to it**. The 1947 Convention is a **separate instrument** with about **131 parties**. **Being party to one is not being party to the other** |
| 4 Universal, and Its Protocols | Geneva Conventions of **12 August 1949** and the Additional Protocols of **8 June 1977** and **2005** | On the ICRC's annual-report map, **as at 31 December 2013**: **195** States party to the Conventions, **173** to Protocol I, **167** to Protocol II, **66** to Protocol III. Other published summaries give **194** for the Conventions and describe them as universally ratified. **The Conventions and the Protocols are four instruments with four memberships** |
| 5 Signed, and Still Not in Force | Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty | Entry into force requires ratification by **all 44 states listed in Annex 2**, and then **180 days**. Published figures give about **178 ratifications as at March 2024** and nine further signatures. **A treaty can have a hundred and seventy-eight ratifications and not be in force, because of how its entry-into-force clause is written.** One state **withdrew its ratification in 2023** while remaining a signatory |
| 6 Choose Your Protocols | Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, 1980, and its Protocols; UNODA's own status page | **128 High Contracting Parties and 4 signatories** on the page **updated 18 March 2025** (**127** on the version of **25 June 2024**). Protocol by protocol: **Protocol I 88 · Protocol II 121 · Amended Protocol II 96 · Protocol III 107 · Protocol IV 117 · Protocol V 111**. **A state consents protocol by protocol, so the framework figure tells you almost nothing about any obligation** |
| 7 One Hundred and Ninety-Three of One Hundred and Ninety-Seven | Chemical Weapons Convention | **193 States Parties.** The denominator is **197**: the 193 UN member states plus the Cook Islands, Niue, the Holy See's state and the State of Palestine. **Near-universal is not universal, and 193 out of 197 is a different sentence from 193 out of 193** |
| 8 The Transit Obligation | Agreement between the United Nations and the United States regarding the Headquarters of the United Nations, **1947** | The Agreement defines the **headquarters district**, provides for **access and transit** of persons having business with the United Nations, and contains a **dispute settlement clause providing for arbitration**. **A host state's obligations are in an agreement, not in courtesy**, and the Agreement says what happens when the parties disagree |
| 9 A Count Nobody Can State Officially | UNESCO Convention of **1970** on illicit import, export and transfer of ownership of cultural property | UNESCO's own page gives **141 states** having ratified. A **2012** Secretariat report gave **122**, having recorded twelve accessions in five years, and a commentary on the same occasion noted that a **disputed ratification** meant the figure **could not officially be counted as 123**. **A published count can be unavailable rather than wrong** |
| 10 The Nineteenth of March, 1815 | Règlement on the relative rank of diplomatic agents, annexed to the Final Act of the Congress of Vienna, **19 March 1815** | The Règlement established **classes of head of mission** and settled **precedence within each class by the date of the official notification of arrival** — a rule adopted to end disputes about rank, and the direct ancestor of the modern rule. **An 1815 instrument is not the law in force today**, and the principle it introduced is |
| 11 Three Instruments, One Subject | Hague Convention of **1954** for the protection of cultural property in the event of armed conflict, with its **First Protocol (1954)** and **Second Protocol (1999)** | Three instruments on one subject, each with its **own list of parties**. **This unit prints no count**: it prints where the counts live, and the sentence a drafter must write instead of a figure |
| 12 The Depositary's Own Notes | The United Nations Treaty Collection's published status of multilateral treaties deposited with the Secretary-General, and the Treaty Section's notes on how treaty actions are recorded | What the publisher says about its **own** table: that it is current **as at a stated date**; that actions are **recorded with the date of deposit**, which is not the date of entry into force for that state; that **accession and ratification have the same legal effect**; that a column headed **participant** may include signatories who are not parties; that **reservations, declarations and objections are reproduced with their dates**; and the standing language that **inclusion implies no position on questions of status or recognition**. **No figure is quoted: the practice is the subject, and the last reading text in this book is the publisher explaining its own table** |

**Twelve honesty notes, printed in the units as well as here.**

- **Unit 1.** Sixteen years between adoption and entry into force, and about thirty-eight parties a
  quarter of a century after that. A desk officer who writes *under the Special Missions Convention*
  about a visiting delegation is probably writing about an instrument that binds neither state.
- **Unit 2.** The threshold is thirty-five; the count is thirty-four. One instrument of ratification
  separates a fifty-year-old convention from entry into force, and nothing in the text obliges
  anybody to deposit it.
- **Unit 3.** 162 of 193 is the most useful figure in the book, because it is the one everybody
  assumes is 193. And twenty-three parties have accepted the Convention subject to reservations,
  which has to be read state by state.
- **Unit 4.** The Conventions are described as universally ratified and published counts differ by
  one. The Protocols are three further instruments with three smaller memberships, and the figures
  printed here carry the date of the map they came from.
- **Unit 5.** The entry-into-force clause, not the number of ratifications, decides whether a treaty
  is in force. And a ratification can be withdrawn while a signature remains, which puts Article 18
  of the 1969 Convention on treaties back on the desk.
- **Unit 6.** The framework figure and the protocol figures differ by up to forty. Quoting the
  framework figure as the measure of an obligation is the characteristic error in this field.
- **Unit 7.** 193 out of 197 and 193 out of 193 are different sentences, and only one of them is
  true. The denominator in this field is almost never the UN membership.
- **Unit 8.** A host state's obligations to a headquarters are in a published agreement with a
  dispute-settlement clause. Describing them as hospitality is a mistake in both directions.
- **Unit 9.** A published count that its own publisher's commentators say cannot officially be
  stated is the purest form of this book's subject: the number is not the fact; the page and its
  date are.
- **Unit 10.** The rule about precedence that every protocol officer uses began as an annex to the
  Final Act of a congress in 1815, adopted because rank disputes were producing duels. The 1815
  text is not the instrument in force today, and the principle is the same one.
- **Unit 11.** Three instruments on one subject, each with its own party list, and this unit prints
  none of them. The point of the unit is the sentence a drafter writes when the figure is not to
  hand, which is the sentence this whole series is about.
- **Unit 12.** The last reading text is the publisher explaining its own table, including the
  language that inclusion implies no position on questions of status or recognition, and the warning
  that a participant column may include states that have signed without becoming parties.
  Everything in these four books rests on somebody having read that paragraph.
