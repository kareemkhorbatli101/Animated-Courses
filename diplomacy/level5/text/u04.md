# Unit 4 · Three-Quarters of Which Parties?
%%SUB One clause, two readings, and sixteen years in which nobody could say how many%%
%%GOALS
Present two readings of one clause without choosing between them
Report how an interpretive question was resolved
Say which parties an amendment binds
%%

An amendment was adopted in 1995. Its parent convention said an amendment enters into force between
the parties that have accepted it when three-quarters of "the Parties who accepted them" have
ratified. Three-quarters of which parties: those that were parties when the amendment was adopted,
or those that are parties today, a number that rises every year? For sixteen years nobody could say
how many ratifications were needed. The question was settled by a decision in 2011, and the amendment
entered into force in 2019 — twenty-four years after it was adopted.

>
> **The amendment.** In **1995** the Conference of the Parties adopted an amendment — known as the

>
> **The clause that caused the trouble.** The Convention's amendment article provides that an
> amendment enters into force **between the Parties having accepted it** on the ninetieth day after
> the depositary has received instruments of ratification, approval, formal confirmation or
> acceptance by **at least three-fourths of the Parties who accepted them**.
>
> **The two readings.**
>
> | | What is counted | The practical effect |
> |---|---|---|
> | **The fixed-time reading** | three-quarters of the states that were **parties to the Convention when the amendment was adopted**, in 1995 | a number that can be **written down** and reached |
> | **The current-time reading** | three-quarters of the states that are **parties now** | a number that **rises every time a state joins the Convention** |
>
> **Why that is not a quibble.** Under the second reading the threshold moves away as the Convention
> succeeds. Each new party raises the number of ratifications required, so an amendment can be
> pursued for decades and never enter into force. Under the first reading the denominator was settled
> in 1995 and the question becomes one of arithmetic. **The difference between the two was, in
> practice, something of the order of thirty ratifications.**
>
> **How it was resolved, and when.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Amendment adopted** | **1995** |
> | **Question left unresolved** | for **sixteen years** |
> | **Resolved by decision of the Parties** | **2011**, in favor of the **fixed-time** reading |
> | **Entered into force** | **5 December 2019** — **twenty-four years** after adoption |
>

### 1 · Presenting two readings

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP5-04-06%%

> **Layla:** Your note says the clause is ambiguous. That is true and it is not advice.
> **Omar:** What should it say?
> **Layla:** Set out both readings and say what each one produces. **On one reading, the parties are
> counted as at the date of adoption, and the threshold is a fixed number. On the other, they are
> counted as they stand, and the threshold rises with every accession.** Two sentences, parallel
> structure, and a reader can see the problem without being told there is one.
> **Omar:** Should I say which reading is better?
> **Layla:** Only if you are asked for a view, and then you say so explicitly: *in our view*. What you
> must never do is present one reading as the meaning of the clause and leave the other out, because
> the other has been argued for sixteen years by people who are not fools.
> **Omar:** And the outcome?
> **Layla:** Report it as an outcome, not as a discovery. **The question was resolved by the Parties
> in 2011 in favor of the fixed-time approach.** Not *it turned out to mean*. Nothing turned out. A
> body with power to decide the question decided it.
> **Omar:** Why does the distinction matter?
> **Layla:** Because *it turned out to mean* implies the answer was always there, and the next time a
> similar clause appears somebody in this building will say the answer is obvious. It was not
> obvious. **It was decided**, and the decision could have gone the other way.

*Why does Layla object to* it turned out to mean?

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| **On one reading … On the other** | **On one reading** the list is fixed. **On the other** it grows. |

| **turns on whether** | The question **turns on whether** they are counted at adoption. |

> **Grammar Note** — Keep the two readings grammatically parallel. If the first is *the parties are
> counted at adoption* and the second is *you have to count them again every year*, the reader will
> prefer the first for reasons of rhythm rather than substance, and you will have argued a case
> without meaning to.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

It is tempting to read this story as a failure of drafting, and partly it is: a threshold expressed
as a fraction of an undated set is an invitation to sixteen years of argument, and any treaty officer
who drafts one now should be sent back to their desk. But there is a second reading of the story, and
it is the more useful one. The question was not settled by a court, an arbitrator, a depositary or a
textbook. It was settled by the parties themselves, when enough of them wanted it settled, by a
decision taken in the ordinary way at an ordinary session. That is how most hard questions in this
field are actually resolved, and it is why delegation work is worth doing. An officer who believes
that ambiguities are resolved by finding out what a clause really means will spend a career
disappointed. An officer who understands that ambiguities are resolved when a body with the power to
resolve them becomes willing to do so will spend that career usefully, and will know what to put on
an agenda.

**Discuss:** Who should settle an ambiguity in a treaty — the parties, a court, or the depositary?
What does each choice cost?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Legal note · the Ban Amendment: threshold, entry into force and effect
> *Legal Affairs · for the Environment desk · 3 February*
>
> **1 The clause.** The Convention's amendment article requires instruments of ratification,
> approval, formal confirmation or acceptance from **at least three-fourths of the Parties who
> accepted** an amendment. **The clause does not state the date at which those Parties are to be
> counted.**
>
> **2 The two readings.**
>
> | | What is counted |
> |---|---|
> | **fixed-time** | the states that were parties **when the amendment was adopted**, in 1995 |
> | **current-time** | the states that are parties **at the time of counting** |
>
> **On the fixed-time reading the threshold is a stable number. On the current-time reading it rises
> with each accession to the Convention.** Both readings were argued; the question remained
> unresolved for **sixteen years**.
>
> **3 How it was resolved. The Conference of the Parties resolved the question in 2011, in favor of
> the fixed-time approach.** It was resolved by decision of the body competent to decide it. **It was
> not discovered**, and the decision could have gone the other way.
>
> **4 Entry into force, and whom it binds.** The Ban Amendment **entered into force on 5 December
> 2019**, **between the parties that have accepted it**. It is **not in force** for a party to the
> Convention that has not accepted it, and **no such party acquired any obligation under it by
> remaining a party to the Convention**.
>
> **5 The sentence for a question about one state. The position for any particular state is on the
> depositary's record for that instrument and must be read there, on the day.** We do not state it
> from the fact of Convention membership, and we do not state it from memory.
>
> *Figures for parties to the Convention carry the date of the secretariat's page. This note states
> no position on the merits of either reading.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Paragraph 3 is the one that teaches a reader something they will use elsewhere:
> that ambiguities in this field are resolved by bodies and not by insight. Every legal note that
> can say *it was decided, not discovered* should say it.
