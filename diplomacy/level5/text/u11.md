# Unit 11 · The Report That Is Not Public
%%SUB Three states of affairs that look like two, and the sentence that keeps them apart%%
%%GOALS
Report an absence without turning it into an accusation
Distinguish *not submitted* from *submitted and not public*
Say what information is not available to us
%%

A treaty requires each party to report annually. The secretariat publishes a table of who has
reported. Reading the table, a drafter sees two columns — reported, and not reported — and writes a
sentence about compliance. The table in fact records three states of affairs: reports submitted and
published, reports submitted and **not published**, and reports **not submitted**. The second is the
one that destroys drafts, and the sentence that keeps the three apart is the whole content of this
unit.

>
> | | What it means |
> |---|---|
> | **submitted and public** | the report exists and you can read it |
> | **submitted, not made public** | the report **exists**, has been provided as required, and is **available to States Parties and not to the public** |
> | **not submitted** | **no report** was provided for that period |
>
> **Why the second row is dangerous.** It looks like the third row to anybody counting ticks, and it
> is the opposite of the third row in every respect that matters. **A party in the second row has
> complied. A sentence that counts it as a failure to report is a false statement about another
> state's compliance with a treaty obligation**, which is among the more serious things a ministry
> can get wrong in public.
>

> **may exclude commercially sensitive or national security information**. So a report may exist, be
> public, and be shorter than you expected — and that, too, is compliance.

> | We may say | We may not say |
> |---|---|
> | the number recorded as having submitted a public report, with the date of the table | that the remainder have not reported |

> | that we cannot read a particular report | that a particular report is inadequate |

### 1 · Reporting an absence

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP5-11-06%%

> **Layla:** Your brief says forty parties failed to report.
> **Omar:** Forty rows have no tick.
> **Layla:** A blank is not a fact about a state. It is a fact about a table. Read the legend: some of
> those rows are parties that submitted a report and did not have it made public, which is permitted
> and which is compliance.
> **Omar:** So the real figure is lower.
> **Layla:** There are two figures and neither is *failed to report*. Write **recorded as not having
> submitted** for the third category, and **recorded as having submitted a report not made public**
> for the second. Those are statements about the record. *Failed to report* is a statement about a
> state's conduct, and you are not in a position to make it.
> **Omar:** Is that not the same thing with longer words?
> **Layla:** It is a different claim with longer words. If the Secretariat's record is wrong about a
> state — and records are sometimes wrong — your sentence is still true, because your sentence was
> about the record. The short sentence would have been false and would have been about a government.
> **Omar:** And the reports we cannot read?
> **Layla:** **We are not in a position to state their content.** Not *we do not know*, which sounds
> like a gap in our work, and not *they are secret*, which sounds like an accusation. The content is
> not available to us. That is the whole sentence and it is complete.

*What is the difference between a fact about a table and a fact about a state?*

**1B  Underline the four absence structures in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | What it is about | Safe because |
|---|---|---|
| **is recorded as not having submitted** | the record | true even if the record is wrong |
| **has not been made public** | publication | says nothing about existence |
| **is not available to us** | our access | makes no claim about anyone's conduct |
| **we are not in a position to state** | our knowledge | neither ignorance nor accusation |
| **no report appears on the record** | the narrowest claim | the fallback when the legend is unclear |
| ~~**failed to report**~~ | a state's conduct | **a claim you cannot support from a table** |

> **Grammar Note** — Every sentence in this chart moves the subject of the claim away from the
> foreign government and onto a document or onto ourselves. That is not timidity; it is accuracy
> about what you have actually examined, which is a table.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Compliance tables are the most quoted and least read documents in multilateral work. They are quoted
because they look like scores and because a rate fits in a headline; they are not read because the
legend is at the foot in small type and the reader has already decided what the table shows. The
professional habit is simple and slightly tedious: read the legend, count the categories, give the
figures separately, and refuse the rate. The refusal is the hard part, because the person asking is
usually not hostile — they are a researcher, a journalist or a colleague with a deadline, and three
figures with a date is a worse answer for their purposes than one percentage. What you are protecting
is not the states in the second category, who do not know you exist. You are protecting the thing
that makes your ministry worth telephoning, which is that its figures have never had to be withdrawn.

**Discuss:** Is refusing to give a rate helpful or obstructive? What would you do if your minister
asked for one?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Brief · reporting under the Treaty: what the published record shows
> *Legal Affairs with Arms Control · 9 June*
>
> **1 The obligation.** Each State Party provides an **initial report** on implementation measures
> within the first year after entry into force for it, and an **annual report by 31 May** for the
> preceding calendar year on authorized or actual exports and imports of the conventional arms
> covered. **A report may exclude commercially sensitive or national security information.**
>
> **2 The record.** The Secretariat maintains a published record of reporting. **The figures below
> are as at 9 June** and should be re-checked before use.
>
> **3 Three figures, and no total.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | submitted and **made public** | **X** |
> | submitted and **not made public** | **Y** |
> | **recorded as not having submitted** | **Z** |
>
> **4 Which of these is compliance. The first two categories are compliance with the reporting
> obligation.** A State Party that submits its report and does not have it made public has done what
> the Treaty requires. **This Ministry does not publish a compliance rate**, because the choice of
> which categories to count is a matter for the Conference of States Parties and not for us.
>
> **5 The limit of our knowledge. We are not in a position to state the content of reports that have
> not been made public.** Nor do we assess the adequacy of reports we have not read, and **a short
> report is not evidence of an incomplete one**, given what the Treaty permits a party to exclude.
>
> *Figures carry the date of the Secretariat's record. This brief makes no statement about any State
> Party's compliance beyond what that record records.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Paragraph 4 exists to stop your reader doing arithmetic. Any brief that prints
> two adjacent figures without saying what their sum would and would not mean has invited a
> calculation it cannot control.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. The annual report is due by (31 March / 31 May).
2. A report not made public (is / is not) compliance.
3. The Ministry (publishes / does not publish) a compliance rate.
4. A short report (is / is not) evidence of an incomplete one.

### 3 · Write

Write **150 to 180 words**: a brief on what a published compliance record shows in your own area.

**State the obligation, including anything the instrument permits a party to leave out. Name the
record and its date. Give the figures by category, and give no total. Say which categories are
compliance, and why you do not publish a rate. End with the limit of your own knowledge.**

**Read your brief aloud to your partner first.** If two figures sit next to each other without a
sentence between them, add the sentence. Then check your own against the list, and swap.

| | ✓ |
|---|---|
| Are the categories separate, with no total? | |

| Is there a sentence on what is not available to us? | |

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's brief as a researcher who wants one number. Which number would
you take, and would you be right to?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Did either of you make a claim about a government rather
than a record? Which sentence did it?
