# Starting Point · What a Desk Officer Is For
%%SUB Before Unit 1: you are now writing sentences that other people will say out loud%%

For two books you have answered. From here on you draft. The difference is that a sentence you say
on the telephone dies in ten minutes, and a sentence you write at eight in the morning can be read
out by a minister at six in the evening, quoted in a newspaper on Thursday and produced at a meeting
in November.

### 1 · What you already have

%%FIG i00-f1 | A single review sheet with six boxes. Each box has a heading — the past tenses, the present perfect, modals of obligation, reported speech, the passive, and conditionals — and one short example sentence from a desk officer's working day.%%

Books Foundation and 1 gave you the tenses, the modals, reported speech, the passive and the
conditionals. This book adds the three things that separate a draft somebody can sign from a draft
somebody has to rewrite: **attribution**, **hedging** and **emphasis**.

**1A  Say one sentence from each box to your partner.** Then say which one you use most in writing.

### 2 · The three things this book adds

%%FIG i00-f2 | Three columns headed attribution, hedging and emphasis. Under each, three short example sentences in smaller type, and at the foot of each column one sentence showing the same fact written badly.%%

| | What it does | A sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **Attribution** | says whose statement it is | *The delegation **stated** that…* · *The secretariat's page **gives**…* |
| **Hedging** | says how sure you are | *It **appears to**…* · *On the material we have, it **may well**…* |
| **Emphasis** | says which part matters | ***What matters is** the date.* · ***It was** the abstention **that**…* |

> **Without attribution** a draft becomes the ministry's own view by accident.
> **Without hedging** a draft is either overconfident or silent, and both get rewritten.
> **Without emphasis** the most important sentence in your paragraph is the one the reader skips.

### 3 · Four sentences a desk officer says every week

%%FIG i00-f3 | A card taped inside a folder, printed with four sentences in bold, each with one line of smaller type under it explaining when to use it.%%

> **1  That is the practice, not the text.**
> *Said when somebody quotes a custom as if it were written down.*
>
> **2  Two-thirds of those present and voting, which is not two-thirds of the membership.**
> *Said before anybody starts counting.*
>
> **3  On the material we have, it appears to.**
> *Said instead of* yes *when* yes *is not yet available.*
>
> **4  The committee has agreed a draft. The Assembly has not adopted anything.**
> *Said before the cable goes, not after.*

**3B  Cover the explanations.** Your partner reads a situation; you say which sentence fits.

### 4 · How a draft is read

%%FIG i00-f4 | A short drafted paragraph in the middle of the page with four arrows pointing into it from the margins, labelled the person who signs it, the person who is quoted from it, the person who disagrees with it, and the person who finds it in a file in two years.%%

Everything you write is read by four people. The one who **signs** it wants to know what they are
being asked to commit to. The one who **quotes** it wants one sentence. The one who **disagrees**
with it is looking for the weakest clause. And the one who finds it **in two years** has no idea what
else was known that morning.

Write for the fourth one. If a paragraph makes sense to somebody who was not there, it will survive
the other three.

**4A  Listen.** %%AUD DIP3-00-03%%

**4B  Think about it.** *Talk with a partner.*

1. Which of the four readers do you usually write for?
2. What would change in your drafting if you wrote for the fourth one?

> **One thing before Unit 1.** Nobody will ever thank you for a caveat. The caveat is not there to
> be thanked for. It is there so that in two years, when somebody reads your paragraph with an
> outcome in front of them that nobody could have predicted that morning, your paragraph still says
> something true.
