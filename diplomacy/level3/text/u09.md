### 1 · Hedging and modality

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP3-09-06%%

> **Caller:** So my trademark is protected in twelve countries.
> **Omar:** Your registration **designates** twelve members. In each of them the mark **will be
> examined** under that member's own law.
> **Caller:** But it is registered.
> **Omar:** It **is likely to** be accepted in most of them. It **may well** be provisionally refused
> in one or two. Whether it is refused and on what ground **appears to** depend on how close the mark
> is to earlier marks on each national register, and that is not something I **can** advise you on.
> **Caller:** Can you tell me which ones?
> **Omar:** I cannot. **On the material you have given me**, I can tell you where to look and what to
> ask your representative. Anything more specific than that would be advice I am not qualified to
> give.

*Which of Omar's hedges is about likelihood, and which is about the basis of what he says?*

**1B  Underline every hedge in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | What it marks |
|---|---|---|
| **is likely to** | It **is likely to** be accepted. | probability |
| **may well** | It **may well** be refused in one. | real possibility |
| **appears to** | It **appears to** depend on earlier marks. | your reading, not a fact |
| **on the material we have** | **On the material we have**, the position is… | the basis |
| **in part** / **broadly** | The registration will survive **in part**. | extent |
| **subject to** | …**subject to** examination in each member. | the condition |

> **Grammar Note** — Three different questions get hedged, and a good sentence answers all three:
> *how likely*, *how much*, and *on what basis*. A sentence hedged only for likelihood — *it may be
> refused* — still implies you have seen everything there is to see.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Economic and commercial enquiries reach a foreign ministry because the ministry's name is on the
building and the company has run out of other doors. The officer who takes the call is almost never
qualified to advise, and the two obvious responses are both bad: pretend to advise, or say *we
cannot help you* and hang up. The way through is to separate information from advice out loud, and
then be generous with the information. Naming things correctly, describing what a system does and
does not centralize, and handing over the three questions a competent representative would ask are
all information. Predicting an outcome is advice. The line is easy to hold once you have said it,
and callers who are told plainly where the line is almost always behave better than callers who
sense it is there and cannot see it.

**Discuss:** What is the difference, in your own work, between information and advice? Who decides?
