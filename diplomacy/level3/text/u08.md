### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Read the top of a table before the bottom. The small print above a table is
> where its publisher tells you what it is for, and a publisher who says *quick reference* has
> already told you not to quote it as authority.

%%FIG i08-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a boxed quotation of a page's own disclaimer in smaller type, a short table with three published figures and what each counts, a paragraph headed an old totals row, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · internal note 8
> *Legal Affairs and Europe Department · citing a status page · page 1 of 1*
>
> The Convention on the Law of the Sea is cited every week in this building, and the figure for its
> parties is usually taken from the ocean affairs division's **status page**.
>
> That page describes itself as providing **unofficial, quick-reference information**, and directs the
> reader to the **United Nations Treaty Collection** as the official record.
>
> | Published figure | What it counts |
> |---|---|
> | **169** | parties, on one published account |
> | **170** | parties, on another, counting the **European Union** alongside the states |
> | **168** | the **totals row** of the division's own table &mdash; **a row dated 2019** |
>
> Three published figures, and none of them is wrong. **169 and 170 differ because of how a regional
> organization is tallied.** The **European Union** is recorded on the table as a party, by **formal
> confirmation dated 1 April 1998** — which is the act by which a regional organization becomes
> bound, and which is not called ratification. **168 is older**, and it is on the page everybody
> quotes.
>
> **What to write.** Give the figure, the base, the date and the page's own description of itself:
> *the division's status page, which describes itself as unofficial quick reference, gives X as at
> [date]; the official record is the Treaty Collection.* It is one sentence, it is long, and it
> cannot be attacked.
>
> **What not to write.** *There are 169 parties.* Not because the figure is wrong, but because a bare
> figure invites the reader to assume you have taken it from the official record, and you have not.
>
> *Source: the ocean affairs division's status page for the Convention, including its own disclaimer,
> its totals row and the date of that row, and published accounts giving 169 and 170 parties and
> explaining the European Union's formal confirmation of 1 April 1998.*

### 1 · Nominalization

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP3-08-06%%

> **Layla:** Your sentence is correct and it reads like a conversation.
> **Omar:** "The division publishes the table and says that it is unofficial, and two accounts
> disagree about the total because they count the European Union differently."
> **Layla:** Forty words, four verbs, and no sentence a lawyer can lift. Try it as nouns.
> **Omar:** "The division's publication of the table carries a disclaimer of officiality; the
> divergence between the two published totals reflects the treatment of the European Union."
> **Layla:** Thirty words, and quotable. Now tell me what you have lost.
> **Omar:** Who is doing what. *The treatment of the European Union* does not say who is treating it.
> **Layla:** Exactly. Nominalization buys you formality and precision, and it pays for them in
> agency. In a legal note that is usually the right trade. In a cable about a disagreement it is
> almost always the wrong one.

*Which of Omar's two versions tells you who is doing things?*

**1B  Underline every nominalization in Omar's second version.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Plain | Nominalized | What you gain, what you lose |
|---|---|---|
| They **disagree** about the total. | the **divergence** between the totals | formal; loses who |
| The page **says** it is unofficial. | the page's **disclaimer** of officiality | compact; loses the words used |
| The state **ratified** in 1998. | the state's **ratification** in 1998 | neutral; nothing lost |
| They **count** it differently. | the **treatment** of it | formal; loses who counts |
| We **cannot** confirm it. | the **absence** of confirmation | sounds impersonal; hides you |

> **Grammar Note** — Nominalization is neither good nor bad style; it is a tool with a known cost.
> Each noun you build out of a verb removes the doer from the sentence. Use it when the doer is
> obvious or irrelevant, and avoid it when the doer is the news.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Every field has a page that everybody uses and nobody has read the top of. The reasons are
understandable: the page is convenient, the official record is slower to search, and the figure is
almost always right. The risk is not that the figure is wrong but that the citation is naked, and a
naked figure transfers all the authority of the official record onto a page whose publisher has
politely declined that authority in writing. There is a second, quieter benefit to citing properly.
An officer who writes *which describes itself as unofficial quick reference* is telling a counterpart
that they have read the page carefully, and in a field where everybody is quoting the same tables,
that signal travels. The long sentence is not caution. It is competence, performed in public, at the
cost of about eleven words.
