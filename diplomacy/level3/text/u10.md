> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · internal note 10
> *Europe Department · committees and the plenary · page 1 of 1*
>
> The General Assembly allocates most items to its **six Main Committees**. **Every member state is
> entitled to be represented on each of them**, which is why a Main Committee can look and sound
> exactly like the Assembly itself.
>
> It is not the Assembly. **A Main Committee considers, negotiates and recommends. The plenary
> decides.**
>
> | What the plenary may do with a committee's recommendation |
> |---|
> | **adopt** it |
> | **amend** it and adopt it as amended |
> | **take note of** it, which is not endorsement |
> | **defer** the matter to a later session |
>
> **The two-sentence rule.** Until the plenary has acted, write **two sentences**: *The Committee has
> agreed a draft. The Assembly has not adopted anything.* Both are short, both are true, and the
> second one cannot be cut without the cut being visible.
>
> **Why this goes wrong so often.** The committee room contains the same delegations, the same
> arguments and often the same chair's gavel. Agreement there feels final, and in the great majority
> of cases the plenary does adopt the recommendation. **The exceptions are rare and expensive**: a
> capital that has been told a text is adopted and then has to be told it was amended will remember
> who told it the first time.
>
> **One more distinction. Adopted without a vote** is an adoption. **Taken note of** is not an
> adoption at all. The two appear in neighboring lines of the same records and are reported
> interchangeably by people in a hurry.
>
> *Source: the General Assembly's structure of six Main Committees on which all member states are
> entitled to be represented, and its practice on the treatment of committee reports in plenary.*

### 1 · *Whereas*, *by contrast*, *while*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP3-10-06%%

> **Fadi:** Your cable says the Assembly has adopted it.
> **Omar:** The Committee agreed it at twenty past eleven. There was no opposition at all.

> identical, the function is not.
> **Omar:** The plenary adopts nearly everything the Committee sends.
> **Fadi:** Nearly. **By contrast** with the Committee, where you were in the room, the plenary is a
> place where things occasionally happen that nobody in the room expected.
> **Omar:** So I write two sentences.
> **Fadi:** You write two sentences. And **in contrast to** the version you have just sent, the
> second one is the part a capital actually needs.

*What is the difference in use between *whereas* and *by contrast*?*

**1B  Underline the four contrast expressions in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Followed by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **whereas** | a clause, inside one sentence | A committee recommends, **whereas** a plenary decides. |
| **while** | a clause | **While** the membership is the same, the function is not. |
| **by contrast** | a new sentence | …**By contrast**, the plenary rarely negotiates. |
| **in contrast to** | a noun | **In contrast to** the Committee, the plenary… |
| **on the other hand** | a new sentence | …**On the other hand**, the text may be amended. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Whereas* joins two clauses in one sentence and is slightly formal; *by
> contrast* starts a new sentence and needs a comma after it; *in contrast to* takes a noun, not a
> clause. The commonest error in drafting is *in contrast to the Committee recommends*, which mixes
> two of the three.

3. What happens in one room by contrast with another?

### 2 · The two-sentence rule

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| sentence one: the stage reached | **The Committee has agreed a draft.** |
| sentence two: the stage not reached | **The Assembly has not adopted anything.** |
| optional third | The plenary is expected to take it up on Thursday. |
| never one sentence | ~~The Committee has agreed a draft, which the Assembly will adopt on Thursday.~~ |
| never a prediction as a fact | ~~The text has been adopted, subject to the plenary.~~ |

> **Grammar Note** — The reason for two sentences rather than one with a comma is practical: the
> second sentence survives summarizing, cutting and translation, and a subordinate clause does not.
> Everything you most need the reader to keep should be its own sentence with a full stop in front
> of it.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Reporting a stage as an outcome is the commonest error in multilateral drafting and the least
punished, because nine times out of ten the stage becomes the outcome and nobody is any the wiser.
That is exactly what makes it dangerous: the habit is reinforced every time it works. The officers
who never make the mistake tend to have made it once, early, on a text that changed. There is also a
reason the two-sentence form is better than a careful single sentence. A subordinate clause —
*which the Assembly is expected to adopt on Thursday* — is the first thing cut when your cable is
summarized for somebody more senior, and the summary is what gets read. A second sentence with a
full stop in front of it is much harder to lose, and the harder it is to lose, the better it is
doing its job.

**Discuss:** What is the commonest reporting error in your own ministry? What makes it survive?
