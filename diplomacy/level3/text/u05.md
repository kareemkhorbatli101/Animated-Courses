### 1 · Relative clauses, including reduced

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP3-05-06%%

> **Rana:** My paragraph is sixty words and it has four sentences in it.
> **Omar:** Read it to me.
> **Rana:** "The Council has forty-seven members. They are states. They are elected by the General
> Assembly. The election is by secret ballot."
> **Omar:** Four sentences, four full stops, and the reader has to hold all of it. Try one.
> **Rana:** "The Council has forty-seven members, which are states elected by the General Assembly
> by secret ballot."
> **Omar:** Twenty words, and nothing lost. Now the one that will be marked: what are the commas
> doing?
> **Rana:** The clause is non-defining. All forty-seven are states.
> **Omar:** Right. Take the commas out and you have said that the Council has forty-seven members of
> which some are states, which is not true and would be a serious error in this field.

*What exactly changes if the commas are removed?*

**1B  Underline the relative clause in Rana's second version.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **defining** — no commas | members **that have ratified** may vote |
| **non-defining** — commas | the members, **which are states**, are elected individually |
| **whose** | a body **whose** members are states |
| **reduced** — participle | the resolution, **adopted in 2006**, created the review |
| **reduced** — noun phrase | the Council's 47 members, **all states** |
| **which** for a whole clause | It was adopted without a vote, **which** is unusual |

> **Grammar Note** — A reduced clause is the shortest honest way to carry a fact: *the resolution,
> adopted in 2006* instead of *which was adopted in 2006*. Use it in drafting and it buys you four
> or five words a sentence, which over four hundred words is a whole paragraph.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Getting the kind of body right is not pedantry; it is the first thing a well-informed reader checks,
and the thing an ill-informed reader is most grateful to be told. There is also a reason the error
runs in one particular direction. *Panel of experts* sounds impartial and *forty-seven elected member
states* sounds political, so the first phrase makes a report sound stronger — which is precisely why
a ministry's own drafts should never use it loosely, in either direction. Describing a political body
as expert inflates its output; describing an expert body as political deflates output that deserves
better. The discipline is the same in both cases: write what the founding instrument says, in six
words, in the first mention, and let the reader draw their own conclusions from an accurate
description.

**Discuss:** In your own ministry's documents, which body is described most loosely? Does the
looseness help or hurt?

### 3 · Write

Write **120 to 150 words**: a paragraph about a body and something it has done.

**Open with a reduced clause carrying the founding instrument, its date, the number of members and
what they are. Say what the body is and is not. Give one figure, with its source. End with what the
paragraph does not assert.**

**Read your paragraph aloud to your partner first.** Reading it out is how you hear a category error.
Then check your own against the list, and swap.
