A nine-member committee reports on whether the papers presented by a delegation are in order. By
Friday that report will be described somewhere as a decision about who governs a country. It is not,
it cannot be, and explaining why is one of the hardest short tasks in this book.

### 1 · Modals of deduction

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP3-03-06%%

> **Rana:** The report was adopted without a vote. So everybody agreed.
> **Omar:** Not necessarily. It **might have been** adopted without a vote because everybody agreed,
> or because nobody wanted a vote. Those are different things.
> **Rana:** Somebody **must have** known which.
> **Omar:** Somebody in the room **must have**. We were not in the room.
> **Rana:** The press release says the Assembly recognized the government.
> **Omar:** Then the press release is wrong. It **can't have** recognized anything — recognition is
> not a decision that body takes.
> **Rana:** Could it be a translation problem?
> **Omar:** It **could well be**. It usually is. But I am not going to write *probably a translation
> problem* in a cable, because I do not know.

*Which of Omar's modals are about what is possible, and which about what is impossible?*

**1B  Underline every modal of deduction in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | How certain |
|---|---|---|
| **must have** + participle | Somebody **must have** known. | the only explanation that fits |
| **can't have** / **couldn't have** | It **can't have** recognized anything. | impossible |
| **might have** / **may have** | It **might have been** adopted without a vote because… | possible |
| **could well be** | It **could well be** a translation problem. | likely, not certain |
| **must be** / **can't be** *(present)* | That **can't be** right. |
| **is likely to have** | The delegation **is likely to have** been instructed. | careful, formal |

> **Grammar Note** — *Must have* is not about obligation here; it is the strongest form of guessing.
> And the negative of *must have* is *can't have*, not *mustn't have*: *it can't have been adopted
> yesterday, because the session had not opened*. In drafting, *must have* is the most dangerous of
> these, because it reads like a finding.

### 2 · Keeping two decisions apart

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **settles** + noun | The credentials decision **settles participation**. |
| **is taken by** | Recognition **is taken by** each state, individually. |
| **is not an act of** | A credentials decision **is not an act of** recognition. |
| **may be connected politically** | The two **may be connected politically**. |
| **a different statement from** | That is **a different statement from** saying one is the other. |

> **Grammar Note** — *They are connected* and *they are the same* are a single word apart in careless
> drafting. Write *connected politically* and then write *they are not the same decision* in its own

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Credentials questions are where procedure and politics touch most directly, and where a careless
sentence from a junior officer can be quoted for years. Three things are worth holding on to. First,
the distinction is real and is not a diplomatic evasion: participation in a body and recognition by
states genuinely are separate decisions, taken by different actors under different procedures.
Second, saying so is not the same as denying that they are connected; in a contested case the
connection is what everybody is actually arguing about, and pretending otherwise fools nobody.
Third, the question *what is your ministry's position* is almost never yours to answer, and the
honest referral is stronger than the best improvisation. Officers who learn that early are the ones
still trusted with contested files ten years later.

**Discuss:** In your own ministry, which questions are above your level to answer? How do you refer
them without sounding evasive?
