### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When you are told that something is "in the Charter", open the Charter. Roughly
> half the rules people attribute to a founding text are practices that have grown up alongside it,
> and the difference is the first thing a lawyer will ask you about.

%%FIG i01-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a boxed quotation of one Article in larger type, a short table giving the composition of the body, a paragraph headed the practice beside the text, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · internal note 1
> *Europe Department · voting in the Security Council · page 1 of 1*
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Members** | **15** |
> | **Permanent members** | **5** |
> | **Elected members** | **10**, for two-year terms |
> | **Votes needed, procedural question** | **9** affirmative votes |
> | **Votes needed, all other questions** | **9** affirmative votes, **including the concurring votes of the permanent members** |
>
> **Article 27(3)** of the Charter, in its own words, requires nine affirmative votes **including the
> concurring votes of the permanent members** for decisions on all matters other than procedure.
>
> **Now the practice beside the text.** Read strictly, *including the concurring votes of the
> permanent members* would mean that an abstention by a permanent member blocks a decision. **That
> is not how the Article has been applied.** For decades a **voluntary abstention** by a permanent
> member has been treated as **not preventing** the adoption of a resolution, and the Court has
> reasoned along the same lines in an advisory opinion. The Article has **not** been amended.
>
> So two sentences, and you need both:
>
> 1. **The text** requires the concurring votes of the permanent members.
> 2. **The practice** treats an abstention as not a negative vote, and that practice is settled.
>
> **What a desk officer must never write.** *Under Article 27, an abstention is not a veto.* The
> Article does not say that. **The practice says it**, and a draft that attributes a practice to a
> text will be marked by Legal Affairs, correctly.
>
> *Source: the Charter of the United Nations, Article 27, and the published composition of the
> Security Council. The reading of a voluntary abstention is a matter of long practice and of
> reasoning in an advisory opinion, not of the Article's wording.*

<sub>**a concurring vote** — a vote in favor · **a practice** — a settled way of doing something,

### 1 · The past perfect

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP3-01-06%%

> **Nadia:** Your draft says the resolution was adopted after a short debate.
> **Omar:** It was. Eleven minutes.
> **Nadia:** And how long **had** the text **been** in circulation before that?
> **Omar:** Nine days.
> **Nadia:** Had the two paragraphs **been deleted** before the meeting or during it?
> **Omar:** Before. They **had already gone** by Tuesday evening.
> **Nadia:** Then your draft describes the eleven minutes and leaves out the nine days. Anybody
> reading it in a year will think the Council decided something quickly.
> **Omar:** It did decide quickly.
> **Nadia:** It **voted** quickly. It **had decided** days earlier, in a side room, and that is a
> different sentence.

*What is the difference between what Nadia means by *voted* and what she means by *had decided*?*

**1B  Underline every past perfect in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **past simple** — the event you are reporting | The resolution **was adopted** at 11:40. |
| **past perfect** — what happened before it | The text **had been** in circulation for nine days. |
| **had already** | Two paragraphs **had already been** deleted. |
| **by** + a time | **By** Tuesday evening they **had gone**. |
| **before** / **after** | **Before** the meeting began, four states **had agreed** to co-sponsor. |
| passive past perfect | The text **had been circulated** on the fourth. |

> **Grammar Note** — The past perfect exists to put two past events in order when the order is not
> obvious. If the order is already clear from *before*, *after* or the sequence of sentences, the
> past simple is enough and the past perfect starts to sound heavy. Use it where it earns its place:
> the thing that had already happened when your story starts.

### 2 · Separating a text from a practice

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **The text** + verb | **The Article requires** the concurring votes of the permanent members. |
| **The practice** + verb | **The practice treats** an abstention as not a negative vote. |
| **has been treated as** | A voluntary abstention **has been treated as** not preventing adoption. |
| **the Article has not been amended** | and that sentence has to be in the same paragraph |
| **not under** + instrument | That is the practice, **not under** the Article itself. |

> **Grammar Note** — Put the subject at the front and let it carry the weight: *the Article
> requires*, *the practice treats*. The passive — *it is understood that an abstention…* — hides
> exactly the thing the reader needs, which is whose statement it is.

### 3 · Reporting what did not happen

**3A  Study the chart.**

| Risky | Safe |
|---|---|
| It passed unanimously. | Fourteen in favor, none against, **one abstention**. Not unanimous. |
| A permanent member blocked it. | A permanent member **abstained**. Nothing was vetoed. |
| Under Article 27, an abstention is not a veto. | The Article requires concurring votes. **The practice** treats an abstention as not a negative vote. |
| There was no opposition. | **No negative votes were cast.** Three delegations gave explanations of vote. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Nobody opposed it* and *no negative votes were cast* describe the same five
> minutes and are different claims. The second is a record of what happened. The first is a claim
> about what delegations think, and you do not know what delegations think.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The gap between a text and the practice that grew up beside it is not a failure of drafting; it is
how institutions survive without constant amendment. Every ministry has its own version — the
clearance that is always sought and is nowhere required, the deadline everybody meets that exists in
no instruction. Three habits make a desk officer reliable about this. Say *the text* and *the
practice* in separate sentences, so that a reader can see the join. Never attribute a practice to an
article, because the moment somebody opens the article your whole paragraph becomes suspect. And
remember that practices are not weaker than texts: a reading settled over decades, accepted in
conduct by everybody concerned, is a very strong thing. The point is not to rank them. It is to say
which one you are relying on.
