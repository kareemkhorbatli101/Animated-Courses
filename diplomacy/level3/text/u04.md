%%FIG i04-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a short table giving the composition and terms of the Court, a boxed quotation of one article in larger type, three bullet lines headed the three routes to jurisdiction, a paragraph headed influence and binding force, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · internal note 4
> *Legal Affairs and Europe Department · reading a judgment · page 1 of 1*
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Judges** | **15** |
> | **Term** | **nine years** |
> | **Jurisdiction** | rests on the **consent** of the states concerned |
>
> **Article 59 of the Statute**, in its own words:
>
> > The decision of the Court has **no binding force except between the parties and in respect of
> > that particular case**.
>
> **The three ordinary routes to jurisdiction in a contentious case**
> — a **special agreement** between the states to submit that dispute;
> — a **clause in a treaty** by which the parties have accepted jurisdiction for disputes under it;
> — a **declaration** lodged in advance accepting jurisdiction in relation to other states accepting
> the same obligation.
>
> No route, no case. A dispute does not reach the Court because it is serious; it reaches the Court
> because both states, in one of these ways, have agreed that it may.
>
> **Influence and binding force are not the same thing.** A judgment binds two states in one case.
> Its **reasoning** is read by every ministry in the world, cited in later pleadings, and relied on
> by states that were not parties. That influence is real and it is not binding force, and the two
> are routinely merged in a single sentence beginning *the Court has ruled that states must*.
>
> **What a desk officer writes instead.** *The Court held, in a case between A and B, that…* And, if
> the reasoning matters beyond the case: *the reasoning is likely to be relied on in comparable
> disputes, although it binds only the parties.*
>
> *Source: the Statute of the International Court of Justice, Articles 36 and 59, and the published
> composition of the Court. An advisory opinion is a separate procedure from a contentious case and
> is not covered by this note.*

### 1 · The passive with reporting verbs

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD DIP3-04-06%%

> **Omar:** Three people have told me what the judgment means and none of them has read it.
> **Layla:** Then write what the Court said, and attribute the rest.
> **Omar:** Attribute it to whom? One of them asked me not to use their name.
> **Layla:** Then use the form that attributes without naming. The reasoning **is said to be**
> unusually wide. The parties **are understood to have** settled the remaining point. The analysis
> **is expected to be** cited in the Marovia case.
> **Omar:** That sounds evasive.
> **Layla:** It is the opposite of evasive. It tells the reader that somebody said it and that it is
> not the Court's holding and not your opinion. What is evasive is writing it as a flat fact and
> letting the reader assume you got it from the judgment.
> **Omar:** And if the Director asks who said it?
> **Layla:** Then you tell him, because he is entitled to know and a newspaper is not. The form
> protects the source in the document, not from your own chain of command.

*Which of Layla's three sentences is about the past, and how do you know?*

**1B  Underline the three passive reporting forms in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **is said to be** | The reasoning **is said to be** wide. *(now)* |
| **is said to have been** | The vote **is said to have been** close. *(earlier)* |
| **is understood to be** | Talks **are understood to be** continuing. |
| **is expected to be** | The analysis **is expected to be** cited. |
| **it is reported that** | **It is reported that** the parties have settled. |
| **reportedly** / **apparently** | The parties have **reportedly** settled. |

> **Grammar Note** — Two patterns do the same work. *It is reported that* + clause is heavier and
> very formal. *X is reported to have* + participle is lighter and reads better in a cable. Both say
> *somebody told me this and I am not vouching for it*, and both are infinitely better than a flat
> assertion of something you have not verified.

### 3 · Naming a source, or protecting one

**3A  Study the chart.**

| Risky | Safe |
|---|---|
| The Court has ruled that states must give notice. | The Court held, in a case between A and B, that A was required to give notice. |
| The reasoning is unusually wide. | The reasoning is said to be unusually wide. |
| They settled on Tuesday. | The parties are understood to have settled on Tuesday. |
| A source at the Court told me… | *(do not write this at all)* The point is understood to be… |

> **Grammar Note** — Never describe your source's position in a document, even vaguely. *A source
> close to the delegation* identifies a person to anybody who knows how many people are close to
> that delegation. Attribute the statement, not the speaker.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Attribution is the most under-taught skill in official writing and the one that separates a cable
that can be relied on from one that cannot. The temptation is always to simplify: three telephone
calls, one paragraph, and the differences between what you read, what you were told and what you
concluded disappear. Six months later somebody acts on the paragraph, and nobody can reconstruct
which part came from where. The habits are small. Use a reporting form for anything you have not
verified yourself, even when you are confident. Attribute the statement and never the speaker, in
any document that circulates. And keep in mind the one distinction that protects sources properly: a
name kept out of a document is professional, and a name withheld from the person who must weigh the
information is not protection but concealment.

**Discuss:** In your own writing, how do you mark the difference between what you read and what you
were told?
