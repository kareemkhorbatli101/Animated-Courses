> ## Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Expatriates · internal note 2
> *Europe Department · voting thresholds in the General Assembly · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Article 18 of the Charter**, in its own words: decisions on **important questions** are made by a
> **two-thirds majority of the members present and voting**. Other questions are decided by a
> **majority of the members present and voting**.
>
> The decisive words are **present and voting**.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | A state that is **absent** | is not present, and is **not counted** |
> | A state that **abstains** | is present but is **not voting**, and is **not counted** |
> | A state that votes **in favor** or **against** | is present and voting, and **is counted** |
>
> **The same vote, two bases.**
>
> | | Calculated on the membership | Calculated on those present and voting |
> |---|---|---|
> | Base | **193** | **150** *(after 30 abstentions and 13 absences)* |
> | Two-thirds threshold | **129** | **100** |
> | Votes in favor | 110 | 110 |
> | Result | **fails** | **carries** |
>
> The arithmetic in the second column is the arithmetic the rule requires. The arithmetic in the
> first column is the one a desk officer produces at home the night before, and it produces the
> wrong answer about the same vote.
>
> **What moves and what does not.** The fraction does not move: it is two-thirds. The **base** moves,
> and it moves during the vote itself, as delegations abstain or leave. **This is why a prediction of
> the outcome is a prediction about two unknowns**, not one: how many will vote in favor, and how
> many will vote at all.
>
> *Source: the Charter of the United Nations, Article 18. The worked example is invented for
> teaching; the rule and its wording are not.*

*Which of Omar's verbs describe finished results, and which describe work still going on?*

**1B  Underline every present perfect continuous in 1A and circle the two simple present perfects.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **have been + -ing** | I **have been working** on it since Tuesday. | still going on |
| with **since** / **for** | **for** three days · **since** Tuesday | the period |
| **have + participle** | I **have counted** a hundred and ten. | a finished result |
| the pair together | I **have been counting** all week and I **have reached** 110. | process, then result |
| repeated change | Five **have been changing** their position. | again and again |
| state verbs stay simple | I **have known** since Tuesday. *(not* have been knowing *)* |

> **Grammar Note** — The continuous puts the emphasis on the activity and the simple puts it on the
> result. *I have been drafting* explains why you have nothing to show. *I have drafted* means there
> is something on the desk. Choosing wrongly is how a desk officer accidentally promises a document
> that does not exist.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Senior readers want one number, and the instinct to give them one is professional rather than
cowardly: a note full of ranges is hard to act on. What separates a good desk officer from a
cautious one is not refusing the number but knowing which number is load-bearing. Here it is the
base, not the fraction, and the base is the one thing a note usually leaves out. There is also a
quieter point about authorship. If you write *likely to carry* and somebody senior writes *will
carry* over the top of it, the file shows both sentences, and in three months the file is the only
witness. That is not a reason to be obstructive. It is a reason to put your careful sentence in
writing early, in your own words, and then to let people do their jobs.

**Discuss:** When somebody senior asks you for a number you cannot give, what do you actually say?
