### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who writes business cases in your practice?
- Where in the document does the recommendation appear?
- What assumptions do your figures rest on?

A business case is a document written by somebody who has already decided, for people who have not, and almost every weak case in dentistry gets the order of its two halves wrong.

The recommendation goes in the first line, with the money and the date in it. I recommend that the partners approve a fourth surgery at a capital cost of ninety thousand, with a decision by the end of July.

Everything after that is assurance: the figures, their sources, the assumptions underneath them, the sensitivities you have tested and the ones you have not.

The commonest failure in the assurance half is not arithmetic but silence about assumptions. A payback of three years rests on a list of things being true, and a reader who has to guess that list will assume the worst of it.

And cost doing nothing on the same arithmetic, because the option nobody prices is the option that happens by default, and in a partnership it happens by default rather often.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A business case drafted with the recommendation in the first line**

**Figure 1.5** — Six parts, and the fourth is where most cases are lost.

A business case drafted with the recommendation in the first line is the difference between a decision in July and a paper that is noted and brought back in October.

Give the system the recommendation you have already reached, every figure with its source, the assumptions each figure rests on, and what the practice would do with the money otherwise.

Ask it to put the recommendation, the sum and the decision date in the first line, because a reader who does not yet know what is being asked spends the first three pages inventing a question of their own.

Ask it to list every assumption as a named item and to mark the ones you have not tested, since a buried assumption discovered by a partner at the table costs you the whole paper rather than that one line.

Ask it to price the option of taking no action on exactly the arithmetic used for the others, so that the course which would otherwise happen by inertia is in the table with a number against it.

What it must not do is decide the recommendation or soften a figure that looks unhelpful. A case recommending what a machine preferred has nobody’s judgement in it, and a number improved for presentation is a number somebody will recalculate in front of you.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A meeting agenda built around decisions rather than updates**

**Figure 2.5** — Six tests, and an item that fails the first one becomes a note.

A meeting agenda built around decisions rather than updates is the half-hour on a Tuesday that decides whether Thursday costs the practice a dozen appointments or buys it four decisions.

Give the system the list of matters people want raised, the papers that exist for each, and how long the meeting actually has.

Ask it to rephrase every item as a question beginning whether, with a decision date against it, because an item named as a noun is an invitation to talk about a subject rather than to settle it.

Ask it to move every item with no decision in it into a note to be circulated beforehand, since reading a report aloud to six clinicians is the most expensive method of distributing a document available to a practice.

Ask it to allot minutes to each item and to put the total against the length of the meeting, so that the arithmetic of over-running is visible on Tuesday rather than discovered at ten past eight on Thursday.

What it must not do is decide which questions matter, or draft the resolutions. What a partnership should settle this month is a judgement about the practice, and a resolution written in advance is a meeting whose outcome was arranged before anybody arrived.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which of your practice’s policies says what it will not do?
- Who decides when an exception is made?

Almost every policy a dental practice writes describes what the practice provides, and almost none of them describes what it does not, which is why so many of them are useless at four o’clock on a Friday.

A policy earns its existence at the moment somebody is being refused. If it cannot be used to refuse, it is a description of good intentions and it leaves the refusing to whoever is on the desk.

So write the refusal in the policy: this practice will not provide treatment on credit. And then write the exception, narrowly, with a name: except where the principal has authorised an instalment arrangement in writing.

An exception without a name in it is not an exception; it is a gap, and a gap in a policy about money is filled four times a week by whoever finds it hardest to say no.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Has your practice ever had a written question from a regulator?
- Who drafted the answer, and who signed it?
- How much of the answer was not asked for?

A written question from a regulator is a narrow instrument, and almost everybody who receives one answers a wider question than the one in front of them.

The reason is human. You know your practice, you can see the misunderstanding behind the question, and you want to supply the context that makes the answer make sense.

The context opens lines of enquiry that nobody had. A practice asked about consent for extractions explains its general approach to communication, mentions an undocumented habit, and spends four months answering questions about that habit instead.

So read the question twice and write down its exact scope before drafting anything: which procedures, which period, which patients, which documents. Then answer in numbered paragraphs matching the question’s own numbering.

And where a deadline cannot be met, ask for an extension before it passes, in writing, with the reason. A late answer with no request is a second matter, and the second matter is about your practice rather than about consent.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A regulator’s question answered within its exact scope**

**Figure 5.5** — Six tests, and the fifth one is a count that must be zero.

A regulator’s question answered within its exact scope is a letter whose greatest virtue is everything it leaves out.

Give the system the question as received, the documents it concerns, and the record of the matters it asks about, and nothing else from the practice’s files.

Ask it to state the exact scope first, in terms of procedures, period, patients and documents, because a scope that has not been written down is a scope that will quietly widen while the answer is being drafted.

Ask it to answer in paragraphs numbered to match the question and to flag any sentence that goes beyond what was asked, since a generous paragraph is how a four-paragraph enquiry becomes a four-month one.

Ask it to check every factual sentence against a named document and to mark any that rest on recollection, because a paraphrase in a letter to a regulator is a sentence that will be compared with the record.

What it must not do is decide what to disclose, settle whether something is within the scope, or advise you on your position. Those are judgements for the registrant and for whoever advises them, and a machine that answers a regulator has nobody’s name on the letter.

Where an enquiry may affect a registrant personally, independent advice is normally obtained.

A letter explaining a price rise is read by people who are being asked to pay more, which means every evasion in it is noticed and remembered.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Has your practice ever consulted patients or staff on a change?
- Was the decision already taken?
- Did anybody say so?

Most consultations are dishonest, not because the people running them intend to deceive anybody, but because the decision has already been taken and nobody is willing to write that down.

A patient reading a consultation document can tell within a paragraph. The language gives it away: the proposal is described in the present tense, the alternatives are listed in descending order of plausibility, and the last of them is obviously absurd.

The test of an honest consultation is whether you can name, in the document, the thing a response could say that would change the proposal. If you cannot name it, you are informing people rather than consulting them, and you should say so.

The second test is whether the alternatives are ones somebody might choose. An alternative included to be rejected is an insult that a reader notices and resents for longer than the closure itself.

And set a deadline, say how responses will be counted and by whom, and say when the decision will be published. A consultation with no deadline is a decision waiting for the objections to stop arriving.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A consultation document drafted with alternatives that are real**

**Figure 7.5** — Six parts, and the fifth is the one that makes the fourth true.

A consultation document drafted with alternatives that are real is the difference between a practice that learns something and a practice that collects objections to a decision it has already taken.

Give the system the proposal, the figures behind it, the alternatives you have genuinely examined with the cost of each, and the kinds of response that could and could not alter the outcome.

Ask it to state the proposal in the conditional throughout, because a proposal written in the future tense is a decision, and a reader identifies the difference in the first paragraph.

Ask it to remove any alternative that nobody could reasonably choose, and to say instead that such an option was considered and discarded before the consultation began, with the reason.

Ask it to draft both lists: the evidence that would change the proposal and the representations that will not, since publishing the second is the only thing that makes the first believable.

What it must not do is decide the proposal, weigh the responses or tell you what the public will accept. Closing a service is a decision with partners’ names on it, and a consultation whose conclusion was drafted in advance is the thing this document exists to avoid.

Obligations to staff on changes to terms or on redundancy are separate and are matters for local advice.  
> Source: general regulatory overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many dentists will your practice need in five years?

A workforce plan is the only document in a dental practice that is about a period longer than anybody’s memory, and it is therefore the one most often written in the wrong grammatical mood.

Written in the future tense it becomes a forecast, and a forecast is wrong on the first unexpected resignation and is then abandoned, and the practice goes back to recruiting in a panic.

Written in conditionals it survives being wrong. If Dr Ferreira retires in 2029 as he currently intends, then the practice needs a third associate from the January. If he stays to 2031, then it does not.

Then separate what the practice controls from what it does not. A rota is controllable. A recruitment market is not, and a plan that treats the second like the first is a plan to be disappointed by.

And write the branch nobody writes: what the plan does if nobody applies. That paragraph is the whole value of the document, because the vacancy that cannot be filled is the one that actually happens.

Everything in these six books has been about writing: letters, consents, records, reports, claims, policies, plans and papers. Almost all of that writing can be helped by a system, and in these pages it has been.

So the last discipline in this series is the plainest. Use the systems for everything they are good at, and sign nothing you cannot explain in your own words to somebody who was not in the room.

**What no system can do for a dentist’s judgement**

**Figure 10.5** — Six pairs, and the right-hand column has a name on it.

What no system can do for a dentist’s judgement is the subject of this last unit, and the honest version of it is shorter than most people expect.

Give any of these systems the drafting, the sorting, the counting and the checking, because they are genuinely good at all four and a practice that refuses their help is wasting its clinicians’ evenings.

Ask them to produce the questions a record request does not answer, the columns a reply falls into, the gaps in an audit and the sentences in your own draft that will not survive being read aloud.

What they cannot do is be accountable, and accountability is not a feature that will arrive in a later version, because it means being available afterwards to be asked why, and to answer in a voice that can be held to the answer.

So the division is not between what a system is clever enough to do and what it is not. It is between what can be checked by somebody else and what can only be answered for by the person who did it.

Everything in these six books ends in the same place: a patient in a chair, a clinician who has read what the machine produced, and a signature that means I have understood this and I will answer for it. That signature is the whole of the profession.
