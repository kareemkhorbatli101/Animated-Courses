### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many empty hours were there last week?
- Who rings the short-notice list?
- What does an hour of your surgery cost?

A surgery costs the same whether somebody is in the chair or not. The nurse is paid, the room is heated, the rent is the same.

So the book has three states for every hour. Booked. Free. Or lost, which means it was booked and nobody came.

Lost is the expensive one. A free hour can be filled this morning. A lost hour is found at ten past nine, with nobody to put in it.

That is what a short-notice list is for. Six names, ten minutes of telephoning, and the hour is not lost after all.

And say what the patient did, not what they are. He did not come on the fourth and the eleventh. Not: he is unreliable.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the appointment book: is booked, did not come, is free**

- Use is booked for the state of an hour. Ten o’clock is booked for forty minutes.
- Use did not come for what happened. He did not come on the fourth or on the eleventh.
- Use is free for what you can sell. Eleven o’clock is free, and I have two names to ring.
- Avoid is plus a judgement. Not: he is unreliable. But: he did not come twice and did not telephone.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An appointment book checked for the hours nobody is using**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the fifth one is the one nobody notices.

An appointment book checked for the hours nobody is using is a short list of empty hours and what each one was.

Give the system last week’s book, the hours each surgery is open, and the slots held on purpose for emergencies.

Ask it to separate free from lost. A free hour is a telephone call; a lost hour is a conversation about reminders.

## Unit 2 · A Written Estimate and What It Covers

Money is the thing patients remember most clearly and dentists discuss worst.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An estimate drafted as what it covers and what it does not**

**Figure 2.5** — Six lines, and the second one prevents most of the arguments.

An estimate drafted as what it covers and what it does not is one page handed over before treatment starts.

Give the system the stages of the work, the figures for each, the charges that sit outside them. The points at which a patient can stop.

Ask it to write the not-covered line as its own paragraph rather than a footnote. A charge in small print is a charge somebody will dispute.

Ask it to name the conditions under which the figure would change. Plain words, and never that it may change if further work is required.

Ask it to say what is payable today and what is payable later, because those are two different figures and patients hear only one of them.

It must not set the figures. What the practice charges is a decision somebody with authority has taken, and an estimate that invents a number is worse than no estimate at all.

**A payment conversation drafted with the options stated**

**Figure 3.5** — Six parts, and the fourth one is the one most practices leave out.

**An induction list written for a dental nurse’s first day**

**Figure 7.5** — Six parts on one page, and the fourth one is the one usually missing.

An induction list written for a dental nurse’s first day is one page with four things on it for today.

Give the system the tasks of the role, the tasks that need a sign-off, the person responsible, and the day the first sign-off happens.

Ask it to keep today to four items. A list of forty is a list that teaches somebody to tick rather than to learn.

It must not decide what a nurse may do. Which tasks need training and a sign-off is set by local law and by the practice procedure. A list that gets that wrong is worse than no list.
