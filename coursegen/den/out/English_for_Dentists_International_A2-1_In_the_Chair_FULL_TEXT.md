### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: roles in a practice: who does, who may, who must not**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A list of who may do what in a practice, checked against the day list**

**Figure 1.5** — Six columns. One page. One week of questions answered.

A list of who may do what in a practice is one page. It has the task, the role, the name on today, and who you ask.

Give the system the task list, the roles in the practice and today’s day list. Ask for one line for each task.

Ask it to mark the tasks that need a second person in the surgery. That is a safety column, not a staffing one.

Ask it to mark the registration each role must hold. Then a new starter can see what is missing.

Ask it to leave a last column: who you ask when a task is not on the page at all.

It must not decide what a role may do. Local law and the practice procedure decide that. The list only puts the answer where somebody can read it.

> **DATA POINT · Roles** — Professional guidance commonly separates tasks a dentist must carry out personally from tasks a trained colleague may carry out, and commonly requires a second person to be present for some treatment. Which tasks fall where, and what registration each role needs, are set by local law and by the practice’s own written procedures.  
> Source: general professional guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Written procedures** — Practice inspection regimes commonly expect a written statement of who may carry out which tasks, together with evidence of training and registration for each person named. What that statement must contain is set by local law.  
> Source: general regulatory overviews, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · Seating a Patient and Starting Well

*CEFR A2 · Grammar: seating a patient: would you, are you, may I · AI: a greeting and seating script drafted for a first visit*

**Figure 2.0** — THE CHAIR GOES BACK WHEN THE PATIENT IS READY, NOT WHEN YOU ARE.: Two minutes at the start that save ten minutes later.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you check a patient’s name where you work?
- Do you introduce your nurse by name?
- What do you ask before you put the chair back?

The first two minutes of an appointment decide the next forty. Everything after that is easier or harder because of them.

So you check the name out loud. You say your own name, and your nurse’s name, and you wait for the patient to answer.

A patient who is sitting down with a coat on their knees is not ready. A patient who cannot see you is not listening properly either.

The chair goes back when the patient is ready. You say what you are going to do first, and then you ask if that is all right.

And you ask what has changed since the last visit. Many patients will tell you something important in that first minute, and only in that first minute.

**A greeting and seating script drafted for a first visit**

**Figure 2.5** — Six steps, and the whole thing takes under two minutes.

A greeting and seating script drafted for a first visit is six short steps. It is not a performance. It is an order of events.

Give the system the information you must confirm, the names in the room and the question you ask about the last visit.

Ask it to put the identity check as an open question. The patient says the name; you do not read it out for them to agree with.

Ask it to put the permission for the chair after the plan, not before it. A patient who knows what is coming can answer properly.

Ask it to leave the last visit question open. What has changed finds more than is everything all right does.

It must not script the clinical part. What you look at, what you find and what you propose are yours, and they come after these six steps.

> Source: general clinical guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Open questions** — Communication literature commonly reports that open questions at the start of a consultation elicit more relevant information than closed ones, and that patients who are interrupted in the first minute disclose less overall.
