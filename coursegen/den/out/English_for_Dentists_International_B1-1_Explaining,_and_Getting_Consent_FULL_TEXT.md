## Unit 1 · Setting Out the Options So a Patient Can Choose

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: options a patient can choose between: either, or, and the difference · AI: treatment options laid out side by side with their differences*

**Figure 1.0** — FOUR OPTIONS IS NOT A CHOICE. IT IS A WAY OF NOT DECIDING.: Two columns on one page, and the patient holds the decision.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many options do you usually set out for a patient?
- Do they get the same facts about each one?
- What do you say when a patient asks which you would have?

A patient who is given four options has not been given a choice. They have been given a problem, and they will hand it back by asking what you would do.

So two options, set out side by side, with the same four facts about each: what it involves, how many visits, what it costs and how long it lasts.

The second option is almost always doing nothing, and that is a real option rather than a threat. It belongs in the left-hand column with the same four facts filled in.

Then you name the difference that matters to this patient. Three visits against one matters to somebody who works shifts. Twelve years against six matters to somebody who is thirty.

And you say which you recommend, and why, in one sentence. A dentist who refuses to recommend has left the whole weight of it with somebody who has been a patient for forty minutes.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: options a patient can choose between: either, or, and the difference**

- Use You can either, or for the two options. You can either have it in one longer appointment, or in three shorter ones.
- Use The difference between them is to name the one that matters. The difference between them is three visits against one.
- Use I would recommend, because for your view. I would recommend the second, because it should last about twice as long.
- Use Doing nothing is also an option, and to put the third column in. Doing nothing is also an option, and this is what it would mean.

> **DATA POINT · Options and consent** — Professional guidance on consent commonly requires that the reasonable options, including the option of no treatment, be explained with their benefits and risks, and that the patient be given the information a reasonable person in their position would want. > Source: general professional guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Number of options** — Decision literature commonly reports that people choose less well and defer more often as the number of options rises, and that presenting comparable information for each option improves the quality of the decision.

## Unit 2 · What Happens If Nothing Is Done

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: doing nothing: if you leave it, it may, it will probably · AI: the no-treatment option written out as a real option*

**Figure 2.0** — DOING NOTHING IS A DECISION. IT DESERVES THE SAME PAGE AS THE OTHERS.: One column, two timescales, and no adjectives anywhere in it.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you describe doing nothing to a patient?
- Is there a timescale in it?
- When do you review a decision not to treat?

**A consent record drafted as what the patient agreed to**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines in the notes, and the fifth is the one nobody writes.

A consent record drafted as what the patient agreed to is a short line in the notes rather than a form in a drawer.

**A refusal recorded without pressure and without a verdict**

**Figure 9.5** — Six lines, and not one of them says how you feel about it.

A refusal recorded without pressure and without a verdict is a short record of somebody exercising a right.

Give the system what you recommended, the consequences you explained, what the patient decided and the words they used if they gave a reason.
