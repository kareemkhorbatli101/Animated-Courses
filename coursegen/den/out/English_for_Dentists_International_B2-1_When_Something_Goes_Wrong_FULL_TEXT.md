### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How does your practice record a treatment that has failed?
- Who writes the account, and on what day?
- What happens to the things nobody yet knows?

When something has gone wrong in a surgery, the first document anybody writes is almost always written in the passive voice, and the passive voice is the problem rather than the style.

It was decided to proceed. The margin was found to be deficient. The patient was advised accordingly. Each of those sentences describes an event with nobody in it, and an event with nobody in it cannot be examined by anybody later.

Write it in the first person instead, with a verb that has you as its subject. I decided to proceed at twenty past three, having looked at the radiograph taken that morning.

Then separate the facts you can establish from the things that are not yet known, and say plainly which is which, because the temptation under pressure is to let a reasonable inference stand quietly in the place of a fact.

And write it the same day. An account written after a meeting has been shaped by the meeting, and everybody who reads it afterwards will be able to tell.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A failure account sorted into facts, actions and what is not yet known**

**Figure 1.5** — Four columns, and the fourth is the one that protects you.

A failure account sorted into facts, actions and what is not yet known is the discipline that keeps an account truthful while the person writing it is frightened.

Give the system your own narrative as you would tell it to a colleague, the times you can establish from the record, and the documents that exist for that appointment.

Ask it to place every statement in one of four columns, and to move anything carrying almost certainly, clearly, obviously or evidently out of the facts column and into the inferences.

Ask it to rewrite every passive construction into the first person, naming you as the subject, because an account in which nobody acts is an account nobody can defend and nobody believes.

Ask it to list the gaps in the sequence, the periods for which no document and no recollection exists, so that a gap is declared by you rather than discovered by somebody else.

What it must not do is decide whether what you did was reasonable, or soften a sentence to make it read better. Judgement of your clinical decision belongs to people who will examine the whole record, and a sentence improved for comfort is a sentence that will be read aloud.

> **DATA POINT · Recording an adverse event** — Professional guidance commonly expects that an adverse event is recorded promptly, factually and in a way that identifies who did what, and that the record distinguishes what is established from what remains uncertain. What must be reported, to whom and within what period are set by local law and the practice’s own written procedure.  
> Source: general regulatory overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Candour** — Many jurisdictions impose a duty to be open with a patient when something has gone wrong, and professional guidance commonly expects an account that neither minimises nor speculates. The scope of any such duty, and the time within which it must be discharged, are set by local law.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who tells a patient when something has gone wrong in your practice?
- How soon?
- What do you say when they ask a question you cannot answer?

There is one structural mistake that ruins almost every conversation of this kind, and it is putting the explanation before the fact.

The patient hears four sentences of context, understands that something bad is coming, and spends the rest of the conversation waiting rather than listening.

So the fact goes first, in one short sentence, with no softening clause attached to the front of it. The crown I fitted on Tuesday has come away, and I need to tell you why that matters.

Then what it means for them, in the terms they actually live in: how long, how much, what they can eat, what they can expect, what they will have to come back for.

Then the next step with a date, and the date is what turns an apology into a plan. And when they ask something you cannot answer, say that you do not know and say when you will.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A disclosure conversation prepared as act, effect and next step**

**Figure 2.5** — Six parts, and the second is the one clinicians skip.

A disclosure conversation prepared as act, effect and next step is twenty minutes of preparation for a conversation the patient will remember for a year.

Give the system the fact as you would state it to a colleague, what you know and do not know about why, the practical consequences for this patient, and what the practice will and will not charge for.

Ask it to produce the opening sentence with no subordinate clause in front of the fact, because every word placed before the fact is a word the patient spends waiting instead of listening.

Ask it to translate the consequences into the terms this patient lives in: how many weeks, how many appointments, what they can eat, what they must avoid, and what it will cost them.

Ask it to list the questions this patient is most likely to ask, including the ones you cannot answer, so that for each unanswerable one you arrive with a date rather than a deflection.

What it must not do is write the apology or decide what you are responsible for. An apology drafted by a machine is audible as one, and what you did or failed to do is a matter for your own account and the record.

**An irreversible error written as what was done and what follows**

**Figure 3.5** — Six lines, and the second one has a full stop after it.

An irreversible error written as what was done and what follows is the one document in a dental career that has to be exactly right.

Give the system the site, the time, what you did, the minute you realised, whom you telephoned and what the practice will not charge for.

Ask it to keep what cannot be undone in a sentence of its own, and to delete any conjunction that joins the loss to the remedy, because but and however are how a loss gets quietly bought.

Ask it to describe any replacement as what it physically is rather than as a restoration of the position, since a patient told that an implant restores the tooth will discover otherwise and remember who told them.

Ask it to lay out the sequence after the realisation minute by minute, because the question anybody will ask in a year is what you did in the first twenty minutes.

What it must not do is draft the apology, decide whether somebody else was also at fault, or soften anything. An apology for this belongs to the person who caused it, and softening is the one thing the patient will never forgive.

## Unit 4 · Refusing to Treat, and Saying Why

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What have you refused to do for a patient?
- What reason did you give?
- What did you offer instead?

Refusing a treatment a patient has asked for is one of the few things in clinical practice that is both entirely ordinary and almost always handled badly.

The reason is that most refusals are delivered as judgements rather than as limits. You should not want this is a judgement. I am not able to do this well is a limit, and a limit is far easier to hear.

So three parts. I cannot do this. Because, in one clause that a patient could repeat to somebody else. And here is what I shall do instead, or where else you can go.

The third part is the one that gets left off when a clinician is tired or has already been argued with twice, and a refusal with no route attached is what turns a disagreement into a complaint.

And the sentence has to be repeatable, word for word, because a patient who argues will argue four times, and a refusal that changes its wording on the third attempt sounds like one that is negotiable.

A patient in pain at six o’clock is the point at which a dental practice either keeps its word or discovers that it has no word to keep.

The failure is almost never a refusal. It is an undertaking given to get off the telephone: we shall try to fit you in, somebody will ring you back, come in and we shall see what we can do.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An urgent-pain reply drafted with what is possible today**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines, and every one of them carries a clock.

An urgent-pain reply drafted with what is possible today is the paragraph that stops a practice promising at six o’clock what it cannot deliver by seven.

Give the system this evening’s remaining capacity, which clinician is still in the building and until when, the practice’s fixed triage questions, and the out-of-hours route that exists.

Ask it to produce a reply in two halves, what can be done today with a clock time against it and what cannot be done today named plainly, because the second half is what makes the first half believable.

Ask it to attach a person and an hour to every undertaking, and to delete the words try, hopefully, later and somebody, since each of those turns a promise into a thing the patient will measure you against and find missing.

Ask it to add the fallback in the patient’s own hands: if the telephone has not rung by the stated hour, telephone us again, which converts a broken promise into a second chance rather than a grievance.

What it must not do is triage. Whether this pain can wait until morning is a clinical judgement that belongs to a clinician who has asked the questions, and a reply drafted around capacity is not an assessment of anybody’s pain.

> **DATA POINT · Urgent care** — Professional guidance and most local arrangements expect that a practice makes arrangements for patients requiring urgent care during and outside its normal hours, and that patients are told what those arrangements are. What must be provided, within what period and by whom are set by local arrangements and the practice’s own written procedure. Clinical assessment and any treatment remain the responsibility of a named clinician.  
> Source: general regulatory overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Telephone advice** — Professional guidance commonly treats advice given to a patient by telephone as clinical advice, requiring that it be given by somebody competent to give it and recorded with the time, the advice and the identity of the caller. What may be delegated to non-clinical staff is set by local arrangements.

Answering a complaint about your own work is the one piece of writing in which your instincts are reliably wrong, and they are wrong in three particular directions.

The first instinct is to answer from memory, because you remember the appointment and you remember being careful. Memory is not evidence, and a letter built on it will contradict the notes somewhere in the fourth paragraph.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Have you ever had a concern about a colleague’s clinical work?
- What did you do with it?
- Did the colleague know you had raised it?

A concern about a colleague’s clinical work is the single hardest document a dentist ever has to write, and most of them are never written at all.

The reason is that people believe they must arrive at a conclusion before raising anything, which would mean investigating a colleague themselves, which is neither their job nor within their power.

So the document has three parts and no verdict. What I saw, with dates and places. What I did about it at the time. And what I am asking the person I am writing to actually to do.

Leave the conclusion out, and leave out the sentence that explains what kind of practitioner you think your colleague is, because the moment that sentence appears the document becomes about you and your opinion of somebody.

And tell the colleague you have raised it, the same day if you can. Not to seek permission, and not as a courtesy, but because the alternative is that they learn it from a letter in three weeks.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When was your practice last inspected?
- What could you produce within two minutes?

An inspection is not an examination of what a practice does. It is an examination of what a practice can demonstrate, which is a narrower and far less comfortable thing.

The commonest failure is answering a wider question than the one asked. An inspector asks how consent is recorded for extractions and receives eleven minutes about the practice’s general philosophy of patient communication.

The second commonest is describing a document instead of producing it. We do have a policy on that is heard as we cannot find our policy on that, and in most cases that is exactly what it means.

The third is the most expensive. A gap you declare is a finding about a system; a gap an inspector discovers is a finding about your honesty, and those two findings have very different consequences.

So answer exactly what was asked, produce the paper, and say plainly where the paper does not exist. Never explain what the practice intends to do instead of answering what it does.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who acknowledges a complaint in your practice, and within how long?
- Who decides what the complaint is about?
- What happens when the answer is going to be late?

The proportion of complaints that become formal cases is determined far more by the first week than by the clinical facts underneath them.

Three things happen or do not happen in that week. The complaint is acknowledged in writing within the period the procedure sets. Its scope is agreed with the complainant rather than assumed by the practice. And a timescale is given that the practice can actually meet.

The second of those is the one nobody does. A practice reads a letter, decides what the complaint is really about, investigates that, and answers it, and the complainant reads an answer to a question she did not ask.

So write back and say what you understand the complaint to cover, in a numbered list, and invite her to correct you. It costs four days and it is the cheapest four days in the whole process.

And if the answer is going to be late, send an interim letter with a new date in it before the first date passes. A missed deadline with no letter is a second complaint, and the second one is about you rather than about a crown.

**A complaint acknowledged with its scope and its timescale stated**

**Figure 9.5** — Six items in the first week, and the second one is the hinge.

A complaint acknowledged with its scope and its timescale stated is four days of work in the first week that decides whether this takes three weeks or eight months.

Give the system the complaint as written, the date it arrived, the periods your procedure sets, and the name of a clinician who had nothing to do with the treatment.

Ask it to extract every distinct matter the letter raises, including the ones raised in a single sentence, and to number them, because the matter mentioned once is often the matter the complaint is actually about.

Ask it to draft the acknowledgement with the numbered scope in it and an explicit invitation to correct the list, since a scope the practice assumed is how a thorough answer arrives addressed to the wrong question.

Ask it to propose a timescale from the evidence that has to be gathered rather than from the period the procedure allows, and to flag anything that cannot be obtained inside that period.

What it must not do is decide what the complaint is really about, investigate anything, or choose who answers it. Deciding that a complainant means something other than what she wrote is the error this whole letter exists to prevent.

> **DATA POINT · Complaint handling** — Most jurisdictions set a period within which a complaint must be acknowledged and a longer period for a substantive response, with provision for extending the latter by agreement. Professional guidance commonly expects the scope of a complaint to be clarified with the complainant. The periods, the routes of escalation and who may investigate are set by local law.  
> Source: general regulatory overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Extending time** — Where a substantive response cannot be sent within the period set, local arrangements commonly allow the period to be extended by agreement with the complainant, and commonly require that the complainant be kept informed. A missed period without notice is itself commonly treated as a failure of complaint handling.
