### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When did you last fail to follow a lecture?
- Where exactly did you lose it?
- Who did you ask?

You do not lose a whole lecture. You lose it at one point, and then everything after that point goes.

So find the point. I missed the second definition, at about minute eighteen.

Then find out whether the gap is yours or the room’s. Ask the person next to you.

If they have the same gap, it is a question for the seminar and the tutor will want it.

If only you have it, you can usually close it yourself: the slide, the textbook, or ten minutes of reading.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: a lecture you cannot follow: I missed, I have, I need**

- Use I missed with the thing and the point. I missed the second definition, at about minute eighteen.
- Use I have for what you do hold. I have the slide. I have the first example in my notes.
- Use I need for what is still missing. I need to know what the second definition means.
- Use did you too for checking the room. Did you lose it there too? Did anybody get the second definition?

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A lecture gap written down as what to ask and whom to ask**

**Figure 1.5** — Four lines, written in the ten minutes afterwards.

A gap you cannot describe cannot be closed by anybody, including you.

Give the system your notes from the lecture, the point at which they stop making sense, and what was on the screen there.

Ask it to write the gap as one sentence saying what you need, rather than as a description of being confused.

Ask it to list what you already have, because that is what tells a tutor where to start.

Ask it to suggest whom to ask: a classmate for a gap of detail, the tutor for a gap in the argument.

It cannot tell you whether the room shares the gap. That takes four seconds and the person sitting beside you, and no tool will ever do it.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Four seconds, or week six

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A department studied 1,180 reported comprehension gaps across one year of first-year lectures.

In 412 cases the student wrote the gap down the same day with the point at which it occurred. In 768 cases the gap was not written down at all.

Gaps closed within one week: 371 of 412 and 189 of 768. Gaps still open at the examination: 8 and 331. Gaps found to be shared by four or more students: 188 and 41 identified. Seminar time spent on shared gaps: 9 minutes a week and 1 minute. Students who told nobody about a gap: 19 and 494. In 62 cases a student reported that the same gap had cost them marks twice, in a test and then in an examination.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many hours do you read a week?
- Do you read with a question?
- When do you stop?

There are three speeds. Skim, read and read again. Most students only have one.

Skimming takes twenty minutes a chapter. You read headings and the first line of each paragraph.

Reading takes about two hours a chapter. You read every paragraph and you make one note a page.

Reading again takes thirty minutes and you only do it for the one chapter that matters most.

And always read with the seminar question beside you. Without a question you are turning pages, not reading.
