### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Have you failed anything?
- What were your options?
- Who told you what they were?

The first thing to establish about a failure is what actually failed, because almost nobody knows and the answer changes everything that follows.

You can fail one component and pass the module. You can pass every component and fail the module on an aggregate rule. You can fail a module and still progress, under a condonement provision that most students have never heard of.

So read the regulations for your own programme before you decide anything, and if that is forty minutes of difficult reading then it is the best-spent forty minutes of the term.

Then set the options out as options rather than as a single fate. A resit in the summer period, a repeat of the whole module in the following year, or in some cases a condoned failure that carries the credit forward without carrying the mark with it.

And say it in plain words to your tutor, in those words. I failed the second coursework component of this module, and the options appear to be these three. That sentence is much harder than the regulations and much more useful.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: a failed module: I failed, the options are, I have decided**

- State the failure in the past simple, naming what failed. I failed the second coursework component of this module.
- Set out the options with the options are and a list. The options are a resit in August, a repeat next year, or condonement.
- State the decision in the present perfect. I have decided to take the resit in August.
- Attribute every rule to the regulations with under. Under regulation nine, a resit mark is capped at the pass mark.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A resit plan built from what the failure actually was**

**Figure 1.5** — Four questions, forty minutes, and the decision becomes arithmetic.

A failure is the one moment in a degree when a student most needs to read a difficult document carefully and is least able to concentrate on anything at all. Is exactly why almost nobody does it and why the reading is worth forcing.

Give the system your transcript component by component with every mark on it, the module weightings used for your degree classification, the assessment regulations for your own programme, and the letter you received with the date it was sent.

Ask it to establish first what actually failed, which means which component, by how many marks against which pass mark, and whether the module as a whole has failed under the aggregate rule that applies to your programme.

Ask it to set out every option the regulations provide, including condonement and including taking no action, with the regulation number against each.

Ask it to compare a capped resit mark against an uncapped repeat using your own module weightings, so that the comparison is in your degree average and not in the abstract.

What it cannot do is tell you whether you can afford a year. That is a question about money, a visa, a family and a place to live, and it is the question on which this decision actually turns.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A mitigating-circumstances claim with its evidence attached**

**Figure 3.5** — Four parts, about three hundred words, and a panel decides in four minutes.

A mitigating-circumstances claim is one of the shortest documents in a degree and one of the most frequently written badly, because students write feelings where a panel needs dates.

Give the system your assessment timetable with dates, the dates of the illness, the evidence you hold with the date each document was issued, and the institution’s regulations on claiming.

Ask it to lay out the certified dates, list every assessment those dates overlap, and state for each one what you were unable to do.

Ask it to check the claim against the window, counting working days from the day after each assessment, and to tell you the last day on which you may claim.

Ask it to flag any assessment you have listed that the evidence does not actually cover, because a claim that overreaches its evidence weakens the part of it that is sound.

What it cannot do is produce the evidence. A claim without a document behind its dates rests entirely on a panel’s willingness to take your word, and some panels will and some will not.

The period and the evidence accepted are set out in the institution’s own regulations.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A hardship application written as figures rather than adjectives**

**Figure 4.5** — Four figures, two dates, and about a hundred and fifty words.

A hardship application is the one document in this book where the quality of the writing matters less than the presence of four numbers and two dates.

Give the system your account balance with its date, your income with the dates it arrives, every bill with its amount and due date, and the cuts you have already made.

Ask it to produce the shortfall as a single figure, name the date by which the money must be there, and list the cuts already made with their amounts.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Is attendance recorded where you study?

- What would a letter ask you?

An attendance letter is the first formal contact many students have with their institution, and it almost always arrives weeks after the week in which the real problem started.

The letter is asking three things: which classes you missed, why you missed them, and what will be different from now. It is not asking you to apologise.

So answer in three tenses. I was working a night shift on the fourth and the eleventh. I should have told my tutor in week three rather than in week seven. I will attend the Monday seminar and the Thursday lab.

The third tense is where students lose everything, because a promise to attend every session for the rest of the year is read as a promise, and a broken one is worse than a modest one kept.

And name the week you stopped going, because the reason you stopped is usually a different and more serious thing than the absences themselves, and nobody can help with a reason you do not state.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An attendance explanation that promises nothing you cannot do**

**Figure 5.5** — Four parts, about two hundred words, and one promise that survives February.

An attendance explanation is the only document in this book whose main risk is not that you will say too little but that you will promise far too much.

Give the system the letter with its dates, the register extract if you have one, your timetable, your paid working hours with their days, and anything else fixed in your week.

Ask it to produce the dates with what you were doing on each, the week attendance stopped with the reason, and what you should have done at the time.

Ask it to build the promise only from sessions that do not collide with your working hours or your other fixed commitments, and to name each session rather than summarising.

Ask it to flag any promise it has drafted that your own timetable contradicts, because a promise that collides with a shift is a letter you will be answering again in February.

What it cannot do is decide what you will actually do on a wet Monday in week nine. The test of this document is not whether it reads well but whether you are in the room.

**An interruption-of-study request stating what you are asking for**

An interruption request is the one document in this book where the main failure is that the request itself is missing, because the circumstances take up all the room.

Give the system the dates you need, the regulations on interruption, your accommodation contract with its end date, and the name of whoever provides any funding you receive.

A complaint about teaching is the most misunderstood document a student ever writes, because almost everybody writes about a person when the procedure can only act on an effect.

A complaints panel cannot make somebody a better lecturer and will not discuss anybody’s character with you. It can replace a missing seminar, release a marking scheme, or reschedule a lost laboratory session.

And choose your stage deliberately. The informal stage is faster, keeps a working relationship, and resolves most of what students complain about; the formal stage exists for what the informal one has already failed to fix.

**An appeal drafted against the stated grounds and nothing else**

**Figure 9.5** — Four parts, under three hundred words, and nothing outside the list.

An appeal is the one document in this book that should be drafted against a list, and the list is the first thing the drafting work needs to be given.

Give the system the procedure with its permitted grounds and its deadline, the decision you are appealing with its date, and every document you hold about what happened.

Ask it to identify which of the permitted grounds your facts could support, in the regulations’ own words, and to say plainly where none of them does.
