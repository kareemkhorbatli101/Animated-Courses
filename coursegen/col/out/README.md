# English for College Students — International Edition

A six-book course for international students studying through English, from
A2.1 to B2.2. Each book is ten units; each unit is a twelve-part lesson of
about 5,000 words with fifteen hand-built figures and a printed answer key.

**Strapline:** *The rules you are graded on are rarely the rules you were told.*

## Why an international edition

Most of the difficulty an international student meets in their first term is
not language. It is a set of procedures, expectations and unwritten rules that
nobody states aloud because everybody local absorbed them at sixteen. What a
seminar is *for*. How long an extension request should be. Which of your own
words count as yours. What a resit does to a degree. Who to ask, and in what
words, when you do not understand.

This course teaches those rules explicitly, in English, at the level at which
a student actually meets them — the registry email at A2, the seminar
disagreement at B1, the misconduct allegation at B2 — and it teaches them
without ever suggesting that a student who did not know them was deficient.

## The twelve-part unit

1. **Warm-up** — three questions, from the student's own experience
2. **Reading** — five sentences of field prose, calibrated to level
3. **Comprehension** — five exercise types, including a classify task
4. **Vocabulary** — twenty terms, each glossed in a working voice
5. **Grammar** — one point, four rules, a loose/precise contrast figure
6. **Dialogue** — two scenes; at least one ends on an open objection
7. **Speaking** — three paired tasks
8. **The AI capability** — one capability, with a six-line tasking model
9. **The boundary line** — what the capability cannot do, always stated
10. **The Sentence You Must Say When You Do Not Understand** — the series
    signature: a three-turn ladder, six hold-your-ground lines, and a redline
11. **The Decision Room** — a clinic on real figures, where a senior voice is
    wrong and a sceptic is right
12. **Brief Lab** — a six-line draft diagnosed against eight named faults, then
    rewritten as a tasking

## The two ladders

**Grammar** runs from *who is who in a department* at A2.1 to *authorship,
honesty and what cannot be delegated* at B2.2 — sixty distinct points, each
introduced once and never repeated.

**AI capability** runs from *a first email to a lecturer, checked before it is
sent* to *what no tool can do for your own thinking* — sixty distinct
capabilities, each with a stated boundary line. The series teaches students to
ask well for what a system can do, and ends by naming the four things that
cannot be delegated: which question is worth asking, what counts as evidence,
which of two defensible answers you believe, and whether a sentence is true.

---

## The eight faults

Every Brief Lab diagnoses exactly three fault types, two entries each, drawn
from a fixed set and introduced progressively by level:

unscoped set · unbounded quantity · unstated criterion · undefined done ·
ambiguous referent · missing condition · unstated exception · hidden assumption

Across the sixty labs each fault appears at least fourteen times.

## Standing rules

- No real institution, examination board, academic or software product is
  named anywhere in the series.
- No deficit framing. A student who did not know a rule was not told it.
- No procedure is presented as universal. Where practice differs between
  institutions, the text says so and tells the student to ask.
- Academic honesty is taught, not policed: the series explains what a rule is
  for before it explains what breaks it.

## The world

Calderbrook University, the Marden Institute of Technology, Alverrey College,
the Kestrel Business School, Thornfield Polytechnic, the Lysander Language
Centre, the Durnford Scholarship Trust, the Harrowfield Student Fund — all
invented, all consistent across six books.

Priya Raman, Tomasz Wrona) and six staff and classmates (Dr Helen Marsh,
