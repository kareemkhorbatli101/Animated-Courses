## Unit 1 · Making a Point That Can Be Answered

*CEFR B1.1 · Grammar: a point with a basis: I think, because, which means · AI: a seminar point drafted with its evidence attached*

**Figure 1.0** — A POINT NOBODY CAN DISAGREE WITH IS NOT A POINT.: Thanh spoke four times in one seminar and nobody replied to any of it, because none of it could be answered.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What was your last seminar point?
- Could anybody have disagreed with it?
- Did anybody reply?

A point that can be answered has three parts: a claim, a basis, and something that would change it.

The claim has to be something a reasonable person could deny. "Context matters" is not a claim, because nobody in the room thinks it does not.

"The second cause mattered more than the first" is a claim, because Lukas can sit up and say that it did not.

The basis is what you would point at if challenged: a page, a figure, a case that the reading describes.

And the third part is the one almost nobody says out loud: what evidence would make you abandon the claim. Say it, and the discussion has somewhere to go.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: a point with a basis: I think, because, which means**

- Draw the consequence with which means. Which means the first cause cannot explain the rise on its own.
- Add the condition with I would change my mind if. I would change my mind if an earlier record were found.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A seminar point drafted with its evidence attached**

**Figure 1.5** — Four parts, and the fourth one is what makes the hour useful.

A point without a basis is an opinion, and a seminar of opinions produces agreement and no learning whatever.

Give the system the seminar question, the reading you did with page numbers, and the claim you want to make.

Ask it to test whether the claim could be denied by a reasonable person, and to tell you plainly when it could not.

Ask it to attach a basis from the pages you actually read, naming the page rather than referring vaguely to the chapter.

Ask it to add the condition under which you would abandon the claim, because that single clause is what lets somebody argue with you.

It cannot tell whether your claim is true, and that is not the point. It can tell you whether your claim is the kind of thing that could be false, which is a different and more useful test.

> **DATA POINT · Seminar contribution** — Many institutions publish criteria for assessed seminar contribution, which commonly distinguish between asserting a view and supporting it with evidence from the set material. The criteria appear in the module handbook.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Evidence in discussion** — Many modules expect contributions to identify the material they rest on, by page or by source. What is expected is set out in the module handbook or the seminar guidance.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: so that something I say in a seminar has to be answered rather than agreed with. Scope: one point for one seminar question; it drafts the point and speaks nothing. Inputs: the seminar question, the reading I actually did with page numbers, the claim I want to make, and the strongest objection I already know of. Criteria: the claim is phrased so that a reasonable person in the room could deny it; the basis names a page, a figure or a case rather than a chapter or a feeling; the consequence is drawn in one clause beginning which means; the condition under which I would abandon the claim is stated explicitly; and nothing in the point rests on reading I did not do. Done: I can name one person in the seminar who would argue with the claim, and what they would say. Fallback: where my claim turns out to be something nobody could deny, I keep the basis and look for the contestable version of the same observation.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Agreed with, or answered

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A department recorded and coded 214 first and second-year seminars across one year, classifying every student contribution.

Of 3,860 contributions, 1,480 made a claim that could be denied and named the material it rested on. The remaining 2,380 asserted a view with no located basis, or stated something no participant disputed.

Contributions that drew a reply from another student: 1,118 of 1,480 and 341 of 2,380. Seminars reaching a stated disagreement that was then resolved from the text: 91 of 214. Seminars in which the tutor spoke for more than half the hour: 22 and 104. Students who could state the seminar’s conclusion afterwards: 78 per cent and 34 per cent. Contributions stating a condition for changing the speaker’s mind: 109 in total, across all 3,860.

Then name the difficulty, and attach it to the claim rather than to the person. The difficulty is that the second account cannot be dated the same way.

Then hand it back. How would you date the second one, given the gap in the record?

Reverse that order and you produce an attack with a compliment on the end, which is the one shape that nobody in a seminar ever recovers from.

**A disagreement drafted so that it is about the claim**

**Figure 2.5** — Four moves in fifteen seconds, and the order is the whole thing.

Being right is about half of a good disagreement. The other half is whether the person you were right about speaks again.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Have you disagreed with a set reading?
- Did you say so?
- What stopped you?

Nothing you are given to read is beyond disagreement, and most of it contains one step that will not hold.

But you cannot disagree with a chapter. You can only disagree with one step in it, and you have to name which.

The most useful move is to find an assumption the author never states. The author assumes the record is complete; this holds only if nothing was lost.

The second most useful is to find a case the argument does not cover. The argument works for the first two districts and says nothing about the third.

Both of those are disagreements you can support from the text itself, which is what makes them worth saying out loud.

- What was the last question you did not ask?

Most unasked questions are unasked because of their phrasing, not their content.

A question about yourself invites reassurance. "I am sorry, I do not really follow this" gets you a kinder version of the same explanation.

That version sounds informed because it is informed: you have located the gap precisely enough to point at it.

And it is almost always the question three other people in the room also had, which is the part nobody believes until they ask one.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A holding answer that is honest rather than empty**

**Figure 6.5** — Three parts, four seconds, and a day on the end.

Nobody is ever ready for every question, and the test is not whether you have the answer but what you do in the four seconds after you find that you do not.

Give the system the question you were asked, everything you actually do know that touches it, and the part you cannot answer.

Ask it to produce the part you can answer first, hedged where your knowledge is from memory rather than from a page.

Ask it to state the gap as the exact thing you do not know, rather than as a general admission of uncertainty.

Ask it to end with a day and a specific thing you will bring, and to produce nothing at all where there is no such day.

What it cannot do is keep the promise. A holding answer with a Thursday on it that nobody delivers costs more than having said nothing, because the room now knows which kind of student you are.
