### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is your dissertation about?
- Can you say it as a question?
- Could you answer that question by June?

The first and most expensive mistake in a long piece of work is to spend three months on a topic in the belief that a topic is a question, and most students make it.

A topic is an area, and migration and labour markets is a topic, whereas a question has an answer in it that could turn out either way and a topic has no answer in it at all.

Narrow it with two moves: replace whether with which, because whether questions usually have answers everybody can already guess, and then add under what conditions, which is where the actual work turns out to live.

So not whether remittances help households, but which household expenditures change when remittances arrive monthly rather than annually, in the four villages for which a usable survey already exists.

Then test the narrowed question against the two hard limits of the months you actually have and the data you can actually get your hands on, because a question that fails either of them is somebody else’s question rather than yours.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: narrowing a question: not whether, but which, under what conditions**

- Not whether sets aside the question whose answer is already obvious. Not whether remittances affect household spending at all.
- But which takes the variable you will actually examine. But which categories of household expenditure change, and in what direction.
- Under what conditions takes the limit that makes the question answerable. Under what conditions, meaning where remittances arrive monthly rather than once a year.
- In takes the scope of your data. In the four villages covered by the 2024 household survey.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A research question narrowed until it can be answered in the time**

**Figure 1.5** — Four tests, one line, and a year that does not go wrong in week nine.

Narrowing a question is the one piece of work in a dissertation where an hour at the start saves whole months later, and where a drafting tool is genuinely useful.

Give the system your topic in your own words, the number of weeks you have after writing and approvals are subtracted, and the exact datasets or sources you can obtain.

Ask it to produce five candidate questions, each on one line, each using which rather than whether and each carrying a stated condition.

Ask it to mark each candidate against your weeks and your data, and to say for each one what would have to be true for you to finish it.

Ask it to name, for every candidate, the finding that would count as an answer, because a question whose answer you cannot describe in advance is still a topic.

What it cannot do is know which question is worth asking in your field, which is what your supervisor is for. It can only make sure that the ones you bring are answerable.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · The question, or the topic

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A graduate school reviewed 416 completed dissertations over four years and sorted them by whether a single-line research question had been approved before the proposal was written.

152 dissertations had a one-line question with a named condition approved in advance, while 264 began from a topic and arrived at a question during the work or never.

**STAGE 5 · The Decision**

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say when a question is first approved.
- Say what the student must state with it.
- Say when it may be revised.
- Say what happens to the wider topic.

*Language bank for the decision*
- A one-line research question is approved provisionally in week three, before the proposal is written, and the open reading happens inside those three weeks.
- With the question the student states the condition that bounds it and the exact source or dataset the answer depends on, together with the week by which that source must be in hand.
- The question may be revised once, in writing, at week six, and is fixed after that, so that nobody discovers in week thirteen that the data does not exist.
- The wider topic is recorded as further work in the proposal itself, so that nothing a student cares about is lost and nothing unanswerable is attempted.

A proposal is not an essay about your interests. It is a promise, read by somebody who has read sixty of them, and the only question in that reader’s mind is whether you can keep it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A proposal drafted with its method and its limits stated**

**Figure 2.5** — Four parts, read in eleven minutes, and six of them spent on the method.

A proposal is the first document in a dissertation where a drafting tool earns its place, because its job is to make a plan legible rather than to have an idea.

Give the system your approved one-line question, the data or sources you hold, the steps you intend to take in order, your submission date and the word limit.

Ask it to write the method as numbered steps that a reader unfamiliar with your work could follow, and to mark any step whose tools or access you have not confirmed.

Ask it to produce the limits as plain sentences beginning this study does not, covering scope, population, period and the kind of claim you are not making.

Ask it to build the timetable backwards from the submission date in weeks, with writing and approvals subtracted first, and to say where the plan has no slack in it.

What it cannot do is want the project. A proposal that reads as a plan and feels like nobody’s idea is approved and then abandoned, and that is a worse outcome than a refusal.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A literature map that shows where your own question sits**

**Figure 3.5** — Four parts, three sentences, and a date you can stop on.

A literature map is the one place in a dissertation where a tool can genuinely organise what you already know, and the one place where letting it read for you destroys the work.

Give it the papers you have actually read with your own one-line note on each, your research question, and the databases, terms and years you searched.

Ask it to group your own notes into what most of them find, what few of them examine, and what none of them does, using only the notes you wrote.

Ask it to name, for the gap it identifies, which of your papers would have been written differently had your answer existed, so that you can test whether the gap is real.

Ask it to write out your search as something repeatable: the databases, the exact terms, the years, the languages and the exclusion rules, in the form your discipline reports them.

What it cannot do is read the papers. A map built from summaries you have not read is a map of a country you have never visited, and an examiner will ask you one question about a paper and find out.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An ethics application that states what the study will not do**

**Figure 4.5** — Four parts, written as negatives, and checkable in eight months.

An ethics application is the document where a drafting tool is most useful and most dangerous, because it is good at producing reassuring language and reassurance is exactly the failure mode.

Give it your method step by step, the people you will approach and how, the data each step produces, and your institution’s own ethics form with its categories.

Ask it to convert every step into refusals: what you will not collect, what you will not keep, whom you will not approach and through whom you will not approach them.

Ask it to attach a condition to each method in the form only where, and to list the consents separately by use rather than as a single permission.

Ask it to state what each participant is owed afterwards, with a withdrawal date, and to flag any step where a participant could be identified from what you intend to publish.

What it cannot do is notice the risk you have not thought of, which is what the committee is for. Strip out every sentence that reassures and keep only the ones that constrain you.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How did you choose your method?
- What did you not do instead?
- Could somebody repeat what you did?

A method section fails for one reason above all others, which is that it describes what happened rather than what was decided, usually in the passive voice and usually at length.

An examiner is not asking whether your method was the best available. They are asking whether you know that you chose it, what you chose it instead of, and why.

So write in three parts. I chose a comparison of means rather than a regression, because the two arrival groups are defined by the survey itself and no further controls were available.

A practical reason is a real reason and you should write it down. I chose the 2024 survey rather than collecting new data because fourteen weeks does not permit a new survey, and that sentence defends itself.

And write down the steps you did without noticing: the recodes, the cases you dropped, the one village whose figures looked wrong. The step you forgot is always the step the examiner finds.

**A method section written as choices and their reasons**

A method section is the one chapter where a drafting tool can turn your own working notes into something defensible, provided the notes exist and provided you wrote them at the time.

Give it your own log of what you did in order, including the recodes and the cases you dropped, your reason for each decision, and the alternatives you considered.

A limitations section is the only part of a dissertation whose quality is measured by what a reader cannot add to it, and almost every student writes one a reader can double in a few minutes.

The test is simple and brutal. Read your own chapter as the most hostile competent reader you can imagine, write down every objection, and then put all of them in the section yourself.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who decides what happens in your supervision?
- What do you send beforehand?

The single largest difference between dissertations that finish well and dissertations that do not is whether the student or the supervisor is driving the supervision, and it is almost always the student who should be.

A supervisor with nine students cannot hold your project in their head between meetings, and they are not being neglectful; they are being nine people’s supervisor on a Thursday afternoon.

So send an agenda the day before, in three lines. I have done this. I am stuck on this. I need a decision on this by this date. Nothing else, and never more than a page.

Then bring the unfinished thing. The instinct is to polish a chapter before showing it, and polishing is the most expensive way to spend a week you could have spent being told the chapter is the wrong shape.

And separate the decisions. Some are yours and you should stop asking about them; some genuinely need your supervisor, and those are the ones an agenda exists to get answered inside the hour you have.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A supervision agenda sent before the meeting**

**Figure 8.5** — Four parts, one page, sent the day before, read on the bus.

A supervision agenda is the smallest document in a dissertation and the one with the highest return, and a drafting tool is useful mainly because it keeps the agenda to a page.

Give it your notes from the last fortnight, the questions you have accumulated, the decisions you are waiting on with their deadlines, and the last agenda you sent.

Ask it to sort the questions into what you have decided yourself, what you can decide yourself and should, and what genuinely needs your supervisor’s judgement.

Ask it to write the third group as decisions with dates, no more than three of them, because an hour will not produce more and an agenda with nine gets none of them answered.

Ask it to name what you will bring, and to check that the item you are most reluctant to show is on the list, because that item is almost always the real agenda.

What it cannot do is attend the meeting or make the decision. It can only make sure the hour is spent on the three things only your supervisor can tell you.

## Unit 9 · The Viva

**Figure 9.0** — A VIVA TESTS WHETHER THE WORK IS YOURS.: Tomasz Wrona defended every sentence of his dissertation for eighty minutes and conceded nothing, and was asked to make major corrections.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is a viva for?
- What would you concede?
- What would you hold?

A viva is not an examination of what you know about your field. It is an examination of whether the work in front of the examiners is yours, and whether you understand what it does and does not show.

Answer in three moves. As I say on page forty-one, pointing at what the dissertation already says. I would now do this differently, where you have genuinely changed your mind. I accept that, where the objection is right.

Concede what costs nothing and hold what does not. An examiner who says the sample is small is right and you knew that; an examiner who says the finding is therefore worthless is making a different and arguable claim.

Saying you do not know is allowed and is often the strongest answer in the room, provided you follow it with what you would do to find out, and provided you do not do it eleven times.

And reread your own dissertation the week before, with page numbers in your head. Nothing undermines a candidate faster than being unable to find a sentence they wrote eight weeks ago.

**Viva answers prepared as claim, evidence and concession**

**Figure 9.5** — Four parts, three columns and a one-page map of your own work.

Preparing for a viva is the last place in this book where a drafting tool helps, and it helps by being a hostile examiner that costs you nothing to annoy.

Give it your finished dissertation, your limitations section, the claims you make with their page numbers, and the three questions your supervisor expects.

Ask it to generate the twenty questions an examiner is most likely to ask, ranked by how much damage each would do if you had no answer ready.
