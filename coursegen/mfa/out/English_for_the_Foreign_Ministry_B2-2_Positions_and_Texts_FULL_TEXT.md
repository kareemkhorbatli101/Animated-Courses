### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A drafting step cannot write an instruction; it can report the silences a drafter cannot see**

**Figure 1.5** — Six outputs, three of which name things that were not in the text at all.

An instruction to a post is a short document with an unusual property: it is read once, under time pressure, by somebody who is about to speak for the ministry and who cannot ask what was meant. Every other document in a foreign ministry can be clarified by a telephone call. An instruction is tested at the moment it is too late to ask.

The characteristic failure is not disobedience and not ambiguity in the ordinary sense. It is silence. An instruction says the post may indicate flexibility on the review date; the other side asks whether a later date would come with movement on the fee; and the post, reasonably, treats the purpose of the instruction as requiring an answer. Nothing has been exceeded, because nothing was said.

A drafter cannot find his own silences, and this is not carelessness. He knows what he meant, so when he rereads the text his knowledge fills the gap and the gap is invisible. The same is true of a colleague who has discussed the case with him. What is needed is a reader with no idea what was intended, and until recently such a reader was a luxury no section had.

That is the use that has been adopted in several legal and treaty sections, and it is deliberately narrow. The step does not draft. It reports: every verb of authority and its category, every permission without a stated limit, every subject on which the text is silent, and every sentence that will carry two readings. The section decides what to do about each item, and the decisions are the exercise of authority that a drafted sentence would have quietly pre-empted.

The refusal to let it draft is the important half of the practice and is argued about. A drafted instruction would be corrected where it was wrong and accepted where it was plausible, and in this field the plausible sentence is precisely the dangerous one: may indicate flexibility reads perfectly well. Authority cannot be conferred by a sentence nobody chose, and an instruction is nothing but a conferral of authority.

> **DATA POINT · Instruction drafting** — Legal and treaty sections in several foreign services adopted assisted review of instructions in 2025, reporting authority verbs, unbounded permissions and omitted subjects, while keeping drafting with the officer.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic legal services, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Instructions that cannot be misread

**Figure 1.7** — The right-hand column is where instructions fail, and none of it is visible to the drafter.

An instruction is read once, at speed, by an officer who is about to speak for the Ministry and cannot ask what was meant. That makes it unlike every other document in the building: it is tested at the one moment when clarification is impossible.

Its characteristic failure is silence rather than ambiguity. Tell a post that it may indicate flexibility on the review date and the other side will ask whether a later date comes with movement on the fee. The post answers, because the purpose of the instruction appears to require it, and nothing has been exceeded because nothing was said. The drafter cannot find that gap by rereading: he knows what he meant, and his knowledge fills the hole every time he looks at it.

So use a reader with no idea what was intended, and keep its role narrow. A drafting step over instructions should report four things and propose none: every verb of authority with its category, every permission with no stated limit, every subject on which the text is silent, and every sentence carrying two readings. Let it draft and it will produce plausible sentences, which in this work are the dangerous ones, and authority cannot be conferred by a sentence nobody chose.

*Say it like this*
- Bound the permission: **The post is authorised to accept a review in 2027 and no earlier date.**
- Close the silence: **The post is not authorised to discuss the fee and shall not refer to it.**
- Require the report: **The post is to report any request to discuss the fee within 2 hours.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A drafting step can report what a text says and what it fails to say; it cannot decide what authority the Ministry wishes to confer. Its value here comes from not knowing what was intended, which is also why nothing it proposes may be adopted unread. A plausible instruction is worse than an obviously defective one.

In September Oristan returned the Ministry’s draft with a counter-text. The two documents ran to fourteen pages each and differed in twenty-nine places.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A version comparison that classifies changes by appearance will miss the change that reverses a default**

**Figure 2.5** — Five rows identical or near-identical, one row reversed, and that row is the agreement.

Comparing two versions of a negotiating text is work that used to be done with two printed copies and a ruler, and done badly, because fourteen pages against fourteen pages at two in the morning defeats anybody. A comparison step produces the complete list of differences in minutes and does not miss a renumbering or a stray cross-reference. That part is settled and is a straightforward gain.

The question is what the list is then sorted by. The natural scheme, and the one most legal sections adopt first, is by kind of change: drafting, substantive, consequential. It is a lawyer’s scheme, it is defensible, and it classifies by what the change is.

A negotiator needs the changes classified by what they do, and specifically by what they do to the default. The two schemes agree about most changes, which is why the difficulty takes months to surface. Where they disagree, the disagreement is invisible: provided that both Parties so agree and unless both Parties otherwise agree are the same length, the same register and the same nineteen words, and they are opposites. One makes silence produce no review; the other makes silence produce a review; and the side that does not want the outcome of silence has to go and obtain agreement, which is the whole of what was negotiated.

The same applies to a small family of alterations that no wording test will catch. And to or. Shall to may. Thirty days to within thirty days. May to is authorised to. Each is trivial as text and each moves who must act first, which in a negotiation between capitals is the thing being argued about.

So the comparison needs one further report, and it is a question rather than a category: of every change, what happens if neither Party does anything? It is cheap to ask of a capitalisation and the answer is nothing. Where the answer differs between the two versions, the change is substantive whatever it looks like, and the report should carry both answers side by side so that a tired negotiator reads a consequence rather than a diff.

> **DATA POINT · Version comparison in negotiation** — Legal and treaty sections in several foreign services adopted automated version comparison in 2025 and added default-effect reporting after wording-based classifications were found to miss conditional reversals.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic treaty work, 2025–2026.

The mechanical half of version comparison is solved. Fourteen pages against fourteen pages defeats a tired officer with two printed copies, and a comparison step lists every difference in minutes including the renumberings and the stray cross-references. Take that and move on to the part that still needs an officer’s decision.

A negotiator, however, needs them sorted by what they do to the default, and the two schemes disagree in exactly the cases that matter. Provided that both Parties so agree and unless both Parties otherwise agree run to the same nineteen words in the same register and have opposite effects: under one, silence produces no review; under the other, silence produces a review, and the side that dislikes that outcome must go and obtain agreement.

*Say it like this*
- Ask the default question: **If neither Party acts, what happens under each text?**
- Order by effect: **Changes that alter the default first, then dates, then wording.**
- Refuse the classification: **Effect not determinable from the text; referred for advice.**

The default question is the only test that does not depend on how much text moved.

## Unit 3 · Inside the Brackets

**Figure 3.0** — FORTY-ONE SETS OF BRACKETS: The rolling text after the fourth round, in the room where it is kept.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does a bracket in a negotiating text actually record?
- When is an ambiguous form of words useful, and when is it a trap?
- How would you find out who objects to a phrase that has been bracketed for a year?

A rolling text accumulates square brackets. Every bracket is a disagreement that somebody, at some point, insisted be recorded rather than resolved, and after four rounds the Ministry and Oristan were working on a text with forty-one sets of them.

Samir Haddad asked a comparison step to produce something no delegation had ever had: for each bracket, the round in which it first appeared, which side had proposed each variant, and whether any delegation had spoken to it since. The work took an afternoon because the records existed and nobody had ever had time to assemble them.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A text records disagreements and does not record their deaths, so brackets outlive the reasons for them**

**Figure 3.5** — Nine brackets, forty minutes, and the only new thing in the room was a list.

A rolling text carries its own history in square brackets. Each set records a point at which a delegation preferred that a disagreement be recorded rather than resolved, and over four rounds a long text accumulates dozens. This is a sound practice: a bracket is honest, it preserves both variants, and it allows a round to move on.

What a text cannot do is record the end of a disagreement that nobody closes. A bracket is inserted by an officer with a reason; the officer is posted elsewhere; the capital’s position shifts; and the bracket remains, now owned by nobody and defended by no one, while reading exactly like the brackets that matter. Every new reader counts it as live, which matters more than it sounds, because the number of brackets is the only figure a text offers and both capitals use it to report progress.

Assembling the history of the brackets is the use that has changed this, and it is clerical rather than clever. For each bracket: the round in which it first appeared, the delegation that proposed each variant, and the last round in which any delegation spoke to it. The records exist in reports and chairs’ texts; nobody had ever had the afternoon required to put them side by side.

What the list enables is a question the chair can ask in forty minutes: does any delegation request that this bracket be retained? Nine of eleven silent brackets will usually fall. The risk, which is real and known to every delegate, is that asking makes somebody read a provision they had stopped thinking about, and occasionally a dying disagreement is revived by the enquiry.

The limit of the method is that it establishes who proposed a variant and never what the variant was for. That lives in an instruction file, in a round brief, or in nobody’s possession at all. A ministry can do this for its own variants and will find, as the Ministry did, that some of its own brackets protect positions it no longer holds, which opens a question the list cannot answer: whether to lift a bracket and reveal the change, or keep one whose purpose a successor will not be able to discover.

> **DATA POINT · Bracket management** — Delegations in several multilateral and bilateral negotiations began assembling full bracket histories in 2025, enabling systematic clearance of provisions no delegation maintained.  
> Source: reported practice in treaty negotiation support, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Bracketed text and the language of compromise

**Figure 3.7** — Everything in the right-hand column has to be supplied by a person or it is lost.

Bracketing is an honest device and should be defended. A delegation that prefers a disagreement be recorded rather than resolved keeps both variants intact and lets a round proceed, and a text without brackets after four rounds is usually a text in which somebody conceded without authority.

The defect is structural and nothing in the practice addresses it. A text records disagreements and has no way to record their deaths. An officer inserts a bracket for a reason, is posted elsewhere, the capital’s position moves, and the bracket remains, indistinguishable from the live ones and counted by every reader as an open question. Since the number of brackets is the only figure a text offers, both capitals report progress with a number that is wrong.

The remedy is clerical. Assemble, for each bracket, the round it first appeared, who proposed each variant and the last round anybody spoke to it; the records exist and nobody has ever had the afternoon. Then a chair can ask of each silent bracket whether any delegation requests that it be retained, and most will fall. Two limits remain: asking can revive a dying disagreement, and no assembly can say what a variant was for, which lives in an instruction file or in nobody’s possession.

*Say it like this*
- Ask the question: **Does any delegation request that this bracket be retained?**
- Separate the silent: **11 brackets unmentioned for 3 rounds, listed separately.**
- Refuse the inference: **Proposer not established; not inferred from the wording.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A bracket history establishes what the records contain: a round, a proposer, a last mention. It cannot establish what either variant protects, cannot tell a live disagreement from a dead one without asking a delegation, and cannot see a position a capital abandoned without telling anybody. The assembly makes a question askable; it does not answer it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A precedent search finds matching words in minutes; a precedent is a match plus the reasons it may not apply**

**Figure 4.5** — Five rows establish the pattern. The sixth is the question the room was actually deciding.

Precedent is the working memory of a legal and treaty section, and it has always been kept badly. A collection of several hundred instruments, indexed by title and date, answers the question "where is the 2019 agreement" and not the question "have we accepted this formula before", which is the question a negotiator asks in a room with somebody waiting for an answer.

A search across the collection answers the second question in minutes, exactly and completely. That is a change in what is possible in a negotiating round, and it is being used on both sides of every table: the delegation that says you have agreed this before can now be checked while it is still speaking, and so can the delegation doing the checking.

The failure that follows is a failure of the question rather than of the answer. Asked which instruments contain a provision, a search returns the instruments containing the provision. It does not say which side of the provision the ministry was on, because it was not asked, and nothing in the list draws attention to the omission. Eleven instruments containing a formula on inspection costs look like eleven precedents, and if the ministry was the receiving party in nine of them, the formula has never once operated against it.

So a precedent search needs two halves, and the second is the expensive one. For each matching instrument: the ministry’s position in it, which is on the face of the document and can be extracted, and every material difference from the case in hand, which cannot, because material means material to the argument about to be had. The practice that works is that the method lists candidate differences across the operative provisions and the parties, and a lawyer marks which matter. Forty minutes against an afternoon, and nothing is skipped.

The deeper change in a negotiation is that the existence of a precedent has stopped being the question. Both sides can establish existence in four minutes; neither can be caught out by a half-remembered 2017 instrument; and the advantage has moved entirely to whichever delegation has read the eleven matches rather than counted them.

> **DATA POINT · Precedent search** — Legal and treaty sections in several foreign services introduced full-text precedent search across their own instrument collections in 2025, adding mandatory reporting of the ministry’s position in each matching instrument.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic legal services, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Precedent: what was agreed before

**Figure 4.7** — The left column takes minutes. The right column is why a lawyer reads the eleven.

A precedent collection indexed by title and date answers the question where is the 2019 agreement, and never answered the question a negotiator actually asks, which is whether the Ministry has accepted this formula before. A full-text search answers that in minutes, completely, and it can be done while the other side is still speaking. Take it: it is one of the clearest gains in this field, and both sides of every table now have it.

Then ask the question that does not occur to anybody in the room. A search told to find instruments containing a provision will find them and will say nothing about which side of the provision the Ministry was on, because nothing in a list of matches draws attention to what the list omits. Eleven matches on a costs formula look like eleven precedents; if the Ministry was the receiving party in nine, the formula has never operated against it, and accepting it by precedent is accepting something new.

So split the work. The first half lists the matches with their references. The second states the Ministry’s position in each, which is on the face of the instrument, and lists every difference from the present case in the operative provisions and the parties, leaving a lawyer to mark which differences matter, since material means material to the argument about to be had. The second half costs four times the first and carries all of the value: a match without its differences is a coincidence of wording.

*Say it like this*
- Ask the second question: **In each match, which party was the Ministry?**
- Separate the judgement: **List every difference; do not judge which are material.**
- Refuse the inference: **Position undeterminable in 1 instrument; not inferred from the others.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A search establishes the presence of words in documents. It cannot weigh a difference, cannot know what a formula was traded for in 2017, and cannot tell a line of practice from a one-off arrangement that was never meant to be followed. The advantage now goes to the delegation that has read its eleven matches, not the one that has counted them.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is the difference between a fallback and a red line?
- Who in your organisation can decide where a limit lies?
- What happens in a meeting if nobody has decided the limit in advance?

A round brief sets out, for each issue, the Ministry’s opening position, the fallback it may move to, and the red line it will not cross. Samir Haddad’s section prepares them for twenty-two issues at a time.

Since the summer the first two columns have been assembled from the files. The opening position is almost always the position stated in the last round, which is on the record. The fallback is almost always a position the Ministry has accepted somewhere else, which the precedent collection can find. Two columns of twenty-two issues, assembled in an afternoon, referenced throughout.

The third column is different in kind. A red line is a statement that the Ministry will accept no agreement rather than cross a point, and nothing in any file records such a decision until a person with authority takes it for this round, on this issue, against this counterpart.

In July Samir instructed that the third column be assembled with the other two, on the ground that the Ministry’s standing positions imply its limits. The column came back full. For eleven of the twenty-two issues the red line was expressed as the Ministry cannot accept any arrangement which prejudices its position, which is a sentence and not a limit.

The delegation went into the round with eleven red lines of that kind. On the second day the Oristani side proposed a formulation on inspection access that the head of delegation believed crossed one of them, had no means of testing, and declined. It later proved to be inside the Ministry’s actual limit by a considerable margin, and the opportunity did not return.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Positions and fallbacks are on the record; a red line is a decision nobody has taken yet**

**Figure 5.5** — Two columns assemble in an afternoon. The fourth row is a decision and the fifth is a gap.

A round brief has a recognisable shape in every foreign ministry: for each issue, the position the delegation opens with, the fallback it may move to, and the limit it will not cross. The first two columns are retrieval work of exactly the kind that has become cheap. An opening position is almost always the position stated at the end of the last round, which is on the record; a fallback is almost always something the ministry has accepted somewhere else, which a precedent collection can find and reference.

Twenty-two issues, two columns, an afternoon, with a file reference against every entry. That is a serious gain, and the sections that have adopted it report that the referencing matters as much as the speed, because a fallback with a precedent attached can be defended in the room.

The third column is not retrieval and cannot be made into retrieval. A red line is a statement that the ministry would accept no agreement rather than cross a particular point, and no file contains such a statement until somebody with authority makes it for this round, this issue and this counterpart. Standing positions imply limits in a loose sense and do not decide them, which is a distinction that survives being argued about and does not survive being ignored.

Asked to produce the column anyway, any method will produce the only thing available: a form of words with the shape of a limit. The ministry cannot accept any arrangement which prejudices its position is a perfectly good sentence that cannot be applied to a formulation at ninety seconds’ notice. What a head of delegation does with an untestable red line is decline, every time, because declining is the only safe response to a limit you cannot locate, and the cost of that is invisible: an opportunity refused, no error recorded, nobody at fault.

So leave the column empty where nobody has decided, and send the empty column to the person with authority on that issue. Two things then surface that a full column concealed. The first is that on most issues nobody has ever been asked who sets the limit. The second is that a named limit can be exceeded and traced, while a sentence about prejudicing a position cannot, which is why such sentences have survived for a generation and why their replacement is resisted by the people who would have to sign the replacements.

> Source: reported practice in diplomatic negotiation preparation, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A drafting step can supply the substance and the structure of a statement; voice carries the information a room cannot get elsewhere**

**Figure 6.5** — Three rows assemble well. The last three are decisions and the room reads only those.

Statement and speech drafting divides neatly into three, and the division is the whole of the practice. The substance is the ministry’s position and is on the file. The structure is a convention of the forum: what is said first, what is acknowledged, what is held, how long an intervention runs. The register is the house voice, which is learnable and consistent.

All three of those can be produced quickly and competently, and a first version that gets them right is worth an hour of a drafter’s time. Press sections that have adopted this report that the structure is the surprise: conventions of the forum turn out to be well enough documented to be followed exactly.

What does not come from any of those three is voice, and voice in a plenary is not decoration. A room reads emphasis. Which of four points a speaker dwells on, which is disposed of in a clause, which sentence is repeated, whether the word unacceptable is used or avoided: delegations report these because they are frequently the only information in a statement that was not already known. Emphasis is in practice a distribution of length, and an evenly written statement distributes it flat, which says that the ministry has no preference among its own positions. That is never true and the room knows it is never true, so the statement reads as a text written by nobody.

The practice that works is to treat the first version as the argument and write the voice by hand, which takes twenty minutes and consists mostly of choosing the sentence the speaker slows down for and then making it longer while the others get shorter.

A harder question sits underneath and is not solved. In a statement of twelve hundred words, perhaps a dozen sentences commit the ministry: would not regard the matter as settled is a position, read as one by every delegation in the room and reconstructable in two years only if somebody chose those words for a reason. The rest is prose. Nobody has a reliable way to mark which dozen, which means a drafter who keeps a well-made sentence because it reads well may be keeping a commitment nobody decided.

> **DATA POINT · Statement drafting** — Press and public diplomacy sections in several foreign services adopted assisted first drafts of statements in 2025, retaining human drafting of emphasis and of any wording that states a position.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic communications, 2025–2026.

Take the first version and keep it. The substance is the position on the file, the structure is a convention of the forum, and the register is the house voice; all three can be produced competently and quickly, and the structure in particular turns out to be better documented than most drafters expect. Then write the voice yourself, because a room reads emphasis and nothing else in the statement is new to it. Which of four points the speaker dwells on, which she disposes of in a clause, whether she uses the word unacceptable: delegations report exactly these things. Emphasis is a distribution of length, so an evenly written statement has distributed it flat, and flat says the Ministry has no preference among its own positions, which no delegation believes.

And hold one question open rather than pretending it is solved. In twelve hundred words, a dozen sentences commit the Ministry: would not regard the matter as settled is a position, and somebody is entitled to ask later why those words. If they were kept because they read well, there is no answer. Nobody has a dependable method for marking which dozen, which means a drafter has to assume that any sentence which could be read as a position was chosen by somebody, and make sure it was.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is a preamble for?
- Can a paragraph that decides nothing still cost you something?
- Which part of a long document does your office read least carefully?

A resolution has two halves that behave differently. The preamble recalls, notes, welcomes and expresses concern; it decides nothing. The operative paragraphs decide, request, authorise and require a report. Negotiating effort goes almost entirely into the second half, and sensibly so.

In March a resolution was tabled with fourteen preambular paragraphs and nine operative ones. Nadia Barakat’s delegation negotiated the nine for three weeks and accepted the fourteen in an afternoon, which is the normal proportion.

Eleven months later two of the fourteen were quoted at the Ministry. A preambular paragraph recalling an earlier instrument had the effect of accepting, for the first time in a text the Ministry had co-sponsored, that the instrument applied to a category of case the Ministry had always said it did not cover.

Nadia had used a comparison step to produce the first draft of the Ministry’s own preambular paragraphs, which it did well: the forms are conventional, the sources are public, and a preamble is the most formulaic text in the trade. What the step had not been asked, because she had not thought to ask it, was which of the fourteen recalled or noted anything the Ministry had not previously accepted in a text of its own.

The practice now is that a preambular paragraph citing any instrument, resolution or report is checked against the Ministry’s record of what it has itself accepted, and the check takes nine minutes. Nadia says the thing she got wrong was not using the step. It was believing that a paragraph which decides nothing can cost nothing.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A preambular paragraph decides nothing and records what a state has recalled, noted and welcomed**

**Figure 7.5** — Every figure in the preamble column is small except the last one.

Resolution language divides sharply. The preamble recalls, notes, welcomes, expresses concern and reaffirms; it decides nothing and creates no obligation. The operative paragraphs decide, request, authorise, urge and require a report by a date. Negotiating effort follows the division: weeks on the operative half, an afternoon on the preamble, and that allocation is correct in almost every respect.

The preamble is also the most formulaic text in the trade, which makes it the clearest case in this field for assisted drafting. The forms are fixed, the instruments cited are public, and the conventions of each forum are documented. A first draft of fourteen preambular paragraphs in the correct forms, citing the right texts, is a matter of minutes and will usually need little correction.

What the division of effort conceals is that a preambular paragraph is a record of what a state has recalled, noted or welcomed, and a co-sponsor has recalled whatever its preamble recalls. A clause recalling an instrument and its applicability to a category of case is not a decision and is evidence, in a text the state put its name to, that the state treated the instrument as applying there. Eleven months later that is quoted, and the answer that the paragraph decided nothing is true and does not help.

The practice that closes this is cheap and specific: every preambular paragraph citing an instrument, resolution or report is checked against the ministry’s own record of what it has previously accepted, and the check runs in minutes because both the paragraph and the record are text. It does not change what a delegation accepts in most cases, because objecting to a recalling clause that forty delegations support costs capital nobody will spend on what looks like furniture.

What it changes is whether the acceptance is knowing. A flagged paragraph can be met in three ways: instructions can be sought, an explanation of position can be tabled, or one sentence can be written in the delegation’s report saying that the paragraph will be quoted later. The third costs nothing and is the difference, when the quotation comes, between a concession the ministry decided to make and an oversight it has to explain.

> **DATA POINT · Resolution drafting** — Multilateral delegations in several foreign services adopted assisted drafting of preambular text in 2025 together with automated checking of cited instruments against their own records of accepted positions.  
> Source: reported practice in multilateral delegations, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What happens when two official versions of a text do not say the same thing?
- Why would a negotiator leave a word undefined?
- What is the difference between endeavouring to do something and doing it so far as is practicable?

The 2019 instrument has two authentic language versions, both signed, both equally valid. In article 11 the Ministry’s version requires the Parties to endeavour to provide access. The other version requires them to provide access so far as practicable.

Those are not the same obligation. Endeavouring is an obligation of conduct: a Party that tries and fails has complied. So far as practicable is an obligation of result, limited by practicability: a Party that could have provided access and did not has not complied, whatever it tried.

Samir Haddad discovered the difference not by reading the two versions, which his section had done in 2019 and found acceptable, but by running a comparison across all eleven bilateral instruments with language versions in two languages. Four showed discrepancies. Three were trivial. This one was not.

The comparison does not resolve anything. Both versions are authentic, neither prevails, and what the Parties agreed is a question about what was negotiated, which lives in the travaux preparatoires and in the memory of people who were in the room in 2018.

What it did was make the question askable. Samir says the useful finding was not that the texts differ. It was that in 2018 the negotiating record shows the phrase was discussed for two hours and left as it was, which means somebody knew and decided, and the Ministry’s position is now that the difference was deliberate on both sides.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A comparison of language versions finds the discrepancy; what was agreed is a question about the negotiation**

**Figure 8.5** — Four rows are settled by the text, one by the record, and the last one by nothing at all.

An instrument with two authentic language versions carries a risk that is well known and rarely examined after signature. Both texts are valid, neither prevails unless the final clause says so, and a difference of meaning between them is not an error to be corrected but a feature of the instrument the parties made.

Sections check the versions against each other at the time, by reading. That catches mistranslation and misses the harder case, which is not a wrong word but a different category of obligation expressed in ordinary language. Shall endeavour to provide access and shall provide access so far as practicable both read as qualified duties; the first is an obligation of conduct, discharged by trying, and the second an obligation of result, bounded by practicability and breached by a party that could have performed and did not. A reader’s eye reports two hedged obligations and moves on.

Comparison across a collection is what has changed, and the value is in the scale rather than the cleverness. Eleven bilateral instruments in two languages each, compared provision by provision, produced four discrepancies, of which three were a definite article, a plural and a date written two ways. The fourth was article 11, which the ministry had read and passed in 2019.

What the comparison cannot do is resolve anything, and this is worth stating plainly because the output looks conclusive. Both versions are authentic. What the parties agreed is a question about the negotiation, answerable, if at all, from the negotiating record and from the recollection of people who were present. In this case the record showed the phrase had been discussed for two hours and left as it stood, which establishes that somebody on each side knew, and establishes nothing about what either thought it had obtained.

The practice that follows is modest. Record every discrepancy found, including the ones the section assesses as immaterial, with the assessment and the name of the officer who made it. Do not reopen instruments in force to correct a plural. And do not treat an immateriality assessment as a finding, because the officer making it has usually just discovered that an earlier assessment of the same kind was wrong for six years.

> **DATA POINT · Language version comparison** — Treaty sections in several foreign services ran systematic comparisons of authentic language versions across their instrument collections in 2025, recording discrepancies with materiality assessments rather than seeking corrections.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic treaty administration, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A simulated counterpart gives a delegation volume and availability; it engages, so it cannot teach absence**

**Figure 9.5** — Five rows favour the new method. The sixth is the one the room used.

Rehearsal before a negotiation is old practice and has always been rationed. A colleague playing the other side costs two hours of a senior officer’s time, which means the head of delegation gets one run and everybody else watches. That allocation is rational and it has meant that the people who most need practice get least.

A simulated counterpart removes the rationing, and the effect is the largest single change in preparation that training institutes report. A third secretary can face the hostile questions eleven times in a week, at midnight, and arrive knowing her brief in a way that was previously available only to people who had done the job for fifteen years. The quality of the questioning is also better than a tired colleague at the end of a Thursday: it asks all the obvious hostile questions rather than the three somebody remembered, it does not let a weak answer pass out of politeness, and it will press the same point six times, which is unpleasant and is a thing real counterparts do.

Its limit is structural rather than a matter of quality, and it follows from what a rehearsal is for. The exercise exists to make somebody answer, so the counterpart engages. Engagement is the premise. What a real negotiator does that is hardest to meet is often not a move but an absence: saying nothing for forty minutes while the other side fills the silence; not objecting where an objection was expected, so that a delegation cannot tell whether its point landed; agreeing at once to something you came prepared to fight for, which means you have misunderstood what they want.

None of that can be rehearsed against something whose purpose is to respond, and a rehearsal that sat in silence for forty minutes would not be run twice. So the practice is to end every rehearsal by naming the gap: prepare for the counterpart doing nothing. That is an instruction rather than an exercise, and it is the honest form of the limitation.

A second cost is harder to state and should be stated anyway. A rehearsal with a senior colleague taught two things: the brief, and what an experienced officer thinks is worth pressing, which was learned by watching them choose. The new method teaches the first very well and does not teach the second at all, and the two hours of senior time that used to carry it were never available in sufficient quantity either. Ministries may be training people who know more and have learned less, and nobody yet has evidence either way.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Preparation can be complete and a round can fail for reasons that exist only among the people present**

**Figure 10.5** — Five rows of excellent preparation and one row that decided the round.

A section that has adopted every practice in this book arrives at a round with a text agreed internally, positions and fallbacks carrying references, limits signed by the competent authority, a bracket history on one page and a delegation that has rehearsed. This is a material improvement on what was possible three years ago and the gains are real on every measure a ministry keeps.

And then a round fails for a reason that is in none of it. The other head of delegation has been told that morning that he is to be replaced, and spends two days declining to settle anything his successor might wish to reopen, including three formulations he proposed himself in the previous round. Nothing in the subject matter is involved. No document on either side contains the explanation, and the explanation is the whole of what happened.

The danger at this point is specific and it follows from the improvement rather than from any failure. A ministry that has invested in preparation, and can see the quality of its own documents, will explain outcomes with the evidence it keeps. If every position is referenced and the round still failed, the reference must have been wrong; the section examines its documents, finds nothing, and examines them again. The general proposition that preparation determines outcomes is true enough to be dangerous: it is true across fifty rounds and false about any particular Tuesday.

What exists only in the room is a short and stable list: who has standing with whom, what the other delegation can and cannot agree this week, whether anybody is tired, whether there is a division on the other side, what will cause a loss of face, and whether somebody is about to be replaced. These decide rounds. None of them is published, most are learned in corridors, and a ministry that keeps no place for them will account for its failures using the only evidence it has, which is paper.

The practice that answers it is small: a line at the top of the round report, headed Room, for what was true of the room and is in no document, carrying how it is known, and saying either none or the delegation’s reading, no source. Most reports will say none. The line earns its place on the rounds that would otherwise be explained wrongly for a fortnight, and it is the one part of a round report that rests on corridors and cannot be checked, which has to be said in the instruction rather than discovered by a successor.

> **DATA POINT · Reporting the unwritten** — Delegations in several foreign services added a short standing field to round reports in 2025 for circumstances bearing on the negotiation that appear in no document, with a requirement to state how each was known.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic negotiation reporting, 2025–2026.
