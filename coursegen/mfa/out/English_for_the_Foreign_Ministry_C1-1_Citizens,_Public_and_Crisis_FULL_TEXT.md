### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

Rana Deeb’s section receives about eight thousand consular enquiries a month, rising by half again in August, and has eleven officers. Before this year the median first response took four days and the section’s own estimate was that two in five enquiries never received a substantive reply at all.

Of the eight thousand, something close to seven thousand six hundred fall into nine categories, each with an answer that has not changed in years: how a passport is renewed abroad, what documents a birth registration requires, whether a marriage certificate needs legalisation, and six others of the same kind. These are not trivial questions to the person asking, and the answers are nevertheless identical from one asker to the next.

Since February a triage step has classified every incoming enquiry and answered the nine categories directly, in the language the enquiry arrived in, citing the relevant published requirement. The median first response is now eleven minutes. Officers see the remaining four hundred, which is approximately the number eleven people can actually handle.

The design decision that made this defensible was not the classification. It was the rule about uncertainty: where the step cannot place an enquiry in one of the nine categories with confidence, it does not guess and does not send a general answer. It sends an acknowledgement naming a human officer and a time, and puts the case in front of that officer.

Rana had initially resisted the acknowledgement, on the ground that a citizen in difficulty wants an answer rather than a promise, and that promising a reply within a stated time created an obligation the section might not meet in August. She was persuaded by the arithmetic: an unanswered enquiry and a general answer to a particular question both produce a second enquiry, and the second enquiry arrives angry.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: register shifting**

- The same content can be stated for a citizen and for a note; only the register moves.
- To a citizen: short clauses, you as subject, concrete nouns, an apology where one is owed.
- In a note: nominalisation, the passive, the third person, no apology and no promise that is not an undertaking.
- Shifting register must not shift content: if the formal version concedes more, the shift has failed.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Triage at volume is defensible only if the uncertain case is routed to a person rather than answered generally**

**Figure 1.5** — Every row improved, and the fifth row is the one that made the second row possible.

Consular work is the part of a foreign ministry that operates at a volume no other part recognises. A section of eleven officers receiving eight thousand enquiries a month cannot give each one the attention a case deserves, and the historical response to that arithmetic was not a system but an informal one: the officers answered what they could reach, and the rest waited, and a substantial proportion were never answered at all.

The great majority of those enquiries have answers that do not depend on the person asking. How a passport is renewed abroad, what a birth registration requires, whether a certificate needs legalisation: these are not trivial questions to the person asking, and the answers are nevertheless the same from one asker to the next, published, and unchanged for years. Classifying an incoming enquiry into one of nine such categories and answering it directly, in the language it arrived in, is the single largest improvement in service that this capability has produced anywhere in a foreign ministry.

What makes it defensible rather than reckless is a rule about the cases it cannot place. There are two ways to handle an enquiry that does not fit: guess at the nearest category, or send a general answer that covers several. Both are catastrophic in consular work, because the enquiry that does not fit is disproportionately the one that matters: the detained citizen, the missing relative, the document that is required for a funeral on Thursday. The rule that works is that uncertainty produces an acknowledgement naming a human officer and a date, and the case goes to that officer.

The acknowledgement is resisted in most sections, on a reasonable ground: a named date is an obligation, and a missed date generates a complaint. The arithmetic answers it. An unanswered enquiry produces a second enquiry and then a complaint, and a complaint costs an officer ten times what the original enquiry would have cost. A date that can be met in the section’s worst month is worth more than a date that flatters the section in March.

Register is the other half of the work and is widely underestimated. The same content has to be said to a citizen and in a note, and only the register moves. The Ministry is not competent in this matter is correct in a note to a post and is a door closing to a woman with a sick child; this is decided by the civil registry, and here is the address and what to take, is the same content and is actionable. The rule that must not bend is that plain language licenses no undertaking the Ministry cannot give: an apology is permitted, a reassurance is not.

> **DATA POINT · Consular triage** — Consular services in several foreign ministries introduced automated triage and direct answering of routine categories in 2025, with mandatory routing of unclassifiable cases to a named officer; reported first-response times fell from days to minutes.  
> Source: reported practice in consular services, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Triage that answers in seconds

**Figure 1.7** — Ninety-five per cent on the left, and the section exists for the right.

Eleven officers and eight thousand enquiries a month is an arithmetic that has never been answered honestly. Officers answered what they could reach and the rest waited, and two in five were never answered at all, which is the true state of consular work in most ministries and is not usually written down.

Most of those enquiries have answers that do not depend on the asker, are published, and have not changed in years. Classify an enquiry into one of nine such categories, answer it in the language it arrived in and cite the published requirement, and a median first response of four days becomes eleven minutes. There is no serious argument against this and it is the largest improvement in service the capability has produced anywhere in a ministry.

The whole of the risk sits in the cases that do not fit, because in consular work the misfit case is disproportionately the one that matters: the detention, the death, the document needed for a funeral. So forbid the two natural responses, which are to guess at the nearest category and to send a general answer covering several. Uncertainty produces an acknowledgement naming an officer and a date the section can meet in August, and the case goes to that officer. Expect the date to be resisted, and answer with the arithmetic: silence produces a second enquiry and then a complaint that costs ten times the original. Then watch the register, because plain language to a citizen invites a reassurance, and an apology is permitted where an undertaking is not.

*Say it like this*
- Forbid the guess: **Where it cannot be placed, send no substantive answer.**
- Name the override: **Detention, death, a missing person, a medical emergency: route in 5 minutes.**
- Hold the register: **I am sorry is allowed; we will sort this out is not.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Triage can place an enquiry in a published category and can answer it. It cannot recognise the case that is unlike anything it has seen, which is the case the section exists for, and a general answer to such an enquiry is worse than no answer because it looks like a reply. Everything depends on what happens to the cases the rule cannot place.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**In consular work the case that must reach a person is the case that does not announce itself**

**Figure 2.5** — Everything the rule examined was handled correctly.

Triage at volume works because most enquiries are what they say they are. Consular work contains a systematic exception to that, and it is not an occasional exception: a person in serious difficulty very often writes about something else.

The reason is ordinary and human. A family whose relative has disappeared does not write to a ministry leading with the disappearance, because they do not believe the ministry can do anything about it. They lead with the question they think is within its competence, which is usually a document. The frightening fact arrives in the middle of a long message, stated once, without emphasis, as background.

A routing rule that reads a subject line and an opening paragraph is reading the part of the message the writer composed deliberately, and the part they composed deliberately is the part that reflects their theory of what a ministry does. Everywhere else in a foreign ministry the subject of a document is at the top. In a consular enquiry from a frightened family it frequently is not, and a rule designed on the ordinary assumption will fail precisely on the cases where failure costs most.

The correction is a word list applied to the whole text: detained, arrested, police, hospital, missing, died, and a dozen others, with any enquiry containing one of them routed to a duty officer within minutes whatever the enquiry appears to be about. This is crude, and crudeness is the point: a list of words has no theory of what the message is about and therefore cannot be misled by one.

It produces false positives at perhaps one in six, since a question about legalising a hospital letter contains the word hospital. The arithmetic is the whole justification. A false positive costs an officer four minutes; the false negative in the April case cost nine days of a detention nobody had looked at. Any routing rule in this field should be designed so that its cheap failure is the one that happens often and its expensive failure is the one that is almost impossible.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Multilingual reply is accurate in the language and blind to the variety and the distance**

**Figure 3.5** — The first two rows are a transformation. The last three are the price, and it is not paid in accuracy.

Multilingual reply, and with it the dialect problem, begins with an older arrangement. A consular section that replies in two languages and asks everybody else to write in one of them has not reduced the number of enquiries in other languages. It has created a queue of them, and that queue is answered unevenly, late, and sometimes by an officer who has asked a relative for help.

Replying in the language of the enquiry changes that completely, and the scale of the change is not in dispute: a section of eleven officers answering in eleven languages within the quarter hour is doing something no consular service of its size has ever done. The grammar is faultless and the information is right.

The failure is not in the language. It is in the variety and in the distance. A reply in the standard variety of a capital city, using the address form that institutions use when they write to a party, is correct and may read, to somebody in a border village, as the beginning of a prosecution. The content was a list of two documents; what arrived was a letter of the kind that precedes trouble.

What makes this hard to govern is that the error is invisible from inside the ministry. Anybody who reads the language as a second language sees correct grammar and appropriate formality, because formality is what a second-language reader has been taught. The judgement required is one that only a first-language reader makes reliably, and a section replying in eleven languages does not have first-language readers in eleven languages.

Two practices are available and neither is sufficient. Record, on every case, the language and variety of the enquiry and of the reply, so that a pattern of complaints can later be traced to a language rather than to a mood. And arrange first-language readers to sample replies in as many languages as can be covered, naming the languages that are not covered rather than implying coverage. The temptation, where coverage is impossible, is to reduce formality everywhere on the theory that the fault was excessive distance; in some languages that substitutes a worse offence, and a section that cannot read its own replies does not know which way to err.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What happens in your organisation at three in the morning?
- What is the difference between what you will do and what you expect to happen?
- In a crisis, what is the cost of an answer that turns out to be wrong?

A crisis does not choose its hour. In February an earthquake in Lysmark began at 02:40 local time in the Ministry, which is to say in the middle of the night, with a duty officer, a security guard and nobody else in the building.

By 06:40 the consular mailbox and the hotline had taken about nine hundred enquiries. The duty officer answered forty-one of them. She did this correctly, she recorded every call, and she went home at six having been awake for twenty hours.

What now happens in that first hour is different. An acknowledgement goes to every enquiry within two minutes, in the language it arrived in, stating three things: that the Ministry is aware of the event, that it cannot yet confirm any information about individuals, and when the next statement will be issued. Enquiries naming an individual are placed in a single list for the morning team, sorted by the time they arrived.

Rana Deeb had resisted the automatic acknowledgement in a crisis specifically, as distinct from the routine mailbox, on the ground that a mechanical reply to a family in terror is worse than silence. She had been in the operations room for two crises and had read the messages people send at four in the morning.

She changed her position after the February event, and the reason is narrow. In the first four hours, eight hundred and fifty-nine families received nothing at all, and of those, two hundred and twelve wrote again before dawn, several of them to ask whether the Ministry was aware that anything had happened. Silence had not spared them a mechanical reply. It had told them nobody was there.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: future forms for undertakings and expectations**

- Will plus verb is an undertaking by the speaker: we will issue a statement at 08:00.
- Is to plus verb reports an arrangement or an instruction, not a promise.
- Is going to predicts from present evidence; is expected to reports an expectation.
- In a crisis, use will only for what the Ministry controls, and is expected to for everything else.

**Dialogue 2 · Will and is expected to**

**Figure 4.4** — Rana and Yara Ibrahim, on the 08:00 statement.

**Yara:** The draft says the airport will reopen tomorrow and flights will resume on Thursday.
**Rana:** Both of those come from the Lysmark authorities.

**Yara:** And where there is no source, we cannot say. Those three words are the most useful sentence in a crisis and the hardest to get past a Minister at eight in the morning.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**In the first hour of a crisis the only things a ministry can give are an acknowledgement and a time it will meet**

**Figure 4.5** — The third row does not change. The fourth and sixth are the whole of the argument.

A crisis arrives at the hour it arrives, and roughly a third of them arrive when the ministry’s own capital is asleep. The staffing reality is a duty officer, a security guard and a telephone, against a volume that would overwhelm the section at midday.

The temptation in that hour is to do what can be done well: answer a few enquiries properly, record them, and work until the next shift. That is what a conscientious duty officer does and it is a rational use of one person, and the arithmetic of it is brutal. Forty-one enquiries answered properly and eight hundred and fifty-nine families given nothing is not a section at its best; it is a section with no rule.

What can be done without a person is narrow and it is not an answer. An acknowledgement, in the language of the enquiry, within two minutes, stating three things: that the ministry is aware of the event, that it cannot yet confirm any information about individuals, and the hour at which the next statement will be issued. That is all. It answers nobody’s question and it is not meant to.

The objection to it is serious and is usually raised by people who have worked in an operations room: a mechanical reply to a family in terror is an insult, and silence at least does not pretend. The answer is that silence is not read as restraint. It is read as absence, and the families who write again before dawn asking whether the ministry is aware that an earthquake has occurred are telling you exactly what the silence said to them. An officer inside the building knows somebody is awake and working; nobody outside it does.

The grammar of the first hour then carries most of the remaining risk, and it is a question of future forms. Will is an undertaking by the ministry and must be reserved for what the ministry controls, which in the first hour is almost nothing: it will issue a statement at a stated time, it will search a registration list, it will report the result. Everything else is an expectation and is reported with its source: the airport is expected to reopen tomorrow, which is the local authorities’ statement of this morning. Every sentence written in a crisis is read again on the day it turns out to be wrong, and the sentence that carried its source survives that reading while the sentence that said will is quoted as a broken promise.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: acknowledge every enquiry arising from the event within 2 minutes, so that no family is left with silence while the Ministry has nothing to tell them. Scope: enquiries received on any channel from the declaration of the event until the crisis cell convenes; enquiries from posts and other ministries are out of scope and go to the duty officer. Inputs: the enquiry, the declared facts of the event, and the next-statement time set by the duty officer. Criteria: state 3 things only, that the Ministry is aware of the event, that it cannot confirm any information about individuals, and the hour of the next statement; give no casualty figure and no information about any named person; use will only of what the Ministry itself will do; place every enquiry naming an individual in 1 list sorted by time of arrival. Done: 1 acknowledgement per enquiry within 2 minutes, in the language of the enquiry, and 1 list of named individuals updated continuously. Fallback: where no next-statement time has been set, state that a statement will be issued within 2 hours and notify the duty officer that the time is unset.

*The gap.* "Handle enquiries" assumes a volume one officer can handle, "the most serious first" requires a judgement at scale, "if needed" leaves the threshold unset, and "when the position is clear" has no time in it at all.

Line five is the failure. In a crisis the position is never clear, so a statement conditioned on clarity is a statement with no time.

**STAGE 4 · The Diagnosis**

- **ambiguous referent** — reference, substitution and cohesion
- **hidden assumption** — articles and attribution for given and new information
- **missing condition** — conditionals; unless, provided that, failing that
- **unbounded quantity** — quantifiers and stated measures
- **undefined done** — modality of requirement: shall, is to, must
- **unscoped set** — defining relative clauses and articles
- **unstated criterion** — comparatives and superlatives with a stated basis
- **unstated exception** — concession: although, notwithstanding, without prejudice to

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Plain writing is read; a careful page that nobody reads has misled its whole audience**

**Figure 5.5** — Nothing was removed. Four lines were added above it, and three in five readers now reach the thing that could kill them.

A ministry publishes for two audiences and writes for one. Travel advice, public statements and explanatory pages are cleared by sections whose professional standard is accuracy and completeness, and the result is correct, exhaustive and written in the register of an institution talking to itself.

The figures, where anybody troubles to collect them, are consistent and brutal. Four in five readers of a travel advice page leave within nine seconds, which is enough for about thirty words; and the thirty words at the top of a cleared page are usually about the ministry rather than about the reader, because the opening is where institutional care accumulates.

A reader who leaves after nine seconds has not received nothing. They have received the impression that the ministry has published something and that nothing at the top looked alarming, from which they reasonably infer that there is no serious problem. An unread warning is not neutral; it is a reassurance.

The structural fix is cheap and should be taken first, because it costs no section anything: put four lines at the top saying what to do, and leave the cleared text underneath unchanged. Do not travel to the three named districts. The rest of the country is safe for ordinary travel. Carry your passport. Here is the number. Three in five readers then reach the district names, where almost none did before, and no clearance has been reopened.

What the fix does not resolve is the sentence in which the qualification is the content. Assistance may be limited in remote areas is unreadable and true; we may not be able to reach you quickly is readable and stronger than the evidence, and a traveller who cancels a journey on it has been misled by a ministry that was trying to be clear. Both sides of this argument are choosing who gets misled, and the usual answer, that the qualification lives in the long text, is an answer only if somebody reads the long text. A section that has just measured its readers knows that nobody does, which makes the honest course to put a bounded figure in the short version wherever one exists, and to record a disagreement where none does.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A readership figure establishes distribution; it establishes nothing about effect, and it rises when you publish more**

**Figure 6.5** — Four measurable rows, one that cannot be established, and one nobody has tried.

Measurement is now cheap in a way it has never been for a press or public diplomacy section. A ministry can know how many people opened a page, how long they stayed, how far down they went, where they came from and what they did next, weekly, for every item it publishes. For a section that spent decades publishing into silence, this is a genuine change and it should be used.

What is cheap to measure, however, is distribution, and distribution is not effect. A reach figure of four hundred and ten thousand is accurate, carefully calculated and answers the question whether an item was distributed. It does not answer the question anybody actually has, which is whether a citizen who needed to register did so, and it has one property that makes it dangerous as a management figure: it rises when the section publishes more, which is something the section controls.

The outcome figure usually exists and is usually held by somebody else. Registrations are in consular records; visa applications are in another system; calls to a hotline are counted by the people who answer them. Nothing technical prevents a section from putting the outcome figure beside its own, and the obstacle is institutional: the outcome figure belongs to another section and does not flatter anybody.

Even with both figures, attribution is not available. Registrations rose eleven per cent in the quarter of the campaign and nine per cent in the equivalent quarter of the previous year with no campaign at all, and no honest sentence connects the four hundred and ten thousand to the nine hundred. The practice that works is to report the proxy, the outcome and the equivalent period of the previous year together, with one sentence stating that no attribution can be made, so that neither figure can be read alone and nobody is invited to infer a connection the section cannot defend.

The temptation, when a measure is shown to be imperfect, is to stop reporting it. That usually produces no better measure and no measure at all, and a section that can say nothing quantitative about its own work is in a weaker position than one reporting a flawed figure with its caveat attached. The answer to a vanity metric is a second figure that does not move when the section publishes more, not the removal of the first.

> **DATA POINT · Measuring public communication** — Foreign ministry communication sections instrumented published material in 2025 and began reporting outcome figures held by other sections alongside reach, with explicit statements that attribution was not established.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic public communication, 2025–2026.

## Unit 7 · Where an Error Is Expensive

*CEFR C1 · Grammar: acknowledgement · AI: where an error is expensive*

**Figure 7.0** — HE MISSED HIS MOTHER’S FUNERAL: Consular Affairs, the morning after the funeral, with the file open.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Where in your work would a small administrative error cost somebody a great deal?
- What does a real acknowledgement of a mistake contain?
- What is the difference between preparing a decision and taking one?

In November a man applied for an emergency travel document in order to attend his mother’s funeral. The application was refused on the ground that the proof of relationship had not been supplied. The ground was correct in law and the document had in fact been supplied, four days earlier, and had been attached to another applicant’s file by an error in the intake step.

The funeral was on the Thursday. The error was found on the Friday. The Ministry’s letter of the following Monday is the document Rana Deeb now uses when she trains new officers, because she wrote it four times and the first three drafts were the three ways of evading the thing that had happened.

The first draft regretted that the application had not met the requirements. The second regretted the distress caused by the circumstances. The third explained the intake process at some length. The fourth said that the Ministry had refused the application because a document he had supplied had been attached to the wrong file by the Ministry, that this should not have happened, that he had been unable to attend his mother’s funeral as a result, and that the Ministry was sorry.

The workflow was then changed, and the change is narrower than the section first proposed. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A step may prepare an adverse decision and must not take one that rests on an absence**

**Figure 7.5** — Two stages are unchanged. The change is at the fourth row, and it is a prohibition.

Document workflows are the part of consular and visa work where assisted processing is most obviously justified. The volumes are large, the tasks are clerical, and the error rate of a step that reads a document, identifies its type and date and attaches it to a file is markedly lower than that of four officers doing the same work at the end of a long day. Any section that removes it in a panic will produce more misfiling, not less, together with a queue.

The errors it does make are nevertheless of a particular kind, and the kind matters more than the rate. A misfiled document is indistinguishable, from inside the process, from a document that was never supplied. The file simply lacks it. Every completeness check then reports an absence, correctly, and every subsequent step is correct on a false premise.

Where that false premise feeds a decision, the consequence falls entirely on the applicant and is often irreversible. A refusal on the ground that proof of relationship was not supplied is lawful, properly reasoned and unanswerable, and the funeral was on Thursday. No appeal mechanism operates at the speed of a funeral.

The rule that follows is narrow and should be stated as a prohibition rather than as a safeguard: a step may prepare an adverse decision and may not issue one whose ground is the absence of something. Where a document appears to be missing, the step says what is missing and an officer searches for it under the applicant’s own identifiers across recent intake before any refusal issues. On the volumes of a medium-sized section that costs about twenty hours a month, and it is the only measure that addresses the actual failure.

The letter that follows an error of this kind is the other half of the work and is harder than the rule. An institution’s instinct is to write about requirements, circumstances and processes, none of which has the institution as its subject. A real acknowledgement names what the ministry did, states that it should not have happened, states the consequence for the person in plain words, and apologises. The third of those is the one every draft omits, because writing down that a man missed his mother’s funeral is the admission, which is precisely why it belongs in the letter.

## Unit 8 · A Condolence Cannot Be Generated

*CEFR C1 · Grammar: agentless structures · AI: a condolence cannot be generated*

**Figure 8.0** — IT WAS SIGNED BY A PERSON: Consular Affairs, where the condolence letters are still written by hand.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What makes a message of sympathy worth keeping?
- When is it right for an institution to speak without naming anybody?
- What would you never want a system to write on your behalf?

The Ministry writes about ninety letters of condolence a year to the families of citizens who have died abroad. They are four or five sentences long, they are signed by a named officer, and they take between thirty minutes and two hours each.

In March the question was put formally whether these letters should be drafted like other correspondence, with an officer editing and signing. The arithmetic was not disputed: ninety letters at an hour each is about eleven working days a year of a section that has eleven officers and eight thousand monthly enquiries.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A condolence is an act rather than a communication, and what is assisted is everything around it**

**Figure 8.5** — Five of six documents are prepared in minutes, which is how the sixth gets its hour.

There is a small class of documents that a ministry should not have drafted for it, and the argument for the class is usually made badly. It is made as a quality argument — a machine cannot write a condolence — and that argument is not true and is getting less true, which means a practice defended on that ground will be abandoned when somebody runs a blind comparison.

The sound argument is about what the document is. A letter of condolence to the family of a citizen who has died abroad contains no information. Everything informative in the case goes in other documents: the arrangements, the certificate, the list of what the family must do. What the letter does is constitute an act, and the act is that a named person read the file about one death and spent an hour on one family.

The evidence that this is what families value is in what they write back about, and they do write back, sometimes years later. They write about a detail: a first name used, a town mentioned, a sentence showing that somebody had understood what the dead man had been doing there. These are traces of reading, and a drafted letter can reproduce the traces without the reading, which is exactly the difficulty: the words would be the same and the act would not have occurred.

So the rule is not that such letters must be good but that they must be written, by a named person who may be written back to, and the practical question becomes where the hours come from. They come from everything around the letter. A case summary, a translated certificate, a list of documents and a draft of the repatriation arrangements took an officer two hours and now take minutes, which is how a section of eleven people continues to write ninety letters a year by hand.

The grammar carries the distinction and is worth stating, because agentless constructions are not simply evasion. A public statement of mourning properly says that the Ministry extends its condolences: no individual is the actor, the state is not a person, and a claim about an officer’s feelings would be out of place. The same construction in a letter to one mother tells her that a procedure has operated. The test is not whether a passive has been used but whether the identity of the actor is part of what is being communicated, and in a letter to one family it is nearly the whole of it.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — The register of a condolence

**Figure 8.7** — The same event, two registers, and the grammar is the difference.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Surge capacity multiplies the handling of the routine and leaves the human caseload exactly where it was**

**Figure 9.5** — Three rows transformed, one row created, and the last row is the one that was never counted at either date.

Surge capacity is the clearest institutional gain available here, and the figures are not in dispute. A section of eleven officers that could not survive a seasonal peak now absorbs it without additional staff: the routine categories are answered directly, in every language the enquiries arrive in, at a median of eleven minutes, and the volume can rise by half without any change in the queue.

The error that follows is a reporting error and it is almost inevitable, because the figures that improve are the figures a section has always collected. Enquiries received, response times, complaints: all of these transform, and a section that reports them honestly will tell its ministry that it can absorb a far higher peak, which is true of everything the section counts.

What constrains such a section is not the enquiry volume but the human caseload inside it, and that caseload scales with the volume while the capacity to carry it does not scale at all. A welfare case requires an officer to read a file, telephone a family, write to a prison and then wait, and the waiting is most of the elapsed time and all of the irreducibility. Four officers can carry sixty open cases each properly and ninety improperly, and nothing in any system changes that number.

What happens at ninety is the part worth attending to. No decision is taken. The officers do the work with deadlines and the work where somebody is waiting on the telephone, and the work that has neither simply stops. In a consular section that is the second welfare visit in a long detention, which is to say the attention paid to the person who has been in a cell for four months and whose family has stopped telephoning. Nobody complains, because the people affected are the ones who have already stopped expecting anything.

The practice that answers it is a stated ceiling and a declared degraded mode: above a named caseload the section informs the ministry that it is operating below its own standard and names what has stopped. This is unwelcome, and the first response will be that other sections manage without declaring anything, which is true in the sense that they let something stop quietly and do not know what it is. A number arrived at from experience and written down is better than a number nobody has ever stated, even where it cannot be proved.

In May the published requirement for registering a birth abroad changed in one respect: the period within which a registration must be made was extended from three months to one year. Standard answer six was updated the same week, correctly, except that the sentence giving the period was not changed.

Standard answer six was sent, unaltered and in eleven languages, to four thousand one hundred and six people over nine weeks, each of whom was told that they had three months to register a birth when in fact they had a year.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Scale makes an error reach further and makes its recipients nameable, and only the second is new**

**Figure 10.5** — The first row is worse at volume. Every other row is better, and the last row could not exist at all by hand.

The objection most often made to answering at volume is that an error is multiplied, and it is true. One wrong sentence in one standard answer reached four thousand one hundred and six people in nine weeks and in eleven languages, which no section answering by hand could achieve.

The objection is nevertheless incomplete, and the missing half is the more important. The same error, in a section answering the same question forty times a month by hand, reaches perhaps a hundred people in the same nine weeks, and reaches them untraceably. There is no record of which officer said what to whom in which words, so the guidance is corrected and nobody is written to. A hundred people continue to believe a wrong thing indefinitely, and the section does not know their names.

That is the trade and it should be stated in both directions: at volume an error reaches forty times as many people and every one of them can be named, written to in the language they were written to in, and in a few cases reimbursed. A smaller mistake and a worse position is the honest summary, and the figure that matters is not the reach but the proportion of the reach that is repairable, which by hand is nil.

Two practices follow and neither is elaborate. Every outgoing communication must be recorded with its exact wording, its recipient and its language, which is what makes a mass correction possible at all. And a changed standard answer should go to a bounded first batch and then hold, for a day, while somebody reads it against the published source: the first hundred receive the error, the next four thousand do not.

What neither practice reaches is the failure that produced the error, and it is worth admitting that it is not understood. Nine officers cleared the amended answer. All nine read the sentence stating three months, in a document whose whole purpose was to implement a one-year rule, and none of them saw it, because each was checking the sentence that had changed. A first batch bounds such a failure; it does not explain it, and a section that tells itself the bounding is a solution will be surprised again.

> **DATA POINT · Scale and correction** — Consular services operating automated reply at volume in 2025 recorded the exact wording, recipient and language of every outgoing communication, enabling full cohort correction within days of an error being identified.  
> Source: reported practice in consular services, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — What scale does to a mistake

**Figure 10.7** — Both columns are true. Only the left one is ever used in the argument.

The standard objection to answering at volume is that it multiplies an error, and it does: one wrong sentence reached four thousand one hundred and six people in nine weeks and in eleven languages. State that plainly and give the exact figure, because a rounded figure reads as an estimate and an estimate reads as a concealment.

Then state the half nobody states. By hand, the same error reaches perhaps a hundred people in the same nine weeks, and reaches them untraceably: no record of which officer said what, to whom, in what words. The guidance is corrected, nobody is written to, and a hundred people go on believing a wrong thing for ever. A smaller mistake, and a worse position.

So build for repair rather than for perfection. Record the exact wording, the recipient and the language of every outgoing communication, which is what makes a mass correction possible at all. Release an amended answer to a bounded first batch and hold it for a day while somebody reads the whole answer against the published source rather than the sentence that changed. And do not tell yourself that this explains the failure: nine officers read a three-month period in a document implementing a one-year rule and none of them saw it, and bounding the damage is not the same as understanding why.

*Say it like this*
- Give the exact figure: **4,106 people were told the wrong period.**
- State the trade: **A smaller mistake, and a worse position.**
- Bound the next one: **The first 100 receive it; the rest wait 24 hours.**

Nothing in a staged release explains why nine officers failed to see a wrong figure in a document whose purpose was to change it, so the bounding is a limit on damage and not a correction of the cause, and a section that confuses the two will be surprised by the same kind of failure.
