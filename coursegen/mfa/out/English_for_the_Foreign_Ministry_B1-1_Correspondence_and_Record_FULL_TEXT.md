Tarek Zayed had put all the papers into date order and numbered them. All four pieces of work were careful and accurate, and none of them was what Dr Mourad had wanted. What she wanted was a single page carrying three points the Minister could raise and one that he should not. She had asked for a file, and a file is not an argument.

Dr Mourad was not angry about this, which surprised Lina more than anything else that week. She explained that in a ministry almost every task is one of four things: you draft something new, or you translate something that exists, or you pull particular facts out of something, or you sort things into piles. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Four verbs cover most of a ministry’s paper, and naming the verb is half the instruction**

**Figure 1.5** — Sixty-nine tasks fit the four verbs. Four do not, and those four are the job.

Nearly all the written work of a ministry is one of four operations: you draft something new, you translate something that already exists, you pull particular facts out of a document, or you sort documents into the right piles. Almost every system now used in a foreign service does one of these four things, and does it very fast.

This is why naming the operation matters so much. An instruction that says do the file does not say which of the four it wants, so the reader has to guess, and reasonable people guess differently from one another. An instruction that says draft one page of talking points cannot be misunderstood by anybody at all.

A complete instruction has three parts. The verb says which operation is wanted, the object says what to act on, and the purpose says what the result is for. The purpose is the part that people leave out, and it is the part that matters most, because a reader who knows why a thing is wanted can make the small decisions correctly without asking.

The same three parts work whether you are instructing a colleague or a system, and the difference lies in what happens when you leave a part out. A colleague will usually come back and ask you. A system will not ask, because it cannot tell that anything is missing, and it will produce something complete and confident that is built on its own guess.

There is a fifth kind of task, and it is the important one. Deciding whether to reply to a note at all, or deciding what a Minister should say about a difficult question, is not drafting or sorting but judgement, and judgement carries responsibility with it. The four verbs are therefore a useful test: if you cannot name which one a task is, it is probably the fifth kind.

> **DATA POINT · Where foreign ministries started** — Published accounts of foreign ministries adopting these systems show the first uses clustering in translation, transcription, document handling and consular triage, rather than in policy analysis.  
> Source: reported practice in several foreign ministries, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — The four verbs: draft, translate, extract, sort

**Figure 1.7** — The right-hand column can be given to a colleague or to a system. The left cannot.

Begin every instruction with one of the four verbs: draft, translate, extract or sort. If you cannot choose one of them, you have not yet decided what you want, and neither a system nor a colleague can decide it for you. Naming the verb takes about a second and removes most of the risk in the whole exchange.

Then add the object and the purpose, because an instruction with a verb alone is still only half written. The object says what to act on and what to leave alone, while the purpose says what the result is for. A reader who knows the purpose can make all the small decisions correctly without coming back to ask you about each one.

Last, notice when a task is none of the four. Deciding whether to answer a note, or what to concede in a negotiation, or what a Minister should say in public, is judgement rather than production. A system will produce a confident answer to those questions as well, and that answer will look exactly like the other four, which is precisely why the test is worth making a habit.

*Say it like this*
- Name the verb: **Draft, translate, list or sort — which one?**
- Add the purpose: **… to prepare the Minister for Tuesday.**
- Test it: **If it is none of the four, who decides it?**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — The four verbs tell you what kind of task you have. They do not tell you whether the task should be done at all, whether the material may be shown to a system, or whether the answer is right. A task that is none of the four is not a task to phrase better; it is a task to take to a person.

## Unit 2 · From Notes to a Record

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: present simple and continuous · AI: notes into a record*

**Figure 2.0** — SEVEN PAGES OF NOTES, ONE PAGE OF RECORD: The Verenia desk, forty minutes after the call ended.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who writes the record of a meeting in your office, and when?
- What must a record contain before anybody can rely on it?
- Have you ever read a record of a meeting you attended, and disagreed with it?

The Ministry keeps a record of every substantive conversation with a foreign official, and this is not a formality. Four years later, when somebody asks what was agreed, the record is what the Ministry has. Lina Khalil writes two or three of them a week, and she writes them within the day, because the procedure says so and because memory does not improve with waiting.

In April she took a telephone call from the Verenian embassy about visa arrangements for the visit. She took seven pages of notes in a mixture of English and Arabic, with arrows and crossings-out, as everybody does. That afternoon she put the notes through a drafting system and asked it for a record of conversation.

What came back was excellent. It ran to one page, it used the Ministry’s house form, it put the points in a sensible order and it read as if a careful officer had written it slowly. Lina read it twice, could find nothing wrong with it, and sent it for clearance the same afternoon.

Omar Nasri, who clears records for the department, asked her one question. The Verenian counsellor had hesitated over the question of whether visas would be issued at the border, and had said that he believed so but would confirm. In the record this appeared as a plain statement that visas would be issued at the border.

Nothing in the record was invented, and the drafting system had not been careless. It had done the thing it does well, which is to turn rough material into smooth prose. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A record is written for a stranger four years away, and it must be exactly as certain as the conversation was**

**Figure 2.5** — Nothing here is invented. Four things are more definite than the conversation was.

A record of conversation is not a story about a meeting; it is the Ministry’s evidence of what was said. It will be read years later by somebody who was not there, who cannot telephone you, and who will treat every sentence in it as a fact about the world. That is why the form is strict and why the drafting is taught.

Systems that turn notes into records do one thing extremely well, and it is worth naming precisely. They take rough, broken material and produce fluent, ordered prose in the house style. The work is fast, the result is usually better written than a tired officer would manage at six in the evening, and the saving is real.

The risk comes from the same ability. Hesitation, qualification and silence are rough material, and smoothing is what the system does. A question mark becomes a statement, a range becomes a figure, and a point that was avoided becomes a point that was not discussed. Nothing is invented; everything becomes a little more certain than it was.

The remedy is at the note-taking stage and not at the drafting stage, which is what most officers get wrong. If the doubt is a question mark in your notes, nothing can carry it across, because the system writes sentences and a question mark is not one. If the doubt is a sentence in your notes, it survives into the record untouched.

So the discipline is to write uncertainty as uncertainty while the conversation is still happening. He believes so and will confirm. She did not answer the question about the centre. The delegation is three or four, not yet fixed. Four extra seconds during a call buys a record that is as certain as the conversation was, and no more.

## Unit 3 · The Note Verbale

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which letters in your work follow a fixed form of words?
- What is the advantage of a form that never changes?
- What can hide inside a correct form?

A note verbale is the ordinary currency of relations between a ministry and an embassy, and its form has barely changed in a century. It is written in the third person throughout, so that neither side says I or you. It opens with compliments and closes with assurances of highest consideration, and between those two formulas it says one thing, briefly.

The form is strict for a reason. A note verbale is not a letter between two people but a communication between two states, and the officials who sign nothing are not the point of it. Because the form is fixed, an experienced reader can see the substance at once, and because the form is fixed, a departure from it is itself a signal.

In April the Ministry began drafting routine notes with a system trained on its own outgoing correspondence. The results were immediately good. Eleven notes were produced in one morning, all in the correct form, all with the right reference numbers, and all in a register that Tarek Zayed described as indistinguishable from the department’s own.

The third note concerned a request from the Verenian embassy to open a cultural centre. The Ministry had not yet taken a view, and the instruction to the drafter said to acknowledge the request. The note that came back acknowledged the request and added that the Ministry would be pleased to assist with the necessary arrangements.

Every word of that sentence appears in dozens of notes the Ministry has sent before, which is exactly why it appeared. The system had learnt what an acknowledgement usually looks like here, and in this house an acknowledgement is usually warm. The note was correct, conventional, and a commitment nobody had authorised.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A fixed form is empty on purpose, and a system trained on your habits will fill it with your habits**

**Figure 3.5** — Everything here is right except one sentence, and that sentence is the most ordinary one.

Formulaic correspondence is the easiest work in a ministry to give to a system and the easiest to get wrong, and both facts have the same cause. The form is fixed, the register is consistent, and there are thousands of past examples in the Ministry’s own files. A system trained on that material will produce notes that an experienced officer cannot distinguish from the department’s own.

That is a real saving, and it should be taken. A desk officer who writes eleven routine notes a week is spending several hours on a form of words that has not changed since before they were born. The form exists precisely so that nobody has to think about it, which makes it exactly the right thing to automate.

The danger lies in the gap between form and substance. A system learns what the Ministry’s notes look like, and what they look like includes the Ministry’s habits. If acknowledgements here are usually warm, a warm sentence is part of what an acknowledgement is, and it will appear without anybody choosing it. Nothing is invented, and a commitment arrives anyway.

The remedy is to instruct on content and not only on subject. An instruction that says acknowledge the request names the subject. An instruction that says acknowledge receipt and say nothing on the substance names the content, and the second kind cannot produce the third note. Writing the second kind takes one extra line.

There is a second rule, and it matters more than it sounds. The system may produce the formula and may never vary it. The opening and closing of a third-person note are empty on purpose, so that a reader goes straight to the middle; a system that improves them has turned an empty form into a message, and departures from the form are a signal the service relies on.

> **DATA POINT · Where ministries began** — Services that have deployed these systems report routine correspondence and translation among the first and least contested applications, with substantive policy drafting held back deliberately.  
> Source: reported practice in several foreign ministries, 2025–2026.

Omar Nasri has translated Verenian correspondence for twenty-two years, and he is now the reviser rather than the translator. The drafts arrive from a system and he reads them against the source. He says the work has changed completely and his job has not changed at all.

Verenian, like many languages, has no definite or indefinite article. A noun stands on its own, and whether it means one of several or the particular one is carried by the sentence around it, or by nothing at all. English cannot do this. In May a Verenian note proposed what the English draft called the establishment of the joint working group. Omar stopped at the second the. The system had not guessed carelessly. It had chosen the reading that is more common in the Ministry’s files, which is the existing group, because that group is mentioned far more often. Omar sent the note back to the embassy’s own English text, asked which was meant, and was told it was the new one.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A machine rendering is a first draft, and calling it a translation moves the responsibility to nobody**

**Figure 4.5** — Five choices the source does not make, made silently and plausibly five times.

Translation systems have become very good, and in a foreign ministry they do two things that are genuinely valuable. They let an officer read an incoming document in minutes instead of waiting a day for the queue, and they give a professional reviser a draft to work from instead of a blank page. Published accounts of ministries deploying them put translation among the earliest and least contested uses.

What they produce, however, is a rendering and not a translation, and the distinction is not pedantic. Every language forces choices that another language does not make. English forces an article on almost every noun; many languages have none. English separates obligation from prediction; some languages use one word for both. The source simply does not contain the answer.

Faced with a choice the source does not settle, a system chooses the likelier option, and likelier usually means commoner in the material it has seen. If a ministry’s files mention one particular working group forty times and a hypothetical new one never, the draft will say the working group. That is a sensible guess and it is still a guess.

The consequence is that a smooth rendering hides its own difficulties. A human translator meets each hard choice while working and can mark it, query it or footnote it. A finished draft arrives with every choice already made and no record of which ones were hard, so the reviser must go looking for them.

The practice that follows is simple to state. Use the rendering to understand, never to rely on. Send nothing out on a machine draft that creates an obligation. And where the two languages are known not to fit, check those places first and ask the other side in writing when the source will not settle the matter.

> **DATA POINT · Translation in diplomatic services** — Foreign ministries deploying these systems have reported multilingual translation and transcription among the first capabilities put into service, with professional revision retained rather than removed.  
> Source: reported practice in several foreign ministries and in UN language services, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Machine translation as a first draft, not a translation

**Figure 4.7** — The right-hand column is where a rendering becomes a position.

Use these systems for incoming material without hesitation. Reading a foreign note in two minutes rather than waiting a day for the queue is a real gain, and a reviser given a draft works faster than a reviser given a blank page. This is one of the places where the saving is obvious and the risk is manageable.

But never let the word translation be used for what comes back. A translation is a text somebody has taken responsibility for; a rendering is a text in which every difficult choice has been made by nobody. Head the document WORKING TRANSLATION, and make the heading a rule rather than a courtesy, because a document that leaves your desk without it will be quoted as though it were authoritative.

Then go to the places where your two languages are known not to fit, because those are where the silent choices are. Articles, if your language has none. Obligation against prediction, if one word does both. Number, register, titles. Check those first, mark each choice, and where a choice would create or remove an obligation, do not make it at all: ask the other side in writing and keep the answer on the file.

*Say it like this*
- Name what it is: **This is a working translation, not for reliance.**
- Mark the choice: **The source has no article here; both readings are possible.**
- Refuse the choice: **We have asked the embassy which is meant.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A rendering tells you what a document says where the two languages agree. It cannot tell you what the writer meant where they do not agree, it does not know which of its choices were difficult, and it reads equally fluently whether it was certain or guessing. Fluency is not evidence of fidelity.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Extraction is safe where a fact sits in one place, and unsafe where a fact has to be understood**

**Figure 5.5** — Four fields you can point at with one finger, and one you cannot.

Pulling particular facts out of a document is one of the four operations, and it is the one with the best ratio of saving to risk. A delegation list buried in three pages of continuous prose becomes a table in seconds, spellings intact and transliterations following the Ministry’s own glossary. An officer doing it by hand at eleven at night will be slower and will miss more.

The reliability, however, is not even across fields, and the division is worth learning because it is simple. Some facts sit in one place, written once, in one form: a surname, a date, a flight number, a sum of money. These are read, not interpreted, and extraction is very good at them.

Other facts are not written anywhere in particular. Rank, function, seniority and relationship are often carried by the shape of a passage rather than by a phrase, and they differ by language and by house style. Where one service writes the rank beside the name, another puts it in a subordinate clause several lines later, and a reader who does not know that will take the nearest words instead.

This gives a test that any officer can apply in a second. Could you point at this fact in the document with one finger? If yes, take it from the extraction. If no, somebody has read the passage and decided, and you must check who that somebody was and against what.

The practical consequence is a short list of fields that are always cross-checked, agreed in advance and written into the tasking. In a ministry that list almost always contains rank, title, precedence and anything that determines where a person sits, because those are the fields where an error is seen by everybody in the room and corrected by nobody.

> **DATA POINT · Document handling in diplomatic services** — Foreign ministries report document handling and information extraction among the earliest uses adopted, alongside translation, with checking retained for fields that determine protocol or entitlement.  
> Source: reported practice in several foreign ministries, 2025–2026.

- Who decides where an incoming letter goes in your ministry?

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Sorting is the easiest thing to automate and the hardest thing to automate honestly**

**Figure 6.5** — Three of the four wrong items had already been marked as uncertain, and nobody was reading the mark.

Sorting incoming material is the oldest work in a ministry and the easiest to describe. An item arrives, somebody decides which department must act, and the item goes there with a deadline attached. Four hundred items a morning is two hours of two people’s time, and a sorting step does the first pass in minutes at an accuracy that surprises everybody who tries it.

The accuracy figure, however, hides the shape of the errors, and the shape is what matters. A sorting step is wrong almost exclusively on items that belong in two places at once: a consular case written on embassy paper, a legal question inside a note about a visit, a citizen’s letter that cites a treaty. These are precisely the items on which being in the wrong pile costs the most.

The cause is in the question rather than in the system. Asked which department, it answers with one department, because that is the question. The two experienced officers it replaced answered a different question: they asked whether this was difficult, and when it was they walked down the corridor or sent two copies and marked one for information.

That behaviour was never written down, which is the general problem of replacing long experience. Twenty years of knowing when to ask somebody does not exist as a document, and it does not appear in any description of the job. It is discovered only when it stops happening, usually in the form of four items in one week.

The remedy has two halves and both are needed. Write explicit rules for the cases you have actually seen, so that known double-belonging items are copied to both departments. Then read the uncertain cases by hand, because a system that reports low confidence is telling you something, and in most ministries nobody has been given the job of reading it.

> **DATA POINT · Routing and triage in foreign services** — Services deploying triage and routing report high first-pass accuracy with errors concentrated in cases spanning two functions, and retain human review of flagged items.  
> Source: reported practice in foreign ministries and consular services, 2025–2026.

**Figure 7.5** — Four facts on the file, four correct steps, and two facts that live in six heads.

A system given a file can read all of it, which is more than any officer does. It will notice a matter raised in 2021 and never closed, connect it to a meeting date, and propose the obvious step. Every link in that chain is correct, and the proposal can still be wrong in a way nobody on the file could see.

The reason is that a ministry runs on two bodies of knowledge. The first is written: notes, records, instructions, reports. The second is not written at all. It is the set of reasons why things are done in a particular way here, learned from a predecessor, from a dinner, from having once got it wrong in front of somebody who explained.

The unwritten body is large and nobody knows how large, because its items are invisible until something touches them. An officer cannot list what they know in this way, any more than they can list the words they can spell. It surfaces only when a proposal runs into it, and before these systems arrived the proposal was usually made by somebody who already knew.

That is the change worth noticing. When the first draft came from a junior officer, the gap appeared as a question in a corridor and was filled in a sentence. When the first draft arrives complete, confident and well sourced, it does not look like a question, and it may reach a Director or a Minister before anybody who holds the unwritten reason reads it.

The use, then, is to treat the proposals as a survey of your own gaps. A confident draft that is wrong for an unwritten reason has located that reason for you, and the reason can be written down in one sentence by the person who holds it. Several ministries have found this to be the most valuable and least expected result of the whole exercise.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The same instruction gives different drafts, and only some of the differences cost anything**

**Figure 8.5** — Five places, one of which is a commitment and one of which never varied at all.

Ask for the same draft twice and you will get two different documents. This surprises people the first time and it is not a fault: these systems are not built to repeat themselves, and nothing in them is trying to. Two drafts from one tasking will share their substance and differ in their wording, their order and their length.

Most of that variation is free. Whether a note runs to three paragraphs or four, whether two middle points come in one order or the other, whether a sentence begins with the date or the subject: none of this costs the Ministry anything, and insisting on uniformity would cost time for no return.

Some of the variation is not free at all. A sentence that gives a date where the last draft said in due course has created a commitment. A sentence that offers assistance has created an expectation. A phrase that characterises another state’s position has created a record of what we think they think. These must be identical every time, because they are not wording, they are substance.

The practical division is therefore between sentences that carry obligation, courtesy or characterisation, and everything else. The first group comes from a standard form or directly from the instruction, and is not drafted at all. The second group may vary freely, and should, because the time spent making it uniform buys nothing.

There is a second use for the variation, and experienced officers find it quickly. Ask twice and read the two drafts against each other: where they differ, the system was uncertain, and that is where to look first. The method finds instability rather than error, and two drafts that agree may be agreeing on something wrong that was in the file all along.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Why one instruction produces two different drafts

**Figure 8.7** — The left-hand column is style. The right-hand column is policy wearing style’s clothes.

Expect two drafts from one tasking to differ, and stop treating that as a defect. These systems are not built to repeat themselves, and most of what varies between two drafts is length, order and ordinary wording, none of which costs the Ministry anything. Insisting on uniformity across all of it would buy nothing and take time.

Then separate the sentences that are not style at all. A date where the other draft said in due course is a commitment. An offer of help is an expectation. A phrase describing another state’s position is a record of what we believe they think. These three kinds of sentence must be identical every time, which means they come from a standard form or straight from the instruction, and are not drafted.

Finally, use the variation. Ask twice for anything that leaves the building and read the two against each other: where they differ, the system was unsure, and that is where to look first. Be careful what you conclude from agreement, though, because two drafts can agree perfectly on something wrong that was in the file all along.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A system cannot say that it does not know, so the safeguard must be a source and not a warning**

**Figure 9.5** — The unsourced row is the one that was quoted, because it was the most useful.

These systems produce text rather than retrieve it. That sentence is the whole of the matter and it is worth reading twice. When a question is asked, the answer is composed from what such an answer usually looks like, not assembled from documents that have been found and opened.

The consequence is that there is no state corresponding to not knowing. A person asked for a citation they do not have will hesitate, qualify, or say they will check. A system has nothing to hesitate with. If it has the period, the names and the subject, it will produce a well-formed citation, and that citation may describe a document that has never existed.

Such a fabrication is not careless and does not read as careless. It arrives in the same register, at the same length, with the same confident structure as the paragraphs around it, which are correct. Readers are not failing to notice something noticeable; there is nothing in the prose to notice.

This is why instructions to be careful do not work and why every ministry that has thought about the problem has ended up in the same place. The safeguard cannot be attention, because attention has nothing to catch. It has to be a rule about sources: every fact that will leave the building names where it came from, in a form a reader can go and look at.

The rule needs its second half to be a rule at all. A fact that cannot be corroborated is removed, even when it is probably true, and is listed at the foot of the brief as removed and not corroborated. That list is useful in itself, because it tells the next officer exactly which questions are still open.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Why it does not say that it does not know

**Figure 9.7** — The right-hand column has no hesitation in it, because hesitation is not a thing it can do.

Start from the mechanism rather than from the warning. These systems compose text rather than look things up, so an answer is built from what such an answer usually looks like. There is no internal state that corresponds to not knowing, and therefore no behaviour that corresponds to admitting it.

Accept what follows for your own checking. A fabricated citation arrives in the same register, at the same length and with the same confidence as the true paragraphs beside it, and the names and dates in it will often be real. You are not failing to notice a warning sign; there is no warning sign in the prose at all.

So make the safeguard structural. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A reason about capability expires; a reason about answerability does not**

**Figure 10.5** — Four reasons about ability, none of which survived. Two about answerability, both of which did.

Every ministry that has begun using these systems has drawn up, formally or informally, a list of work that is not to be given to them. The lists are usually written quickly, they are usually sensible, and they are usually justified in a way that will not last the year.

The common justification is capability. We do not delegate this because a system cannot judge tone, cannot weigh conflicting reports, does not know which silences are deliberate. These statements are typically true when written, and that is precisely the problem: a rule resting on what something cannot do has an expiry date attached, and the date will be announced by somebody selling the replacement.

Worse, the rule will be revisited at the worst possible moment. A capability claim is challenged by a demonstration, and a demonstration arrives with a salesman, a deadline and an audience. Deciding a question of principle in that room is not a decision; it is a reaction.

The reason that holds is answerability. For some decisions, a named person must be able to explain themselves to somebody outside the Ministry: the state whose note went unanswered, the citizen whose case was decided, the Minister who was briefed. A process cannot be answerable to anybody, however well it performs, and that is not a statement about performance at all.

Rewriting a list on this basis makes it much shorter, and the shortening is the point. Four items with reasons that hold are worth more than ten with reasons that expire, and the exercise also tells you which items you are protecting out of habit rather than principle. Where you cannot write the reason, say so on the page, so that the next person knows which entries were certain.

*The gap.* "Where they are not good enough yet" names the kind of reason in advance, "the obvious areas" asks for areas rather than decisions, "a short reason" sets no test for what counts as a reason, and "keep it to a page" is the only measurement anywhere.

The word yet in instruction two did the damage. It told the reader that the reasons were to be about current capability.

**STAGE 4 · The Diagnosis**

- **unbounded quantity** — quantifiers and stated measures
- **undefined done** — modality of requirement: shall, is to, must
- **unscoped set** — defining relative clauses and articles
- **unstated criterion** — comparatives and superlatives with a stated basis

3. [4] "a short reason for each" — of what kind?
4. [5] "keep it to a page" — finished meaning drafted, or agreed?
5. [6] no instruction about what remains permitted
