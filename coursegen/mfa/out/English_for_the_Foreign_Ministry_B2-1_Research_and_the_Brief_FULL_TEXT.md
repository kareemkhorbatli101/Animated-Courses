In October the Minister’s office asked Policy Planning a single question: is Kavara building a naval facility at its southern port, and if so, why. Dr Hana Mourad had six working days and two officers.

Her section used an open-source research step over published material, and within two days it had assembled a great deal. Dredging had been under way at the southern basin since March; the dredged depth, visible in dated imagery, had gone from nine to fourteen metres; a tender notice in the official gazette of April named works described as harbour deepening and quay reinforcement; customs figures showed a rise in imported steel sections of a kind used for heavy quays.

All of that is checkable. Each item has a source that an officer in the Ministry can open, with a date on it, and each can be wrong in a way somebody can discover. Fourteen metres is either right or it is not.

The second half of the question is not checkable in the same way, and this was the point at which the section nearly produced something it would have regretted. Fourteen metres is deeper than any commercial vessel calling at that port requires. It suggests a military purpose. It does not establish one, and the published material contains nothing about what Kavara intends, because intentions are not published.

Samir Haddad, reading the draft, objected to one sentence: Kavara is seeking to establish a naval presence. He asked what document that rested on. The answer was that it rested on the depth, which is to say on an inference the drafter had made and the sentence did not admit to making. The brief went up with the sentence rewritten and the inference marked as the section’s own.

**Grammar: hedging with appears, suggests and is reported to**

- It appears that and it would appear that mark the writer’s own reading of evidence.
- X suggests Y attributes the inference to the evidence, not to the writer.
- Is reported to have attributes the claim to somebody else, unnamed in the sentence.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Open-source research establishes activity, dates and quantities; it does not establish intention**

**Figure 1.5** — The last line is not a failure of research. It is the boundary of the method.

Open-source research has become the first move in almost every foreign ministry’s analytical work, and the reason is not that published material has grown more revealing. It is that the volume of it has grown beyond what a section of three officers can read, while the capability to search, translate and extract from it has become cheap. A question that would have taken a fortnight of reading now takes two days.

What the method establishes is a particular class of thing: activity, dates, quantities, wording. Dredging either began in March or it did not, and dated imagery from two passes settles it. A tender notice either described quay reinforcement or it did not, and the official gazette settles that. Customs figures either rose or they did not. Each of these claims has a source an officer can open and each can be shown to be wrong, which is what makes it worth writing down.

What the method does not establish is intention, and this is not a limitation that better searching removes. States do not publish their intentions, and the documents that record them are precisely the documents that are not in the public record. A brief can say that a dredged depth exceeds the requirement of any vessel now calling at the port, which is checkable against the port’s own published limits. It cannot say what the depth is for.

The failure that follows is predictable and it is a failure of writing rather than of research. A section that has established five checkable things and been asked a sixth question will write a sentence that reads like a finding and is in fact an inference: Kavara is seeking to establish a naval presence. Nobody has lied. An inference has simply been given the grammar of a fact, and a Minister who quotes it will be asked for evidence that does not exist.

The practice that holds, across services that do this work seriously, is to mark every sentence in a brief as one of three things: established and sourced, inferred by the section from stated evidence, or not available by these means. The third category is the one offices are most reluctant to write and the one that protects them, because a gap the brief names cannot be filled by whoever is most confident.

> **DATA POINT · Open-source research in foreign ministries** — Analytical units across several foreign services expanded open-source collection and automated extraction through 2025, while tightening requirements that each assertion in a brief carry a source or be marked as the unit’s own assessment.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Open-source research and its boundary

**Figure 1.7** — The left column is checkable and quotable. The right column is where briefs get into trouble.

Begin with what open-source research is for. The volume of published material now exceeds what any section can read, and searching, translating and extracting from it has become cheap, which means a question that took a fortnight of reading takes two days. That is the whole of the gain and it is a large one.

Be exact about what it yields. It yields activity, dates, quantities and wording: the dredging began in March on two dated passes of imagery; the April gazette described quay reinforcement; imported steel sections rose by a published percentage. Each of those has a source an officer can open and each can be shown wrong, which is the test of whether a claim belongs in a brief at all.

Then hold the boundary, because this is where the work fails. Intention is not published, and no amount of better searching will produce it. A section that has established five things and been asked a sixth will write an inference in the grammar of a fact, and the Minister who quotes it will be asked for evidence that does not exist. So mark every sentence as sourced, as the section’s inference from stated evidence, or as not available by these means, and write the third kind even though it is the one nobody wants to sign.

*Say it like this*
- Source it: **Dated imagery of 3 and 19 March; the gazette of 14 April.**
- Mark the inference: **In the section’s assessment, on the depth alone, this suggests …**
- Name the gap: **The purpose of the works is not available from published material.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Open-source material records what leaves a trace. Intention leaves none, and a confident sentence about intention is not research but writing. The method cannot tell a drafter that an inference has been given the grammar of a fact; only a reader asking what the sentence rests on will catch it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Counting sources is easy; establishing that they are independent is the work**

**Figure 2.5** — Four steps, one origin, and at step four it is described as two.

Every analytical unit has a rule about how many sources a claim needs before it may be stated rather than reported. The rules are short, they are followed, and they are almost all written as though the difficulty were counting. It is not. The difficulty is establishing that what you have counted are separate origins.

The mechanism by which a chain becomes a confirmation is entirely ordinary and involves no bad faith at any step. An evening bulletin carries a claim from an official it does not name. A wire agency reports the bulletin, naming it. A foreign newspaper reports the agency, naming it. An analyst reads the newspaper and the agency, four hours apart, and has what looks exactly like two reports. Nothing in either document says that one is the other at a remove, because each has attributed honestly to the step immediately behind it.

Verification capability has changed what is possible here, and this is one of the places where the gain is largest. Tracing a claim backwards through published material used to take an afternoon of telephone calls and often failed; it can now be done in minutes across languages, which means the question "does this reduce to one origin?" can actually be asked of every claim rather than of the ones somebody found suspicious.

What follows is a two-part rule rather than a number. Two sources count as independent only where neither can be traced to the other and neither can be traced to a common origin. And where the chain cannot be traced at all, the claim is reported as a claim, whatever the number of outlets carrying it. The second half is harder to live with than the first, because it means a brief will often carry claims where the office would prefer statements.

One gap remains and honest units record it. Two genuinely separate origins can both carry a claim that is false, where somebody has placed it with each of them. No counting rule reaches that. What reaches it is an analyst noticing that a convenient claim has arrived twice in a week, which is judgement, and judgement is not improved by pretending the rule covers it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A fabricated answer has no internal sign of being wrong, so it must be checked from outside**

**Figure 3.5** — The page passes every test that can be applied to the page.

The most discussed risk in this field is fabrication, and the discussion is usually unhelpful because it treats the matter as one of reliability. The question is not how often a confident answer is wrong. The question is what a wrong one looks like, because that determines whether any procedure can catch it.

What it looks like is this. The language is correct and in register. The structure is the structure a competent officer would use. The detail is specific: years, article numbers, figures to a decimal place, short quotations in the right idiom. There is no hedging, no vagueness and no internal contradiction, because a fabricated citation is not a reasoning error and reasoning errors are what internal inspection detects.

From that one fact everything practical follows. A failure with no internal sign cannot be caught by rereading, by a second opinion on the text, or by a more careful officer. It can only be caught by opening something outside the answer: the instrument, the article, the gazette, the figure at its published source. An officer who checks by rereading is performing a ritual, and a section whose safeguard is the seniority of the reader has no safeguard at all.

Two practices follow, and services that take this seriously run both. The first is a rule at the point of use: a citation quoted is a citation opened, and where it cannot be opened the brief says so and somebody senior decides whether to carry it. The second is measurement: a set of questions whose answers the ministry already holds, put through the same process unmarked and mixed with ordinary work, with the error rate published by question type rather than as an average. The average is of little use; the failure mode is stable and tells an officer which kinds of question to distrust.

The limitation of the second practice deserves stating rather than hiding. A known-answer test measures performance on questions that can be scored, which are by definition questions somebody has already answered. The questions that matter are the ones nobody has answered. Generalising from the first set to the second may be reasonable; it is not established, and a unit that records the gap in its own minute will be believed later by people who would otherwise think it had been concealed.

> **DATA POINT · Fabrication testing** — Analytical units in several foreign services introduced unmarked known-answer questions into live research work in 2025, publishing error rates by question type rather than as a single figure.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic analytical quality control, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Assembly produces background in a night; objectives and the line to take are decisions and cannot be retrieved**

**Figure 4.5** — Four sections out of six. The two that failed are the two a Minister actually uses.

A country brief is among the clearest wins available in this field, and the reason is that most of it is retrieval. A political overview is a summary of published material. A trade profile is official statistics arranged. Outstanding issues, previous commitments and pending requests are in the ministry’s own files with dates and references. Personality notes are a compilation from both. None of this requires a judgement that has not already been made, and all of it is tedious, which is exactly the combination the capability suits.

The scale of the gain is easy to understate because the comparison is usually drawn wrongly. A ministry does not compare an assembled brief with a perfect brief, because it never had the officers to write a perfect brief for eleven countries in nine days. It compares an assembled brief with no brief, and against that the result is not a convenience but a change in what is possible.

Two sections do not assemble, and they are the two a Minister reads in the car. Objectives and the line to take are not in any file, and the reason is not that the files are incomplete. A position is what the ministry holds and is therefore recorded. An objective is what this side wants out of ninety minutes with one person on a particular afternoon, and nobody has decided it until somebody decides it.

Asked to produce such a section anyway, the method does what it must: it generalises from positions and produces careful, plausible, interchangeable prose. Eleven briefs will say strengthen relations, deepen cooperation and address outstanding issues, in eleven sets of words. If those sections were left blank somebody would fill them. Because they are full, nobody does, and that is the whole of the harm.

The division that works is simple to state and requires somebody to insist on it: the ministry assembles the four retrievable sections, and a named officer or the post writes the two decided ones and signs them. Where the brief does not say which sections were written by a person, nobody will be able to tell in two years, and the next office to inherit the system will assume the whole brief assembled itself.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A chronology establishes order only where every entry has a time, and a converted one**

**Figure 5.5** — Two entries move when the clock is made common, and two cannot be placed at all.

Chronology is one of the oldest instruments of the trade and one of the most laborious. Establishing who said what in what order used to mean a week of reading in several languages, and the result was often a sequence a desk officer half remembered. Assembling thirty entries with dates, sources and references now takes a couple of hours, which means the question of order can be asked whenever it matters rather than only when somebody has a week.

The first thing to get right is the clock. Every published statement carries a time in the time zone of whoever published it, and an assembly that records the published time has done exactly what was asked and produced a sequence that can be wrong by a working day. The practice is two columns: the local time as published, and a converted time on a single stated clock. Dropping the local time looks tidier and causes an officer checking against the original to believe the Ministry has misquoted.

The second thing is harder and gets less attention. A chronology is a list of lines that look alike, and some of those lines rest on a timestamp while others rest on somebody’s judgement about where an undated item belongs. Nothing in the appearance of the document distinguishes them. A sequence of thirty-one entries in which three are placed by inference reads as a sequence of thirty-one established facts.

The remedy that offices reach for first is to exclude undated material, and it is usually wrong. A chronology of a dispute that omits one side’s only ministry statement because the statement bears no hour is less useful than one that carries it in a marked block. What works is a separate undated section, items dated to the day and not placed within it, and an instruction that anybody arguing from an undated entry says so.

And the limit of the whole instrument deserves saying, because its outputs are persuasive. An order is not a cause. Showing that the Ministry’s note preceded Kavara’s statement disposes of the claim that the note was a response; it does not show that Kavara’s statement was a response to the note. A chronology that is used to argue causation is being used for something it cannot do, however exact its columns.

> **DATA POINT · Chronology building** — Analytical units in several foreign services adopted assisted chronology assembly in 2025, with requirements for a single stated reference clock and separate treatment of undated material.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Monitoring counts published words across more titles than anyone could read; it does not measure opinion**

**Figure 6.5** — One reported figure, five unreported ones, and the five are the story.

Media monitoring is work no press section has ever been able to do properly. Forty titles in five languages cannot be read every morning by four officers, and the sections that tried read the three they knew and inferred the rest. Assembling a daily report across the whole set, with volumes, themes, recurring phrases and tonal movement, is now routine, and the gain is real: a press section can see what it is actually being written about rather than what it happens to read.

The first trap is concentration. A rise of four hundred per cent sounds like a change in a country’s press and may be a change in two newspapers, one of which reprinted the other. The remedy is not subtle. Report volume, the number of distinct titles, the number of distinct owners, and the baseline the rise is measured from, because four hundred per cent of four items is nineteen items and nineteen items is a sentence, not a shift.

The second trap is the sentiment score. A number from minus one to one, produced daily, carries the authority of measurement without the properties of it: a press section usually cannot say who built the scale or what it was validated against, and its usual defence is that the movement matches what its officers would have said by reading. That defence is also the objection, since a measure that agrees with your judgement adds nothing, and on the day it disagrees you have no way of knowing which to believe. The practice that holds is to use such scores for direction and never for level, and to keep the number out of briefs while putting the direction in words.

The third limit is the one that matters most and admits no remedy. Monitoring counts published words. What a ministry wants to know is whether coverage is changing what anybody thinks or does, and counting words does not bear on that except by assumption. Coverage is one of the places opinion is formed; the others are conversations, institutions and experience, none of which is published and none of which is monitored.

An honest monitoring report therefore states what it measured and declines the larger claim. The risk of not doing so is specific and well known across press sections: a ministry that reports what it can count will in time manage what it can count, and will begin treating a rise in two newspapers as a problem to be solved.

> **DATA POINT · Narrative monitoring** — Foreign ministry press sections scaled automated media monitoring across many more titles and languages in 2025, and several added distinct-title and distinct-owner counts after concentrated coverage was reported as broad shifts.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic press monitoring, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A pattern without a base rate is a description; the base rate is the work**

**Figure 7.5** — Forty-two cases in a year. The July pattern belongs to the last row, which holds a quarter of them.

Pattern detection across a monitoring archive is one of the genuinely new things available to an analytical unit. A phrase with no history appearing in eleven titles across four jurisdictions in six days is the kind of fact nobody could previously have established, because nobody could read eleven titles in four languages and remember what had not been said in the preceding year.

The capability therefore produces a stream of patterns, and every one of them looks meaningful, because a pattern is by construction an arrangement that stands out. This is where analytical units get into difficulty, and the difficulty is not technical. It is that a pattern is only informative against a background, and the background is usually unknown to the people reading the pattern.

The missing quantity has a name. A base rate is how often the thing you are looking at happens in the ordinary course of events, when nothing is being coordinated and nobody is doing anything. Until you know it, a pattern is a description of what you found and not a finding about the world. The July phrase appeared in eleven titles in six days with no history, and so did forty-one other phrases that year, of which thirty-one had causes as ordinary as a court judgment or a football result.

Establishing a base rate is work and it is work the capability can do. The count took an hour across a year of archive. The examination of each case, asking whether something ordinary explains it, took a day of an officer’s time and could not be skipped, because a raw count of forty-two would have been as misleading in the other direction.

What the base rate does and does not settle is worth being exact about. It did not show that the July phrase was uncoordinated. It showed that the pattern was one of about eleven unexplained cases a year, which means either that such patterns arise ordinarily or that the Ministry has eleven campaigns a year it has never examined. Both are live. What the base rate removed was the word assessed, and the honest form of the note gives the pattern, gives the rate, and declines the conclusion.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A figure in a brief is unusable without a comparator, a definition, a basis and a status**

**Figure 8.5** — One correctly taken figure, six true statements, and five different meetings.

Statistics reach briefs faster than they used to. A trade profile that took a desk officer a morning of looking up series now assembles in minutes, with the figures correctly taken from official publications. The accuracy of the extraction is not the problem and has stopped being interesting.

The problem is that a figure alone carries an implication, and the implication is supplied by the reader. Trade rose thirty-one per cent is true, correctly sourced and, standing by itself in a trade profile, reads as growth. If the preceding year fell forty-eight per cent, the honest description is a partial recovery from a collapse, and the two years together leave trade lower than before.

Four neighbours are needed and none of them can be inferred from the figure. A comparator: against what, over what period. A definition: what exactly is counted, since goods-only and goods-and-services series differ by a great deal and look identical in a sentence. A basis: nominal or at constant prices, which over a period of rising prices is the difference between a real rise and none. And a status: provisional, revised or final, because a figure that was accurate on the sixth may have been revised on the ninth and the document will never say so.

The second half of the discipline is the language of confidence, and offices are usually worse at this than at the figures. Briefs accumulate believed, thought, appears, understood and probably, used interchangeably, so that a reader has no way to distinguish a published figure from a corridor remark. The practice that works is a short closed set of levels, commonly four: established, meaning sourced and openable; likely, meaning the balance of named evidence; possible, meaning consistent with the evidence and not supported by it; and not known.

Not known is the level offices resist and the one that earns its place. A ministry that can write not known in a brief gives its Minister something no other formulation does: a clear instruction to decline. The alternative is a comfortable phrase that commits nobody and, in the room, will be read as a position.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A reference is adequate when a reader who has never seen the document can open the passage in a minute**

**Figure 9.5** — Eight extra words, and the difference between a reference that is followed and one that is not.

Sourcing rules are among the first things an analytical unit adopts once research is being assisted, and they are usually written as a form: every assertion carries a reference, and the reference gives a title and a year. The rule is followed, the references are accurate, and nothing improves, which puzzles the office that wrote it.

The reason is that a reference has a purpose and the form does not serve it. A reference exists so that a reader can open the passage, read it and put the document down. If opening it takes nine minutes, because a title matches four documents in the registry and the passage is somewhere in a forty-page annex, then a busy reader will stop following references, and a brief whose references are never followed is no better sourced than one with no references at all.

The standard that works is therefore a test rather than a form: a reference is adequate if somebody who has never seen the document can open the exact passage within a minute. In practice that means a registry file number rather than a title, which identifies a document rather than a class of documents; a paragraph or page reference rather than a reference to the whole; and the version and date, because a document cleared in June may read differently from the draft of May.

This costs something and the cost should be stated. Footnotes roughly double in length and a brief grows by about a tenth. Whereas a form can be complied with cheaply, a test has to be applied, which means somebody has to try one reference and see whether it works. The practice that holds is for the checker, not the drafter, to pick a reference at random and record on the face of the brief which one was opened and how long it took.

The effect worth having is not the Director’s time, although that is what the argument is usually made about. It is that a drafter who expects a particular sentence to be opened writes that sentence differently, and where nobody expects any sentence to be opened, the sourcing rule is decoration which the office has mistaken for a control.

> **DATA POINT · Sourcing standards** — Analytical units in several foreign services replaced form-based citation rules with usability tests in 2025, requiring that a named reference be opened and timed during clearance.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A brief that establishes everything and selects nothing moves the judgement to the worst possible place**

**Figure 10.5** — The same forty-one facts. One document is complete and the other is usable.

Once an analytical unit has sourcing, verification, base rates and graded confidence, it becomes capable of producing documents in which every sentence is defensible. This is a real achievement and it introduces the last failure in the sequence, which is the hardest to see because nothing in the document is wrong.

A brief exists to serve a decision. A six-page document containing forty-one sourced assertions about a subject serves the subject, which is a different thing. The reader who has a meeting on Thursday must work out which assertions bear on Thursday, and that work is a judgement requiring knowledge of the decision, performed in whatever time is left, usually by somebody more senior and more rushed than the drafter.

The usual defence of the complete brief is a principled one and it is worth taking seriously: an analytical section establishes what is true and the choice belongs to the person who must answer for it. The reply is that the section has already chosen. Deciding which forty-one facts to establish out of the thousands that could have been established is an act of selection, performed earlier, by the same people, and recorded nowhere. Treating the invisible selection as neutral and the visible one as interference is not impartiality; it is the same act, done where nobody can argue with it.

What the practice requires is therefore narrower than a recommendation and goes further than a list. State the decision as the section understands it, which allows a reader to correct a misunderstanding in one line. Name the facts that bear on it, and what follows from each. State what is not known. Say what follows if nothing is done, because silence is the commonest course and the least analysed. And annex the rest, so nothing established is lost.

The grammar matters at this point more than anywhere else in a brief, because the one-page form puts facts, inferences and opinions next to each other. Each has to be marked inside its own sentence: the fact with its reference, the inference with the section’s assessment named as such, the opinion with in the section’s view. A reader must be able to tell, in every sentence, what the Ministry can be held to and what is the section’s reading of it, and clearly and obviously will not do that work. They are the words a drafter reaches for when the marking is missing.

> **DATA POINT · Briefing for decisions** — Analytical units in several foreign services adopted decision-led brief formats in 2025, requiring the decision, the material facts and the consequence of inaction on the first page, with supporting material annexed.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic briefing standards, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Selection, and the brief that is accurate and useless

**Figure 10.7** — The same facts in both columns. Only the right-hand one has been thought about.

With sourcing, verification and graded confidence in place, a section can produce a document in which every sentence is defensible, and will then produce the last failure in the series: a brief that is entirely accurate and does not help. Nothing in it is wrong, which is why it survives review.

The cause is that a brief serves a decision while a complete document serves a subject. Forty-one sourced assertions about transit do not tell a Director which four bear on Thursday, so she performs that selection herself, late, under pressure, having read six pages twice. The principled defence, that the section establishes and the Minister chooses, does not survive one question: who chose which forty-one facts to establish? The selection happened earlier, invisibly, and calling that impartiality mistakes where the judgement is for whether it occurred.

So select visibly and keep the line that matters. State the decision as you understand it, which lets a reader correct you in one line. Name the facts that bear on it, with what follows from each. Say what is not known, and say what follows if nothing is done, since silence is the course most often taken and least often examined. Annex everything else. And mark each element inside its sentence, the fact with its reference, the inference as the section’s assessment, the opinion as the section’s view, because a one-page brief puts all three within a line of each other and clearly will not tell them apart.

*Say it like this*
- State the decision: **The decision for Thursday, as the section understands it, is …**
- Mark the three: **The basin is 14 metres (file 2019/114, para 11), which the section assesses to exceed commercial need; in the section’s view the Ministry should not raise it.**
- Analyse silence: **If the Ministry says nothing, Astrene moves within 6 weeks.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A brief cannot be both complete and selective, and completeness is the easier of the two to defend in review. Selection requires knowing the decision, which means a section that declines to select has not avoided the judgement; it has moved it to the reader with the least time. No method supplies the decision, and a brief written without one serves whatever question the reader brings.
