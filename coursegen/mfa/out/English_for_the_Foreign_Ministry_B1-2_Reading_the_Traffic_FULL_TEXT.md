### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A summary of a document and a summary of what the document is for are not the same thing**

**Figure 1.5** — Five sections of fifty-nine pages survived. The one page that was the purpose did not.

Summarising is the capability most ministries adopt first after translation, and the arithmetic behind it is unarguable. A post sends sixty pages; four people read it; forty need to know what is in it. A summarising step turns the sixty pages into one, accurately, in about a minute, and the page is usually better organised than a tired officer would manage.

What such a step does is proportionate. It reads the whole document and gives each part weight according to how much of the document that part occupies. Fourteen pages on trade become three lines; sixteen pages on the border become three lines; and one paragraph becomes nothing, because one paragraph in sixty pages is a rounding error.

Official documents are not written proportionately. A report from a post is usually an argument with a single request at the end of it, and the length is there to make the request credible rather than to indicate its importance. The request may be one paragraph in sixty pages precisely because it is difficult, and difficult requests are made with a great deal of context in front of them.

The remedy is to ask a different question. Do not ask what is in this document; ask what the writer wants, and require the summary to answer that in its first line, with the reasons the writer gave and in the number they gave them. Everything else in the summary then takes its place underneath.

There is a second-order point that experienced officers raise. A request separated from its context can read more sharply than it was meant, and a summary that gives the ask without the reasoning may make a cautious Ambassador sound demanding. Carrying the reasons is therefore not a courtesy to the writer; it is part of reporting accurately what was said.

> **DATA POINT · Summarisation in diplomatic reporting** — Foreign ministries report summarisation of incoming reporting among the earliest capabilities adopted, with review retained because generated summaries weight material by length rather than by significance.  
> Source: reported practice in several foreign ministries, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Summarising a long report without losing the point

**Figure 1.7** — The same sixty pages. The right-hand column is what the Ambassador wrote them for.

Use summarising freely, because the arithmetic is overwhelming: four officers read the sixty pages and forty need to know what is in them. The page that comes back will be accurate and better ordered than a late-evening draft of your own, and the time saved is measured in hours a week rather than minutes.

Then notice what a summary weights by. It gives space in proportion to space, so fourteen pages on trade get more room than one paragraph of recommendation. Official documents are not written in that proportion at all: a post report is an argument with a request at the end, and the length exists to make the request credible.

So change the question you ask. Do not ask what is in this document; ask what the writer wants, and reserve the first line of every summary for the answer, with the reasons they gave and in the number they gave them. Where there is no request, say that there is none, rather than letting the most emphatic passage stand in for one.

*Say it like this*
- Reserve the line: **Line one says what the writer wants.**
- Keep the number: **Three reasons, because he gave three.**
- Say when there is none: **This review makes no recommendation.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A summary tells you what a document says, weighted by how much of the document says it. It cannot tell you which paragraph the writer cared about, it cannot see the argument the length is making, and a request separated from its reasoning will read more sharply than it was written.

Yara Ibrahim produces a press digest every Monday. It covers Verenia, Oristan and Kavara, in four languages, and until this year it took her two days. Since March a monitoring step reads the week’s output and produces a draft digest by seven on Monday morning.

The gain is large and nobody disputes it. Four languages, perhaps two hundred items, and a draft on the desk before the building opens. Yara now spends her Monday checking and rewriting rather than reading, and the digest reaches the Director two days earlier than it used to.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A quantifier with no base sounds like a measurement, and a digest will be quoted as one**

**Figure 2.5** — Three numbers were needed to understand one word, and the digest gave none of them.

Reading foreign press is work that these systems do extremely well, and the gain in a ministry is not marginal. Four languages, two hundred items, and a draft digest on the desk before the building opens, where the same work took an officer two days. The digest also reaches the Director two days earlier, which is worth more than the hours saved.

The risk in a digest is not inaccuracy but imprecision, and the two are easy to confuse. A sentence saying that most outlets were critical can be perfectly true and still tell a reader nothing, because the reader does not know most of what. Three of five monitored, out of thirty in the country, is a very different picture from twenty-two of thirty.

This matters more than it would in an internal note, because a digest is quoted. A Minister reads the page before a meeting and says that the Verenian press has been critical. The sentence has then left the building, and the base it rested on — five outlets chosen six years ago — has not gone with it.

The remedy has two parts. Write figures wherever a figure exists: three of five, not most. And state the base at the top of the digest, so that every sentence below it is read against the right denominator: we read five outlets of about thirty; these five were selected in 2018.

That second line is uncomfortable, and it should be. A monitored set chosen years ago by somebody who has since retired is a very common arrangement, and the digest is the only place where the arrangement becomes visible. Making it visible is not a criticism of the digest; it is the first step in reviewing a selection nobody has looked at since it was made.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Triage makes a senior person better informed about less, and the question is who reads the rest**

**Figure 3.5** — Attention has become deeper and narrower, and the skimmed layer has gone entirely.

Triage is among the most immediately useful things a ministry can do with these systems, and the figures are striking. A Director who received ninety items a day and read thirty now receives eleven and reads all of them. For the first time the senior person is fully informed about everything that reaches them, which has not been true in most ministries for a generation.

What has changed, however, is not only the volume. The old arrangement had three layers: read properly, skimmed, never reached. Skimming is a weak form of attention and it is easy to disparage, but it catches things, and a filter removes it entirely. Everything is now either read in full or not seen at all.

The items that fall out are the ones nothing in them marks. A ranking works on what an item contains: its length, its form, its urgency marking, the words in it. An item is important when it connects to something the reader knows and the item does not say — a conversation in March, a hint from a counterpart, a name that means something to one person.

Watchlists help and they are structurally late. Adding consular agreement to the list after a note about a consular agreement was missed will catch the next note about consular agreements. It will not catch the next item that matters for a reason nobody has written down, and that is the category the triage removed.

So the serious question is not the threshold but the second list. In most places nobody has been given the job of reading what the filter held back, and the list is opened a handful of times a year by whoever built it. Twenty minutes a day of somebody reading headings is the only part of the old skimming layer that can be deliberately rebuilt.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Triage: what reaches the Director and what does not

**Figure 3.7** — The middle layer was weak and it was not nothing, and it is the layer that vanished.

Use triage, because the gain is one most ministries have wanted for a generation. A senior person who read a third of what arrived now reads all of it, and the difference in the quality of their decisions is visible within a month. Nobody who has had this arrangement wants to go back.

Then notice which layer has disappeared. The old pile had three: read, skimmed, missed. Skimming is weak attention and it is real attention, and a filter does not produce less skimming — it produces none. Everything is now read in full or not seen, and the item that falls out is the item nothing in it marks.

A ranking can only work on what an item contains, and items matter for reasons that live outside them: a conversation in March, a name that means something to one person. Twenty minutes a day of somebody reading headings is the only honest replacement for the layer you removed.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A transcript records utterances; a record of conversation records what happened, and only one of them is useful**

**Figure 4.5** — Two of six things are in both. Three are in only one, and they are the three that decided it.

Transcription is among the first capabilities a foreign service adopts, and the reason is straightforward. A delegate who has spoken for twenty minutes and listened for three hours cannot also take a full note, and a complete, accurate text arriving minutes after a session closes removes an evening of reconstruction and a set of errors nobody could otherwise avoid.

What a transcript is, however, is a list of utterances. It records who said what, in order, and it is very good at names, figures and the exact wording of a proposal. It is not a record of a meeting, and the difference is not a matter of completeness. A transcript can be a hundred per cent complete and still contain almost none of what a delegation needs to report.

The things it cannot contain are the things that are not utterances. A pause before an answer. Who looked at whom while the pause lasted. The difference between a formula spoken as a refusal and the same formula spoken as an acceptance. In a committee room these carry a great deal of the meaning, and an experienced delegate reads them as naturally as the words.

So the transcript does not replace the record of conversation; it changes what the record is for. Freed from reconstructing the words, the delegate writes what the words alone do not show: that Kavara will in fact accept, that the Lysmark delegation was being consulted silently, that the chair moved on faster than the agenda required.

One further matter follows from holding a complete text, and it is usually noticed late. A recording that begins when the room is unlocked captures informal remarks made by other delegations in the belief that nothing was being recorded. Those words then sit on a ministry file. Deciding where the transcript begins, and writing that decision down, is part of using transcription properly.

> **DATA POINT · Transcription in multilateral work** — International organisations expanded speech-to-text and automated transcription through 2025 under budget pressure, while delegations continued to require human oversight and to treat the written record as subordinate to it.  
> Source: reported practice in multilateral conference services, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Transcription, and what a transcript is not

**Figure 4.7** — The left-hand column is free. The right-hand column is the one the Ministry acts on.

Take transcription without argument. A delegate cannot speak for twenty minutes, listen for three hours and take a reliable note of the whole session, and a complete accurate text arriving before they leave the building removes an evening of reconstruction and a class of errors nobody could avoid otherwise.

Then be exact about what you now have. A transcript is a list of utterances. It will be right about names, figures and the wording of a proposal, and it contains nothing that is not speech: not the pause before an answer, not who was being looked at during it, not the difference between a formula spoken as a refusal and the same formula spoken as consent.

So write the record of conversation differently rather than stopping writing it. The words are now free, which means the record can spend its space on what the words do not show: what was decided, what in the room showed a delegation moving, and which of those judgements is yours rather than quotation. And decide where the recording starts, because a machine that runs from the moment the room is unlocked will hold other delegations’ informal remarks on your file.

*Say it like this*

- Mark the reading: **In my judgement, Kavara will accept.**
- Set the start: **Recording begins when the chair opens the session.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A transcript is a faithful list of utterances. It cannot record a silence as a decision, it cannot show who was being addressed, and completeness does not make it a record. A delegation that keeps only transcripts has a perfect text of every meeting and no account of any of them.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A comparison lists what two accounts do not share; it cannot say which of them is right**

**Figure 5.5** — The comparison produced this list. It produced no verdict, and could not.

A record of conversation is written by a person, from notes, hours after the event. Where two officers attended, the Ministry holds two such records, and for most of the history of diplomacy nobody read them side by side, because doing so took an afternoon and the afternoon was never available.

Setting two accounts side by side is now cheap. A comparison step can list, in a minute, what the first account contains and the second does not, what both contain, and where the two describe the same item in terms that do not match. Several foreign services use exactly this to close the gap between a meeting and the moment its record becomes final.

What such a comparison produces is a list of differences. It does not produce a finding. It was not in the room, it has no way to know which officer heard correctly, and any tool that offered to tell you would be inventing. The value is that the list arrives while both officers can still remember, and a divergence that takes four minutes to settle on the evening of the meeting takes three weeks and a letter if it is found in May.

Two cautions follow. The first is that silence is a difference too, and the hardest kind: the item that appears in neither account, because both officers assumed the other had written it down. A comparison between two documents cannot see what is in neither.

The second is that agreement will stop being informative. Once a transcript arrives minutes after a session, two officers writing from it will produce accounts that match, and the match will say nothing about the meeting. The practice only works if the accounts are written independently, and the requirement has to be stated and recorded, because nobody will remember why it was there.

> **DATA POINT · Reconciling records** — Services that introduced automated document comparison in 2025 report its value as speed of flagging rather than adjudication, and pair it with a required human conversation between the drafters.  
> Source: reported practice in foreign-service record management, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Comparing two accounts of the same event

**Figure 5.7** — Four things it does for nothing, and four it must never be asked for.

Where two officers attended a meeting, the Ministry has two accounts, and until recently nobody set them side by side, because the comparison took an afternoon. It does not give a finding, because it was not in the room and nothing in either document records what a remark was meant as. Ask it which account is right and you will get an answer, and the answer will be an invention.

**Indexing is cheap and retrieval is the point; the subject heading is still a judgement**

A ministry’s records are its memory, and the memory is only as good as what can be found in it. The problem is never that documents are lost. It is that they were named by the person who wrote them, on the day they were written, for the purpose of that afternoon, and nobody asked what a stranger would type in four years.

Indexing is one of the clearest uses of the capability in a foreign service, and several have adopted it. The step reads an incoming document and proposes the fields that make it findable: a name to the house convention, the date, the states concerned, a subject heading from a fixed list, and a small number of keywords. A records officer then accepts or corrects the proposal in well under a minute.

The gain should be stated with its basis, because a vague claim here is worse than none. The index is not more accurate than a skilled records officer working carefully. It is faster than one, by a very large factor, and it is available for every document rather than for the ones somebody had time for. Where a ministry has one records officer and twenty thousand incoming documents a year, that is the whole of the argument.

The weak point is the subject heading, because a heading is not a summary of the words in a document; it is a judgement about what the document is for. A consular case about a detained citizen at a border crossing contains the word transit nine times and the person’s name twice, and a method that follows frequency will file it under transit. **A template makes records comparable, and quietly tells a drafter that anything with no field did not happen**

Before a template, a ministry’s records of conversation are as various as its drafters. Some are four pages and some four lines; the headings differ; and a reader who wants to know whether a question has ever been raised with a particular state must read everything and still cannot be sure. The fix is a fixed shape, and the fix works.

Several foreign services have put a drafting step behind the template: the officer’s notes go in, the nine or ten fields come out populated, and the officer corrects them. Records are produced faster, they have been indexed without special effort, and the empty field becomes informative, since a reader can now distinguish between an item that did not arise and an item the drafter did not mention.

The cost arrives later and is easy to miss. A template is a statement about what a meeting consists of, and a drafter who meets something the template has no field for will usually not record it. This is not laziness. The instruction says the fields are not to be altered, the subject line is not the place, and writing a separate covering minute requires a confidence that a junior officer back from a difficult trip may not have.

What was lost in the case most often cited was not an opinion but a fact: a delegation arrived two hours late, at a third of its notified strength, and left after twenty minutes. Every field was answered correctly and four readers concluded that a routine meeting of no consequence had been held.

The repair is a bounded exception field: a heading that says what the other fields do not record, a hard word limit, mandatory to consider and optional to fill. It is a compromise and it is argued about, because a free-text box in a form tends to grow until the form is gone. The limit is what holds it, and the limit has to be short enough to be unattractive to anybody who wants to write an essay.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Templates for the record of conversation

**Figure 7.7** — Both columns are real. The right-hand one is the one nobody notices for a year.

A template was the right decision and should be defended. Before it, records of conversation were as various as their drafters, and no reader could tell an item that had not arisen from one the drafter had not mentioned. With it, records are comparable, they have been indexed without special effort, and an empty field carries information.

Behind the template, a drafting step populates the fields from the officer’s notes and the officer corrects the result. That is the right division of labour: the step does the arrangement, the officer does the judgement, and nothing is filled from anywhere but the notes. Where the notes do not cover a field, the field is left empty and nothing is inferred.

Now the part that is learned late. A template says what a meeting consists of, and a drafter who meets something it has no field for tends not to record it at all. A delegation that arrived two hours late at a third of its notified strength was recorded in nine correctly answered fields, and four readers concluded that nothing had happened. The repair is a bounded field for what the others cannot hold: a clear heading, a hard cap of fifty words, mandatory to consider and optional to fill.

*Say it like this*
- Set the division: **The step populates; the officer corrects.**
- Forbid the inference: **Where the notes do not cover a field, leave it empty.**
- Bound the exception: **Field 10: what the other 9 do not record. 50 words.**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A closed collection makes an answer traceable; it does not make the Ministry’s own documents right**

**Figure 8.5** — Two claims hold, one rests on a superseded note, and three rest on nothing at all.

Ask a general research step what a particular ministry’s position was on a particular question in a particular year and you may get a confident answer with a citation to a document that does not exist. This is not dishonesty and it is not rare. Arrangements between states follow patterns, and a plausible instrument can be assembled from the pattern alone, complete with an article number.

The response adopted across several foreign services is to restrict the work to a closed collection of the ministry’s own holdings. Documents are indexed; the research step may draw on nothing outside the collection; every statement carries a file reference; and where the collection holds no answer, the step says so instead of reaching further. This is the single most important design decision in the whole field, and it is not a technical one. It is the decision that an answer about the Ministry must come from the Ministry.

What the closed collection buys is traceability. An officer can open the reference, read the passage and see whether it says what the answer said it said. Invented instruments become impossible, because an instrument must be in the collection to be cited.

What it does not buy is correctness, and this is the part that is easy to state and hard to act on. A ministry’s holdings include positions it has abandoned, notes that were superseded without anything on their face saying so, and drafts that were never cleared. A grounded answer citing a real 2017 note may hand an officer a position the ministry left behind in 2019, and the file reference makes it less likely, not more, that anybody checks.

So the collection is a boundary, not a guarantee. The practices that go with it are a marking of superseded material wherever it is known, a requirement that the officer open the reference before quoting it, and an honest statement in the record that the second failure has not been solved. A ministry that tells itself the problem is closed has exchanged a loud error for a quiet one.

A question about what the Ministry holds must be answered from what the Ministry holds. Put it to anything wider and you may get an instrument assembled from the general shape of such instruments: a 2019 agreement, article 4, paragraph 2, confidently cited and never written. The error is not dishonesty; it is a question answered from the wrong collection.

So restrict the collection and make the restriction absolute. The Ministry’s own documents, indexed; nothing from outside; a file reference on every statement; a quotation of the passage relied on; and where the collection holds no answer, a plain statement that it holds none, rather than a reach for something further away. This is the single most important decision in the field and it is a decision about boundaries, not about tools.

Then be honest about the limit, because this is where offices deceive themselves. A closed collection buys traceability, not correctness. The Ministry holds positions it has abandoned, notes superseded without a word on their face, and drafts never cleared. An answer citing a real 2017 note can hand an officer a position dropped in 2019, and the reference makes it less likely that anybody looks. - Set the boundary: **No source outside the collection may be used.**
- Require the reference: **A file reference for every statement, and the passage quoted.**
- Permit the silence: **Where the collection holds no answer, say so and stop.**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A summary keeps what looks substantial, and a judgement is the least substantial-looking thing in a telegram**

**Figure 9.5** — Every paragraph with a name, a figure or a quotation survived. The one without them did not.

A mission’s reporting telegram is written for two readers at once. One needs the detail: who said what, in what order, with the texts attached. The other has eleven minutes before a meeting. For most of the history of the trade the second reader was served by whoever happened to be free to read the long version first.

A summary step serves that reader properly, and several foreign services now use one. The telegram goes in; a one-page version comes out, with the paragraph numbers preserved so that a reader who wants paragraph 11 can go to paragraph 11. The compression is good, the facts survive, and the forty officers who need to know what happened now know within minutes rather than by the afternoon.

The failure is specific and it is not random. Any method of shortening a document keeps what looks substantial: names, figures, quotations, the long paragraph with the texts in it. A comment paragraph has none of those. It is four lines of plain prose containing the only thing in the document that the Ministry cannot get anywhere else, which is the judgement of the person who was in the room.

So it goes, and it goes silently, because the summary that results is a good summary of everything else. A Director reads an accurate page about a difficult conversation and sends a letter that the Ambassador had advised against in the paragraph she did not see.

The repair is a rule rather than a better method. The comment paragraph is reproduced in full at the top of the one-pager, whatever its length, and where the telegram marks no comment paragraph the summary says so. That second half matters as much as the first, because it turns a silent loss into a visible gap, and a visible gap gets fixed by the mission that caused it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Summaries lose conditions before they lose facts, and the test is not truth but whether the reader would act the same**

**Figure 10.5** — Three of six versions send the reader the wrong way, and all six are accurate.

Once a ministry summarises everything, it has a new class of error, and it is not inaccuracy. Summaries are generally accurate. What they lose, reliably and in one direction, is the condition attached to a statement: the proviso, the margin, the degree of confidence, the two exceptions at the end of the paragraph.

The reason is structural. A condition is a qualification of something else, so it is grammatically subordinate, usually short, and never the main clause. Every method of shortening a sentence keeps the main clause. The result is a clean, true sentence from which the negotiation has vanished: Kavara agrees to the figure, where the position was that Kavara agrees provided the review in 2027 is binding.

This is worse than an obvious error, because nothing looks wrong. An officer reading the folder has no way to know that a clause has gone. There is no gap, no ellipsis, no note. The sentence reads as a finished statement of a position, and the only sign of trouble appears later, when two officers who have read different versions give the Minister different advice and neither can explain the other.

The practical test used in several services is a two-reader test on a sample. One officer reads only the summary; another reads the document; each is asked what the Ministry should do next. Where the answers differ, the summary has lost something that matters, whatever its accuracy. Where they agree, the summary is safe for that purpose, which is a smaller and more useful claim than true.

And one instruction is worth writing down, because it settles an argument that otherwise recurs. A hedge and a proviso are not the same thing. A hedge softens a claim and may be cut: it is the writer’s own uncertainty. A proviso is the other side’s condition. It is a fact about the world, it is reproduced, and it is reproduced inside the sentence it qualifies rather than in a note underneath.
