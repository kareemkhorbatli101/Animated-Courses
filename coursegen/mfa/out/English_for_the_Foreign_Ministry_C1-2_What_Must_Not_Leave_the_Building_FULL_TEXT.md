### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A marking governs a document; it says nothing about what a set of unmarked documents will yield**

**Figure 1.5** — Six rows of nothing, and the last cell is the Ministry’s position on three issues.

Classification is the oldest control in a foreign ministry and it operates on documents. A document is assessed, marked, and handled according to its marking: who may see it, which systems may hold it, how it is transmitted and when it may be destroyed. The logic is sound and the unit of assessment is the document.

The unit of assessment has become the wrong one, and the reason is not that marking has failed. It is that inference across a set of documents has become cheap. Aggregation risk — the possibility that items individually harmless reveal something protected in combination — has been in the handling literature for fifty years and has been almost entirely theoretical, because inferring a negotiating position from eleven unremarkable documents required somebody to read all eleven with that question in mind, and nobody had the hours.

Where that work costs nothing, the theoretical risk becomes the ordinary case. An officer who assembles a tender notice, some press material, a published figure, an attendee list and a few pieces of unobjectionable internal correspondence, and asks for a summary, may receive the ministry’s position on three issues, because that position is the only hypothesis under which all eleven documents are consistent. No rule has been broken, nothing has been misclassified, and nothing has leaked.

The correct rule is easy to state and impossible to apply: material is not to be assembled where the assembly would permit a protected inference. An officer cannot apply that, because knowing what inference a set permits requires having made the assembly, which is the act the rule is meant to govern. Faced with such a rule at the end of a working day, officers comply when cautious and do not comply when busy, which is the characteristic failure mode of every unusable security rule.

What works is a crude countable limit: not more than a stated number of sources assembled in an unaccredited system without the authority of a records officer. The number is arbitrary and should be described as arbitrary in the instruction itself, because it will prevent hundreds of harmless assemblies and permit some harmful ones. Its single virtue is that an officer can apply it by counting, in four seconds, without any judgement about inference, and a rule that is applied badly by everybody is worth more than a rule that is applied correctly by the cautious alone.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A dependence costs nothing until it is withdrawn, and the cost cannot be argued for without a figure**

**Figure 2.5** — Three rows of good fortune, one row of contract, and two rows of silence.

A foreign ministry acquires a capability it could not have built, on infrastructure it does not own, in a jurisdiction it does not control, and the service is excellent. This is the ordinary case and it should be described without drama: the consular service of a small ministry answering eight thousand enquiries a month in eleven languages is a real public good that did not exist before and could not have been produced domestically on any budget the ministry was ever going to have.

The exposure that accompanies it is also ordinary, and it is not a prediction of bad faith. A commercial provider may withdraw a service on notice, may raise a price, and may hold material where its own arrangements take it. None of these is a hostile act. All of them are terms, and terms are what a ministry is supposed to read.

What makes the position uncomfortable is a sequence rather than a clause. The arrangement works; the section is rebuilt around it; the capability becomes the ordinary standard of service; and the notice period, which was adequate for a pilot, is now the whole of the time available to replace something an entire section depends on. Nobody decided that. It happened by the service being good for four years.

The practical defect is the absence of a figure. A ministry that cannot say what leaving would cost and how long it would take cannot negotiate for a longer notice period, because the request sounds like caution and caution is the cheapest thing to concede in a negotiation. With a costing, the request becomes an argument: our replacement time is eighteen months, so a ninety-day notice period is not a term we can accept.

So the rule has two halves. No dependence is entered into without a costed exit, which gives the negotiator an argument at the only moment when terms are available. And the costing is repeated at a stated interval, because the exposure grows with the dependence while the contract stays as it was signed. The error in most such cases is not the original decision, which is usually right, but the years in which nobody wrote down what it had come to cost.

> **DATA POINT · Dependence and continuity** — Foreign ministries reviewing externally hosted capability in 2025 introduced requirements for costed exit plans before procurement and at fixed intervals thereafter, citing notice periods negotiated for pilots and retained for production services.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The record must show what was in front of the decision-maker, which is no longer the file**

**Figure 3.5** — Four questions answered and the two that an inquiry asks unanswered.

A ministry’s record of a decision has traditionally consisted of the material on which the decision was taken, because the material on which it was taken was the file. An officer read the file, took the decision, and recorded it; the file and the decision together constituted a complete account, and a reviewer four years later could see exactly what the officer saw.

That identity has quietly broken. Where an intake step produces a one-page summary and the officer reads the summary, the file is no longer what was in front of the decision-maker. The decision rests on a document that is accurate, derived, and in most ministries not retained, because nobody classified it as a record: it looked like a working aid, and working aids are thrown away.

Nothing was decided here either. The change happened because reading a summary takes four minutes and reading a file takes twenty, and within a year the thing an officer actually read had changed while the definition of the record had not. The consequence appears only when somebody asks the one question a reviewer always asks, which is not whether the decision was right but what the decision-maker had in front of them.

The rule that answers it is crude and has to be: any document presented to a decision-maker is retained with the decision, for the same period as the decision, whether or not anybody regards it as a record. The volume is large and the storage cost is not the difficulty.

The difficulty is that retention creates discoverability, and this is a genuine conflict rather than a problem with a solution. A one-page summary written for speed compresses, and compression loses qualifications; retained, it becomes a document that can be requested, quoted and compared unfavourably with the file it summarised. A ministry that does not retain cannot show what its officer saw; a ministry that retains has manufactured many thousands of compressed documents a month that can be quoted against it. The honest position is to choose, to state which exposure you prefer, and to record that it is a preference.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A signature is read as a personal assurance, and in a five-deep chain it has usually become a procedural one**

**Figure 4.5** — Six rows, five defensible positions and one unqualified sentence.

A brief that reaches a minister has usually passed through four or five hands, and once a drafting step produces the first version it has passed through one more. When something in it turns out to be wrong, the examination that follows produces a characteristic result: everybody in the chain did what a reasonable officer would have done, and nobody checked the thing that was wrong.

The temptation is to treat this as a problem of attribution, and to ask which link failed. It is better treated as a question about what a signature means. A minister reading a brief with a name at the bottom understands that a named person has satisfied himself about its contents. In most ministries, by the time a document has passed through a chain, the signature certifies that the chain operated, which is a different thing, and the difference is invisible to the reader.

An assisted first draft sharpens this because it adds a link that cannot be asked anything. A step that drew a figure from the source it was configured to use did exactly what it was told; there is no officer in that link to acknowledge, concede or maintain anything, and a chain with such a link in it cannot be audited by asking its members what they did.

The rule that works is about the sign-off and not about the drafting. A signature carries a short statement of what the signatory has personally verified: the figures, the argument, the sources, or none of them. The statement changes nothing about responsibility, which stays with the signatory, and changes what the reader is entitled to infer.

Two effects follow, one intended and one not. The intended effect is that officers writing the statement discover what they actually do, and a number of senior signatories find that the honest entry is none of them, which was always the case and had never been recorded. The unintended effect is an unequal exposure: an officer who writes figures verified has made a personal assurance, while a Director who writes none of them has not, and the officer is junior. That is a real objection and the answer to it is not obvious; what can be said is that under the previous arrangement nobody’s exposure was recorded at all, and a reader was invited to infer a checking that had not occurred.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The slant is in the record rather than in the method, and the method returns it in four seconds**

**Figure 5.5** — Nothing was excluded, and the sixth row is why the fourth is zero.

Bias in an assisted brief is usually discussed as though it were a property of the method, and the discussion is mostly misdirected. A search over a closed collection and the open record has no view about the state whose conduct is being assessed. It returns what is indexed, digitised and searchable.

The slant is in the record. Material published in certain languages, by institutions in certain cities, with certain funding, is catalogued, digitised and findable; material published elsewhere, in other languages, often is not. This is a fact about forty years of publishing, library funding and digitisation programmes, and it was exactly as true when an officer walked to a reading room in 1990.

What has changed is the speed, and the speed changes what an officer learns. A week in a reading room teaches you where the empty shelves are: you go looking for the Kavaran material, you fail to find it, and the failure is part of your education. A search returns nine sources in four seconds, all of them good, and reports nothing about the nine thousand that do not exist in searchable form. The officer is better informed and worse calibrated.

The remedy is an audit of composition rather than of conclusion. A source inventory on every brief, giving the number of sources by language, by provenance and by relation to the subject — how many from the state being assessed — puts the empty shelf back in front of the officer. It does not say what the missing material would have said, and claiming that it corrects the bias would be a second error.

Two cautions about reporting this. The first is that the word bias will be read as a criticism of the section that wrote the brief, which did nothing wrong and excluded nothing, and the officers who did nothing wrong are the ones who will stop cooperating with an auditor. The second is harder: an officer who has read in a language for twenty years experiences a search that returns it as a search that has worked, and no inventory reaches that. What an inventory does is make the composition visible to somebody who did not choose it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An exclusion needs a stated reason and a review date, because a habit cannot be defended and is removed in an afternoon**

**Figure 6.5** — Three of the five would be done well, which is why the reasons cannot be about quality.

A ministry that has adopted this capability across its work arrives at a short list of things it has decided not to do with it, and the list is worth writing down for a reason that has nothing to do with sentiment. An exclusion that exists as a practice is defended by the people who remember why, and those people leave.

The first discipline is to be honest about capability. Of five excluded tasks, three would be performed well tomorrow. A register that implies otherwise invites the first ambitious officer to demonstrate the opposite, and when they do the whole register goes, including the entries that matter. State what could be done and say that the ministry has decided not to.

The second is to give each entry a reason of a stated kind, because the kinds differ and they are differently durable. Some exclusions are about what a document is: a condolence is an act rather than a communication, and an acknowledgement of error has to come from somebody capable of being embarrassed. Some are about authority: a refusal and a limit are exercises of power, and a person must own them in a way that can be examined. And some are about calibration, which is the hardest to hold: an ambassador’s assessment paragraph is often a ministry’s only independent check on its own reading of events, and a drafted assessment built from the ministry’s own reporting will agree with the ministry.

That third kind deserves particular attention because its cost is invisible. Nobody notices the absence of a disagreement. A ministry that loses the one paragraph in its traffic capable of contradicting it will not record a loss, will not receive a complaint, and will discover the consequence years later in a decision taken on an unchallenged reading.

The third discipline is a review date, and it is not altogether satisfactory. An entry that lapses unless renewed in writing prevents a register from accumulating entries nobody can explain, and it loses a good exclusion whenever the officer who understood it is away in the month it falls due. Making renewal easier makes it automatic, and an automatic renewal is the accumulating register with extra steps. The choice is between losing good exclusions occasionally and keeping bad ones permanently, and a ministry that cannot say which it prefers has chosen the second.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Correction improves with repetition and composition disappears without it, and they are not the same skill**

**Figure 7.5** — The lower three rows explain the upper three, in both directions.

A service that has used assisted drafting for four years will find, if it troubles to test, that its officers have become markedly better at one thing and have lost another entirely, and that nobody noticed either change because the output of the section improved throughout.

What improves is correction, and it improves for an ordinary reason: an officer who corrects twenty drafts a week performs a task twenty times a week that their predecessor performed twice. On a test of finding defects in a drafted document, a current cohort outperforms a cohort from before assistance by a wide margin and in a third of the time. This is a real gain and it is rarely mentioned, because the discussion of skill loss is conducted entirely in the language of loss.

What disappears is composition, and the word to use is disappears rather than decays. An officer who has never built a document from a file and a blank page has not lost a skill; they have never had it. In a tested cohort of fourteen, eleven had never done it, and of those eleven one produced nothing at all within the hour allowed.

Whether that matters is the question a ministry usually answers by assumption, and the honest answer has two defensible parts and one that is not yet evidenced. Composition matters when there is nothing to correct, which happens on the day the system is unavailable. It matters when a draft is subtly wrong in a way that is recognised only by somebody who has built one. The first is demonstrable. The second is widely believed, including by the present author, and has not been shown.

A practice requirement is the available remedy and it has a characteristic political problem. The quantity that would maintain the skill is large enough to require evidence of benefit, and the evidence does not exist because nobody has been practising. A smaller quantity will not maintain the skill and will measure how fast it decays, which is the evidence required to ask for the larger one. A trainer who presents the smaller quantity as a remedy has overstated it; one who presents it as a measurement will not have it approved. That is a real dilemma and the usual resolution is to write both halves and let the first be read.

> **DATA POINT · Skill maintenance** — Diplomatic academies in several foreign services reintroduced unassisted drafting exercises in 2025 after testing showed strong improvement in editing and the absence of composition experience in recent cohorts.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic training, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A fallback tested in a quiet hour has been tested in the one circumstance in which it will never be needed**

**Figure 8.5** — Rows four and five are identical in both columns. Rows one to three are the drill.

Every ministry has a continuity plan and most of them are good documents: a reversion to paper, a printed reference set, a telephone tree, a manual register. The elements work. They have been checked, and the checking was not a formality.

The checking was nevertheless conducted at a time the checkers chose, and the time they chose was a quiet one, for a reason that has nothing to do with bad faith. A person designing a drill wants the section to be able to complete the exercise; a drill that collapses after eleven minutes feels like a wasted morning, and a drill that the section passes feels like a validated plan.

The consequence is that the plan has been tested in the only circumstance in which it will never be used. A system that fails on a quiet Tuesday is an inconvenience managed by waiting. A system that fails at nine in the morning in the busiest week of the year is the reason the plan exists, and that is the hour at which nobody has ever tried it.

What fails at volume is usually not the procedure but a quantity buried inside it. A printed reference set in two languages is adequate until the enquiries arrive in eleven. A manual register with one page per case is sensible at forty an hour and unusable at nine hundred. Neither is a defect in the plan as written; both are assumptions about scale that nobody wrote down because the plan was never read against a real hour.

Two practices follow. A continuity plan should state its capacity in its first paragraph — this plan handles two hundred enquiries an hour in two languages, and above that enquiries are acknowledged and queued — because a plan with no stated capacity will be assumed to have the capacity of the system it replaces. And a drill should be designed by somebody who did not write the plan, at an hour they choose, with a stated condition that would count as a failure. A drill that cannot be failed produces a reassurance, and a reassurance about a continuity plan is worse than no drill at all, because it is believed.

> **DATA POINT · Continuity drills** — Information security sections in several foreign ministries moved continuity drills to peak-volume periods in 2025 and began publishing stated fallback capacities, having found plans validated only under low load.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic continuity planning, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A judgement that cannot be evidenced is still a judgement; what disqualifies it is presenting it as evidence**

**Figure 9.5** — Four elements and a fifth that kept the dissent from blocking anything.

Every ministry contains judgements that cannot be evidenced. An officer who has sat opposite the same delegate for three years knows things about him that are in no document: what he concedes easily, which of his own instructions he does not believe, what his acceptance sounds like when he expects to be replaced before the commitment matters. This knowledge is real, it is frequently right, and it cannot be shown.

The institutional response to such a judgement has historically been to say it in a corridor and not on a file, which has exactly the effect of not having it. An officer who was right in 2022 and said so only in conversation has contributed nothing, and the ministry that acts on the majority assessment and is surprised in 2026 concludes that it was misled rather than that it was warned.

What makes such a judgement recordable is a form with four elements. The disagreement, identified by paragraph. The ground, stated as what it is: a reading of one person over a period. The limit, stated expressly: this cannot be evidenced. And the circumstance in which the minute should be re-read, which is the element officers omit and the only one that makes the minute findable, because nobody reads a file in order to discover a dissent. A fifth element, that the writer does not oppose the decision, is what prevents the dissent from being treated as obstruction and quietly discouraged.

This machinery records a disagreement and does not transfer the reading behind it, and that distinction is worth holding. Each sentence of what an officer knows about a counterpart can be written down; the whole cannot be handed over. A successor given the rule that he never concedes a date has a rule, and when the man concedes a date in the fourth round she cannot tell whether the rule was wrong or the concession is significant, which is precisely the judgement the original officer would have made in a second.

A service has always lost a reading when an officer moves. What is new is the volume of material that resembles one: a handover note, a chronology, four summaries and a well-indexed file can leave a successor believing she has inherited a reading when she has inherited a tenth of it. The only honest device available is to say so at the top of the handover, and to expect that sentence to be read as modesty.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Preparation can be transformed and the undertaking cannot be delegated, because it is a relationship between states carried by individuals**

**Figure 10.5** — Four rows transformed, and the last two are the negotiation.

This book has argued, at every point, that the gains are real and should be taken. A ministry of eleven officers answering eight thousand consular enquiries a month in eleven languages; a legal section comparing fourteen pages against fourteen pages in minutes; a delegation arriving with every fallback referenced and every bracket traced; a crisis acknowledged in two minutes at three in the morning. None of that was available ten years ago to a ministry of this size, and a service that declines it is not preserving a tradition but failing its citizens.

It has argued with equal consistency that each of those gains has an edge, and that the edge is where the work is. The summary that drops the comment paragraph. The classification that governs documents and not sets of them. The brief that is accurate and useless. The reach figure that rises when you publish more. In every case the capability does exactly what it was asked and the failure is in the asking, which is why this has been a book about writing instructions.

What remains at the end is narrower than the usual closing paragraph about the human element, and it is not a claim about creativity or warmth. An arrangement between states is concluded when a person with authority to bind a state says that the state will do something, and is believed because that person’s predecessors did what they said. The belief is the asset. It is accumulated by individuals, held across a succession of them, and destroyed by a single failure to perform.

Nothing in that has a technical component. A text can be assembled, a precedent found, a limit retrieved from a file; the limit itself is set by somebody who will answer for it, and the undertaking is given by somebody whose word has a history. That is why the register of the last three units has been about accountability, dissent and what is kept: a service that cannot say who decided, cannot record who disagreed, and cannot show what its officer read has nothing to be believed about.

And the boundary will move. In one officer’s service it has moved twice, and both movements were resisted on grounds that felt like principle and turned out to be habit. That should produce neither complacency nor the opposite. Two of the five exclusions in the Ministry’s register can be defended in any decade; three are provisional and their defenders cannot reliably tell which kind they are holding. The duty is not to defend the present line but to know which side of it you are standing on, to say which, and to write down why, so that whoever finds the file in four years knows what you thought and what you could not show.
