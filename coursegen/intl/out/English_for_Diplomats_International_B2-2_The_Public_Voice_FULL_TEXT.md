### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: stance adverbs — frankly, regrettably, understandably**

- A stance adverb comments on the whole sentence and usually stands first, with a comma: frankly, nobody had thought of it.
- Frankly and honestly claim unusual candour; regrettably and unfortunately express the speaker’s own regret; understandably and admittedly concede that somebody else’s position is reasonable.
- They may also stand mid-sentence between commas: the delay was, admittedly, longer than we expected.
- Use them sparingly. One in a paragraph is emphasis; three are a mannerism, and in an interview a mannerism is what gets quoted.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A journalist asks about what readers have seen, not about what you have planned**

**Figure 1.5** — Eleven prepared subjects, five actual questions, and one point of contact between them.

Preparing for an interview is a task at which a system excels, provided that it is asked for the right thing. Asked to prepare answers on a policy agenda, it will produce accurate, well-hedged answers of about ninety words each.

The journalist, however, is not interviewing the policy agenda. A correspondent in the host country asks about what that country’s readers have noticed, and what they have noticed is physical, local and recent.

The instruction must therefore be built from the host media rather than from the Mission’s own priorities: the last twelve months of coverage of this Mission and this country, the questions put to your predecessor, the complaints published in readers’ letters, and anything visible from the street.

Ask for the twenty questions most likely to be asked, in the order of likelihood, with the coverage that gives rise to each, and require that no question be omitted because it is awkward or trivial. A boarded window is trivial and will be the first question.

Then prepare the answers in the form they will be used. An answer of ninety words will be quoted in twenty, and the twenty will be chosen by somebody else unless the first twenty words of your answer are the ones you would choose.

And prepare for the loaded premise, which is the commonest hostile form. Answer the question in the first half of the sentence and decline the word in the second, once, however many times the premise is repeated. Frankly, an interview is lost far more often by an improvised answer to an obvious question than by a difficult one.

> **DATA POINT · Interview preparation** — Media practice commonly recommends that preparation for an interview in a host country be based on the host media’s own recent coverage rather than on the interviewee’s policy agenda.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Loaded questions** — Media practice commonly advises that an unacceptable premise be declined once and briefly, with the substantive answer given first, since repeated objection to the premise tends to become the reported story.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · A Statement Written to Be Translated

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: fronting and emphasis in spoken English · AI: a statement written so that its translation says the same thing*

**Figure 2.0** — EIGHTY-ONE WORDS IN ENGLISH, AND THREE DIFFERENT STATEMENTS IN THREE PAPERS: A press statement of eighty-one words, as issued and as published in three languages.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What have you written that somebody else translated?
- What went wrong, and whose fault was it?
- Which English sentences are hardest to translate?

The statement is eighty-one words long and was written in forty minutes. It is clear, dignified English and contains one sentence of thirty-one words with two subordinate clauses.

**Grammar: fronting and emphasis in spoken English**

- Fronting moves an element to the beginning for emphasis: this the Mission could have prevented. The object stands first and the subject follows.
- A cleft sentence places the emphasised element after it is or what: it is the delay that concerns us; what we regret is the delay.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A statement is finished when it cannot be made to say anything else**

**Figure 2.5** — Six choices the English leaves open, and six places where three papers may differ.

Drafting a public statement is quick, and the result will be clear, dignified English. Clarity in English, however, is not the test when the statement will be read in three other languages by people who will never see the original.

The test is whether the text leaves a translator anything to decide. English tolerates an unresolved pronoun; the reader carries two possibilities forward and selects one at the end of the sentence. A translator cannot do that, because the grammar of the target language usually forces a choice at the moment the pronoun appears.

So the instruction must require the properties that remove choices: sentences of no more than about fifteen words; nouns repeated rather than replaced by pronouns; no idiom, metaphor or cultural reference; figures with their units; dates written out; titles given in full; and the active voice with the actor named.

It must also require a structure that survives cutting. A sub-editor in any language cuts paragraphs from the bottom, so the sentence that must survive stands first, and it is short enough to be carried whole.

Send the statement with your own translation where you can, marked as the authoritative version, and with a covering line identifying the one sentence you ask to be carried. Neither costs anything, and both remove the places where competent people would otherwise differ.

What we regret, in such cases, is almost never the translation. It is the four repeated words we did not write, and the thirty-one-word sentence we admired too much to cut.

> **DATA POINT · Statements for translation** — Practice for public statements intended for translation commonly requires short sentences, repeated nouns in place of pronouns, and the avoidance of idiom, on the ground that a translator must resolve every ambiguity the text contains.  
> Source: general public communication guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Cutting practice** — Editorial practice in most languages cuts paragraphs from the end, with the consequence that the opening sentence of a statement is the most likely to be reproduced and the last the least.  
> Source: general public communication guidance, 2025–2026.

**Grammar: attribution and distance — according to, it is reported that**

- According to + a source attributes a claim without adopting it: according to the newspaper, the inquiry will report in June.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A clip is availability, not malice, and construction is the only defence**

**Figure 3.5** — Three constructions that survive any window, and three that supply the headline.

A message can be drafted quickly and will read well. Whether it survives being cut into eleven seconds is a separate property, and it is not improved by the care with which the whole was written.

The mechanism is availability rather than hostility. A broadcaster takes a passage of a few seconds from a feed that is already there; two more outlets take the same passage within the hour because it is the one that has been clipped.

Three properties make a message clip-resistant. Conditions precede what they govern, and are repeated after it. Every claim is positive, stating what is so rather than denying what is alleged. And every clause is complete in itself, with no pronoun depending on a sentence that may not be in the window.

The commonest failure is the denial that repeats the charge. A sentence containing the words not a hostile presence provides, in your own mouth and with your name attached, the two words the headline requires.

Where a claim must be mentioned at all, attribute it or distance it: according to the newspaper, it is reported that, the allegations are said to be. Attribution costs four words and prevents a clip in which you appear to have adopted the claim.

Then build the whole message in thirty words, because a bulletin has room for about thirty, and a message that is already thirty words long is a message nobody else has to shorten.

> **DATA POINT · Clipping** — Broadcast practice commonly extracts consecutive passages of some seconds from longer answers; practice for public speaking accordingly recommends that conditionals and qualifications precede rather than follow the words they govern.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Denials** — Communication practice commonly advises against repeating an allegation’s own terms in a denial, since the denial supplies an attributable instance of the words complained of.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: mixed conditionals in explaining consequences**

- A mixed conditional joins a past condition to a present result: if we had rehearsed it aloud, we would know where it fails.
- It also joins a present condition to a past result: if she were a careless speaker, she would not have cut the passage.
- Had + subject + past participle is a formal inversion of if … had: had she known this, she would have cut eight ideas.
- Mixed conditionals are the natural form for explaining consequences after the event, which is most of what a post-mortem consists of. Keep them to one a paragraph: they are heavy.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A speech for the ear is not a shorter text; it is a different structure**

**Figure 4.5** — Six differences, the same twelve minutes, and about three times as much retained.

A twelve-minute speech can be drafted quickly and will read admirably. Reading admirably is the problem: the properties that make a text good on the page are a poor guide to what an audience can follow by ear in a second language.

Three constraints govern such a speech, and all three must be stated in the instruction. There are at most three main ideas. Each is stated twice, in different words, the second time more simply. And each is preceded by an explicit signpost, of the kind a published text would regard as crude.

Numbers are rounded for the ear. One figure a speech, given twice, will be remembered; four figures to one decimal place will not be heard at all, and a decimal place in speech is almost always noise.

One concrete local example is worth more than any statistic, and it must be genuinely local and genuinely checked: a workshop on the next street, visited on Tuesday, employing a number of people somebody has counted.

Rate matters as much as content. A hundred and twenty words a minute is comfortable for an audience in an additional language; a hundred and sixty is a reading speed, and at a hundred and sixty the second half of every sentence is lost.

Had any of this been difficult, it would be worth the eleven ideas. It is not difficult: it is simply uncomfortable, because the work consists almost entirely of removing good material that the speaker would prefer to keep.

> **DATA POINT · Speaking to second-language audiences** — Practice for addresses to audiences listening in an additional language commonly recommends a reduced number of main ideas, explicit signposting, deliberate repetition of key formulations and a moderated rate of delivery.  
> Source: general public speaking guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Local examples** — Communication practice commonly finds concrete local illustration more durable in audience recall than aggregate statistics, provided the illustration is verifiable and used with the consent of those described.  
> Source: general public speaking guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: modal perfects for accountability — should have, could have, need not have**

- Should have + past participle marks an obligation that was not met: we should have asked a local colleague to read the original.
- Could have marks a possibility that was not taken: they could have checked the screenshot, and did not.
- Need not have marks something that was done and was unnecessary: we need not have drafted anything at all. Did not need to means it was not done.
- These forms allocate responsibility, so choose them deliberately. Should have blames; could have observes; need not have excuses the result while regretting the effort.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Fluency belongs to the translation, and reach decides the response**

**Figure 5.5** — Six properties of the source, and none of them visible in good English.

A story in a language you do not read can be translated in seconds, and the translation will be fluent. That fluency is the first and largest trap, because fluency is a property of the translation and tells you nothing whatever about the source.

A thin story and a solid story translate into the same confident English. A phrase that in the original marks a rumour disappears; a hedge becomes an assertion; and an unnamed official becomes sources say, which in English sounds like a newspaper and not like a corridor.

So the instruction must ask for what the translation cannot supply, and it must be read by somebody who reads the language: the sources actually cited, by name or as unnamed; what documents exist and whether anybody has seen them; and the register of the original, including any phrase that locally signals rumour.

Then ask for the facts that decide the response rather than the truth of the story: the outlet’s reach, whether any other outlet has taken it, and where the claim first appeared. A false story in an outlet of four thousand readers, taken up by nobody, is not yet a problem.

Rebuttal is amplification. A denial from a mission carries a story to the audience of the mission’s own credibility, which is usually a hundred times larger than the outlet that published it, and the denial repeats the claim in a form that can be quoted.

The usual right answer is to prepare and to wait: keep a dated copy of the original, have local colleagues watch for uptake, draft a response and do not send it. We need not have answered most of the stories we have answered, and we should have read more of them in the original first.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: plain English for translation — one idea, one clause, the active voice**

- One idea to a sentence, and one clause where possible: the police closed the street at seven thirty-five.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**In the first hour, plain English is not a style but a safety measure**

**Figure 6.5** — Six blocks of ten minutes, and the public line is the fifth of them, not the first.

In the first hour of an incident, drafting help is genuinely valuable and the discipline of the instruction matters more than at any other time, because nothing will be revised and everything will be read.

The order is fixed and the public line is not first. Account for people; start a timed log; secure the premises; establish which facts are confirmed and by whom; inform the capital; and only then say something publicly.

The holding line itself obeys four rules. One idea to a sentence and one clause where possible. The active voice with a named actor. Every time, number and place stated exactly. And a named hour for the next update, because in due course cannot be translated and will not be believed.

Nothing unconfirmed is mentioned, and the absence is not explained. A holding line says what is known, who is safe, what the authorities have done and when more will be said. Nine unconfirmed reports have no place in it, and a sentence beginning we are aware of reports repeats them.

The log is the other half of the hour’s work, and the part that is always neglected. It records the time at which each fact moved from reported to confirmed and who confirmed it, because in two days somebody will ask when the Mission knew, and only the log can answer.

And the holding line is issued in both languages at the same minute, by the Mission, so that the first translation in circulation is the one the Mission chose.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: parallelism and the rule of three in a short speech**

- Parallelism repeats a grammatical form across items: we have listened, we have recorded, and we shall act.
- A triad of infinitives keeps to in each: to thank you, to hear you and to answer you.
- The rule of three gives a short speech its shape: three ideas, three examples, three promises. More than three is a list; fewer is an assertion.
- In a second language, parallelism is a kindness rather than a flourish, because the second and third items are predictable once the first has been understood.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A third general assurance is heard as a refusal**

**Figure 7.5** — Three identical assurances in six years, and three concrete offers never made.

Preparing an address to a community is work a system does well: the association’s history, its projects, the names of its officers, the religious calendar, the correct modes of address and a form of thanks in the community’s own language.

Two things it cannot supply, and both of them decide how the speech is heard. The first is what the community has already been promised, which exists in nobody’s file because no record of undertakings has been kept. The second is which promises are actually within the speaker’s power.

The first matters because a general assurance repeated for the third time is not received as warmth. It is received, politely, as a refusal, and the politeness conceals the damage until somebody quotes the 2019 version back at you.

The second matters because the attractive promises are almost always the ones beyond your power. The appointment system belongs to a capital; the nationality rules belong to a legislature; the waiting time belongs to arithmetic. What belongs to a mission is small, concrete and deliverable: three expedited appointments a week, a published waiting time, a letter answered in ten days.

So require two things in the instruction that no document will volunteer: every undertaking given to this community in the last ten years, with its outcome, and a list of possible offers divided into those within the Mission’s own gift and those requiring a decision elsewhere.

Then write down what you said, within the week, and put it where your successor will find it. We have listened, we have recorded, and we shall act is a triad that only works if the second clause is literally true.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: hedges, boosters and neutral verbs**

- A hedge marks genuine uncertainty: it appears that, it is likely that, as far as I know, on the information I have.
- A booster strengthens: I am certain that, without any doubt, plainly. Use one only where the evidence carries it, since a booster on a weak claim destroys the credit of the strong ones.
- A neutral verb reports without taking a side: the Mission takes no view; the position is that; the authorities have stated.
- Hedges, boosters and neutral verbs are not degrees of politeness. Two hedges and a booster in one sentence is not caution but evasion, and will be heard as such in every language.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An admission with a deadline sounds like competence in any language**

**Figure 8.5** — Six situations, six formulations, and not one of them a prepared phrase about review.

Preparing answers is a task a system performs well, and it produces, among others, the formulations that sound informed and commit to nothing. Those formulations are the most dangerous output it offers, because they are the easiest thing to say under pressure in a second language.

The difficulty is not linguistic incompetence but the opposite. A speaker working in an additional language reaches for the prepared phrase precisely because it is prepared, and the prepared phrase is almost always the evasive one.

There are three distinct situations and they require three different answers. Not knowing requires a plain admission and a deadline. Knowing and not being permitted to say requires a statement that you cannot discuss it and the real reason why. A matter belonging to another authority requires the name of that authority.

Each needs its own instruction and none of them may be answered with the words used for another, because a listener who hears the same formula twice concludes, correctly, that one of them was untrue.

Hedges and boosters do the fine work. One honest hedge marks real doubt — it appears to be about nine hundred — and one justified booster marks real certainty. Two hedges and a booster in a single sentence is not caution; it is the sound of evasion, and it translates into every language unchanged.

And the deadline is the whole of the discipline. An officer who says I shall telephone you before five tomorrow has bought the credit of an honest answer and incurred an obligation that must be met at a quarter to five.

> **DATA POINT · Admissions of ignorance** — Communication practice commonly finds that an express statement of what is not known, coupled with a specific undertaking to revert, is received more favourably than a general formulation that avoids the question.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Reasons for refusal** — Practice distinguishes an inability to comment, which requires a stated reason, from an absence of knowledge, which requires an undertaking to revert; using one formulation for both is commonly found to damage credibility.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: quoting and paraphrasing accurately**

- A direct quotation reproduces the speaker’s exact words within quotation marks, with the speaker named: she said, “the project has my full support”.
- A paraphrase restates in your own words and must be marked as one: in substance, she said that she supported the project.

- Never present a paraphrase as a quotation, and never attribute to a speaker a conclusion you drew. In a handover both faults are permanent, because your successor cannot ask the speaker what was meant.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A handover transfers three things, and only two of them can be written**

**Figure 9.5** — Twenty-four pages of judgement and impression written in the voice of fact.

A handover note can be assembled quickly from four years of files, and it will be accurate, comprehensive and long. Length and accuracy are not the difficulty.

The difficulty is that a file does not distinguish between what was established and what the outgoing officer concluded. Both appear as sentences, in the same voice, and a successor who has never served in the region cannot tell them apart.

So the instruction must require every statement to be marked: fact, with its source and date; assessment, with whose assessment it is; or impression, labelled as impression. The marking costs nothing in length and converts a description into something that can be acted on.

Quotation requires the same discipline. Exact words in quotation marks with a date; paraphrase marked in substance; inference marked as inference. A successor cannot telephone a minister to ask what he meant in March, so what you write is all there will ever be.

The third category cannot be written at all. Information received in confidence, an assessment of a colleague that would be unfair on paper, and anything that would put a person at risk belong in a room, and a note that contains them is a note that will be read by somebody it was not written for.

What the note can do is record that they exist: a line saying that there are five matters to be conveyed in person, and the date on which that will happen. A handover that ends without that line has quietly decided that four years of confidences die with the posting.

> **DATA POINT · Confidences** — Handover practice commonly provides for information received in confidence to be conveyed orally and for the handover note to record that such matters exist and when they will be transmitted, rather than omitting them without trace.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: nominalisation and summary in a final report**

- A nominalisation turns a verb into a noun: we established the programme becomes the establishment of the programme. It compresses, which is why summaries use it.
- Its danger is that the agent disappears. The establishment of the programme does not say who established it, and in a final report that information is the point.

- Where a judgement is yours, write it in the first person. A final report that says an assessment was made has concealed the only person who can be held to it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Everything can be drafted, and nothing can be represented, by anything but a person**

**Figure 10.5** — The left column is work. The right column is why a person was sent.

A final report on a posting can be drafted in an afternoon from four years of files, and the draft will be well ordered, properly summarised and accurate. There is no reason to refuse that help, and this book has spent six volumes explaining how to ask for it well.

The division it leaves behind is the subject of this last unit. Almost everything a mission produces can be prepared with assistance: the record, the summary, the comparison, the checklist, the glossary, the translation, the first draft of nearly every document that leaves the building.

What cannot be is representation itself. A signature is a person accepting consequences. A judgement about whether somebody can be trusted is four years of meetings and no document. An apology is a person in a room, and its value lies precisely in the fact that it cost something to give.

The practical consequence runs through every unit of this series. Specify the work so precisely that the help is genuinely useful, and keep the irreducible part — the decision, the judgement, the presence, the apology — unmistakably your own.

A final report should also contain the paragraph that most reports omit: a recommendation that proved mistaken, in the first person, with the reasoning as it stood at the time and not as it looks now. Hindsight makes every judgement look foolish, and a successor facing the same decision needs the reasoning rather than the verdict.

And the last thing is not written at all. Four things were told to this Ambassador in a room by her predecessor; she will tell four to her successor in another room; and the only continuity that matters is the one that passes between two people who are both present.
