### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the second conditional for options and consequences**

- Use the second conditional for a situation that is not the case, or not likely: if the note gave its sources, I could check it.
- The pattern is if + past simple, then would, could or might + infinitive.
- In careful writing use were for all persons after if: if it were my first meeting; if I were you.
- Would, could and might differ in strength: I would ask (I am sure), I could ask (it is possible), I might ask (perhaps).

**Figure 1.2** — If + past simple, would + infinitive. Will never follows if in this pattern.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A background note without dates is a story, not a briefing**

**Figure 1.5** — Six true sentences, five of them true about a different year.

Research before a first meeting is one of the things a system does genuinely well. It can read a registry, a website, three annual reports and a dozen news items faster than any officer, and it can write them up in good order.

What it cannot do is date them. Published material does not say which of its sentences is still true, and a summary flattens four years of change into a single confident present tense.

If you asked for the material with its dates, you would get a different document: fewer statements, each attached to a year, and a short list of the things that could not be established at all.

That document is less impressive and far more useful. It lets you walk into a room knowing the difference between what you know and what you knew.

It also leaves room for the three questions that no search answers: who decides, what they have had to stop doing, and what they would refuse money for.

Ask those in the room, write the answers in the file with the date, and the second meeting begins where the first one ended rather than where the internet did.

> **DATA POINT · Background research** — Practice in cooperation work commonly requires that a background note distinguish published fact, with its date and source, from inference, and that figures about an organisation be dated.  
> Source: general development cooperation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Dating information** — Where a background note draws on published material, practice commonly requires each statement to carry the date of the source, and unestablished points to be listed as such rather than omitted.  
> Source: general development cooperation guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A summary keeps what is interesting and drops what is binding**

**Figure 2.5** — Everything descriptive survived. Everything binding disappeared.

Summarising a forty-page document is one of the things a system does best, and the result reads better than most officers could write at the end of a long day.

The problem is in the word summary. A summary keeps what is important in a text, and in ordinary prose what is important is what the text is about.

A project document is not about anything. It is an instrument: it creates obligations, conditions and the circumstances in which a party may walk away. The descriptive parts are the least operative parts of it.

So do not ask for a summary. Ask for two things: a half-page description of what the project will do, and a complete list, in the document’s own words, of every condition, obligation and deadline, with its page number.

The second list cannot be shortened, because a condition reproduced in half is not a condition. It can be long; it is still read faster than forty pages.

And the conditions go first on the page. A head of mission with twenty minutes should learn what can stop the project before learning what the project hopes to do.

> **DATA POINT · Conditions in project documents** — Practice in cooperation work commonly requires that any summary of a project document reproduce the conditions precedent and reporting obligations in full, since these determine whether the project can begin at all.  
> Source: general development cooperation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Summaries of binding texts** — Where a binding text is summarised for decision-makers, practice commonly requires conditions, obligations and deadlines to be reproduced in full and cited by page, with description kept separate.  
> Source: general document practice literature, 2025–2026.

- In order to + infinitive gives a purpose with the same subject: they include the salaries in order to keep the staff employed.
- With the result that + clause gives a consequence, usually unintended: nobody asked, with the result that a third of the line may be refused.
- Keep purpose and result apart. So that looks forward to something wanted; with the result that looks back at something that happened.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A translated budget is a budget you can read and cannot yet use**

**Figure 3.5** — Six headings, two administrations, and not one of them read identically.

A system will translate a budget quickly and well, and it will produce a clear note on each line. That is genuinely useful, and it is not the thing you need.

A budget heading is not a word to be translated. It is a rule about what may be charged to it, and that rule lives in the administration that wrote it, not in the dictionary.

Two honest parties can read local costs, overheads or per diem in two different ways for years, and nothing will reveal the difference until an invoice arrives, or an auditor asks for a document that was never kept.

So use the translation to find the lines worth asking about, and then ask. For every heading over about a tenth of the total, get two sentences in writing: what it includes and what it excludes, with one real example of each.

Those two sentences are agreed before signature so that both administrations have the same rule, rather than after the first audit, with the result that a partner is asked to repay money it spent in good faith.

The exercise costs an afternoon. The alternative costs a partner, and partners are harder to replace than afternoons.

> **DATA POINT · Budget headings** — Practice in cooperation work commonly requires that each budget heading be accompanied by a statement of what it includes and excludes, agreed in writing before signature, since translation alone does not establish coverage.  
> Source: general development cooperation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Repayment risk** — Where expenditure is later found ineligible under a heading, practice commonly places the obligation to repay on the implementing body, whatever the understanding it held at the time, which is why written definitions are sought in advance.  
> Source: general development cooperation guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Read the call for what it forbids before you read it for what it wants**

**Figure 4.5** — The order a call is read in is almost the reverse of the order it is applied in.

Reading a twenty-page call and turning it into a list of requirements is a task a system performs well, and it is one of the few places in this book where you can accept most of what comes back.

Ask for it in the right order, though. Not the objectives first, but the gates: who may apply, what must be true at the date of submission, what documents must be attached, what limits cannot be exceeded.

Each requirement should come back with the page and paragraph it sits in, because a requirement without a reference cannot be checked by anybody else, and somebody else will check it at eleven o’clock at night.

Then read those four pages yourself, with a pen. A system will not notice that your partner has applied for registration and not received it, because the call does not discuss pending applications and neither does the summary.

Where a rule could be read two ways, use the clarification window. The question is free, the answer is published, and an answer in writing before you start is worth more than nine days of good drafting afterwards.

None of this makes the proposal better. It makes the proposal readable, which is the condition of its being good at all.

> **DATA POINT · Eligibility and award** — Calls for proposals commonly separate eligibility conditions, which are checked before assessment, from award criteria, which are scored; an application failing an eligibility condition is normally not assessed at all.  
> Source: general grant practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Clarification questions** — Call procedures commonly provide a period during which applicants may seek clarification, with answers published to all applicants, closing a fixed number of days before the submission deadline.

**A refusal that cannot be acted on is a refusal you will have to write twice**

**Figure 7.5** — The left column protects the writer. The right column serves the reader.

Asking a system to draft a refusal produces a letter of unusual warmth and perfect courtesy, and it will contain no fact that the reader could act on.

The test is simple. Read your letter as the partner and ask what you would do differently next time. If the answer is nothing, you have written a letter that protects you and costs the Mission a partner.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: conditions and precautions — as long as, in case, otherwise**

- As long as states a condition: as long as the wording is agreed, either word may be used. It takes a present tense.
- In case states a precaution against something that may happen: keep a copy in case the file is lost. It also takes a present tense, never will.
- Otherwise states what will happen if the thing is not done: define the term now; otherwise the argument will continue.
- Do not confuse in case with if. Take an umbrella in case it rains (a precaution, before the rain); open it if it rains (a condition, when the rain comes).

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A glossary is cheap, and it is written after the argument instead of before it**

**Figure 8.5** — Six of eleven lines. Each one had already cost somebody a letter.

Reconciling two institutions’ vocabularies, term by term, is one of the best things you can ask a system to help with. Give it both sets of documents and it will find the words that appear in both with different company around them.

It will not know which meaning is correct, and it should not: that is a decision for the two parties. What it can do is produce the candidate list, and the candidate list is most of the work.

Ask for three columns rather than two: the term, how each side appears to use it, and the evidence — the sentence in each body’s documents that shows the usage. A glossary without evidence is an opinion.

Then sit down with the partner and agree a wording for each line, term by term. Where a word is loaded on both sides, invent a third that nobody has a habit about; where a term is genuinely the partner’s, use theirs and explain it in a line.

Annex the result to the agreement, in case anybody reverts to an old usage, and send it to the auditors as well; otherwise you will have two definitions in two files and a third in somebody’s head.

An afternoon, eleven lines, and the eighteen-month argument stops. It will not be the last argument, but it will be the last one about words.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A comparison of two texts finds what is there, and a mood is not there**

**Figure 9.5** — Four clean lines, two findings, and the most important finding is an absence.

Comparing a translated agreement with its original, clause by clause, is work a system does well and a tired officer does badly. Numbering, cross-references, defined terms, periods and amounts: all of it can be checked in minutes.

Ask for the check in categories rather than as a general comparison. Numbers and periods first, because a difference between ninety days and three months is invisible to a reader and decisive in February.

Then obligation verbs. Many languages have no exact equivalent of shall, and a translator choosing between their nearest three options is making a legal decision without being asked to.

Where a verb cannot be made to match, do not argue about the verb. Write the consequence: what happens if two consecutive meetings are not held, what happens if a report is not delivered by the fifteenth. A consequence is a fact and survives any translation.

And ask the question the texts cannot answer for themselves: is there a clause saying which language prevails? An absence leaves no trace in either document, and in two authentic texts without such a clause every difference is a quarrel without an answer.

The whole check costs an hour and a legal review. The alternative is to discover the difference on the one day in three years when it decides something.

> **DATA POINT · Authentic texts** — Where an instrument is concluded in two languages and both texts are authentic, practice commonly provides for a clause stating which text prevails in the event of divergence, and for both texts to be initialled page by page before signature.

> **DATA POINT · Divergence between texts** — Where authentic texts diverge and no prevailing language is stipulated, interpretative practice commonly seeks a meaning common to both texts, which may be neither party’s reading, and which is why a prevailing-language clause is preferred.

**Grammar: hedged recommendation — appears to, suggests that, tends to**

**The narrowest claim you can prove is worth more than the widest claim you can make**

**Figure 10.5** — Three claims the project owns, and three it must hand back.

An end-of-project report drafted from the project’s own documents will claim everything the documents mention, because the documents mention what happened in the towns where the project worked.

That is not dishonesty; it is the absence of a counterfactual. Nothing in a project file records what else was going on, so nothing in a summary of the file can subtract it.

So supply what the file lacks. Ask the partner, the district authority and your own notes for everything else that changed in those towns in those years, and require the report to list the other plausible causes in a paragraph of their own.

Then hedge properly. “The time households spend fetching water appears to have fallen by about half, on a sample of sixty households in four towns” is a sentence that survives a hostile reader. “The project transformed lives” does not survive a polite one.

A report like this is shorter, less flattering and far more useful. It is also the document that will be read before anybody funds your next proposal, which is the real reason to write it honestly.
