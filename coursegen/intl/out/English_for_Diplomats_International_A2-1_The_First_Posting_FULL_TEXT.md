**Grammar: introductions and word order: the verb to be with names and roles**

- Use am with I, is with he, she and it, and are with you, we and they.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A system can make a long text short, and a short text can lose a small word**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines of one notice. Four of them changed when the text became short.

Mira has a two-page notice in English. It is written for lawyers. She has five minutes.

She asks a system to say the same thing in simple words. The answer is one short paragraph. It is clear. She reads it in forty seconds. This is a real gain on a busy day.

The simple version is also smaller. Small words go first. “Only on Tuesdays” becomes “on Tuesdays”. “Up to one hour” becomes “an hour”. “Between 09:00 and 12:00” becomes “in the morning”.

These small words carry the rule. “Only” means no other day. “Up to” means one hour or less. A simple text that loses them is easy to read and wrong to act on.

So ask for two things, not one. Ask for simple words. Then ask: which words in the original are conditions, and are they all still here?

And when the answer still matters, ask a person. Farida is two doors away and she has read this notice every year for six years.

> **DATA POINT · English in diplomatic work** — In many international settings English is used as a working language by people who all speak it as a second or third language, which changes how misunderstandings happen and how they are repaired.  
> Source: general applied linguistics literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Small words carry conditions** — Plain-language guidance commonly notes that words such as only, up to, before and not carry the conditions of a rule, and that simplification which removes them changes what the rule requires.  
> Source: general plain-language guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Asking for the same thing said more simply

**Figure 1.7** — Forty seconds to read the short version. Two minutes to check the small words.

Give the whole text, not half of it. Ask for simple words and short sentences. Say how many lines you want.

Then ask one more question: which condition words are in the original, and are they all in your version? Words like only, up to, before, after and not are the rule.

When the answer costs money or time, ask a person as well. A colleague who has seen the text every year knows what it means in practice.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for simple: **Please say this again in simple words, in eight lines or fewer.**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A system can read the letters of a name, and the letters do not say who the name belongs to**

**Figure 2.5** — Five written versions of one name, and one person who knows which is hers.

A name in another alphabet has to be written in this one, and that is a real problem with no perfect answer. Two spellings can be reasonable, and only one of them is the person’s own.

A system is useful here, and it is fast. Give it a spelling and it will write the sounds, mark the stress and suggest a polite form of address, all in a second.

Use it before the meeting. A first try is much better than a guess, and a guess in front of a visitor is the one thing you cannot take back.

Then remember what it is reading. It reads letters, and letters do not say which family a person comes from, which language the family speaks at home, or how the person says the name herself.

In Varkent two families write one name in the same way and say it differently, because one family came from the north and one came from the coast. Both families hear the difference at once.

So ask. Say good morning, apologise once, and ask the person how she says her own name. Then write it down exactly as she writes it, and change the staff list the same day.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A system knows the published hours, and the week is also a set of habits**

**Figure 4.5** — Six rows. A published timetable is right about all of them and useful about one.

Ask a system about working hours in a city and the answer is quick, clear and usually right. Offices open at nine, they close at five, and the week runs from Monday to Friday.

That is worth having on your first day. It also tells you about public holidays, which are published, and about time differences, which are arithmetic.

**A form translates quickly, and a term on a form carries a meaning the translation does not**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A short email is easy to answer, and a cold email is easy to leave until Friday**

**Figure 8.5** — Three versions. The last one is five words longer than the short one and was answered in a day.

An email to another ministry is read by somebody who has ninety messages, a meeting at eleven, and English as a second or third language. That is the reader to write for.

A system is good at the mechanical part of this. Give it a long polite draft and ask for a shorter one with numbered questions, and you will have it in seconds.

The short version is nearly always better. The questions come out of the prose and stand on their own lines, and the reader can answer them in order without reading the rest twice.

Something is lost as well. Courtesy is the first thing a shortener removes, because a polite sentence carries no information. The result is clear and a little cold, and a cold email from a new officer is easy to leave until Friday.

The repair is small. Put one true courtesy line back at the top, about them and not about you, and keep the numbered questions and the date exactly as they are.

And remember what you are asking a system to judge. It can count words and make sentences shorter. It does not know that this official is senior, that he answers his own email, and that he once waited forty minutes in your reception.

> **DATA POINT · Politeness and clarity** — Guidance on international correspondence commonly observes that heavily hedged English — layered modals and indirect questions — is harder for second-language readers to act on, and recommends courtesy in the opening line with direct requests in the body.  
> Source: general correspondence practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Reading in a second language** — Comprehension research commonly finds that readers working in a second language process direct requests and numbered items more reliably than hedged or embedded ones, particularly under time pressure.  
> Source: general applied linguistics literature, 2025–2026.

**Grammar: linking words: and, but, because, so, then**

- And joins two things of the same kind: I left early and I took the tram.

**A route is correct on the day it was written, and a city changes without telling anybody**

**Figure 9.5** — Six rows. Only the third one would have got her to the meeting on time.

Asking for a route takes seconds and gives you a clear answer: the stop, the number, the change and the walk. For an ordinary journey this is excellent, and it is far better than asking at a bus stop in a language you are learning.

An address becomes a map pin, a postcode becomes a district, and a timetable becomes a departure time. None of that is a small thing in your first month.

The information has a date, even when the date is not shown. A route is a photograph of a city, taken on the day somebody wrote it down, and cities change afterwards.

Bridges close for repairs. Markets fill a street on one day of the week. A tram runs every six minutes in winter and every twelve in August. None of this is secret, and none of it reaches a published route quickly.

Amara has crossed that city twice a day for six years, and she has not been late to a meeting in all that time. She told Mira on Monday evening, and Mira did not listen, which is a mistake you only make once.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A report that reads well is easy to write, and a reader needs to know how you know**

**Figure 10.5** — Six lines. Four have a source, and two came from the shape of a report.

Turning four pages of notes into one page is slow work for a new officer, and a system does it in under a minute, in good order, with short paragraphs and a clear first line.

That is worth having on a Thursday evening at seven o’clock, and the result is usually better organised than a first attempt by somebody who has never written a report before.

A report has a usual shape, and the shape has places in it for things like an atmosphere, a sense of willingness and an agreement on next steps. When your notes do not fill those places, a draft may fill them anyway.

That is where the invented sentences come from. They are not lies in any ordinary sense: they are the sentences that reports contain, written by something that has read a great many reports.

Your reader cannot tell the difference. The officer in the capital reads one page and cannot see your four pages of notes, so every sentence in the report has the same authority.

So after a draft comes back, do one pass with a pencil. For every sentence ask: did I see it, was I told it, or do I think it? Cut whatever answers none of the three, and name the source of everything you were told.

> **DATA POINT · Reporting what was observed** — Reporting guidance commonly distinguishes between what an officer observed directly, what was said to them, and the officer’s own assessment, and requires the three to be distinguishable in the text.  
> Source: general reporting practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Three kinds of sentence** — Reporting practice commonly requires observed facts, reported statements and the writer’s own assessment to be separated, so that a reader can weigh each differently without asking the writer.  
> Source: general reporting practice literature, 2025–2026.
