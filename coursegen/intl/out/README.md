# English for Diplomats
### International Edition · six books · A2.1 → B2.2

For diplomats of any nationality: embassies, consulates, permanent missions and
delegations. The sending state is never named, the host countries are invented,
and nothing in the six books depends on one country's practice, politics or
religion. The working language is English — for most of the people in these
books, as for most of the people who will use them, it is a second or third
language, and the course is written on that assumption throughout.

## What is in each unit

Twelve parts, in this order:

1. **Warm Up and Read** — a figure, three questions, the reading, a sourced
   data point, and five comprehension exercise types
2. **Vocabulary** — twenty terms, defined, with two exercises
3. **Grammar and Dialogues** — one grammar point with a LOOSE/PRECISE figure
   and fifteen items, then two twelve-line dialogues, the second ending on an
   open question
4. **Speaking** — three pair tasks
5. **Extended Reading** — six paragraphs on the capability, with a figure, a
   second data point and comprehension
6. **Guided Writing** — the six-line tasking: Purpose, Scope, Inputs, Criteria,
   Done, Fallback
7. **Working with AI** — how to use the capability, what to say, and the
   boundary line: what it cannot do
8. **The Decision Room** — a case with an exhibit, three voices, five steps and
   a documented outcome
9. **The Brief Lab** — a six-line instruction as somebody actually gave it,
   what came back, what was wrong with the instruction, and the rewrite
10. **Across the Language Line** — an unscripted spoken round for the moment a
    meaning fails between two languages: the ladder, six repair moves, a red
    line and a five-row score sheet
11. **Six-Skill Review** — reading, listening, speaking, writing, vocabulary,
    grammar
12. **Can-Do and Foundations** — five statements to tick, four foundations, and
    the answer key for the unit

**Part 10 is this edition's signature part.** Every unit ends with a situation
in which a word, a formula, a legal form, an acronym or a convention does not
survive the journey between two languages — and nobody in the room has done
anything wrong. The exercise is repair, not correction.

## Teaching it

**One unit is two to three ninety-minute classes.** A workable split:

- Class A: Parts 1–3 (reading, vocabulary, grammar and dialogues)
- Class B: Parts 4–7 (speaking, extended reading, guided writing, working with AI)
- Class C: Parts 8–12 (the Decision Room, the Brief Lab, Across the Language
  Line, review, can-do)

**Across the Language Line needs no preparation and must not be prepared.**
Student B reads one turn at a time; Student A answers immediately, out loud,
with nothing written down first. Then both score the answer against the five
rows. It is the only part of the course where a wrong answer out loud is the
point.

**The Decision Room works as a plenary.** Read the brief, show the exhibit,
take the three voices as three readers, then work the five steps and write the
four-line rule as a class.

**Self-study** works: every exercise has an answer key at the end of its unit,
and the Part 10 rounds can be read aloud alone, slowly, which is less useful
than a partner and better than nothing.

**For mixed-nationality classes** the invented world does real work: Arvenia,
Belmora, Corvadia, Dalmane, Eskavia, Fenmark, Grevia, Harraden and Ilmaria
carry every politically delicate example, so a class of twelve nationalities
can argue about a case without anybody defending their own ministry.

## What the course argues

One thing, in sixty units: a system is very good at everything that has been
written down, and the part of diplomatic work that decides outcomes is almost
never written down.

So the books teach delegation and verification together. Every unit states the
gain in figures — forty-one slips sorted in two minutes, thirty-one clauses
compared in minutes, a hundred and six pages reduced to eighteen — and then
states exactly where it stopped, and what the officer had to do instead: ask a
local colleague to read the original, telephone the four delegations that moved
in a corridor, read page four with a pen, walk to the water point and talk to
whoever is carrying a container.

The second argument is specific to this edition. English in these books is
nobody's first language, which changes the technique: one term for one concept,
one proposition to a sentence, conditions before the words they govern, figures
read twice, lists numbered rather than separated by commas, and every
qualification reproduced verbatim because a translator's grammar will otherwise
decide it for you.

And four things the books say cannot be delegated at all: being present in a
room, being trusted by a person, being answerable for a decision, and
apologising in a way that costs something.
