### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: comparatives and superlatives for places, people and options**

- Short adjectives add -er and -est: short, shorter, the shortest.
- Long adjectives use more and the most: important, more important, the most important.
- Good and bad are irregular: better, the best; worse, the worst.
- Use than after a comparative: Tuesday is more convenient than Thursday.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Five facts come off a card in seconds, and the sixth is about a relationship**

**Figure 1.5** — Six rows. The appearance of the card predicts the importance of the event in none of them.

A card in another language can be read in seconds. Ask for the date, the time, the place, the dress and the day for a reply, and you will get all five, set out as a list.

For a new officer this removes a real fear. You no longer stand with a card you cannot read and a reply date you cannot find, and you no longer ask a colleague to translate eleven cards on a Monday.

The five facts are the easy part, because they are printed. The sixth question is not printed and is the one that matters: should the Mission go, and who should go?

That question is answered by things no card contains. Who are the hosts? Which of them was a minister until two years ago? Which committee does one of them chair, and does it touch your files?

The appearance of an invitation tells you nothing. The gold card from a large hall may be a routine evening for a junior officer. The plain card from a cultural institute may be the most useful two hours of the month.

So let a system read the card, and take the names to somebody who has been in the city for years. Farida needs four seconds and gives you the one fact that decides the answer.

> **DATA POINT · Invitations and precedence** — Protocol practice commonly treats an invitation as carrying information about the relationship between institutions, so that the decision to accept, and at what level, is taken on grounds that are not stated on the card.  
> Source: general protocol practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Attendance decisions** — Protocol guidance commonly notes that the decision whether to accept an invitation, and at what level, depends on the relationship with the host and on current business, rather than on the formality of the invitation itself.

**A list of names can be sorted in minutes, and the room will not match the list**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A rule of precedence is correct, and the evening is made of people**

**Figure 4.5** — Three decisions the rule makes well, and three it cannot make at all.

Ask about precedence and seating and you will get a clear, correct answer in a minute. Rank, guests of honour, the host’s right and left, and a workable plan for sixteen.

This is useful. Most officers learn seating by making a mistake at a dinner, and the mistake is remembered by the person who was seated wrongly.

A rule of precedence is about offices, not about people. It knows that two guests hold the same rank. It does not know that they have not spoken since March.

It also does not know who leaves early, who is deaf on one side, who will talk for the whole evening, and who is shy and needs a friendly neighbour.

All of that lives with one or two people in a Mission. Farida was in the room in March, and she changes a plan in one sentence.

So build the plan from the rule, and then walk it past a colleague before the place cards are printed. Four minutes of her time is worth more than any amount of careful arithmetic about rank.

**A draft can be cut to length in seconds, and the line people repeat has to be yours**

Cutting a text to a fixed length is a task a system does quickly and accurately. Ask for two hundred words and you will get two hundred, in ten seconds, with the main points kept.

A toast is remembered for one concrete thing: a name, a date, a small fact. Eleven words about an official who came to the Mission on a Tuesday in March will be repeated for a year.

So let a system cut the general parts, and write the specific line yourself. It will be short, it will be about somebody who is sitting in the room, and nobody else could have written it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**History is stable and arrangements are not, and a briefing cannot tell the difference**

**Figure 6.5** — Four stable facts right, one arrangement wrong, and one habit nobody wrote down.

A short country briefing is one of the most useful things you can ask for. The date, what the day marks, the head of state, the language, two safe subjects and one thing to avoid: all of it in an hour, for two countries.

Without it you walk into a reception knowing nothing, and you spend two hours talking about the weather to people who have come a long way from home.

The history is stable. A founding date does not change, an anthem does not change, and the subjects that are difficult in a country are usually difficult for a long time.

Arrangements change constantly. A reception moves to a hotel because a building is being repaired. A host arrives late and leaves early. A minister visits and the whole evening runs an hour behind.

None of that reaches a briefing, and the invitation in your hand is usually right about the venue while the briefing is wrong.

So read both, believe the invitation, and ask a colleague who went last year. Farida knows the venue, the hour the host leaves, and which two of her colleagues always attend, and she tells you all three in a corridor.

**A programme can be ordered in minutes, and a city is what makes it possible**

**Seven warm notes take two minutes, and the one that is remembered has a dog in it**

Writing seven notes on a Friday morning is a real task, and most officers do not do it. The notes are owed, the week has been long, and nothing happens if they are never sent.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A general answer describes a country, and you are invited to one house**

**Figure 9.5** — Six answers. Two are about a country, three are about a house, and one was right.

Questions of custom are the most natural thing to ask a system, and the answers are genuinely useful. They are clear, they are quick, and they save a new arrival from several obvious mistakes.

Not white flowers. Not thirteen of them. Chocolates are safe. Remove your shoes if your host does. Arrive on time, or ten minutes late, depending on the country.

All of that is true at the level of a country, which is the level at which such answers are written. It is the same kind of statement as “people in this country drink coffee in the morning”.

You are not invited to a country. You are invited to one house, where a grandmother believes that flowers belong in hospitals, where the children are eight and eleven, and where dinner is at seven because of a train.

Nobody has written any of that down, and nobody ever will. It lives with the family, with your colleague, and with anybody who has been inside that house.

So take the general answer as a floor, and then ask a person. Ask the host what to bring, and listen to his second sentence. Ask a colleague who knows the family. Two questions, one minute, and you arrive correctly.

> **DATA POINT · Custom and variation** — Guidance on cultural practice commonly warns that customs vary by region, community and family, and that general national descriptions may not apply to a particular household or occasion.  
> Source: general intercultural practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Asking the host** — Hospitality guidance commonly advises guests to ask the host directly about expectations, noting that such questions are generally received as a mark of respect rather than as ignorance.  
> Source: general intercultural practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — A question of local custom, and why a general answer fails

### Part 10 · Across the Language Line — Across the Language Line · “No, thank you” said three times

At dinner the host offers Mira more food. She has eaten enough and says, once, “No, thank you.” The plate is filled anyway, warmly and without comment.

In that house an offer is made three times, and a guest is expected to refuse twice and accept on the third, or to refuse all three times in the same words. One refusal is heard as politeness, not as an answer.

**Figure 9.12** — Three offers, one refusal, and a full plate.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A contact note writes itself from what you dictate, and a request you forgot is not in it**

**Figure 10.5** — Six kinds of line. Five are in the note, and the sixth was the only one with a deadline.

A contact note is the easiest thing in the world not to write. It is eleven o’clock, the evening is over, and nothing happens tomorrow if the note does not exist.

Dictating it is much easier than writing it. Say what you remember into a system and a clean, ordered note comes back in a minute, with the names spelled as you spelled them.

The note will also be smooth. It will say that the evening was useful, that contacts were made, and that several people expressed interest, because that is what contact notes say.

None of that is false and none of it helps anybody in two years. What helps is a name, an organisation, a subject and a request, in four lines.

The request is the line that matters and the line that is lost. A man asks for the text of a speech, gives a reason, and four days later nobody has sent it, because nobody wrote it down.

So dictate the requests first, while you are still in the car. Begin with “he asked me to”, and the rest of the note can be as smooth as it likes.

> **DATA POINT · Contact records** — Practice in contact management commonly provides that any request made by a contact is recorded verbatim with the date, since requests create expectations and are the most frequently forgotten element of a meeting.  
> Source: general contact management literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Timeliness of records** — Practice in contact management commonly recommends that notes of meetings be made on the same day, since recall of names and specific requests falls sharply after a few hours.  
> Source: general contact management literature, 2025–2026.
