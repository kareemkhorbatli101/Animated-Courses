The Friday distribution runs to a hundred and six pages in eleven documents. The session opens at ten on Monday, and Elena has, in practice, one evening.

It is widely assumed that length indicates importance. In this distribution the longest document, at forty-one pages, is a secretariat compilation of positions already known, while the shortest, at two pages, contains the only text on which a decision is sought.

A reduction is produced: eleven summaries of a paragraph each, accurately drawn and uniformly weighted, in which the two-page draft decision and the forty-one-page compilation occupy the same space.

Uniform weighting is the error. What is required is not a summary of each document but a ranking of all of them, by whether anything is decided in them and whether the delegation has an instruction on it.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: formal register — impersonal subjects and nominal style**

- Impersonal subjects remove the actor where the actor is not the point: it is widely assumed that … ; it was noted that … ; consideration should be given to … .
- Nominal style carries the action in a noun rather than a verb: the choice of criteria is a matter for the delegation, rather than the delegation must choose the criteria.
- Use the register where the institution uses it — in records, papers and interventions — and not in instructions to your own staff, where it hides who must act.
- The commonest fault is nominal style without an agent anywhere: “a reduction was produced” leaves the reader unable to ask whom to correct.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A reduction is useful in proportion to the weighting you impose on it**

**Figure 1.5** — A hundred and six pages reduced to eighteen that must be read, and quoted where they bind.

The reduction of a large distribution is among the most valuable operations available, and it is also the one in which the shape of the request determines almost the whole of the value obtained.

A request for a summary of each document will be answered with a summary of each document. Uniform treatment is the natural output of an undifferentiated instruction, and it destroys exactly the information a delegate needs, which is the difference between the documents.

What must be supplied, therefore, is the weighting. Documents in which something is decided are treated differently in kind from documents in which something is described, and the distinction is carried in the document symbol rather than in the title or the length.

For a text on which a decision is sought, summary is not merely insufficient but positively harmful: operative paragraphs are to be quoted entire, and bracketed wording reproduced as it stands, since a bracket records a disagreement which no summary has the authority to resolve.

Information papers, by contrast, may be reduced to a line, and several of them to a list of titles. It is no disrespect to a forty-one page compilation to record that it compiles positions already held.

The output of such an evening is not a document but two or three questions to the capital, put early enough to be answered. A delegate who arrives with an accurate summary and no instruction has read a hundred pages to no purpose.

> **DATA POINT · Conference documentation** — Documentation practice in multilateral bodies commonly distinguishes documents submitted for decision from those submitted for information, with the distinction carried in the document symbol rather than in the title or length.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Instructions** — Delegation practice commonly requires that the absence of an instruction on a matter before a body be identified and reported in time for instructions to be sought, rather than resolved by the delegate at the table.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · Reading an Agenda for the Real Decisions

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: inversion after negative adverbials — not only, at no time, only if · AI: an agenda read for where the decisions actually sit*

**Figure 2.0** — ITEM 11 WAS CALLED “ORGANISATION OF WORK”, AND IT DECIDED EVERYTHING: A provisional agenda of fourteen items, annotated, for a four-day session.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who writes the agenda for your meetings?
- Where do your real decisions get taken?
- What is the most boring item that matters?

The provisional agenda has fourteen items, with annotations. Items 4 to 9 are substantive and are where every delegation will prepare to speak.

Item 11 is entitled organisation of work. Its annotation runs to four lines and proposes that items 6 and 8 be taken together, that item 7 be referred to a drafting group, and that the session close at one o’clock on the Thursday.

Not only does that annotation decide which proposals can be amended; it decides which cannot be discussed at all. Items taken together are debated once, and a text referred to a drafting group leaves the plenary for the rest of the session.

At no time is item 11 described as substantive. It is the organisation of the work, and the organisation of the work is where the work is actually distributed.

Only if the agenda is read as a set of consequences, rather than a list of subjects, does the shape of the session become visible before it begins.

**Grammar: inversion after negative adverbials — not only, at no time, only if**

- When a negative or restrictive adverbial opens the sentence, the subject and the auxiliary change places: at no time was the item described as substantive.
- With a simple tense and no auxiliary, do, does or did is supplied: not only does it decide the order; it decides the outcome.
- With only if, only when and not until, the inversion falls in the main clause, not the subordinate one: only if the agenda is read closely does the shape become visible.
- The construction belongs to formal written and spoken register. It emphasises the adverbial, and it is spoiled entirely by using it twice in a paragraph.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An agenda is a list of subjects and a schedule of consequences**

**Figure 2.5** — Read as subjects, item 11 is the least interesting. Read as consequences, it is the session.

An agenda can be analysed quickly and well: items listed, annotations summarised, documents matched to items, and the whole set against the previous session’s outcomes.

The analysis will nonetheless mislead, because it answers the question the agenda is written to answer — what will be discussed — and not the question a delegation needs answered, which is what each item does to the others.

Three mechanisms deserve particular attention. Items taken together are debated once, which halves the opportunities to intervene and converts an amendment to one text into an amendment to a joint one. A referral to a drafting group removes a text from the plenary, often for the rest of a session. And a closing hour fixes how much time any late text can possibly be given.

None of the three appears as a substantive proposal. All three appear under an organisational item, in annotations of three or four lines, and at no time are they described as decisions about outcomes.

The instruction must therefore ask for effects rather than contents: for each item, what is decided, what is foreclosed, and which other items are affected. Only if the request is put in those terms does the analysis become usable at the table.

And where an organisational proposal would disadvantage you, the objection is made on procedural grounds, which are frequently genuine. A text that reports two hours before the close cannot be translated, and no delegation will say in public that it should be adopted untranslated.

> **DATA POINT · Organisation of work** — Conference practice commonly places decisions on the joint consideration of items, referral to subsidiary bodies and the allocation of time under an organisational item, the effect of which on substantive outcomes is frequently decisive.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Referral** — Where an item is referred to a subsidiary or drafting body, conference practice commonly provides that the plenary resumes consideration only on that body’s report, which may not be presented within the same session.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you say no at a meeting without saying no?
- Which words signal movement, and which signal none?
- How do you record what somebody else said?

The delegation of Corvadia speaks for eleven minutes. The summary that reaches the capital says, accurately, that Corvadia supports the draft text.

What was said was that Corvadia can support the text, although not in its present form; that while it shares the objective, it has difficulty with the third paragraph; and that even if the paragraph were redrafted, instructions would be required.

A report that records the position and discards the conditions tells its reader the one thing that is least useful: what another delegation said, rather than what it can be brought to accept.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**In an intervention the subordinate clause is the content**

**Figure 3.5** — Six elements of one statement, and five of them live in the subordinate clauses.

An intervention of eleven minutes can be transcribed, translated and summarised with great accuracy, and the summary will be useless in proportion to its elegance.

The reason is structural rather than linguistic. Ordinary summary preserves main clauses and discards subordinate ones, because in ordinary prose the main clause carries the meaning. In an intervention it carries the position, which everybody already knows, while the subordinate clauses carry the only new information there is: what the delegation would require in order to move.

The instruction must therefore reverse the usual priority. Qualifications, conditions and reservations are to be reproduced verbatim; the position may be given in four words.

Certain formulations are to be quoted and never paraphrased: although, while, even if, subject to, at this stage, in principle, not in a position to. Each is a phrase of art, and each has been chosen in preference to a stronger or weaker one.

Two further elements belong in the analysis. The first is silence: what a delegation did not mention, particularly where it was expected to. The second is what the statement commits them to if it is quoted back at them in three months, which is the only test of a formulation that matters.

Impressions of tone are valuable and belong on a separate line, marked as impression. Firmness heard in a room has a way of becoming firmness recorded in a file, and a capital reading firmly opposed will not make an approach that at this stage was designed to invite.

> **DATA POINT · Reporting statements** — Reporting practice in multilateral work commonly requires that qualifications, conditions and reservations expressed in an intervention be reproduced, since the negotiating value of a statement resides principally in them.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Phrases of art** — Negotiating practice attaches settled meanings to certain formulations, including in principle, at this stage and not in a position to, which are customarily reproduced verbatim in reporting rather than paraphrased.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

## Unit 4 · Amendments and the Shape of a Text

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: the language of amendment — insert, delete, replace, retain · AI: amendments tracked through five versions of a text*

**Figure 4.0** — BY THE FIFTH REVISION THE OBLIGATION HAD BECOME AN ASPIRATION: Paragraph 9 of a draft decision, traced through five revisions over nine days.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How does a text change during a negotiation?
- Who keeps the earlier versions?
- What happens to a word that disappears gradually?

Paragraph 9 of the draft decision has existed in five versions in nine days. In the first it provided that parties shall submit annual data. In the fifth it provides that parties are encouraged to submit data periodically where practicable.

No single revision was objectionable. Shall became should in revision two, submit became endeavour to submit in revision three, annual became periodic in revision four, and the qualification where practicable was inserted in revision five.

Each change was proposed as a drafting improvement, each was accepted in a room where the immediately preceding version was the only one on the table, and the whole set has the effect of removing the obligation altogether.

This is an ordinary feature of negotiation rather than a trick, and the remedy is mechanical. Every provision that matters is to be compared with version one and not with the version that has just been superseded.

A delegation that holds instructions on a paragraph is therefore obliged to track that paragraph through every revision, which is a task of exactly the kind that can be delegated to a machine and must be specified with care.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The text to compare with is the first one, not the last one**

**Figure 4.5** — Five versions, each a small step, and a change of legal character over nine days.

Tracking a provision through successive revisions is among the most reliable uses of a machine in a negotiation, and it is also among the easiest to specify badly.

The ordinary request — what has changed since the last version — produces exactly the comparison that a negotiating room already performs, and a room comparing each text with its immediate predecessor will accept an unlimited series of small weakenings.

The instruction must therefore name version one as the baseline and require every provision on which instructions are held to be compared against it, with each intermediate version listed so that the sequence of changes is visible.

It must also name the elements that matter, since a general comparison returns every difference with equal weight. The operative verb, the scope, the time limits, the qualifications and the brackets are what determine the character of a provision; punctuation and the order of subparagraphs are usually not.

A particular class of change deserves separate treatment. Words moved into or out of a chapeau change their scope without changing their wording, and a comparison that reports wording only will record such a move as no change at all.

Where the cumulative effect is substantive, the amendment is drafted against the current text and the explanation is given against version one. A delegation that says shall was replaced by four separate dilutions in nine days is making a stronger case than one that objects to the fifth of them.

> **DATA POINT · Successive revisions** — Drafting practice commonly observes that the cumulative effect of a series of individually minor revisions may alter the legal character of a provision, and recommends comparison against the original rather than the preceding text.  
> Source: general drafting practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Chapeau and scope** — Drafting practice recognises that qualifying words transferred between a chapeau and individual subparagraphs alter the scope of their application without any change in their wording, and treats such transfers as substantive.  
> Source: general drafting practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A text for the record and a text for the booth are two documents**

**Figure 5.5** — Six differences, and none of them changes a single element of the substance.

A system will produce an excellent intervention: well ordered, properly subordinated, economical and in the register of the body addressed. It will be a text for the record, because that is what good written English is.

The requirement, however, is a text that can be carried by six booths, two of which may be working through relay from a third, at a delivery rate the interpretation can actually sustain.

The constraints are specific and can all be specified in advance. One proposition to a sentence; subordinate clauses after the main verb rather than before it; acronyms expanded at first use; proper names spelled; figures read twice with the unit attached each time; lists announced before they are read.

Where a condition carries the whole weight of a position, it is stated in a short sentence of its own and repeated at the end of the intervention. The alternative is that a delegation reading its own notes afterwards records support without the condition, and acts on it.

The prepared text is handed to the booths half an hour in advance, which costs nothing and improves everything, and the paragraph that matters most is identified when it is handed in.

None of this makes an intervention less dignified. It makes it audible, insofar as the physical arrangements of a multilingual room allow anything to be audible, and an intervention that is not heard has no effect whatever on the text.

> **DATA POINT · Delivery and interpretation** — Conference practice commonly recommends a delivery rate in the region of a hundred words a minute for texts to be interpreted, and the provision of any prepared text to the booths in advance.  
> Source: general conference interpretation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Relay interpretation** — Where a language combination requires relay, each additional rendering increases the loss of qualification and detail, and practice accordingly recommends simpler syntax and slower delivery in such sessions.  
> Source: general conference interpretation guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: obligation and endeavour — shall, shall endeavour to, may**

- Shall creates an obligation: parties shall establish a review mechanism within two years.
- Shall endeavour to creates an obligation of effort only, which is satisfied by trying: parties shall endeavour to establish a mechanism.
- May creates a permission, and imposes nothing at all: a party may submit additional data.
- The three are not degrees of the same thing. A movement from shall to shall endeavour to removes the result; a movement from shall to may removes the duty. Both are substantive, and neither is a matter of style.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A comparison must report against instructions, not against a position**

**Figure 6.5** — Five met and two not, and the two carry the whole of the delegation’s discretion.

Comparing a nineteen-paragraph text against written instructions at one in the morning is exactly the kind of work that should be delegated, and the comparison will be accurate.

It will nonetheless answer the wrong question unless the question is specified. Asked whether a text is consistent with a position, a comparison will weigh the whole against the whole and return a judgement — broadly consistent, largely acceptable, substantially met — which is a conclusion the delegation alone is entitled to reach.

The instruction must therefore require a point-by-point table: each numbered instruction, the paragraph that addresses it, the operative verb in that paragraph, and the status met, met in substance or not met. Judgements of overall acceptability are to be excluded expressly.

The operative verb must be reported as it stands. Shall, shall endeavour to and may are three instruments rather than three strengths, and a comparison that renders shall endeavour to as broadly providing for has concealed the only fact that matters.

Where instructions distinguish essential from desirable points, the table is ordered by that distinction and not by paragraph number, so that the first lines a tired reader sees are the lines on which no discretion exists.

And the table ends with what is missing from the instructions themselves: the absence of a fallback on an essential point is a finding, and it is better discovered at one in the morning than at four in the afternoon.

> **DATA POINT · Instructions and discretion** — Delegation practice commonly distinguishes points on which a delegation has no discretion from those on which it may exercise judgement, and requires that a text failing an essential point be referred rather than accepted.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Acceptance subject to confirmation** — Practice permits a delegation to accept an element ad referendum, which commits it politically to refrain from reopening the element if confirmation is given, and is customarily recorded by the chair.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

**Grammar: passive reporting — it is understood, it was agreed, it was noted**

- It is understood that reports information without attributing it: it is understood that nine delegations support the text. It protects a source and signals that the information is not first-hand.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A map of what was said is an exact answer to a question about the past**

**Figure 7.5** — Two accurate columns, counted a day apart, with four delegations in different rows.

Mapping the public positions of twenty-one delegations is a task of exactly the right shape for a machine: many sources, a single question, and an output that can be cited line by line.

The output should be required to carry its citations, and every entry should be dated, since a position stated eleven days ago and a position stated this morning are different facts about the same delegation.

What such a map cannot contain is movement. A delegation that changes its position in a corridor leaves no trace in any record until it votes, and the four most important entries in any map are usually the four that have moved since the sources were written.

The map is therefore the beginning of the work rather than its result. Its proper use is to identify which delegations must be spoken to: those whose position is unknown, those whose position is stated but old, and those who obtained a drafting change after stating it.

In reporting the result, the distinction between what may be cited and what may be counted on must be kept absolutely. A corridor indication is reported to a capital without attribution, in the passive, and never repeated in a room where the delegation concerned could be embarrassed by it.

And silence is to be investigated rather than recorded. Three delegations may be silent for three unrelated reasons, and the procedural question — do you expect to speak on this item? — yields most of the answer while asking nothing of anybody.

> **DATA POINT · Public and private positions** — Practice distinguishes positions stated on the record, which may be cited, from indications given informally, which are customarily reported to a capital without attribution and never quoted publicly.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Reporting informal indications** — Reporting practice commonly requires informal indications to be conveyed in impersonal form and without attribution, and prohibits their repetition in any forum where the source could be identified.  
> Source: general conference practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: parallel structure in lists and operative paragraphs**

- Items in a list must be the same kind of word: monitoring, reporting and verification, not monitoring, to report and verification.
- Verbs in a series share the subject and the form: each party shall collect, verify and publish the data.
- Where a qualifying phrase is meant to apply to every item, it goes in the chapeau; where it applies to one, it goes with that item and the list is split if necessary.
- Parallelism is not decoration. A list whose items are of different kinds raises a genuine question about what the qualification applies to, and that question will be asked in the room.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**In a multilingual room, repetition is a courtesy and variation is a cost**

**Figure 8.5** — Six differences of form, no difference of substance, and thirty-one minutes.

A system will produce excellent English prose, and excellent English prose is written on principles that are precisely wrong for a multilingual drafting group.

Good style varies its vocabulary; a drafting group reads a new word as a new thing. Good style prefers the economical nominalisation; a second-language reader must unpack it before knowing who is obliged to do what. Good style tolerates an idiom; six delegations will ask what it means, and two will suspect it of substance.

The instruction must therefore specify the opposite of literary quality: one term for one concept throughout, repeated as often as necessary; one proposition to a sentence; verbs in the active voice with the actor named; no idiom, no metaphor and no double negative.

Lists require particular discipline, since an unparallel list raises a real question about the scope of any qualification attached to it. Every item is to be the same kind of word, and a qualification applying to all items is to be placed in the chapeau rather than at the end.

The final test is not a reading but a hearing. A draft is read aloud to two or three members of the group before the session, and every place at which a listener hesitates is rewritten, whether or not the written sentence is defensible.

None of this produces a text anybody would admire. It produces a text that seven delegations understand identically on first hearing, which is the only property of a draft that shortens a negotiation.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A record is read by people who need to act, not by people who were there**

**Figure 9.5** — Eleven pages of accurate narrative, and two pages that can be acted on.

A record of a four-day session can be produced quickly and faithfully from notes, audio and the documents distributed, and the result will be a competent chronological account.

Chronology, however, is the structure of a record written for participants. It answers the question what happened, in the order in which it happened, and participants are the only people who do not need the answer.

The readers who matter were not in the room. They require three things in this order: what was decided, in the adopted wording; what each decision requires of the mission, with the person responsible and the date; and what remains open, with the date by which it must be settled.

The discussion that produced the decisions belongs after those three parts, and the chronology belongs in an annex. Nothing is lost by this ordering, and the eleven pages become two pages and nine of annex.

Attribution requires judgement rather than a rule. Delegations are named where attribution changes what the capital would do, and reported collectively where it does not, which protects both an informal source and the mission when the record is read in six months.

And the chair’s summary is quoted entire, because it is what the body will be taken to have agreed, with any observation of your own in a separate line beneath it. A record that paraphrases a chair’s summary has replaced the only authoritative text in the document with an opinion about it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A consensus covers the words, and the understandings must be recorded separately**

**Figure 10.5** — One line of agreement, and five of things that will be argued about in March.

A note on what a consensus covered can be drafted from the adopted text, the records and the statements made, and it will be accurate on the first question: what the words say.

The three questions that matter are harder and are not answered by the text. Which provisions were left ambiguous on purpose; what each delegation understands itself to have agreed to; and what the decision does not cover at all.

The first requires a judgement about the negotiation, which only somebody who was present can make: a provision reached after three days of drafting and adopted unchanged in the final hour was very probably a formula rather than a solution.

The second is established from the statements made at and after adoption, which is why a delegation makes its own. An interpretative statement reopens nothing, takes three sentences and will be read, by somebody, in three years.

The third is the hardest to see because nothing records it. A question that was never asked leaves no trace: no paragraph, no bracket, no reservation. Funding, in this decision, is not a gap in the drafting; it is the reason the drafting succeeded.

So the post-adoption note has four parts: the text as adopted, our understanding of the provisions that bear two readings, the matters the decision does not cover, and the precedent it creates. The first part writes itself, and the other three are the work of a delegation.

> **DATA POINT · Interpretative statements** — Practice permits a delegation, on or after adoption, to place on record its understanding of a provision, which does not reopen the text and may be relied upon in later interpretation.
