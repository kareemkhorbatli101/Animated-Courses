### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the passive in procedure and instruction**

- The passive puts the action first and the actor last, or leaves the actor out: the slips are collected at the door.
- It is the usual voice for describing a procedure, because the actor is obvious or does not matter.
- Form it with be plus the past participle: is checked, are taken, was refused.
- Use the active when a particular person must act: Amara sees distressed callers first.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Sorting is fast, consistent and literal, and the urgent case is often the one that asks for nothing**

**Figure 1.5** — Forty of forty-one sorted correctly, and the forty-first had four hours.

A consular counter produces a particular kind of material: short, handwritten, in many languages, written by people who are tired, worried or in a hurry. Sorting it by hand is slow and uneven, and the unevenness is the real problem.

A system sorts forty-one slips into five categories in two minutes, reads the four that nobody at the desk can read, and sorts them the same way on a bad Monday as on a quiet Thursday.

That consistency is worth more than the speed. The old method depended on who was in the doorway and how long the queue was, and nobody could say afterwards why a slip had gone where it went.

Sorting works on what is written. A slip that asks for a passport is a passport case. A slip that asks for nothing is sorted into “other”, because “other” is what a system does with a sentence it cannot place.

People in trouble write the fewest words. They assume that the Mission already knows about the accident, the hospital and the flight, because the whole of their week has been about nothing else.

So add two rules to any sorting: every slip of fewer than ten words is read by a person, and every slip containing a hospital, a police station or a child is read by a person. Both rules cost about a minute a day.

> **DATA POINT · Triage at a public counter** — Consular practice commonly provides for the early identification of urgent cases at a public counter, since the cases that cannot wait are frequently the ones that are least clearly expressed.  
> Source: general consular practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Expression and urgency** — Studies of public-service counters commonly find no relationship between the clarity of a written enquiry and its urgency, and often an inverse one, since distressed callers write less and assume more.  
> Source: general public service practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — A queue triaged when the desk shares no language with it

**Figure 1.7** — Two minutes for the sorting. One minute for the two slips a person must read.

A queue is triaged well when the desk shares no language with it only if the triage is honest about that. Photograph the slips and ask for each one to be translated and assigned to a standing category, with the original wording kept beside the translation so that nothing is lost between the two.

Ask for two flags that have nothing to do with categories: any slip of fewer than ten words, and any slip that names a hospital, a police station, a court or a child. Those flags cost nothing and catch the cases that ask for nothing.

Then have a person read the flagged slips before the window opens. The woman with eight words and a bag is not asking for a document. She is asking somebody to tell her what happens if she misses her flight.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for the sorting: **Translate each slip and assign it to one of these five categories.**
- Ask for the original: **Keep the original wording beside the translation.**
- Ask for flags: **Flag every slip under ten words and every slip naming a hospital.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Sorting works on what is written. It cannot see a face, a bag or a boarding pass, and it cannot know that the shortest slip in the pile belongs to the only person in the room who has not slept since Tuesday.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An automatic reply is right nine times in ten, and the tenth message is why you have a duty telephone**

**Figure 2.5** — Six rows. The right-hand column was available from the first minute.

Almost everything that arrives at a consular mailbox is routine: fees, forms, appointment requests, questions about documents. An automatic reply that promises an answer within five working days is honest and useful for all of it.

The exception is the message that cannot wait, and the exception is what the whole system exists for. A detention, a hospital admission, a death, a child alone: in those cases the first hours decide what help is possible.

A system is well suited to finding them. It reads every message as it arrives, in every language, at any hour, and it looks for a small list of words: police, arrested, hospital, court, prison, accident, child.

When one of those words appears, two things must happen. No automatic reply is sent, because a service standard is the cruellest possible answer to somebody in a cell. And an alert goes to the duty telephone within the minute.

The cost of being wrong is small. Somebody writes “police” about a stolen wallet, the duty officer reads four lines at eleven at night, and goes back to sleep.

The cost of not doing it is fifty hours in which a citizen is in a police station and her Mission does not know. Nothing a Mission does later makes those fifty hours smaller.

> **DATA POINT · Out-of-hours alerting** — Consular guidance commonly requires that communications indicating detention, serious injury or risk to a child be brought to the attention of a duty officer immediately, irrespective of office hours.

- Should and ought to give advice: you should photograph it; we ought to ask the office.
- Ought is followed by to; should is not.
- Had better is stronger and suggests a bad result if you do not: you had better write it down now.
- The negative of had better is had better not, with no do.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A check reads the page, and the page cannot say whether anybody issued it**

**Figure 3.5** — Five features examined in forty seconds, and the sixth question was never asked.

Checking a document against its known features is careful, slow work, and it is exactly what a system does well. The paper, the seal, the position of the numbers, the wording of a heading and the shape of a reference number can all be compared in under a minute.

That is a real gain at a counter, because the alternative is a tired officer at four o’clock looking at a certificate he has seen twice in his life.

There is a limit, and it is not about accuracy. A check establishes whether a document looks like the kind of document it claims to be. It does not establish that anybody issued it.

Those are different questions. A good forgery is a correct copy of a real model, and it will pass a features check precisely because it was made from a genuine document.

Everything else lives outside the page: whether the office existed, whether it issued certificates that month, whether its numbering changed in January, whether that signature belongs to somebody who retired.

So use the check, and then do two things. Ask a colleague who knows the local system to look at the top of the page. And send a verification request to the issuing authority, because that is the only answer to the only question that matters.

> **DATA POINT · Document examination** — Document practice commonly distinguishes between the examination of a document’s physical and formal features and the verification of its issuance, which can be established only with the issuing authority.  
> Source: general document practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Verification with the issuer** — Practice on official documents commonly provides that doubts about authenticity are resolved by enquiry to the issuing authority, since physical examination can establish the form of a document but not the fact of its issue.  
> Source: general document practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: indirect questions and polite enquiry**

- An indirect question begins with a phrase such as Could you tell me, Do you know, or May I ask.
- After that phrase, the word order is that of a statement: could you tell me when the office opens.
- Do not use do, does or did in the second part: not “do you know how much does it cost”.
- For yes or no questions, use whether or if: I wonder whether you could come on Friday.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Machine interpreting carries nine conversations in ten, and the tenth is the reason interpreters exist**

**Figure 4.5** — Nine transactional conversations carried, and one that should never have been attempted.

A machine interpreter at a public counter is one of the clearest gains in this book. It works in both directions, it needs no booking, and it turns a return visit into a three-minute conversation.

For the ordinary business of a counter — fees, forms, opening hours, appointments, where to go next — it is accurate enough, and the alternative is a caller who must come back on Monday with a friend or with somebody they have paid.

The limits are not about fluency. The output is fluent in both directions even when it is wrong, which is precisely the danger: nothing in the voice tells you that a word has gone the wrong way.

Ambiguous terms are the usual point of failure, and the worst of them are legal. One word can cover custody and guardianship, charge and accusation, detention and arrest, or adoption and fostering.

So agree the categories where you stop: anything about a child, a court, the police, immigration status, health or money owed. In those cases you apologise, you stop, and you book a qualified interpreter.

And in the conversations you do carry, use a check-back. Every three or four turns, ask the caller to say in their own words what they have understood. It adds two minutes and it is the only thing that catches a fluent mistake.

**A script built from your own case notes is better than any general list, and it cannot contain a silence**

A duty script is a small thing that works. Six questions in a fixed order, in large print, beside the telephone, with a box for each answer.

The useful part is where the six come from. Give two years of your own case notes to a system and ask which facts had to be obtained in every case and which were missing when something went wrong, and you will have a card in an afternoon.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A standard reply answers a category, and the writer asked about a Thursday**

**Figure 6.5** — Two categories produce eighty per cent of the second letters, and both are about one case.

Eleven standard replies for four hundred enquiries a month is an obvious gain. The texts are current, they cite the rules correctly, and they are in plainer English than most people write by hand at the end of a long day.

Matching an enquiry to the right text is also something a system does well. It reads what the enquiry is about, chooses the nearest reply and fills in the name and the reference.

The trouble is that it matches a category, and a category is not a question. “Documents required for a certificate” is a category. “Can my mother sign for me on Thursday while I am in hospital” is a question.

A standard reply about the rule is then entirely correct and entirely beside the point, and the reader writes again, beginning with the two words that tell you everything: but can.

Each of those second letters costs the section twice: once to read and once to answer, and it costs the writer a week of worry and the belief that nobody read what they sent.

So add two lines by hand to every standard reply. The first repeats the question in the writer’s own words. The second answers it: yes, no, or not unless. Twenty seconds each, and ninety second letters disappear.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A record in one language is easy to read, and the conversation happened in three**

**Figure 8.5** — Six stages between a woman’s words and a sentence somebody will rely on.

Turning an officer’s rough notes into a clean record is a good use of a system. Fifty minutes of interview becomes an ordered page in two minutes, and it is written in better English than anybody produces at six in the evening.

The danger is invisible and comes from the format. A record written in one language reads as though the conversation happened in that language, and nothing on the page says otherwise.

In an interpreted interview, the important sentences have travelled. They were spoken in one language, carried into a second by a person, and written in a third by an officer listening at speed.

Every crossing is a place where meaning can turn, and the sentence that matters is usually the one with the most crossings, because it is the one that was hardest to say.

So mark the chain. Beside any sentence that is likely to be relied on, write the languages it passed through and the name of the person who carried it. Four lines on a page, and the record stops pretending.

Then read that sentence back, slowly, through the interpreter, before anybody leaves. Changes are made in the margin and initialled. Five minutes at the end of fifty, and the one sentence that will ever be disputed is the one that has been confirmed.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A number without its rule is a sentence without a verb**

**Figure 9.5** — Five honest ways to count one month. The last row is what the capital saw.

A monthly return looks like the simplest document in the Mission. It is a small table of whole numbers, and it takes twenty minutes.

It is in fact a measuring instrument, and an instrument is only useful if it is the same instrument every time. The figure matters far less than the rule that produced it.

A section can change its rule for good reasons: a new system, a better definition, a different officer with a clearer idea of what one case means. However, if the change is not stated, the next reader sees a trend that does not exist.

Therefore every figure sent for comparison needs three things beside it: what was counted, the dates it covers, and when the rule last changed.

This is three lines of footnote under a table of six rows, and it is the difference between a return that informs a decision and one that provokes a false alarm.

Where you are asked to produce the numbers quickly, the quick part is the arithmetic. The slow part is agreeing what one case is, and that agreement is the real work of the return.

> **DATA POINT · Monthly reporting** — Reporting practice commonly requires that any figure sent for comparison carry the counting rule used to produce it, since a change of rule between periods is the commonest source of apparent change.  
> Source: general statistical reporting guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Comparability** — Where a counting definition changes between periods, reporting practice commonly requires that the earlier period be restated on the new definition, or that the break in the series be marked in the table itself.

**Grammar: the present perfect continuous for effort over time**

- Use have / has been + -ing for effort that began in the past and is still going on: we have been drafting the list for a month.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A red line is short, absolute, and ends with a sentence about doubt**

**Figure 10.5** — Six of the eleven. None of them is a judgement, and none of them has an exception.

Almost everything in this book has been a matter of degree. How full a record should be, how warm a letter, how a month is counted: these are judgements, and judgement improves with practice.

A red line is different in kind. It is not the strict end of a scale; it is a sentence with no scale in it. Either a passport number was typed into an outside system or it was not.

Lists of this sort fail in two ways. They fail when they are long, because nobody reads two pages before writing a letter. They fail when they are vague, because “be careful with personal data” tells an officer nothing at half past six.

So the list is short, concrete, and written in the words the section actually uses. It has been tested on real files, because a rule that cannot be applied to last year’s work will not be applied to this year’s.

It ends with the only sentence that makes any red line usable: if you are not sure whether something is on this list, it is. Without that line, every difficult case becomes an argument; with it, doubt has a default.

And the list is posted where the keyboards are, not filed where the policies are. A rule that must be looked for has already failed.

> **DATA POINT · Describing without identifying** — Where outside assistance is sought on wording or procedure, practice commonly provides that the matter be described in general terms only, and that where the circumstances themselves would identify an individual, no outside assistance be sought at all.
