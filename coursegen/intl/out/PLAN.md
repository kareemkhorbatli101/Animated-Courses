# English for Diplomats — International Edition
## Design brief for a six-book course, A2.1 to B2.2

### 1. Who this is for, and what is different about this edition

Diplomats and foreign service officers **of any state**, serving abroad:
bilateral embassies, consulates general, permanent missions to international
organisations, delegations and joint commissions.

The reader's own state is never named. The sending service is **the Service**,
its headquarters **the Ministry**, its post **the Mission**. The cast carry
names drawn from everywhere on purpose, so that a reader of any nationality can
put their own service in the place of this one.

That neutrality is not cosmetic. It licenses the thing that makes this edition
different from a national one: in the room, **English is nobody's first
language**. The counterpart is working in a second language, the interpreter is
working from a third, the text being drafted will be read in nine, and the
sentence that fails is not the complicated one — it is the idiom, the tag
question, the negative question and the irony.

So the course teaches two kinds of English at once:

- the English a diplomat must **produce** — exact, instructable, defensible;
- the English a diplomat must **survive** — a counterpart's second-language
  English, an interpreter's rendering, a translation nobody will ever check.

Books 3 and 4 are the widest departure from the Syria Edition: a full consular
book built around working across languages at a counter, and a full
cooperation book built around project documents, budget lines, calls for
proposals and partners who use the same words to mean different things.

### 3. The world

Invented throughout: **Arvenia, Belmora, Corvadia, Dalmane, Eskavia, Fenmark,
Grevia, Harraden, Ilmaria**; the cities **Varkent** (embassy), **Port Sable**
(consulate general), **Lindhall** (the Assembly and the Secretariat) and
**Tarrow**; the bodies **the Assembly**, **the Secretariat** and **the Joint
Commission**.

Three refusals hold across all sixty units and are enforced by QA gates: no
real organisation, product, platform or model is named anywhere; no serving
official, no real state's position and no live political dispute appears; and
nothing in the books forecasts what systems will be able to do next.

### 4. Three locked ladders

Every unit is the intersection of one **AI capability**, one **grammar point**
and the **precision faults** its Brief Lab diagnoses. All sixty capabilities and
all sixty grammar points are distinct within the series and are enforced by
exact-string comparison on every build.

The capability ladder is written for this audience: asking for the same thing
said more simply; names from another language said correctly; machine
interpreting at a counter and the sentence it cannot carry; plain English for a
reader whose English is weaker than yours; a record of a conversation held in
three languages; two institutions' terminology reconciled; a translated
agreement checked against its original; speaking so that an interpreter can
carry it; drafting in a language that is nobody's first; a statement written so
that its translation says the same thing; a false story in a language you
cannot read.

The grammar ladder runs from *the verb to be in introductions* to
*nominalisation and summary in a final report*, through the points where
second-language English actually fails: articles, quantifiers, indirect
questions, reporting verbs, the language of amendment, obligation and
endeavour, plain English for translation.

### 5. The twelve-part unit, and the part that is new

Parts 1–9 and 11–12 are the architecture the franchise already has: the
situation, comprehension, vocabulary, grammar, dialogues, speaking, the
capability with its boundary, the six-line tasking (Purpose · Scope · Inputs ·
Criteria · Done · Fallback), the skill, the six-skill review and the can-do
check — plus, in every unit, the **Decision Room** and the **Brief Lab**.

**Part 10 · Across the Language Line** is this edition's own.

Each unit gives one exchange in which meaning went wrong between two
second-language speakers, and teaches the repair:

- the situation, in two paragraphs;
- a three-rung figure, **WHERE THE MEANING BROKE**, naming the three points of
  failure;
- three lines **exactly as they were said**, read aloud by a partner;
- six **repair moves you can use without correcting anybody** — the check-back,
  the reformulation, the number read twice, the written confirmation;
- a red box, **WHAT DOES NOT SURVIVE TRANSLATION**, which always states a
  prohibition;
- six questions, and a five-row score sheet for the repair.

The rounds escalate: at A2.1 a visitor's name is written down three different
ways; at B2.2 a translated statement says, in the other language, something the
Mission never said.

### 6. Quality

Twenty-three per-book gates and three series gates, all of which must pass
before a book is delivered: structure, CEFR register measured by mean sentence
length, vocabulary uniqueness across 1,200 terms, ladder alignment, neutrality,
redundancy, precision of model answers, no products, no forecasting, the Brief
Lab's three-faults-by-two rule, the boundary line in every unit, a dissent
quota in the Decision Rooms, and the completeness of Part 10.

### 7. Deliverables

Six `.docx`, six PDFs, six full-text Markdown files, an alignment table of
sixty rows, a vocabulary ledger of 1,200 terms, this plan and a build record.
