The daily report says that the hospital is experiencing significant capacity pressures, which is a sentence that can be true of a quiet Tuesday and of the worst night in a decade.

What is actually happening, at twenty past seven in the morning, is that eleven patients are on trolleys in the corridor beside the emergency department, four of them having been there for more than twelve hours.

The hospital has four hundred and twelve beds, of which three hundred and ninety-six are in use. Nine are closed for infection control and seven because there is nobody to staff them, so the true position is worse than a bed count suggests.

When you write it plainly, two things follow, and the first is that people outside the organisation can tell how bad it is; the second is that nobody inside can pretend not to know.

The temptation to soften is not cowardice but habit: the escalation area has been open for fourteen months, it is referred to by a name that sounds like a ward, and most of the people working in it have never known it closed.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: describing a full hospital without euphemism**

- Replace an abstraction with a count and a duration: not under pressure, but eleven patients on trolleys, four of them for more than twelve hours.
- Report beds in three numbers rather than one: beds available, beds in use, and beds closed with the reason for each closure, since one figure conceals the difference between a full hospital and a shrunken one.
- Name the place as the place. A corridor is a corridor even when the organisation has given it a name that ends in unit, and the renaming is the thing a reader will remember if it is ever exposed.
- Distinguish what a clinician says from what a manager says, using attribution: Dr Rahman records that the position is unsafe; I record that it is unsustainable. The two sentences carry different weight and should not be merged.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A capacity position described honestly, without euphemism**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the last one is the one an organisation leaves out.

A capacity position described honestly, without euphemism replaces a sentence that could describe any morning with one that can only describe this one.

Give it the bed base with closures and their reasons, the patients awaiting admission with their waiting times, the escalation space in use and the date it opened.

Ask it to remove every abstraction that a count could replace, and to flag any phrase that would read differently to somebody outside the organisation.

Ask it to describe escalation space by what it physically is and by how long it has been open, because fourteen months of temporary capacity is a different fact from a bad night.

Ask for clinical and managerial judgements to be attributed separately, since unsafe and unsustainable are different claims and merging them weakens the stronger one.

What it cannot do is decide whether to divert ambulances tonight; that decision moves risk to another hospital and another set of patients, and it belongs to people who will answer for it.

> **DATA POINT · Capacity reporting** — Operational guidance commonly recommends reporting capacity as in use beds, closed beds with reasons, and patients awaiting admission with their waiting times, rather than as summary descriptions. Definitions of escalation capacity are set nationally or by the organisation’s own policy.  
> Source: general operational management guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Normalisation** — Operational and safety literature commonly reports that escalation capacity opened as temporary is frequently incorporated into routine bed bases, and that reporting in summary language is associated with reduced external visibility of sustained pressure.  
> Source: general operational management literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · The corridor with a name

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A hospital opened eight trolley spaces in a corridor as temporary escalation capacity in September — the space was given a name ending in unit, a staffing rota and a sign.

Fourteen months later the space was included in the reported bed base, was in use on 412 of 426 days, and appeared in public reporting only as escalation capacity. Over the same period mean trolley waits rose from 3.1 hours to 7.8, and incident reports about corridor care fell from 31 in the first quarter to 4 in the fifth.

The space was removed from the bed base and reported as a corridor with eight trolley spaces, open since September. Within two quarters incident reports rose to 26 and a capital scheme for fourteen additional beds was approved.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the first day of an incident: facts, actions, what is not yet known**

- Write facts in the past simple with times and sources: at sixteen twenty the infusion pump was recorded as running at the prescribed rate.
- Write actions in the present perfect passive with times: the pump has been removed and sealed; the records have been secured as at eighteen hundred.

**An inspection answer drafted from the evidence that exists**

**Figure 8.5** — Six lines of evidence, and the second one is the one organisations invent.

An inspection answer drafted from the evidence that exists keeps an organisation from describing a version of itself that will not survive a Tuesday.

Give it the questions or the framework, the documents that exist with their dates and ratification status, the known problems with the date each was first recorded, and the practice as it is actually carried out.

## Unit 9 · An Apology From an Organisation

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: an organisational apology: what we did, what we are changing · AI: an organisational apology drafted as act, effect and change*

**Figure 9.0** — “WE ARE SORRY THAT YOU FEEL” IS NOT AN APOLOGY.: One act, one family, two changes made and one still outstanding.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What was the last apology you received from an organisation?
- What did it apologise for?
- What changed afterwards?

An apology from an organisation has three parts and nothing else: what we did, what it did to you, and what we have changed.

The first part must name an act or an omission in the active voice. We did not check the second pump. We did not tell you for nine days. Not errors occurred in the escalation process, which has no subject and no verb anybody can own.

The second part must be about a particular person. Your mother spent her last night in a corridor, and you learned about the drug error from a letter rather than from a doctor.

The third part separates what has changed from what has not. Two changes are made and dated; one is promised for March, with a name against it; and one thing they asked for is not going to happen, and the letter says so.

And it is said aloud before it is written. A letter that arrives before anybody has sat in a room with a family is a document about the organisation’s process rather than an apology to a person.

**Grammar: an organisational apology: what we did, what we are changing**

- Name the act with we and a past simple verb: we did not check the second pump; we did not tell you for nine days. The subject is always we.
- Name the omission with we should have: we should have told you within a day, and we did not. The construction admits the standard and the failure in one clause.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A pressured decision reconstructed into a record with its basis**

**Figure 10.5** — Six lines before the end of the shift, and the sixth dates the other five.

A pressured decision reconstructed into a record with its basis lets a decision be judged on what was known rather than on what happened.

Give it the decision with its time, the information you had with the timestamp of each item, the people you reached and could not reach, and the options you considered.

Ask for every option to be recorded with the reason it was rejected, since an unrecorded option becomes, in an inquiry, an option nobody thought of.

Ask for the risk you accepted to be stated as accepted rather than as unavoidable, because I had no choice is almost never true and never survives examination.

Ask for the record to carry the date and hour at which it was written, which is the single fact that determines how much weight it can bear.

**Model**

Purpose: so that a decision I take in nine minutes can be judged on what I knew at the time rather than on what happened afterwards. Scope: operational decisions I take or authorise, including diversion, escalation and closure; clinical decisions are recorded by the clinician who takes them, and nothing in my record characterises anybody else’s clinical judgement.
