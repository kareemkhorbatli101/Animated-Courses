### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: roles and responsibility: who does, who is responsible for**

- Use who does for the action. Who does the ordering? Who does the fire check? The answer is a person.
- Use who is responsible for for the ownership. Who is responsible for the beds? Responsible for is followed by a noun.
- Say what you are not. I am not responsible for the chairs. This is useful and it is not rude.
- Ask for the name, not the department. Not "who looks after this?" but "who is the person I ring?"

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A list of who is responsible for what, checked against the rota**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the second one must be a person.

A list of who is responsible for what, checked against the rota, is the first thing a new manager needs. It is also the last thing anybody gives her.

Give the system the jobs you have met this week, the names you have been told and the rota for the next four weeks.

Ask for one name against each job, not a team. A team is not an owner and it never answers a telephone.

Ask it to mark every job where you have two names, because two names mean the job is not owned at all.

Ask for a night column, because the answer at two in the morning is often a different person.

What it cannot do is tell you that the man you were given left in April. Only the rota does that, and only if you check.

> **DATA POINT · Ownership** — Management guidance often says that every task needs one named owner. Two owners mean no owner. Local structures and job titles differ between hospitals.  
> Source: general management guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Named owners** — Operational guidance commonly recommends one named owner and one date for each action, with a second name for out of hours. Arrangements differ between organisations.  
> Source: general management literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: a written request: please can you, by, and tell me if**

- Use please can you with the thing. Please can you send me the two figures. It is shorter than I would be grateful if you could and it is not rude.
- Use by with a day and an hour. By four o’clock today. By ten tomorrow morning. By is the only word in an email that produces an answer.
- Use tell me if for the alternative. Tell me if you cannot, and I shall ask ward nine. It gives the reader a second way to reply.
- Put the figure in the first line, not in an attachment. Two numbers: beds empty and beds expected by eleven.
