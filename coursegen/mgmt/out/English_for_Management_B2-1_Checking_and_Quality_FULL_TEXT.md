### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is the difference between an answer that is wrong and one you cannot check?
- When somebody says a process is "broadly reliable", what have they told you?
- Which errors at your work would nobody ever discover?

Victor Sandoval joined Arclight when it was eleven people, as the first person whose job was quality rather than delivery, and on his second day he asked a question that nobody had a satisfactory answer to: how does anybody here know the work is right? The answers he received were honest, and between them they did not amount to a method.

He spent his first fortnight sorting four hundred outputs into three piles rather than two, which turned out to be the single most useful thing he did that year. The first pile was work that was wrong, where a figure or a name or a date did not match the source and anybody could establish that in under a minute by looking.

The second pile was work that was plausible: output that was not obviously wrong, that read well, and that would have required somebody to go back to the source document in order to confirm it. Nobody had gone back. The work had been accepted because it was reasonable, which is a different standard entirely, and a much weaker one.

The third pile was the one that changed how Arclight thought about the problem. It held outputs that could not be checked at all, because the thing they asserted was not recorded anywhere: a summary of what a client had meant, a judgement that a complaint was minor, a statement that an account looked dormant. These were neither right nor wrong. They were unverifiable, and there were more of them than anybody had expected.

Victor’s argument, which took him four months to win, is that the three piles need entirely different treatment. Wrong work needs a check. Plausible work needs a sample, because checking all of it costs more than it saves. Unverifiable work needs something harder: either a decision that it is acceptable without evidence, taken deliberately and in writing, or a change to the work so that it produces something checkable instead.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The pile you cannot check is the one that grows**

**Figure 1.5** — The middle row is the expensive one and the bottom row is the dangerous one.

Most discussions of quality treat error as a single category, which works reasonably well when a human being is producing the work at human speed, because the person doing it carries a continuous and largely unconscious sense of how confident they are. When output arrives at volume, with no accompanying signal of confidence, that single category stops being useful and has to be broken into at least three.

The first kind is error that can be established cheaply, because the output asserts something that a source document also asserts, and the two can be set beside each other. A total that disagrees with its invoice, a date that contradicts a contract, a name that does not appear in the client’s own file: all of these are settled in under a minute by anybody at all.

The second kind is output that is plausible, which is to say not obviously wrong, written well, and unchecked. It may be entirely correct; the point is that nobody knows, and that finding out would cost several minutes an item. At four hundred items a day the arithmetic of checking everything stops working, which is why sampling exists and why it is the subject of a later unit in this book.

The third kind is output nothing could check, because what it asserts is not recorded anywhere. A judgement that a complaint is minor, a summary of what somebody meant rather than said, an assessment that an account looks dormant. These are not failures of process. They are statements of a kind that has no external referent, and no amount of checking will ever confirm one.

The practical consequence is that the third category has to be managed rather than verified, and there are only two honest ways to do it. Either somebody decides, in writing, that an unverifiable output is acceptable for a stated purpose, which is a real decision with a name on it. Or the work is changed so that it produces something checkable instead: not "the client seemed satisfied" but "the client said that is fine, leave it with me".

> **DATA POINT · Unverified output** — Quality-assurance surveys in service organisations commonly find that a majority of outputs are accepted without any comparison against a source, with acceptance resting on the absence of complaints.  
> Source: general quality-assurance research, 2026. Practice varies widely by sector.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Three kinds of error: wrong, plausible, unverifiable

**Figure 1.7** — The right-hand column is not badly produced. It is of a kind no check reaches.

Before designing any quality arrangement, sort a week of real output into the three piles, because the proportions decide everything that follows and almost nobody knows them. The exercise takes a day and it replaces an argument about how good the work is with an argument about numbers, which is a much more productive kind of argument to have.

For each output, the operative question is not whether it looks right but what it would have to be compared against. If you can name a document, the output is checkable, and the remaining question is only how many you check. If you cannot name one, you have found an item for the third pile, and the honest response is to notice that rather than to look harder at the output itself.

Then deal with the third pile explicitly, because it does not shrink on its own and it tends to grow as more judgement is handed over. Either somebody accepts it with their name attached, which is a legitimate commercial decision, or the work is redesigned so that it reports what was said rather than what was meant, which converts an unverifiable output into a merely plausible one.

*Say it like this*
- Name the comparison: **This would be checked against the 14 March ledger.**
- Admit the gap: **There is no document against which that judgement could be confirmed.**
- Convert it: **Report what the client said, not what the client appeared to feel.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Sorting outputs into three piles tells you what could be checked, not what is wrong. A week’s work can be sorted carefully and completely, with every comparison source named, while every item in the first two piles remains entirely unexamined; the sorting is preparation for checking and is not itself a check.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The signals of care are produced by the writing, not by the knowing**

**Figure 2.5** — Every signal in the first column pointed away from the correct document.

When we cannot verify something directly, we fall back on a set of indirect signals that have served reasonably well for a long time. A document that is well organised, specific, internally consistent and free of hedging has usually been produced by somebody who knew what they were doing, because producing those qualities without knowledge used to be difficult and slow.

That relationship is not a law of nature; it is a correlation that held because of how documents were made. Where output can be fluent without being informed, the correlation breaks, and every one of the signals continues to be emitted. The reader’s instincts are not faulty. They are calibrated to a world that, for this particular class of document, has changed.

The consequence is specific and worth stating precisely. It is not that output is untrustworthy in general; much of it is correct. It is that the surface of a document no longer carries information about whether it is correct, so reading more carefully does not help. Careful reading of a fluent and wrong document produces a confident wrong reader.

What does help is asking for the strength of a claim alongside the claim, and for the grounds alongside the strength. The figure is quarterly, stated on page three of the minutes, is a different kind of sentence from the figure is quarterly, and a reader can do something with the difference. The second sentence can be checked in thirty seconds; the first cannot be checked at all.

This is why the grammar of deduction matters commercially rather than only grammatically. A document that distinguishes must from might, and attaches the reason to each, is reporting its own reliability. A document that states everything in the same register is withholding exactly the information a reader most needs, whether or not anybody intended to withhold it.

> **DATA POINT · Fluency and judgement** — Research on processing fluency consistently finds that statements which are easier to read are judged more likely to be true, with the effect persisting even when readers are warned about it.  
> Source: general cognitive-psychology research, 2026. Effect sizes vary by task.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Confident and wrong: why fluency is not accuracy

**Figure 2.7** — The marking is not bureaucracy. It is what makes a four-minute check possible.

Ask for the strength of a claim and its grounds in the same sentence, every time, because a claim arriving without either is indistinguishable from a claim with both behind it. This is a small change in what you request and it transfers the reader’s problem from judging a whole document to examining a short list of marked assertions.

Insist on the vocabulary of deduction, which exists precisely for this and is not decoration. It must be quarterly because the annual figure is four times this is a deduction with a warrant attached. It might be quarterly is a possibility honestly reported. The figure is quarterly is neither, and it is the sentence you will get unless you ask for something else.

And remember what the marking does not do. Marking a claim as stated does not make it stated; the mark is itself an assertion, produced by the same process, and it can be wrong in exactly the same way. What the marking buys is a short list of places to look, which turns an unbounded checking problem into a bounded one.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for strength: **Mark each claim as stated, inferred or assumed.**
- Ask for grounds: **For anything inferred, name the line it was inferred from.**
- Permit the gap: **Where the source does not say, write "not stated".**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Marking does not verify. A claim marked "stated, page three" can be marked that way wrongly, by the same process that produced the claim, and the mark will read exactly as convincingly as a correct one. What marking produces is a shorter list to check, not a checked list.

## Unit 3 · How Much to Check

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: quantifying evidence · AI: sampling*

**Figure 3.0** — SIXTY ROWS OUT OF NINETEEN HUNDRED: Sampling exists because the two obvious answers are both unaffordable.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many of something would you check before you believed the rest were fine?
- What is the difference between "most of them" and "the majority of them"?
- If you choose which items to check, what have you accidentally decided?

Arclight produces about nineteen hundred checkable outputs a month, and Victor established early that checking all of them would consume roughly eleven working days, which the company does not have and would not spend if it did. Checking none of them, which was the arrangement in place when he arrived, costs nothing and establishes nothing.

Sampling is the only remaining answer, and it is worth being clear about what it is for. A sample does not tell you which outputs are wrong; it tells you roughly how many are, which is a different and often more useful piece of information. It converts an unknown quantity into an estimate with a stated range, and an estimate can be argued about.

The number of items you need is smaller than most people expect and the way you choose them matters more than how many you take. Sixty rows drawn at random from nineteen hundred will give a usable estimate of the error rate; sixty rows that somebody chose because they looked interesting will give an estimate of nothing except what that person finds interesting.

This is the part that Arclight got wrong twice. The first attempt sampled the first sixty rows of each month, which were consistently the oldest invoices and consistently the cleanest. The second attempt let whoever was doing the checking pick the sixty, with the predictable result that the difficult-looking ones were examined and the error rate came out higher than the truth rather than lower.

What the company does now is draw the sixty by a rule nobody can influence, examine every one of them against its source, and publish the result as a proportion with the date and the population attached. In the month that produced three errors in sixty, the sentence written down was not "quality is good" but "3 of 60 sampled rows disagreed with their source, drawn from 1,870 rows produced in March".

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A sample tells you how many, never which**

**Figure 3.5** — Two methods gave numbers. Only two of the four gave numbers that meant anything.

Once the volume of work exceeds what anybody can examine, there are three possible arrangements and only one of them is sensible. You can check everything, which is honest and rapidly unaffordable. You can check nothing and rely on complaints, which is what most organisations do without describing it that way. Or you can check a part, chosen so that what you find in the part tells you something about the whole.

The purpose of sampling is often misunderstood, including by people who do it. A sample is not a way of finding errors, although it finds some; it is a way of estimating how many errors there are. These are different objectives and they want different methods. If you want to find errors, you examine the items most likely to contain them. If you want to know the rate, that is precisely the thing you must not do.

The size people worry about turns out to matter less than the selection they do not worry about. Sixty items drawn without human involvement will support a reasonable estimate of the rate in a population of a few thousand. Six hundred items chosen by somebody who thought about which ones to take will support nothing at all, because the number they produce reflects the chooser as much as the work.

The practical rule is therefore that the selection must be made by something nobody can lean on. Every thirty-first row, every item arriving at a fixed minute, every file whose reference ends in a particular digit: the method does not matter provided that no human judgement enters it and that the rule is written down before the month begins.

Where the population contains kinds of item that differ in how much a mistake costs, draw separately from each kind rather than from the pile. This costs more items in total and it buys something specific: an estimate for the small, dangerous category that a proportional sample would barely touch. Credit notes at Arclight are three per cent of volume and most of the irreversible risk.

> **DATA POINT · Sample size and selection** — Statistical practice generally holds that beyond a modest absolute size, the method of selection affects the reliability of an estimate more than further increases in sample size.  
> Source: general sampling methodology, 2026. Required sizes depend on the precision sought.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Sampling: how much to check when you cannot check it all

**Figure 3.7** — Both columns are legitimate. Using the left-hand method to answer a right-hand question is not.

Decide first which question your sampling is answering, because the two questions want opposite methods and the confusion between them is the commonest fault in this area. Looking for errors means going where errors are likely. Estimating a rate means going nowhere in particular, deliberately, and accepting that most of what you examine will be fine.

Then remove the human being from the selection, which is harder than it sounds because it feels wasteful. Somebody who knows the work will see a row that looks wrong and want to include it, and every time they do, the resulting number stops being an estimate of anything. Write the rule down before the period begins and allow no substitutions once a row has been drawn.

Finally, stratify wherever a mistake costs very different amounts in different parts of the population. A proportional sample of Arclight’s work would contain about two credit notes, which supports no estimate at all for the category carrying most of the irreversible risk. Thirty from each kind costs more rows and answers the question you actually have.

*Say it like this*
- Name the question: **We want the rate, not the errors; the method differs.**
- Fix the rule: **Every 31st row by reference, set before the month begins.**
- Stratify: **Thirty from each of the three kinds, not sixty from the pile.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A sample estimates a rate; it does not clear the items it did not examine. Three errors in sixty says nothing whatever about any particular row among the remaining eighteen hundred, and a month with a clean sample can still contain the single expensive mistake that matters.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Attention is the thing that does not scale**

**Figure 4.5** — The difference between the two rows is the whole economics of quality at volume.

Checks divide into two kinds, and the division is sharper and more consequential than it first appears. A comparison check asks whether two things agree: a total against an invoice, a date against a contract, a name against a client file. It has a fixed shape, it needs no knowledge of the client, and anybody can be taught to perform it in about four minutes.

A judgement check asks something that has no second document behind it. Would this embarrass us. Is this client unhappy in a way the words do not say. Is the tone wrong for this particular reader on this particular week. These require somebody who has context, and they cannot be decomposed into comparisons without losing exactly the thing that makes them worth doing.

The economic consequence is the point of this unit. Comparison checks are close to free at any volume, because the cost of running the same comparison again is almost nothing. Judgement checks cost a fixed amount of human attention per item, and that cost is identical whether the item is the first of the year or the four hundredth of the week.

What follows is uncomfortable and almost universal. As volume rises, the proportion of output that receives a judgement check falls, because attention is fixed and output is not. This is not a decision anybody makes; it is an arithmetic consequence of growth, and the people inside it usually describe it as being busy rather than as a change in standards.

The honest response has two halves. Apply every comparison check to everything, without sampling, because they cost almost nothing and they clear the whole of the left-hand column. Then decide explicitly which items receive attention, write the rule down, and accept that the rule will be a compromise. Choosing badly is bad. Not choosing means the volume chooses, and the volume has no view about what matters.

> **DATA POINT · Where attention goes** — Studies of inspection in high-volume settings generally find that the share of items receiving expert review declines as throughput rises, in the absence of an explicit rule allocating it.  
> Source: general quality-management literature, 2026. Observed across several industries.

The requirement Arclight settled on, after the contract answers that quoted a clause which did not exist, is that every factual claim leaving the company must carry a locator: a document, a version and a position within it, so that anybody holding the document can arrive at the claim in under half a minute without knowing anything about how the answer was produced.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A reference is not a locator**

**Figure 5.5** — The middle row looks sourced and behaves almost exactly like the bottom row.

Requiring output to show its source is the single most effective change a small company can make to the reliability of what it sends out, and it is effective for a reason that is worth stating precisely: it converts an assertion, which can only be accepted or rejected, into a claim with an address, which can be examined by anybody in well under a minute.

The requirement only works, however, if what is produced is a locator rather than a gesture towards one. "As set out in the service policy" names a document that exists in four versions and runs to nine pages, which means that checking it costs several minutes rather than thirty seconds, and a check that costs several minutes is a check that will not be performed. The citation then functions as reassurance rather than as evidence.

A usable locator has three parts, and the third is the one most often omitted. It names the document, which almost everybody does. It names the version or the date, which about half of people do. And it names a position within the document, whether a clause number, a page or simply the first few words of the passage, which hardly anybody does unless the rule explicitly requires it.

The second lesson, which Arclight learned by sampling rather than by reasoning, concerns what happens to sentences that carry no source at all. On a page where most sentences are referenced, an unreferenced sentence does not stand out; it disappears. Readers register the general property of being sourced and stop auditing individual sentences for it, which is a sensible economy in most circumstances and a dangerous one here.

The remedy is to make absence positive. A sentence that cannot be sourced should say so, in the text, in words a skimming reader will see. This feels like an admission of weakness and it is the opposite: a document that marks its own unsupported claims is telling a reader exactly where to spend their attention, which is the most useful thing any document can do.

> **DATA POINT · Checking cost and behaviour** — Research on verification behaviour generally finds that the probability of a reader checking a cited claim falls sharply as the effort required rises beyond a few seconds.  
> Source: general information-behaviour research, 2026. Thresholds vary by setting.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Making it show its source

**Figure 5.7** — The whole apparatus exists to make the fourth box take seconds rather than minutes.

Require three things rather than one, because a source named loosely produces the usual result. Name the document, which everybody does; name the version, which stops an answer resting on something superseded; and name a position inside it, which is what turns a reference into something a reader will actually follow. A clause number, a page, or the first half-dozen words of the passage will all do.

Then set a time test and state it in the rule, because the time is what the rule is really about. A locator that lets a reader reach the passage in twenty-five seconds will be used; one that takes four minutes will not, and a citation nobody follows provides the appearance of evidence without any of its value, which is a worse position than citing nothing at all.

Finally, require absence to be marked in the text. A page carrying references trains the eye to stop checking for them, so a sentence without one becomes invisible exactly when it most needs to be visible. Three words in square brackets cost nothing and convert a silent gap into a signal that a reader cannot skim past.

*Say it like this*
- Require the position: **Name the clause, the page or the first six words.**
- Set the time test: **A reader must reach the passage in under thirty seconds.**
- Mark the absence: **Any claim without a source ends with "[no source]".**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A locator establishes where a claim came from, not whether the document it came from is correct, current or relevant. A perfectly formed citation to clause seven of a policy that was superseded in February points precisely at the wrong place, and the precision will make it more convincing rather than less.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A working you could repeat, not a story you could believe**

**Figure 6.5** — The last two rows are the ones that matter, and they point in opposite directions.

Asking for the working is the second of the two interventions that reliably improve the quality of what a company sends out, the first being asking for the source. The two are related but distinct: a source says where a claim came from, and a working says how a result was arrived at from material that may be perfectly well sourced.

The May episode at Arclight illustrates why the working matters independently. The figure was correct, every input to it was correctly extracted from a properly cited invoice, and the method connecting the two was faulty in a way that would have produced a wrong answer in roughly one month in four. No amount of checking the answer or the sources would have found this, because both were sound.

There is, however, a trap that catches people almost immediately, and it is worth more attention than it usually gets. What is produced in response to a request for reasoning is frequently an account of reasoning rather than a derivation: fluent prose describing an approach, which reads as an explanation and from which nothing can be reproduced. Such an account can be constructed to fit any answer, including a wrong one, and it is more persuasive than the arithmetic it replaces.

The test that separates them is mechanical and it is the only one worth applying. Could somebody with a calculator, and no knowledge of the subject, start from the first line and arrive at the last? If every line follows from the one above by an operation a stranger could repeat, the working is genuine. If a line appears without a route from what preceded it, that line is where the fault will be.

What a working does not establish is also worth being clear about. It is an output, produced by the same process as the answer, and it can be wrong. What makes it valuable is not that it is more trustworthy but that it is more constrained: a wrong working that is internally consistent and reaches the stated answer is substantially harder to produce by accident than a wrong answer on its own.

> **DATA POINT · Explanation and persuasion** — Research on explanation generally finds that the presence of an explanation increases acceptance of a conclusion, with the effect only weakly related to whether the explanation is actually informative.  
> Source: general cognitive research on explanation, 2026. Effects vary by expertise.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Making it show its working

**Figure 6.7** — The right-hand column is longer, more reassuring and could accompany any number at all.

Ask for the working as intermediate results rather than for reasoning, because the words you use decide what you get. A request for reasoning produces prose; a request for the intermediate results, one line per operation, produces lines. The second is harder to produce and much easier to check, which is the correct direction for that trade to run.

Write the reproduction test into the brief rather than keeping it in your head. A reader with a calculator and no other knowledge must be able to start at the first line and arrive at the last. And refuse prose explicitly, because it will otherwise arrive. Fluent description of an approach is the most persuasive and least informative thing in this subject, it is produced readily, and it will be offered in good faith by anything asked to explain itself. - State the test: **A reader with a calculator must be able to reproduce the figure.**

Having established that comparison checks cost almost nothing, Victor proposed running the invoice extraction twice by two different routes and comparing the results, on the theory that two independent attempts will rarely go wrong in the same place. What Arclight does now is cheaper and better. Agreement between two of your own attempts is reassuring. Agreement with somebody else’s record is evidence.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Two checks catch only the faults they do not share**

**Figure 7.5** — The last two rows are not failures of the cross-check. They are its definition.

Doing a job twice and comparing the results is among the oldest quality devices there is, and it works for a specific and limited reason: two attempts that fail independently will rarely fail in the same way at the same place, so a disagreement localises a problem and an agreement is weak evidence that neither went wrong.

The phrase that carries the whole argument is "fail independently", and it is almost always assumed rather than examined. Two routes built by the same people, following the same written rules, interpreting the same ambiguous conventions, are independent in the trivial sense that they are two pieces of work and not independent in the sense that matters. They share every assumption that neither of them was told to question.

Arclight’s nine misread dates are a clean example because nothing went wrong in either route. Both encountered an ambiguous date, both applied the same reasonable default, and both produced the same answer, which the cross-check correctly reported as agreement. The arrangement did exactly what it was built to do and the information it returned was worth nothing for those rows.

What follows is a rule about where to spend checking effort. Cross-checking is worth its cost where the plausible failures are independent: reading, transcription, mechanical handling. It is worth very little where the plausible failures come from a shared rule, a shared convention or a shared ambiguity in the source, because those are precisely the failures both routes will reproduce.

Where you can get it, comparison against an external record beats cross-checking, because the external record was produced by somebody who does not share your assumptions. A bank statement has no view about date conventions; a supplier statement was prepared by the people who wrote the invoice; a client’s own ledger reflects what the client believes happened. Each is slower, and each carries information that two of your own runs never will.

> **DATA POINT · Shared-cause failure** — Reliability engineering has long distinguished independent failures from common-cause failures, and generally finds that duplicated systems reduce the first class substantially and the second hardly at all.  
> Source: general reliability-engineering literature, 2026. Proportions depend on design.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Two systems, one task: cross-checking

**Figure 7.7** — Nothing in the right-hand column is a failure of the arrangement. It is the arrangement.

Before building a cross-check, write down what the two routes share, because that list is the complete specification of what the arrangement cannot see. Shared written rules, shared defaults for ambiguous input, shared source documents, shared people who built them: anything on that list will be reproduced by both routes and will be reported as agreement.

Then exclude those fields from the cross-check explicitly, rather than leaving them in and allowing the agreement rate to include them. An agreement rate that counts fields where agreement is guaranteed is not a measurement, and it will be quoted as though it were. Saying that dates, tax and currency are outside the cross-check is a sentence that costs nothing and prevents a recurring misunderstanding.

Where you can tolerate the delay, prefer comparison against something produced outside your organisation. A supplier statement, a bank record or a client ledger shares none of your conventions and is therefore capable of disagreeing with you about the things two of your own routes never will. It is slower, it is sometimes awkward to obtain, and it is a different kind of evidence.

*Say it like this*
- List what they share: **Both routes apply the same date, tax and currency rules.**
- Exclude it: **Those three fields are outside the cross-check, by design.**
- Prefer outside: **Compare the monthly total with the supplier statement instead.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A cross-check establishes that two routes agree, which is not the same as either being right. Where both routes share a rule, a convention or a source, agreement between them carries no information whatever, and the agreement rate will not distinguish those rows from the ones where it does.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

Arclight’s error rate on extraction settled at about two per cent of rows after Victor’s first year, which is to say roughly thirty-eight wrong rows a month out of nineteen hundred. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Zero is a price, not a standard**

**Figure 8.5** — The same rate costs very different amounts in the four rows, which is the entire argument.

Every organisation has an error rate and most of them have never said what it is. The rate is not chosen; it emerges from whatever checks were put in place for whatever reasons, and it drifts as volume changes. Describing it as acceptable, which is the usual formulation, conceals the fact that nobody has compared its cost with the cost of changing it.

The first thing to establish is the shape of the cost curve, and it has the same shape nearly everywhere. The initial checks are cheap and remove the majority of errors. Each subsequent reduction costs several times what the previous one cost, because what remains is progressively harder to catch, and removes fewer errors than the step before. The cost per error removed therefore rises steeply, and it rises without limit.

This means that zero is not a standard that a well-run organisation reaches and a badly run one does not. It is a point on a curve whose price is effectively infinite, and every organisation that appears to achieve it in some area has either defined errors narrowly or is spending a great deal more on that area than is visible from outside.

What makes the conversation possible is pricing errors rather than ranking them. An error in an internal report consumes somebody’s time and nothing else; an error in a credit note costs money that has left the building and some amount of client confidence that nobody can price honestly. With those figures beside the cost of reduction, a rate can be chosen for each category rather than inherited for all of them.

The part that requires discipline is recording what is not priced. Client confidence, staff morale and the slow erosion of a reputation are real and resist measurement, and a table of costs that silently omits them will be read as though they were zero. A line stating that they are excluded is worth more than a number somebody invented to fill the space.

> **DATA POINT · The cost of quality** — Quality-cost models in manufacturing and services generally show prevention and appraisal costs rising sharply as defect rates approach zero, with an optimum well above zero for most processes.  
> Source: general quality-cost literature, 2026. Optima depend heavily on failure cost.

## Unit 9 · We Should Have Seen It

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: past modals · AI: where errors become invisible*

**Figure 9.0** — AN ERROR THAT TOOK SEVEN MONTHS TO ARRIVE: The gap between making a mistake and meeting it is the subject of this unit.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How long would it take for a mistake in your work to reach somebody?
- What is the difference between "it must have failed" and "it should have failed"?
- Who at your work would find out first if something had gone quietly wrong?

The cheapest error anybody can have is the one found by the person who made it, within a minute, because at that point the cost is the minute. Every step the error travels away from its maker multiplies what it costs to correct and reduces the chance that anybody correcting it understands what actually went wrong.

This is not a new observation, and the risk it describes is older than any of the subject matter of this book. What changes when work is handed over to a system is the distance an error can travel before meeting anybody at all. A person producing forty letters notices, somewhere around the eleventh, that something is odd. A process producing four hundred notices nothing, because noticing is not among the things it does.

Arclight’s February currency fault is the clean illustration. A conversion was applied at the rate of the invoice date rather than the payment date, which is defensible and was not what the client’s own contract specified. It was correct arithmetic applied under the wrong rule, it produced plausible figures every month, and it was discovered in September by a supplier doing their own year end.

The question Victor asks about any error is not how it happened but where it would have been caught, and that question usually has a clear answer that nobody likes. The February fault would have been caught at the first reconciliation against a supplier statement, which Arclight did not do at that time because it had not yet had the conversation in the previous unit.

So the useful work after an incident is not apportioning blame, which produces nothing, but identifying the detection point: the earliest place where the error was in principle visible to somebody who was looking. If nobody was looking there, that is the finding, and it is a finding about the design rather than about any person.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Automation moves errors away from the person who made them**

**Figure 9.5** — The second column is identical in every row. Only the third column changed.

The oldest principle in quality work is that errors are cheapest when they are found closest to where they were made, in time and in distance. A person who makes a mistake and notices within a minute spends a minute. The same mistake found by a customer eight months later costs the correction, the investigation, the explanation and some portion of the relationship.

Handing work to a system does not change this principle and it changes the distances dramatically. The person who used to produce the work was also, incidentally and without anybody specifying it, the detection mechanism: they saw every item, they had a sense of what the output usually looked like, and they would stop at the eleventh of forty letters and say that something was odd.

That incidental detection disappears, and because nobody wrote it down as part of the process, nobody replaces it. The output is produced correctly in the sense that each step did what it was told, it is produced at volume, and the first entity to examine it as a whole is frequently the recipient. This is the mechanism by which a seven-month lag becomes normal rather than exceptional.

The useful distinction, and the one Arclight’s post-mortem now makes, is between an error that produces no observable difference anywhere and an error that produces a difference nobody is observing. The first is genuinely hard; the second is a design omission that looks identical from inside and is entirely fixable, usually for about twenty minutes a month.

So the question after an incident should be where it would have been visible rather than who made it. The first question produces a finding about a missing check, which can be added. The second produces a finding about a person who made a defensible choice without anything to test it against, which is almost always what happened and almost never worth establishing.

> **DATA POINT · Detection lag** — Incident studies across sectors commonly report that the cost of correcting a fault rises with the time between its introduction and its detection, often by more than an order of magnitude.  
> Source: general quality and reliability literature, 2026. Multipliers vary by domain.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Where errors become invisible, and why that is the real risk

**Figure 9.7** — The incidental detection that came free with human production does not come free any more.

After an incident, ask where errors would have been visible rather than who made them. The first question produces a detection point, which is a thing you can put a check at. The second produces a name, and the name almost always belongs to somebody who made a reasonable decision in the absence of anything that would have told them otherwise.

Then establish whether the error produced an observable difference at all. An error that changes nothing anybody could see is genuinely difficult and rare. An error that produces a difference in a document nobody opens is a design omission, it is common, and it is usually fixable for a small and specific amount of work.

And record the two dates separately, because the gap between them is the number that matters. Introduced in February, found in September is a different incident from introduced in August, found in September, even if the error itself is identical. The first tells you about your detection; the second tells you that your detection worked.

*Say it like this*

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Count four things, and say what you do not know**

**Figure 10.5** — The last row is the only defence against the fourth column.

Measuring quality fails in two opposite ways and both are common. It fails by measuring nothing, which is the position most small companies are in and which leaves every argument about quality to be settled by whoever speaks with most conviction. It fails by measuring everything, which produces a page nobody reads and a false sense that the subject is under control.

Four numbers is an arbitrary figure and the principle behind it is not. A set of measures is useful only if everybody who should act on it has read all of it, and the number of figures a busy person will actually hold in mind week after week is small. Thirty measures in a system and four on the page is a reasonable arrangement; thirty on the page is the same as none.

Choose at least one outcome measure and at least two that move before it. The error rate tells you what happened and tells you late, because a change in how work is produced takes weeks to appear in a sampled rate. The number of items given attention, the number of releases stopped and the age of the oldest unresolved flag all move within days of anything changing, which is when you can still do something.

Every watched measure degrades, and the degradation is not usually dishonesty. People respond rationally to what is counted, and a measure that is easy to move without moving the underlying thing will drift in that direction through a thousand small reasonable decisions. The age of an open flag can be improved by closing flags; nobody has to decide to cheat for that to happen.

The partial defences are worth using even though they are partial. Have the count made by somebody who does not benefit from it. Report the measures together, so that improving one at the expense of another shows up on the same page. And write down what you do not know: a page that says the rate fell and nobody has established why is more useful in six months than one that attributed it confidently to whatever had most recently been done.

> **DATA POINT · Measures under observation** — It is widely observed in management research that a measure adopted as a target tends to lose its value as a measure, as behaviour adapts to the measurement.  
> Source: general management and economics literature, 2026. Strength of the effect varies.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Measuring quality: what to count, weekly

**Figure 10.7** — The right-hand column moves within days. The left-hand column moves within quarters.

Pick four and mean it, because the discipline is in the exclusion rather than in the selection. Keep the other twenty-six and make them available on request.
