### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does your employer do well that customers would never know about?

Victor Sandoval moved from quality into client work in his second year, and the first thing he did was something nobody had done: he asked each of the eleven clients what they looked at when an Arclight document arrived. The answers took about four minutes each and they were not what anybody had expected.

Samuel Boateng looks at three places on a page and takes about forty seconds. Nora Lindqvist opens the attachment, checks that the total matches what she was expecting, and files it. Priya Menon reads everything, because she has nobody else to read it, and is the only client of the eleven who does.

Set against that, Arclight had spent the previous year improving four things: the accuracy of extraction, which went from ninety-six to ninety-eight per cent; the speed of the monthly reconciliation, which halved; the consistency of client letters, which was transformed; and the layout of the client page, which was tidied twice.

Of those four, clients noticed the consistency of the letters, which was the cheapest. Nobody mentioned the accuracy, because an error rate of two per cent against four per cent is invisible to somebody who sees eleven documents a month. Nobody mentioned the speed, because the page had always arrived before they needed it. Two clients complained about the layout change.

What Victor took from this is not that the unnoticed improvements were worthless. The accuracy work is what prevents the single expensive error that ends a relationship, and no client will ever thank anybody for it. It is that the reasons for doing a piece of work should be stated honestly: some things are done because clients will value them and some because the company cannot afford not to, and conflating the two produces bad decisions in both directions.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: indirect questions**

- An indirect question takes statement word order: could you tell me what you look at.
- Do not use do or did in the embedded clause: I wondered why you changed, not why did you change.
- Use if or whether for yes/no questions: may I ask whether you read it.
- The softening phrase carries the politeness; the question itself stays exactly as sharp.

**Figure 1.2** — The commonest error in English business writing, and a one-word fix.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Most of a service is invisible, and most of it should be**

**Figure 1.5** — The two rows worth continuing are one noticed and one invisible, for different reasons.

There is a question that very few companies ask their customers and that takes about four minutes to answer: what do you actually look at? Not what do you think of the service, which produces a polite generality, and not what would you like us to improve, which produces a wish list. What do you look at, in what order, and for how long.

The answers are usually startling and they are usually consistent. Most customers of most services engage with a very small part of what the service produces, in a fixed order, for a short time, and they have built a habit around it. The rest of the output exists, is paid for, and is not read by anybody outside the company that makes it.

This has an immediate practical consequence for where improvement effort goes, and the consequence is not the obvious one. The obvious inference is to stop improving what nobody sees, and it is wrong. The invisible parts of a service are frequently the ones that prevent the failures that end relationships, and a customer who never notices your accuracy will notice its absence exactly once.

The useful distinction is between work done because customers will value it and work done because the company cannot afford the alternative. Both are legitimate. What causes trouble is conflating them, which happens in two directions: expensive invisible work gets justified with invented claims about customer perception, and cheap visible work gets dismissed as cosmetic when it is the only thing the customer experiences.

The honest position is harder to write in a plan and better to hold. Some of this year’s effort is for the customer and we expect them to notice it. Some is insurance, nobody will thank us for it, and we are doing it because the failure it prevents would cost more than the work. Stating which is which costs a sentence and prevents a great deal of confusion about why anything is being funded.

> **DATA POINT · What customers report** — Customer research commonly finds a weak relationship between measured service quality on internal metrics and customer-reported satisfaction, with visible touchpoints dominating the reported experience.  
> Source: general service-quality research, 2026. Relationships differ by sector.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — What the customer notices, and what they do not

**Figure 1.7** — The fourth box is the whole of the customer experience. The first three are the service.

Ask about behaviour rather than opinion, because opinion about a service is a polite social performance and behaviour is not. What do you look at first is answerable in four seconds and is almost never wrong; what do you think of our reporting produces a sentence that was composed while you were asking.

Then write down which parts of your output nobody mentioned, because that list is more useful than the answers. It tells you what is being produced for nobody, and some of it will turn out to be genuinely for nobody and some of it will turn out to be the part that quietly prevents disasters. Those two require opposite decisions and look identical on the list.

And say which category any piece of work falls into when you fund it. Visible work is justified by what customers will experience; invisible work is justified by what it prevents, and the honest form of that justification admits that the prevention cannot be demonstrated. A plan that pretends otherwise is building an argument that will collapse the first time somebody tests it.

*Say it like this*
- Ask about behaviour: **Could I ask what you look at first when it arrives?**
- Find the unread part: **Which sections have you never read?**
- Name the category: **This is insurance; no client will notice it unless it fails.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Asking what customers look at tells you about the present and about habits already formed. It cannot tell you what they would value if it existed, because nobody reports a need for something they have never seen, and the answers will systematically favour whatever you already produce.

## Unit 2 · Checking You Understood

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: question tags and checking · AI: complaints as a data source*

**Figure 2.0** — FOUR COMPLAINTS IN TWO YEARS, AND WHAT THEY MEANT: The complaints you receive are a sample, and nobody drew it by a rule.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When did you last decide not to complain about something? Why?
- What does "you have read it, haven’t you?" ask that "have you read it?" does not?
- What would your organisation know if every unhappy customer said so?

Arclight received four complaints in its first two years, and for most of that time the figure was quoted inside the company as evidence that the work was good. Victor’s first memorandum on the subject pointed out that four complaints is a measure of how many people complained, which is a different quantity from how many had cause to.

The point is not a clever one and it is persistently ignored. Complaining is work. It requires the customer to decide that the problem is worth the effort, to compose something, to risk an awkward conversation with somebody they may need next month, and to accept that the likely outcome is an apology rather than a remedy.

Most people, most of the time, decline to do this. They absorb the problem, adjust their own behaviour to avoid it recurring, and say nothing. Occasionally they say something to another customer, and very occasionally they leave without ever having mentioned why, which is the pattern Arclight has observed in both of the clients it has lost.

What a complaint record can do, properly handled, is point at categories rather than count incidents. Four complaints in two years is not a rate. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The complaints you receive are a sample nobody drew**

**Figure 2.5** — As a count, four means nothing. As a pattern, the fourth column is the finding.

Complaint counts are among the most reassuring numbers in business and among the least informative. A low complaint rate is consistent with excellent service and it is equally consistent with customers who have decided that complaining is not worth the effort, and nothing about the number itself distinguishes the two.

The reason is that complaining is costly for the person doing it. It takes time, it requires composing something, it risks an awkward relationship with a supplier you may need next month, and the usual outcome is an apology rather than a remedy. A rational customer with a mild problem and a busy week does not complain, and most problems are mild and most weeks are busy.

What happens instead is quieter and more expensive. The customer absorbs the problem, adjusts their own behaviour to avoid it recurring, mentions it to a peer rather than to you, and in a small number of cases eventually leaves without ever having told you why. Companies that lose customers this way usually describe it as unexpected, and from inside it genuinely is.

The complaint record remains useful if it is read as what it is: a sample selected by severity rather than drawn by a rule. It will not tell you how often something goes wrong. It will tell you which categories of failure were bad enough to overcome the cost of complaining, and that is a genuinely informative list.

The other half of the discipline is asking in a way that makes the answer easy. A question that presupposes a problem, such as what was the last thing that annoyed you, costs the customer nothing to answer and removes the implication that they are making a fuss. It produces answers that bear little relation to the formal complaint record, which is precisely why it is worth asking.

> **DATA POINT · Unvoiced dissatisfaction** — Customer-service research consistently reports that only a minority of dissatisfied customers complain to the provider, with the remainder taking no action or switching without explanation.  
> Source: general customer-service research, 2026. Proportions vary widely by sector and value.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The same reply is welcome or insulting depending on what the customer was doing**

**Figure 3.5** — The fourth column changes entirely while the third column stays the same.

An automatic acknowledgement is not a message. It is an act, and like any act its meaning depends on what it is a response to. The same fourteen words can relieve somebody of an overnight worry or demonstrate to them that nobody has read what they wrote, and nothing in the wording distinguishes the two cases.

The distinction lies in what the customer wanted. Somebody requesting a document wants the document; an acknowledgement is useful because it removes uncertainty about whether the request arrived, which was the only thing they were going to think about. The exchange is transactional in the precise sense that nothing personal is at stake.

Somebody writing to end a relationship, to report a bereavement or to express a grievance wants something different and more specific: a person to have read what they wrote. An immediate reply proves that this did not happen, and it proves it faster and more clearly than silence would, because silence leaves the possibility open until morning.

This is why the rule must be about the kind of exchange rather than about the tone of the reply: the same fourteen words are a welcome machine answer in one exchange and an insult in the next. Softening the wording does not help; a gentler automatic reply to somebody ending a contract is still an automatic reply to somebody ending a contract. The problem is not that the words are wrong but that the act is wrong.

And it is why a closed list beats a screening rule, which is the point people resist. Screening attempts to detect sensitive messages and lets through the ones it misses, and a message that gets through a screen designed to catch it has been positively classified as routine. A closed list makes no claim about anything outside it; everything else simply waits, which is the position every company occupied before any of this existed.

> **DATA POINT · Automated responses in sensitive contexts** — Service research commonly finds that automated acknowledgement improves satisfaction for transactional contacts and reduces it for contacts involving complaint, loss or relationship change.  
> Source: general service-research literature, 2026. Effects differ by channel and sector.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A clause nobody reads protects you and informs nobody**

**Figure 4.5** — The two middle columns are the ones that come apart, and the first is the one companies optimise.

Disclosure is an old subject with a settled vocabulary, and nothing about the present situation changes its structure. The questions are the same ones that arise about any material fact: what would a reasonable customer want to know, when would they want to know it, and does telling them require them to ask.

The first trap is confusing protection with information, and it is easy to fall into because the two are usually produced by the same document. A clause in a contract establishes that a customer was told, in the sense that matters to a lawyer, and it does not establish that they know, in the sense that matters to a relationship. Both purposes are legitimate and only one of them is served by small print.

The second trap is disclosure that is accurate and useless. "Some elements of our service may involve automated processing" is true of nearly every company in the world and tells a reader nothing they can act on. A disclosure is useful to the extent that it lets somebody ask a better next question, and a sentence that applies to everybody permits no question at all.

The most useful element Arclight found is the one that commits the company to answering. Saying that a client may ask which parts of their own work are produced which way, and will be told, converts a general statement into a specific obligation, and it does so in a way that forces the company to be able to answer. A great deal of the value of that sentence was consumed internally before any client used it.

What the experience does not support is any expectation that customers will respond. Two of eleven mentioned it; one asked a question; nobody changed anything. Disclosure done properly is mostly invisible, like most of the work in the previous unit, and it should be justified on that basis rather than on a hope that clients will reward it.

> **DATA POINT · What customers do with disclosure** — Research into consumer and business disclosure consistently finds low rates of reading and lower rates of acting, with the main effects arising at the point of a specific question rather than from general statements.  
> Source: general disclosure research, 2026. Patterns differ between consumer and business contexts.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Disclosure: what you tell customers, and when

**Figure 4.7** — A sentence that fits every company tells a reader nothing about yours.

Separate protection from information deliberately, because one document will not do both well. A contract clause is the right instrument for establishing that a customer was told; a conversation at the start of a relationship is the right instrument for their actually knowing. Doing both is not duplication, and treating the first as though it had achieved the second is the commonest failure in this area.

Then test every sentence against your competitors. If it would be equally true printed on their letterhead, it conveys nothing about you and the reader cannot do anything with it. Specificity is what makes a disclosure useful, and specificity is what makes it uncomfortable to write, which is why most published statements on this subject are interchangeable.

And include something that commits you to answering. A client may ask which parts of their own work are affected, and will be told within two working days, is a sentence with consequences: it means the company must be able to answer, and the work of becoming able to answer is where most of the value sits, long before any client uses the right.

*Say it like this*
- Separate the purposes: **The clause protects us; the conversation informs them.**
- Test for specificity: **Would this be true on a competitor’s letterhead?**
- Commit to answering: **You may ask which parts are affected, and we will tell you.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A disclosure tells a customer what you have decided to tell them, in the words you chose. It does not establish that they understood it, that they would have chosen differently had they understood it, or that the categories you named are the ones they would have cared about.

Victor ran a second experiment in the autumn. He collected one client letter from each of five companies in the same business, removed every name, address and reference, and asked eleven colleagues and two clients to say which was which. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Consistency is not the same as a voice**

**Figure 5.5** — Three rows out of five are simply competence. Two of them are the voice.

Most organisations that invest in a house style achieve consistency, which is a real and valuable thing, and then describe what they have achieved as a voice, which is usually not true. Consistency means that everything you send looks as though it came from one place. A voice means that somebody could identify which place without being told, and the second is much rarer and much harder.

The reason is structural rather than a failure of effort. Most of the rules in any good house style are the right answers to questions with right answers: put the reference near the top, use the reader’s surname, keep it short, state the amount plainly. Every competent competitor arrives at the same rules, because they are what the job requires, and the result is a sector in which good writing is uniform writing.

This convergence predates any of the technology in this book and the technology accelerates it, for a reason worth understanding. The phrasing that arrives most readily is the phrasing that is most common in the material the system learned from, which is by definition the sector’s existing average. Nothing is wrong with the output. It is the median of the genre, which is exactly what it should be and exactly what makes it indistinguishable.

The practical response is narrow. Identify the small number of things that are genuinely yours, protect those specifically, and let everything else converge, because defending the thirty-seven rules you share with everybody costs effort and buys no distinction. Arclight found three, and two of the three came from a client remark rather than from anybody inside the company.

That last point is the one worth generalising. Nobody inside an organisation can hear its own voice, because everything it does sounds normal from inside. The features that identify you are the ones somebody outside has noticed and mentioned, and the only reliable way to find them is to ask, which almost nobody does because it feels like fishing for compliments and is nothing of the kind.

> **DATA POINT · Convergence in business writing** — Studies of institutional writing commonly report strong convergence of structure and phrasing within sectors, with distinguishing features concentrated in a small number of recurring choices.  
> Source: general discourse-analysis research, 2026. Degree of convergence varies by genre.

Personalisation used to be expensive, which meant it was rationed, which meant it was reserved for occasions where it mattered. A letter that referred to a specific previous conversation was a letter somebody had thought about. The signal was reliable because producing it cost something.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Accuracy is not the test; purpose is**

**Figure 6.5** — All five are accurate and all five were supplied. Only three pass.

Personalisation has changed its meaning without changing its appearance. For most of the history of business correspondence, a message that referred to your particular circumstances was evidence that somebody had looked at them, because looking was the only way to produce the reference. The personal detail was not the point; it was a by-product that happened to be a reliable signal of attention.

When the cost of producing that detail falls to nothing, the signal stops working, and it stops working in both directions. Recipients no longer infer attention from a personal reference, which removes most of the benefit, and companies continue to behave as though the inference were still available, which produces letters that are more detailed and no more valued.

The second effect is less obvious and more damaging. A detail that is accurate, supplied by the customer and used lawfully can still produce a complaint, because the customer gave it for one purpose and encountered it serving another. Arclight’s client supplied her payment history so that her account could be reconciled. Seeing it deployed to make a reminder letter feel attentive was a different transaction from the one she had entered into.

This means the governing test cannot be accuracy or permission, both of which the offending detail passed. It has to be about purpose: was this supplied for the use you are now making of it. That question is harder to answer than the other two and it is the one the customer is actually asking when they write to say how do you know that.

In practice a short set of tests works better than a principle. A detail may be used where the customer supplied it for this purpose, where its presence helps them do something, and where its absence would make the message worse. The third test is the one that gets argued about and it is the one that prevents a letter from including everything the company happens to know.

> **DATA POINT · Personalisation and perceived intrusion** — Marketing research consistently finds an inverted relationship between personalisation depth and customer comfort, with perceived intrusion rising sharply where data use departs from the context in which it was given.  
> Source: general marketing research, 2026. Thresholds vary widely by culture and sector.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Personalisation at volume

**Figure 6.7** — Everything on the right is accurate, permitted and supplied. That is not the test.

Stop treating accuracy as the licence, because the complaint you will receive is not about accuracy. The customer who asks how you know something is not disputing that you know it. They are objecting to the fact that you have shown them you know it, in a context they did not expect, and no amount of correctness addresses that.

Use purpose as the governing test instead. Ask what the customer supplied each fact for, and whether the use you are now making of it is that use or a different one. A ledger supplied for reconciliation is not a ledger supplied for making reminder letters feel attentive, and the customer is entitled to treat those as separate things because they are separate things.

Then cap the number of variables, because personalisation at volume otherwise applies the tests one at a time to a growing list and every individual decision will be defensible. Six variables in a letter is a different document from twelve, even if each of the twelve passes, and the aggregate effect is what the recipient experiences.

*Say it like this*
- Reject accuracy as the test: **The complaint is not that the detail was wrong.**
- Ask about purpose: **What did they supply this for, and is that what we are using it for?**
- Cap the total: **No more than six variables in any one message.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — The three tests govern what you put in a message; they say nothing about what you hold, what you infer, or what a customer may reasonably assume you know. A company that passes all three tests on every letter may still hold a picture of a customer that the customer would find uncomfortable if it were described to them.

The Not Yet list from Book 1 had three items on it and has had four since the second year. Three of the four are about irreversibility. The fourth is different in kind and Marta added it: the first call after a failure.

There is no technical reason for it. A failure notification could be produced faster, more consistently and with better information than Fatima manages at eleven minutes a call, and in the one case where Arclight tested a written notification the client said afterwards that it had been clear and prompt and that she had found it cold.

What a person provides in that exchange is not information. It is the demonstration that somebody has spent something. Eleven minutes of a named person’s attention, offered when the company is embarrassed and would rather not, is a cost, and the fact that it is a cost is the whole of what it communicates.

This is why the item resists the usual arguments. A faster notification is better information and worse communication; a more detailed one is the same. Improving it in any of the directions the company would naturally improve things makes it worse at the thing it is for, which is a pattern that recurs wherever the signal is the expenditure rather than the content.

The rule is therefore written in terms of occasions rather than quality. After any failure that reached a client, a named person telephones within four working hours. Not writes, not arranges for somebody to write, and not after the investigation is complete. Ines added the last clause after a month in which two calls were delayed while the facts were established, which is a reasonable thing to do and the wrong thing to do.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Where the signal is the expenditure, improvement makes it worse**

**Figure 7.5** — The written version wins four of the five rows and loses the one that was the point.

Nearly everything in this book improves when it is made faster, more consistent and more detailed. There is a small class of exchanges in a customer relationship where those three improvements make the thing worse, and recognising the class is more useful than arguing about any individual case, because the arguments are always the same.

The class is the one where the signal being sent is the cost of sending it. An apology delivered by a named person, in real time, when the company would rather not, communicates that somebody has spent something uncomfortable. The information content is low and it is not the point. The expenditure is the message, which is why a cheaper version of the same message conveys less.

This runs against every instinct a well-run company has, and it should be stated plainly because otherwise it will be improved away. Somebody will observe, correctly, that a written notification is faster, contains more detail and can be produced consistently by anybody. All three observations are true and all three are reasons the written version does not work.

The rule has to be written in terms of occasions rather than of quality, for the same reason. A rule saying the response must be warm and personal will be satisfied by a warm and personal message produced in six minutes. A rule saying a named person telephones within four working hours cannot be satisfied any other way, which is its entire value.

The last element is the hardest to hold. The call should happen before the facts are established, because its function is not to convey findings. Waiting until you can explain properly is the behaviour of a company doing its best, and from the other side it is indistinguishable from a company that has not noticed, which is what the client spends the intervening days believing.

> **DATA POINT · Service recovery** — Service-recovery research commonly finds that speed of acknowledgement and the involvement of an identifiable person predict relationship outcomes more strongly than the completeness of the explanation offered.  
> Source: general service-recovery literature, 2026. Effects vary by severity and sector.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — What must never be automated in a customer relationship

**Figure 7.7** — The right-hand column is short, and everything on it resists the usual arguments.

Identify the class before arguing about the case, because the cases all look like exceptions and the class is a pattern. Wherever the message is carried by what it cost to send, making it cheaper removes the message. Apologies, condolences, personal thanks and the first contact after a failure all sit in this class; almost nothing else does.

Write the rule as an occasion and a person, never as a standard of quality. "The response must be warm and personal" is satisfiable by a warm and personal message produced in six minutes, and it will be satisfied that way within a year, by somebody reasonable, for good operational reasons. "A named person telephones within four working hours" has only one way of being complied with.

And forbid the delay that feels responsible. Waiting until you can explain what happened is the instinct of a company that takes the failure seriously, and it produces two days in which the client believes nobody has noticed. The call conveys that you know. Everything else can follow in writing when it is ready.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Answer in the words they used**

**Figure 8.5** — Four words, no cost to change, and nine months of a quiet insistence nobody intended.

Every organisation develops its own names for things, and the names are usually better than the ones customers use, because they come from the system, the file name or the specification. They are also unknown outside the company, and the gap between the two vocabularies is invisible to the people inside who created it.

For most of the time this costs nothing. Two parties use two words for one document, each understands the other, and the translation happens silently in both heads. The cost appears at the moment a reply travels further than the person it was sent to, which with email is most of the time and is never planned for.

There is a second cost, smaller per occasion and more cumulative. A company that always answers in its own vocabulary is making a small claim about whose terms the relationship runs on, several times a week, for years. Nobody ever objects to it, because objecting would be absurd, and it contributes to a relationship in which the customer is the one doing the adapting.

The rule that fixes both is cheap: use the words the customer used. If they wrote manifest, the reply says manifest. This requires no judgement, no list and no training, and it is one of the very few improvements in this book that costs nothing at all and can be adopted on a Tuesday.

The same principle extends to answering in another language, where it becomes much harder to apply and much more expensive to get wrong. A translation can be checked line against line, which establishes that the content survived. Whether it uses the vocabulary that the reader’s own trade uses is a separate question, nobody inside the company can hear the difference, and nobody outside it will ever complain about it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A choice offered without a view is a transfer of work**

**Figure 9.5** — Two rows the client owns, three the supplier owns, and a choice that needs all five.

Falling unit costs produce a genuine choice rather than a straightforward improvement, because the saving can be taken in three different currencies: as a lower price, as a faster turnaround, or as more checking at the same price and speed. These are mutually exclusive at the margin, and a supplier who pretends otherwise is simply deciding for the customer without telling them.

The instinct to put the choice to the customer is correct and the common execution of it is not. Setting out three options without a recommendation looks like candour and lands as abdication, because the customer cannot evaluate the trade-off: most of the information the comparison requires sits on the supplier’s side of the relationship and has no way of crossing it.

Specifically, a customer can price what a failure costs them, often to the hour, because they have lived through failures. They cannot price a difference in error rate, because they have never seen a month at either rate and have no basis for imagining one. Asking them to weigh the two is asking them to do arithmetic with one number missing.

A recommendation fixes this provided it carries its reason, and the reason has to be about the customer rather than about the service. We recommend the faster option because you pay suppliers on a fixed day is a reason a client can agree or disagree with. We recommend the faster option because it suits most clients is not, and it returns the problem to them in a politer form.

The last element is accepting that a real recommendation will sometimes be declined, and treating that as the arrangement working rather than failing. A client who takes a different option has usually supplied a fact you did not have, and a quarter of them doing so is a healthier sign than all of them deferring, which would mean the recommendation had become an instruction.

> **DATA POINT · Choice and decision deferral** — Research on consumer and business choice generally finds that offering options without guidance increases deferral and reduces satisfaction, while a reasoned recommendation alongside options increases both decision rates and confidence.  
> Source: general decision-research literature, 2026. Effects vary with expertise.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Faster, cheaper or better: you must choose

**Figure 9.7** — A client can disagree with the right-hand column, which is what makes it a recommendation.

Offer the choice, because the saving is genuinely divisible and deciding silently is deciding for somebody else. But offer it with a view, because the comparison requires information that sits on your side of the relationship and the customer has no way of obtaining it. Candour without a recommendation is a transfer of work rather than a transfer of control.

Write the reason about the customer rather than about the service, and make it a fact they could contradict. Because you pay suppliers on a fixed day invites them to say that they changed that in March. Because it suits most clients invites nothing and leaves them exactly where they were, which is being asked to choose with one number missing.

Then expect to be declined, and record what you learn when you are. A client who takes a different option has almost always supplied a fact about their own business that you did not hold, and that fact is worth more than the sale of the option you preferred. If nobody ever departs from the recommendation, the page has stopped being a choice.

*Say it like this*
- Give the view: **We recommend the faster option, and here is why.**
- Make it about them: **Because you pay suppliers on a fixed day.**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**When the unit cost collapses, decide what you sell before somebody decides for you**

**Figure 10.5** — Only the rows about outcomes survive both columns, and only one of them is a promise.

When the cost of producing something falls by a large factor, the price does not have to fall with it, but the justification does have to change. A price defended by the labour it contains becomes indefensible the moment the labour leaves, and the fact that no customer has yet asked is a statement about timing rather than about safety.

Two arguments compete here and both are respectable. Value-based pricing holds that what a customer pays should reflect what the outcome is worth to them, in which case a cheaper production method is simply the supplier’s good fortune. The relational argument holds that where a customer has been told a price rests on effort, the price carries an implied account of itself, and withdrawing the effort silently is a breach of something even when it breaches no contract.

The argument that usually decides the matter is neither of these, and it is about competition. Where production costs approach zero, a rival will eventually offer the same output at a fraction of the price, and a business whose entire answer is the output has nothing left to say. That is the definition of a commodity, and commodity competition is decided on price alone, which favours whoever can survive the lowest one.

The way out is to sell something the cheap competitor cannot promise, which usually means selling a guaranteed outcome rather than a performed activity. A guaranteed error rate with a credit attached is a different product from a check, although the work behind it is identical, because it transfers a risk that the customer currently carries alone and that a low-cost rival cannot afford to take on.

This has a real cost and it is not the credit. Guaranteeing a number means owning it when the underlying system changes for reasons you did not cause and cannot see, which happens, and which can take weeks to diagnose. Businesses that make this move successfully tend to be the ones that priced that exposure deliberately rather than discovering it in the first bad quarter.

> **DATA POINT · Commoditisation and differentiation** — Industry analyses of services whose delivery costs fall sharply generally find rapid price convergence among undifferentiated providers, with sustained margins concentrated in providers selling guaranteed outcomes, integration or liability.  
> Source: general strategy literature, 2026. Patterns vary by sector and regulation.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Pricing when your cost per unit collapses

**Figure 10.7** — The left column is what a cheap rival also says. The right column is what it cannot afford to say.

When the cost of doing something collapses, the price can stay where it is but the account of the price cannot. Work out, in one sentence, what your customer is actually buying, and notice whether that sentence describes an activity or an outcome. An activity is what a cheaper competitor will sell at a third of your price within a couple of years, and at that point your experience and your history are not arguments, they are sentiment.

Then build the outcome into the offer, as a number with a measurement window and a consequence attached. A guaranteed error rate with a credit is a different product from the same work performed without a promise, and the difference is that you are now carrying a risk the customer used to carry alone. That is precisely why a low-cost rival cannot copy it, and precisely why it costs you something real.

Price that exposure before you offer it, using your worst month rather than your average one, and write the exceptions as a closed list rather than as reassuring language. Then decide the thing most businesses leave undecided: what would make you withdraw the guarantee. A promise you have not costed is not a strategy, it is an invoice arriving later.

*Say it like this*
- Name what they buy: **You are buying an error rate, not a check.**
- Attach the number: **Under two per cent, measured monthly, credited if missed.**
- Close the list: **It does not apply in these three cases, and no others.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A guarantee transfers risk; it does not remove it. Where the underlying system changes for reasons outside your sight, you will be liable for a rate you cannot yet explain, and the only protection is having priced the worst month you have actually seen rather than the one you expect.
