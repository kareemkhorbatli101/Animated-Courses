### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do you do at work that only you know how to do?
- What is the difference between a task you repeat and a process?
- Which part of your job would be hardest to explain to somebody taking over?

Arclight is eleven people now, and in March Dalia worked out that she was personally involved in thirty-one separate pieces of work a week. Not managing them. Involved in them, in the sense that if she were ill on a Tuesday, each of the thirty-one would either stop or be done badly.

She wrote them all down on a Sunday and the list was not what she expected. She had assumed the problem was volume. The list showed something different: nine of the thirty-one were genuinely one-off, and twenty-two were the same four or five jobs happening over and over with different names on them.

That distinction turned out to be the whole of the next six months. A task is something you do once and then it is finished. A process is a task you have done more than three times and will do again, whether or not anybody has ever written it down. Most of what drowns a small company is processes being treated as tasks.

The test Dalia uses now is blunt. If the same job comes round again, and you find yourself making the same decisions in the same order, you are running a process. The fact that it has never been described as one does not change what it is. It only changes whether anybody else can do it.

She had the four biggest written up properly over a fortnight, which was not a pleasant fortnight. Writing a process down means discovering how many decisions you make without noticing. By May she had got three of the four running without her, and the fourth turned out not to be a process at all. It was a relationship, and it stayed where it was.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: causative have and get something done**

- Have something done: we have the invoices extracted every Friday.
- Get something done is less formal and often suggests effort or arranging.
- The pattern is have/get + object + past participle. The doer is often left out.
- Use it when who did the work matters less than the fact that it was done.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A task you have done four times is a process pretending to be a task**

**Figure 1.5** — Four rows look identical in a diary and are not the same kind of thing at all.

There is a distinction that decides most of what a small company can and cannot hand over, and it is not the one people expect. It is not hard against easy, and it is not important against unimportant. It is whether the job is a task or a process, and the test is almost embarrassingly simple: when it comes round again, do you make the same decisions in the same order?

A task is a thing you do and finish. Negotiating a new contract is a task. Apologising properly after a failure is a task. They may happen often, but each one is shaped by circumstances that have not occurred before, and the thinking you do is new thinking.

A process is a job where the thinking happened once, some time ago, and has been quietly repeated ever since. The reconciliation is a process. Chasing overdue invoices is a process. They feel like work because they take time, but the decisions inside them were settled long ago by somebody who has stopped noticing that they settled anything.

What makes this worth caring about is that processes can be handed over and tasks mostly cannot. Not because of difficulty, but because handing something over means describing it, and you can only describe a job whose decisions are stable. A task reshapes itself each time, so any description of it is a description of last time.

The uncomfortable part, which Dalia found and most people find, is that writing a process down is harder than running it. Running it uses judgement you no longer notice having. Writing it down requires you to find that judgement, name it, and discover that about a fifth of it is not a rule at all but a habit you have never tested.

> **DATA POINT · Repetition in small firms** — Surveys of small-business owners frequently report that a majority of weekly work is repeated activity, although respondents often estimate the share lower than time-use records show.  
> Source: general small-business time-use research, 2026. Self-report and observation diverge.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — A task and a process: what can be repeated

**Figure 1.7** — The left column is not harder. It is less stable, which is a different thing.

Begin by sorting your own week, because almost nobody has done this and almost everybody is surprised by the result. Write down everything that would stop if you were away, and then ask of each one whether the same decisions arise in the same order. The things that repeat are processes, however different the client names look, and the things that do not are tasks.

Then write one process down properly, and expect it to be unpleasant. The steps are easy. The difficulty is the three or four moments where you do not follow a rule, you decide something, and you have been deciding it the same way for two years without ever saying why. Those are the points where handing over actually succeeds or fails.

For each of those moments you have two honest options and one dishonest one. You can decide it in advance and write the decision down. You can mark it to be flagged, so that the process stops and asks. The dishonest option is to leave it unmentioned and hope, which produces a process that works for five weeks and then produces something indefensible in week six.

*Say it like this*
- Name the shape: **We have the reconciliation produced by the fifth, every month.**
- Pre-decide it: **Where two due dates appear, use the later one. Decided, not judged.**
- Or flag it: **Where the amount is in another currency, stop and flag it.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Writing a process down does not make it a good process. A badly designed job, carefully documented, becomes a badly designed job that now runs four hundred times a week without anybody present to notice. Documentation preserves whatever it finds, including the parts that were never right.

## Unit 2 · Boundaries and Permissions

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: modals of permission and ability · AI: permissions and limits*

**Figure 2.0** — THE FOLDER NOBODY MEANT TO SHARE: Permissions are boring until the first Tuesday on which they are not.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What can you open at work that you have no business opening?
- Is there a difference between "you can" and "you may"?
- Who would know if somebody read a file they should not have read?

When Jonas set up the first automated runs, he gave them access to the whole client folder. This was not carelessness in the ordinary sense. Narrowing the access would have taken a morning, nothing in the runs needed anything outside their own area, and the folder was already restricted to staff. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Give the smallest permission that does the job**

**Figure 2.5** — Five runs, five different needs, one permission given to all of them.

Access is given broadly for an entirely understandable reason: narrowing it is work, and the work has to be done before anything has gone wrong, which is exactly when nobody wants to do it. The broad permission is not a decision anybody makes. It is a decision nobody makes, which is a different and more common thing.

The principle that fixes it is old and has a name in several industries: give the smallest permission that lets the job be done. Not the smallest permission that feels safe, and not the permission that seemed about right at the time. The smallest one that works, determined by looking at what the job actually touches.

It is worth being honest about what this does and does not protect you from. It does very little against somebody determined to misuse access they have been given, because they have been given it. What it protects against is the ordinary accident: the file in the wrong folder, the column with an unexpected name, the run that finds something nobody remembered was there.

Those accidents are much more common than misuse, and they are the ones nobody plans for, because planning for an accident requires you to imagine being careless, which people are poor at. The narrow permission does the imagining for you: if the run cannot see the sale list, no sequence of reasonable mistakes causes it to use the sale list.

The second half of the discipline is the record. Every grant written down with a name, a date and a reason, and every grant reviewed when the reason stops applying. This is dull and it is the part most often skipped. The cost of skipping it is that nobody will later be able to say why anything has the access it has.

> **DATA POINT · Accidental exposure** — Analyses of data incidents in small organisations consistently find that accidental exposure through over-broad access is reported more often than deliberate misuse.  
> Source: general information-security reporting, 2026. Categories differ between studies.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Permissions and limits: what it may touch

**Figure 2.7** — Every row on the left was true in February. Nobody had written a single row on the right.

Write the permission as a list of folders rather than as a level of trust. Trust is not a specification and it does not survive being handed to somebody else; a list of folders does. This run may read the invoice folder and the supplier index. Two lines, and anybody can check them against what the run actually touched.

Then write the forbidden list, which almost nobody writes because it feels like imagining crimes. It is not. It is imagining accidents, which are far more likely, and naming two or three things you know should never be reached means that no sequence of ordinary confusion gets to them. The sale list, the staff folder, the client’s own system.

Finally, make the grant an event rather than a state. Somebody gives it, on a date, for a reason, and the reason can expire. Without that, the access a process holds becomes the sum of every emergency anybody ever had, and nobody can say which of them still applies.

*Say it like this*
- List what it may read: **This run may read the invoice folder and the supplier index.**
- Name what it may not: **It may not open the sale list or the staff folder.**
- Make the grant an event: **Marta granted this on 3 September, for the April reporting cycle.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A permission record does not tell you what was actually read. It tells you what was allowed, which is a different thing, and it will look complete and tidy even in a month when a run read nothing at all or read the same file nine hundred times. The register proves intention, not behaviour.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A step has an input, an output and somewhere to fail**

**Figure 3.5** — One row in the last column reads differently from the others. That row was the problem.

Describing a process properly means three things and only three: the steps in order, what each step needs before it can start, and what it produces when it finishes. This sounds thin, and it is in fact the whole of it. A description with those three elements can be handed to somebody else, and a description missing any of them cannot.

The steps are the easy part and they are nearly always undercounted. People name the three or four steps that involve doing something visible and leave out the waiting, the checking and the small corrections, because those do not feel like work. They are frequently most of the elapsed time and almost all of the variation.

The inputs are where the real information is. For each step, ask what has to exist before this can begin, and then ask where that thing comes from and when it arrives. This second question is the one that finds the problems. A step that depends on a file with a schedule is predictable. A step that depends on somebody noticing something is not a step, it is a hope.

Outputs matter for a different reason: they are where you can put a check. If step five produces a list of gaps, you can count the gaps, and an unusual count tells you something is wrong at step five or earlier. Without named outputs, a process has exactly one place to detect failure, which is the end, by which time you cannot tell which part failed.

There is a quieter benefit that people notice only afterwards. A process described this way can be improved in pieces. You can change step six without touching anything else, because you know what it needs and what it owes. An undescribed process can only be improved by the person who holds it in their head, and only all at once.

> **DATA POINT · Where elapsed time goes** — Process studies across service industries commonly find that waiting and handover time exceeds active working time in end-to-end measurements.  
> Source: general process-improvement literature, 2026. Ratios differ by industry and measurement method.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Steps, inputs and outputs: describing a process so it can run

**Figure 3.7** — The right-hand column is longer, duller and can be given to somebody else.

Write the steps first, and write more of them than you think there are. If you produce fewer than six for a job that takes an afternoon, you have left something out, and what you have left out is almost certainly waiting or correcting. Both of those are steps. Both of them take time. Only one of them is usually fixable, and you cannot tell which until they are on the page.

Then do the inputs, which is where the useful discoveries are. For every step, ask what must exist before this can start, where that comes from, and when it arrives. Any input whose answer to the third question is "whenever somebody gets round to it" is the reason your process has a variable duration, and you will usually find exactly one of them.

Finally write the outputs, because an output is a place to put a check. A step that produces a named thing can be tested; a step that merely contributes to a general sense of progress cannot. Once outputs are named, you can count them, and counting is the cheapest form of quality control anybody has invented.

*Say it like this*
- Name the input: **Step 6 needs the supplier index, updated by 17:00 on Monday.**
- Name the output: **Step 5 produces a list of gaps, which goes to step 6.**
- Name the wait: **If the bank file has not arrived, halt at step 4 and record the wait.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A mapping does not tell you whether the process is worth running. It describes faithfully whatever it is given, including nine steps that could have been four, and it will make a badly designed process easier to repeat without making it any better. Describing is not designing.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How would you describe "professional" to somebody who had never seen your work?

The house style ran to one page and it was a good page. It said what register to use, how to open, where the reference number went and how to close. People read it, agreed with it and then produced letters that still did not look like each other.

The problem was the word professional, which appeared twice. Everybody who read it nodded, and everybody meant something slightly different by it. Fatima meant warm and brief. Thomas meant precise and slightly formal. One of the new people meant the register of a bank, which is where the whole problem had started eighteen months earlier.

The third one turned out to be the valuable one, and nobody had expected that. The good letter showed the middle of the target and the bad letter showed something far outside it, but neither of them showed where the edge was. The nearly-right letter was the edge, with two sentences marked.

Three examples, one good, one bad and one marginal, communicate more in ten seconds than a page of careful adjectives does in five minutes, and they cannot be misread in the way that professional can.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Three examples beat a page of adjectives, and one example beats none**

**Figure 4.5** — The fourth row is why three is the usual number, and the fifth is why none of them is optional.

When people are asked to describe a standard they reach for adjectives, because adjectives are how we talk about quality in ordinary speech. Professional, clear, friendly, concise. Each of these words is doing a great deal of work and none of it is work anybody can check. Two people who agree about the word will produce different things and both believe they followed the instruction.

An example does something an adjective cannot: it fixes the meaning in a form that cannot drift. Here is a letter we were pleased with. This is what we mean. Nobody has to decode anything, and if somebody disagrees, they disagree about a concrete object rather than about a feeling, which is an argument that can actually be resolved.

One example is a great deal better than none and it has a known weakness: it shows the middle of the target and nothing about its edges. People given one good example tend to produce close copies of it, including copying things that were accidental, and they have nothing to go on the first time a case arrives that the example did not cover.

This is why the usual advice is three, and why the third one matters most. A good example and a bad example bracket the target very widely, which is easy and not very informative. An example that is nearly right, with the specific failures marked, says where the line is, and the line is the only part anybody actually needs to learn.

What examples cannot do is explain why. They show the what, reliably and without ambiguity, and they leave the reason entirely implicit, which means that a case the examples did not anticipate will be handled by guesswork. So the practice that works is both: three examples to fix the meaning, and one sentence of reason to cover what the examples missed.

> **DATA POINT · Examples in instruction** — Instructional research generally finds that worked examples improve performance on similar tasks more than descriptive rules alone, with the advantage narrowing as learners gain experience.  
> Source: general instructional-design research, 2026. Effects vary with task type.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Pointing the question at your own documents**

**Figure 5.5** — The second column fixed speed. The third column was still wrong. The fourth fixed it.

A great deal of what a company needs to know is already written down somewhere inside it, and the difficulty has never been that the knowledge does not exist. The difficulty is that it is distributed across folders, attachments and old messages in a way that makes finding it cost more than the answer is worth, so people guess instead, or ask somebody who guesses.

Pointing a question at your own documents rather than at general knowledge changes this completely, and it is probably the single most useful thing a small company can do in the first month. The answers become specific to you, they can be quoted, and the quotation can be checked against the file by anybody with thirty seconds.

What it exposes, immediately and uncomfortably, is the state of the documents themselves. A person searching a folder applies judgement without noticing: they see policy_v3_final and policy_v3_final_revised, register that one of them has a later date, and open that one. Nothing in the retrieval of a document does that for you unless somebody has made the folder say which is which.

So the preparation is unglamorous and it is the whole job. One place for current documents. One version of each. A date and a named owner on every one. Everything superseded moved somewhere else and labelled as superseded. This takes an afternoon for a small company and it is the afternoon that decides whether the rest works.

The same discipline has a human half, which is older and harder. When somebody reports what a document or a person said, the report drifts towards the stronger reading: looked into becomes dealt with, considering becomes agreeing, may becomes will. Nobody lies. Each step is a small reasonable compression, and after three steps the sentence commits somebody to something they never said.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Working from your own documents

**Figure 5.7** — The left column is true in general and useless in particular.

Say where to look, and mean exactly one place. Answer from the Current folder is a specification; answer from our documents is not, because our documents includes eleven years of attachments and four versions of everything. The whole value of pointing a question at your own material depends on the material being a defined set.

## Unit 6 · One Shape Every Time

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: the passive, present and past · AI: templates and fixed output formats*

**Figure 6.0** — THE SAME DOCUMENT, FOUR MONTHS RUNNING: A fixed shape is not tidiness. It is where you put your attention.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which documents at your work look the same every time?
- Does it matter who did something, or only that it was done?
- How would you notice if a figure was missing from a familiar page?

Samuel Boateng’s reconciliation has had the same shape since March: seven columns in a fixed order, the summary in the top five lines, the unreconciled total bottom right. He does not read it. He looks at three places on it, in the same order, every month, and the whole thing takes him about forty seconds.

In July the unreconciled total was missing. It had not been calculated, because a supplier had sent two files instead of one and a step had stopped early. Samuel noticed in the time it took him to look at the bottom right of the page and find nothing there.

This is the argument for fixed formats that people usually miss. The tidy appearance is the least of it. The real value is that a fixed shape turns reading into looking, and looking is fast enough that an absence becomes visible. On a page whose layout changes each month, a missing total looks exactly like a different layout.

The template Arclight uses is written as labelled slots rather than as a description. Not "include a summary at the top" but a block of five lines with labels on them, each of which must be filled or marked not available. An empty slot is a question. A slot that was never there is silence.

The grammar that goes with this is the passive, which people are often told to avoid and which is exactly right here. The reconciliation is produced on the fifth business day. Nobody cares who produces it. What matters is that it is produced, that it is checked, and that the unreconciled total is shown, and the passive puts all three of those facts where the attention belongs.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A fixed shape is where you put your attention**

**Figure 6.5** — The last two rows are the only ones where an absence announces itself.

Fixing the format of a recurring document is usually presented as a matter of professionalism, and that is the least interesting reason to do it. The interesting reason is about human attention. A reader who has seen the same shape eleven times stops reading it and starts looking at it, and looking is about twenty times faster than reading and considerably better at noticing that something is not there.

This only works if the shape really is the same. A page whose columns move, whose summary is sometimes at the top and sometimes at the bottom, and whose length varies cannot be looked at. It has to be read every time, which costs minutes rather than seconds and, more importantly, means that an omission is indistinguishable from a rearrangement.

The way to specify a format so that it holds is as labelled slots rather than as a description of contents. A description says what should be included; a slot says where it goes and insists that something occupy the space. When a slot cannot be filled, the instruction is to write not available in it, which is a statement. A section that was quietly omitted is not a statement, and nobody can see it.

There is a real cost, which the people who like standards tend to understate. A rigid format applied to cases it does not suit produces pages full of empty columns, and an empty column that is empty every month stops being information and becomes furniture. The second time somebody has to explain why three columns are always blank, the format has begun costing more than it saves.

So the honest position is that format rigidity is a trade, not a virtue. Fix the shape wherever a reader has built a habit on it, because that is where the detection value lives. Allow variants where the standard shape carries nothing, write the reason down beside each variant, and accept that somebody will have to look at the list of variants once a year and kill the ones that have stopped making sense.

> **DATA POINT · Reading and looking** — Human-factors work on familiar documents generally reports that experienced readers shift from reading to targeted visual search, with large gains in speed and in the detection of missing values.  
> Source: general human-factors research, 2026. Depends heavily on familiarity.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A check that cannot be failed is a ceremony**

**Figure 7.5** — The fourth row is where almost every checkpoint ends up if nobody looks at it.

A human checkpoint is the main safeguard most small companies have, and it decays in a predictable way that nobody plans for. It is created carefully, performed thoroughly for a few weeks, and then gets faster, because nothing ever goes wrong and the person doing it has eleven other things to do. The decay is not carelessness. It is a reasonable response to a long run of good outcomes.

The problem is that the checkpoint still exists on paper, and everybody continues to behave as though it is being performed at the standard of week one. Decisions get made downstream on the strength of a signature that now means something much weaker than it did, and nobody has been told, because nobody has noticed.

The fix is not to demand more diligence, which lasts about three weeks. It is to change what the checkpoint asks. A checkpoint that asks somebody to confirm that the work is fine will decay, because fine is unbounded and cannot be done quickly. A checkpoint that asks for four specific facts can be done properly in four minutes and either is done or is not.

The test of a good checkpoint is whether it can be failed. If nobody has ever refused to sign, one of two things is true: either the work has been perfect for two years, which is unlikely in any organisation with people in it, or the check does not examine anything capable of being wrong. The second is far more common and much harder to see from inside.

There is a related trap worth naming. A checkpoint that examines a summary produced by the same process it is checking is not examining anything independent. It will report that the run covered what the run believes it covered, which is true by construction and useless. Whatever the signer looks at has to come from somewhere other than the thing being signed off.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — The human checkpoint: where a person signs

Place the checkpoint where the work becomes irreversible, which is usually immediately before something leaves the building. Earlier is cheaper and catches less; later is not possible. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The silence is worse than the failure**

**Figure 8.5** — Every row in the third column reads the same. That is the whole failure.

Processes fail, and a company that designs on the assumption that they will not is designing for a world it does not live in. The useful question is not how to prevent failure but what state a failure leaves things in, and whether anybody can find that out quickly. Those two questions are answerable in advance and almost nobody answers them.

A run that stops part way leaves three categories behind: the work it finished, the work it never started, and one item in the middle whose state is genuinely unknown. The third category is small and it is where almost all of the recovery time goes, because establishing whether one letter was sent can take longer than establishing what happened to the other three hundred and forty.

What makes this expensive is silence. A failure that announces itself costs you the failure. A failure that says nothing costs you the failure plus however long it takes somebody to notice, plus the work done in the meantime on the belief that everything was fine. At Arclight that was two days, three people repeating that the run had gone well, and a client finding out before the company did.

The cure is cheap and dull. Write a line as each item completes, so that where did it get to has an answer. Make a run that stops raise an alert rather than simply ending. Name the last completed item in the alert, so that the recovery starts from a position rather than from an investigation.

There is a harder question underneath, which is what a run should do when it meets something it cannot handle: stop everything, or skip and carry on. Stopping is safe and expensive, and it makes one client’s odd file into every client’s delay. Skipping is cheaper and requires you to be genuinely certain that the skipped item is recorded as skipped, which is exactly the certainty a company that has just had a silent failure does not yet have.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — What happens when it fails halfway through

**Figure 8.7** — Both columns describe the same failure. Only one of them costs a day.

Decide in advance what a run does when it cannot continue, because it will meet something it cannot handle and the decision will otherwise be made by whatever happens to occur. And make the stop loud. A process that ends early and says nothing produces a company that believes the work was done, which is strictly worse than a company that knows the work failed. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A chain converts small errors into confident ones**

**Figure 9.5** — Every step did exactly what it was asked. The last column is why that was not enough.

Chaining is the arrangement that makes most of the value in this book possible, because few useful jobs are one operation. Extract, then classify, then draft, then check. Each step is simple, each can be specified properly, and the combination does something no single step could.

It also introduces a property that surprises people the first time they meet it: a chain does not merely pass errors along, it dresses them. The original fault at Arclight was a comma rule applied to four names in a file of eleven hundred. Three subsequent steps treated the result as a fact, performed correctly on it, and produced a finished letter with a company name at the bottom.

Nothing in the chain was capable of noticing, and this is the important part. Step two classified by address, which was correct; it had no business looking at the name and no reason to doubt it. Step three and step four were presented with consistent, plausible input. The error only became visible when a human being who knew the person read the result.

This changes where checks belong. In a single-step job the natural place to check is the output, because that is where errors are. In a chain the natural place is wherever an error would become invisible, which is usually early, and specifically at any step that transforms something into a form the later steps cannot question.

The second thing worth building is lineage: a record of which step produced each field, so that a wrong result can be traced in seconds rather than in an hour of working backwards. The hour matters less than it sounds, because errors are rare. What matters is that an hour of tracing is a strong discouragement, and people stop investigating things that take an hour, which is how a recurring fault becomes a known quirk.

> **DATA POINT · Error propagation** — Quality-engineering practice generally holds that the cost of correcting a fault rises with the number of downstream steps it has passed through before detection.  
> Source: general quality-engineering literature, 2026. Ratios are context-dependent.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An assistant does the work; an agent takes the decision**

**Figure 10.5** — Only the first four rows differ. The fifth is the same, and it is the one people forget.

The distinction between an assistant and an agent is not technical and it is much older than any of this. An assistant does work that you then act on. An agent acts, within limits you set in advance, and tells you afterwards if it tells you at all. Every profession that has ever delegated authority has a body of thinking about this, and almost none of it is about capability.

Companies cross the line without noticing, which is the main practical danger. Nobody at Arclight held a meeting about agency. Somebody added an out-of-hours acknowledgement because clients were waiting until nine in the morning for a reply, and it was a good idea, and it was also a decision to let something speak for the company without anybody reading what it said.

The useful test is not whether something is capable of acting alone but what happens in the worst exchange it will meet. Routine acknowledgements are fine ninety-nine times. The hundredth is somebody writing at eleven at night to end a relationship, and the cost of that one is not a hundredth of anything. It is a particular kind of insult that people remember for years.

So the limits should be written in terms of the exchange rather than the content. Filtering on words is tempting and it fails, because the next case will use different words. A rule that says an agent may handle requests for documents, delivery dates and passwords, and nothing else, is narrow enough to be safe without requiring anybody to anticipate what a distressed client will write.

And the answerability line has to be explicit, in writing, before anything goes wrong. When an agent acts on a company’s behalf, the company is answerable; that is what acting on behalf of somebody means, and it has meant that for several hundred years. The reason to write it down is not that anybody disagrees. It is that under pressure somebody will reach for "the system did that", and a client hearing it once will never hear anything else from you again.

### Part 9 · The Brief Lab — Brief Lab · The out-of-hours reply

**STAGE 1 · The Task**

This is the brief that created the acknowledgement in January. It was written in four lines by somebody solving a real problem: clients writing at night and hearing nothing until nine the next morning.

It does not contain the word agent. Nobody involved thought of it as a decision about authority, and that is exactly how the line gets crossed.

**STAGE 2 · Draft One, As It Was Sent**

**Figure 10.10** — A four-line convenience that gave something standing permission to speak for the company.

**[2]** Let clients know we have got their message.
**[3]** Keep it warm and reassuring.
**[4]** Send it to anything that comes in after six.
**[5]** Use the usual sort of wording.
**[6]** Keep an eye on it for a bit.

**STAGE 3 · What Came Back**

**Figure 10.11** — Four hundred and twelve replies, one of which thanked a client for leaving.

| We asked for | What came back |
|---|---|
| A closed list of 3 routine categories | Every out-of-hours message answered |
| Silence for everything else | Warm wording applied to a cancellation |
| A named party answerable for what it sends | No statement of who answers for it |

*The gap.* "Anything that comes in after six" is an open set, "warm and reassuring" is applied before anybody knows what the message says, and nothing names who is answerable when it goes wrong.

The brief never asked whether this was an assistant or an agent. The distinction did not come up until the eighth week, when it came up all at once.

**STAGE 4 · The Diagnosis**

- **ambiguous referent** — pronoun reference, cohesion and substitution
- **missing condition** — conditionals; unless, provided that, failing that
- **unbounded quantity** — quantifiers and exact measures
- **undefined done** — modality of requirement, purpose and result
- **unscoped set** — defining relative clauses and articles
- **unstated criterion** — comparatives and superlatives with a stated basis

0. [1] "messages that arrive out of hours" — which messages, of what kind?  — *unstated criterion*
1. [2] "keep it warm and reassuring" — even when the message is not?
2. [3] "anything that comes in after six" — how many kinds is anything?
3. [4] "the usual sort of wording" — usual by what standard?
4. [5] "keep an eye on it for a bit" — for how long?
5. [6] "set up an automatic reply" — and when it answers badly, who answers?

**STAGE 5 · Draft Two**

Rewrite the brief so that the permitted situations are a closed list, everything else is met with silence, and somebody is named as answerable.

| Line | What goes on it |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Why clients should not wait until nine, in one clause |
| Scope | The categories it may answer, and the explicit treatment of everything else |
| Inputs | The inbox and the acknowledgement template, both named |
| Criteria | What a reply may say, and what it may never refer to |
| Done | What comes back in the morning, and by when |
| Fallback | What happens to a message that matches no category |

- Acknowledge … so that …
- It may answer only … ; anything else …
- Work from … and template …
- A reply states … ; it may never …
- Return … by … .
- If a message matches no category, …

*Check your rewrite*
- Is the list of permitted situations closed?
- Does the brief say that silence is the intended behaviour for everything else?
- Is there a line naming who is answerable?
- Does the purpose appear in a so that clause?
