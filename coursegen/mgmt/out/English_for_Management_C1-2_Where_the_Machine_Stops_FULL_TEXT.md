### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who answers for the last significant error your organisation made?
- Was that written down before the error, or worked out afterwards?

The filing that went out on the eleventh of March contained a figure that was wrong by a factor of ten, and was signed, in the sense that somebody’s name appeared on the covering letter. What it did not contain, and what nobody could afterwards supply, was any record of who had decided that the figure was right.

The sequence was ordinary. A system drafted the schedule from the client’s own records; a reviewer in the checking team confirmed it; a client manager assembled the filing; and a director signed the covering letter without reading the schedule, which is what a covering letter means in that firm and in most others. Each of those four acts was correct according to the process as written.

Marta Olsen’s question, put to the board three days later, was not who was to blame. It was narrower and harder: for each of the four steps, what exactly was the person asserting when they completed it? The reviewer believed she was confirming that the schedule matched the source. The client manager believed the schedule had been checked. The director believed the client manager would not have brought him something unchecked. Nobody believed they were asserting that the figure was right.

What made this difficult to repair was that the process document was, in the ordinary sense, complete. It described every step, every input and every output. What it omitted was the one element that the grammar of the document had quietly dropped: who asserts what. Confirmed, reviewed, released and signed had all been written as things that happen rather than as things somebody does.

The rewritten process is barely longer and reads, to several people in the firm, as pedantic. Each step now names a role and states the assertion in the first person: I have compared the schedule with the source and the figures agree; I have read the schedule and the figures are consistent with what I know of this client; I have not read the schedule and rely on the client manager. The third of those sentences is the one that caused an argument, and it is the only one that was true before the change.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: ellipsis, and what must never be left out**

- Ellipsis omits what the reader can recover: I have read it; he has not (read it).
- It is correct and economical where the omitted element is unambiguous.
- The passive is a kind of ellipsis: it was confirmed omits the person entirely.
- In any record of responsibility, never omit the actor, even at the cost of elegance.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A process document that names no actor records a sequence, not a responsibility**

**Figure 1.5** — Four correct steps and one unasserted fact, which is the one that mattered.

Where work is produced partly by systems and confirmed partly by people, the question that decides every subsequent dispute is which named individual asserted which specific fact. This is not the same as who performed which step, and most process documents answer only the second question, with the result that an organisation can execute a process perfectly and discover afterwards that a central fact was never asserted by anybody at all.

The grammatical cause of this is worth stating because it is so easily remedied. Process documents are written in the passive and the nominal: the schedule is confirmed, review takes place, the filing is released. Each of those constructions omits the actor, which reads as neutrality and functions as a gap. A reader cannot tell from such a sentence whether one person, four people or nobody is asserting the thing, and nor, it turns out, can the people performing the steps.

The remedy is to write each step as a first-person assertion attached to a role: I have compared the schedule with the source and the figures agree; I have read the schedule and it is consistent with what I know of this client. Converting a process into a list of such sentences takes a morning and reliably exposes one or two facts that everybody assumed somebody was asserting and nobody was.

Two of those sentences will be uncomfortable. The first is the admission that somebody signs without reading, which is extremely common, usually defensible, and almost never written down. The second is the admission that a system’s output is relied upon without independent verification in a particular class of case. Both are defensible as reliance and indefensible as concealment, and the difference lies entirely in whether a responsible body approved the reliance in advance.

The practical test for any such record is to ask what a stranger reading it long afterwards, in a dispute, would be able to establish. They should be able to say, for every material fact, which role asserted it, on what basis, and on what date. Where the answer for some fact is that nobody asserted it, that is the finding, and it is better reached in a drafting session than in a deposition.

> **DATA POINT · Accountability in automated processes** — Studies of accountability in automated or semi-automated decision processes generally find diffusion of responsibility, with participants each believing a different party verified the critical input.  
> Source: general organisational-accountability literature, 2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Judgement under responsibility: who answers for it

**Figure 1.7** — Four steps either way. Only the right-hand column can be asked a question.

Rewrite your critical processes in the first person, naming the role that answers for each step, and do it before anything goes wrong rather than afterwards. The sentence I have compared the schedule with the source and the figures agree can be interrogated; the schedule is confirmed cannot, because there is nobody in it. The exercise takes a morning and its output is a document that a stranger could use in a dispute.

Then list the material facts separately from the steps, and put a role against each one. This is the part that produces the finding, because performing every step correctly is entirely compatible with some central fact having been asserted by nobody, each participant having reasonably assumed that a different participant was asserting it. Any fact with no role against it is a hole, and holes are cheap to fix in a drafting session.

Finally, write down the reliances, including the awkward ones. Somebody signs without reading; some class of system output is accepted without independent check. Both of these are usually reasonable and both become indefensible the moment they are concealed, because the question in any dispute is not whether you relied but whether the reliance was known and approved. An approved reliance is a policy. An unrecorded one is, in the only sense that matters later, a misrepresentation.

*Say it like this*
- Name the asserter: **Which role asserts that this figure is right?**
- Find the hole: **Which material fact has no role against it?**
- State the reliance: **I have not read it and rely on the client manager.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A chain of responsibility establishes who asserted what. It does not make the assertions true, it cannot prevent an error in a source everybody checked against, and it tells you nothing about whether the person asserting a fact had the time or knowledge to assert it properly. It makes failures attributable, not less frequent.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**What you must be able to show afterwards is rarely what you thought to keep**

**Figure 2.5** — Four rows of evidence about the answer, and none about the question.

Records policies are written against the questions of the period in which they were drafted, and they age in a specific way: they continue to describe correctly everything that existed when they were written. A schedule listing nine classes of document, each of them something a person made or received, remains a perfectly good schedule for a firm where that is all there is, and becomes silently incomplete the moment work begins passing through a stage that produces material belonging to no person.

The question that exposes the gap is not whether the output was correct. It is how much of the output was the system’s and how much was the firm’s, and it is asked by advisers, auditors and regulators because the answer bears on whose judgement was exercised. Answering it requires three things to have been kept: what the system was given, what it returned before anybody touched it, and what a person then did. Most organisations keep only the last, fused into the finished document.

Reconstruction after the fact is possible and is worth much less than it costs. An account assembled from logs and recollections may be entirely accurate and will read as assembled, because it was; and a true answer delivered in that form persuades considerably less than the same answer produced from contemporaneous material. Rarely is the gap between the cost of keeping something and the cost of reconstructing it so wide as it is here, where storage is measured in euros a month.

Keeping everything, however, is not the conclusion, and the reason is disclosure rather than storage. Material held is material that must be produced when somebody is entitled to it, searched at your expense and handed over whether it assists you or not. The sound position is to decide deliberately: keep what you would want to be able to show, for as long as somebody could reasonably ask, which for most professional work means the limitation period.

Where that formulation runs out is at the boundary between a record and a thought. A reviewer’s half-formed working notes are not a record of a decision, and six years of them would create precisely the disclosure burden the policy exists to control. Organisations that have thought about this carefully tend to draw the line at material a process asked somebody to produce, and to say openly that the line is a judgement rather than a principle.

> **DATA POINT · Records and reconstructed accounts** — Dispute-resolution practice generally holds contemporaneous records substantially more persuasive than accounts reconstructed afterwards, and retention policies are commonly found not to cover machine-generated intermediate material.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Whether it is safe and whether you may are different questions, and only one is usually asked**

**Figure 3.5** — The eight were not the problem; the nineteen the firm had already breached were.

Decisions about sending data to a supplier are routinely made as security decisions, because security is the question that has an expert attached to it and a certificate at the end. While that question matters, it is the second question. The first is whether you are permitted to send the material at all, and permission comes from contracts, statute and the undertakings you have given, none of which are improved by encryption.

The permission question is tedious to answer and that is the only reason it is skipped. It requires somebody to read every confidentiality clause the organisation has signed, in one sitting, and to classify them: those permitting disclosure to subcontractors performing the work, those permitting it on notice, and those forbidding transfer outside a jurisdiction in terms admitting no exception. A firm of any age will find all three, and will find that the categories do not correspond to the size or sophistication of the client.

What such a review almost always also finds is the uncomfortable part, which is that systems already in use are already sending material somewhere nobody has established. The question where is this held has no natural owner: the people who chose the system asked about security, the people who signed the contracts never saw the system, and the supplier answers truthfully in terms that mention two jurisdictions in a footnote.

Granted that no harm has occurred and granted that the supplier is careful, a firm in this position is nonetheless in breach, and the decision in front of it is about disclosure rather than about repair. The repair is straightforward. The disclosure is where the real argument happens, and it has a shape worth recognising: the probability of discovery is genuinely low and the cost of disclosure is genuinely certain, which is an argument for silence that is correct on both of its premises.

The answer to it is not about probability. A firm that discloses is a firm that found a breach and reported it; a firm that does not is a firm that knows and is concealing, and it must renew that decision every year in front of people who joined after it was taken. Even if disclosure costs a client, which it sometimes does, it is paid once; the alternative is an obligation to keep choosing concealment indefinitely, and that obligation is the thing that eventually fails.

> **DATA POINT · Disclosure of self-discovered breaches** — Regulatory practice in most jurisdictions treats self-reported breaches substantially more leniently than those discovered externally, and concealment after discovery is commonly treated as an aggravating factor in itself.  
> Source: general regulatory practice, 2026. Regimes differ considerably.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Bias usually enters through the record, not through the rule**

**Figure 4.5** — The unexplained gap between 1.3 and 4 is the whole of the finding.

Discussions of bias in automated decisions tend to begin by looking for a prejudice in the rule, and in professional settings that search is usually fruitless and always insufficient. A rule built from an organisation’s own records inherits every systematic feature of those records, including the organisation’s past failures, its past refusals, and the groups it has simply never dealt with. Nobody has to hold a view for a disparity to appear.

The commonest mechanism is a selection effect in which the record contains few cases of a kind and those few went badly, usually because the organisation was not then equipped to handle them. A rule learning from that record reports, accurately, that such cases go badly here. It cannot know that the processes responsible were replaced four years later, because a record of outcomes contains no account of why the outcomes occurred.

This mechanism has a second property that makes it stable, and it is the one that matters most for management. Every decline prevents the acquisition of the case that would have corrected the record. A disparity of this kind does not decay; it is maintained by its own operation, and the absence of counter-evidence is produced by the very decisions the missing evidence would overturn.

The diagnostic sequence is short and worth running on any rule derived from historical data. First, measure the outcome rate by group and compare it with the rate the known causes would predict; the gap is the finding. Second, count the cases of that group in the record. Third, read what happened to them and why. Fourth, ask whether the cause still exists. Most of the work is in the third step and it requires reading files, not running analyses.

The remedy, where intention is absent, is unsatisfying and should be stated as such. There is nothing to remove; there is only evidence to generate, which means deliberately serving the cases the rule declines, recording the rule’s output without acting on it, and waiting until enough cases exist to retest. That wait is usually measured in years, and the temptation to write a shorter review date should be resisted in the document itself, because a date nobody believes is worse than a date nobody likes.

> **DATA POINT · Historical data and disparate outcomes** — Research on statistical decision systems consistently finds that disparities more often originate in the training record than in the model, and that feedback loops can maintain a disparity indefinitely by suppressing counter-evidence.  
> Source: general literature on algorithmic fairness, 2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A red line that rests on capability is an estimate; the reason has to be about answerability**

**Figure 5.5** — Six of the seven, each with an external party who is owed an answer by a person.

Most organisations have decisions that are not automated, and in most organisations the reason is that nobody has got round to it. That is an absence rather than a prohibition, and absences close quietly: a project arrives with a deadline, proposes to include one of them, nobody can point to a rule, and what should have been a question of principle becomes a question of scheduling. Writing the list down is the whole of the difference.

The temptation in writing it is to justify each item by what systems do badly, and that temptation should be resisted, because such a justification is an estimate with a shelf life. A rule that says we do not automate this because it would be done badly expires the moment somebody demonstrates otherwise, and the demonstration will be offered by whoever is selling the capability. There is a case for making capability assessments; there is no case for building permanent rules on them.

The durable reason is answerability. For some decisions, a named person must be able to explain themselves to somebody outside the organisation: the client who was terminated, the employee whose job changed, the complainant who named a colleague, the regulator who was told of a breach. Answerability is not a property a process can hold, however well it performs, and that reason does not change when performance does.

Stating the reason this way makes the list both stronger and considerably narrower, which is the point most often missed. It gives no grounds for keeping a system out of the drafting, the analysis, the preparation or the recommendation; it requires only that a named person decide, be able to explain, and bear the consequence. A firm that confuses the two ends up defending manual work it has no reason to defend, and loses the argument about the work it does.

It remains to be seen whether such lists hold under commercial pressure, and the honest answer is that nobody has had one long enough to say. What can be said is that a written prohibition must be argued against in writing, which converts a scheduling decision into a board decision, and that the people most likely to defend a list are sometimes those who voted against having one, because a rule they opposed is still a rule they are now administering.

> **DATA POINT · Reserved decisions in regulated firms** — Governance research generally finds that organisations with explicitly reserved decisions, stated in advance, depart from them less often under pressure than those relying on custom, and that justifications based on current capability are revised more frequently.  
> Source: general corporate-governance literature, 2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A skill that is never used is not retained, and nobody finds out by waiting**

**Figure 6.5** — Four rows worse and one better, and the better row was not being tested for.

An organisation cannot tell whether a skill has been retained by reasoning about it, and the ordinary measures are silent, because output quality stays high for as long as the system producing it stays available. The only way to establish the position is to arrange for the skill to be needed, which means a drill: a day on which the system is switched off and a known set of work is done by hand against a known standard.

Such an exercise reliably produces three kinds of finding, only one of which is the one it was designed for. The first is the expected decline, usually smaller than the pessimists feared and nowhere near the standard the system achieves. The second is a collapse in some area nobody had on the risk list, typically a convention the system has handled silently and correctly for years, which was never a decision anybody made and is therefore written down nowhere.

The third kind of finding is the most valuable and the easiest to lose in the reporting. In some respect the people will be better than the system, usually because they stop. A system given internally inconsistent input resolves it by its ordinary rule and does not record that it had to choose; a person stops, writes a note and asks. Switching the system off is frequently the only way an organisation discovers that this has been happening.

The limitation of the method is severe and should be stated in the report rather than discovered by a reader. An announced exercise with reference material available and no client waiting is a best case, and the circumstance that matters is the unannounced morning with a deadline. Making the exercise realistic requires either deceiving colleagues or risking real work, and an organisation that resolves this by inserting unmarked cases into the real flow will find that the first disclosure converts every future drill into a test.

The practical position is therefore to run announced drills, report them explicitly as best cases in those words, measure the same tasks each time so that the direction of travel is visible even if the absolute level is flattering, and treat the second and third kinds of finding as the main product. A drill that merely confirms people are slower has been run badly; a drill that tells you what the system has been deciding silently has paid for itself.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An explanation must describe what produced the decision, including the part you did not produce**

**Figure 7.5** — Only the third row describes the decision; the fourth and fifth belong with it.

When a decision is taken by a person with a system’s analysis in front of them, the explanation owed to an outside party is harder to write than either pure case, and the two easy formulations are both misleading. Saying the decision followed a review of the evidence is true and invites the reader to believe the decision-maker performed the review; saying the analysis recommended it invites the reader to believe nobody exercised judgement at all.

An adequate explanation has four parts and each of them is uncomfortable in a different way. What the analysis said; that the decision-maker read it and whether they recomputed anything; which factors the decision-maker gave weight to, named specifically; and what they would have decided without it. The fourth part is usually unanswerable, and the honest treatment is to maintain the belief, concede that it cannot be demonstrated, and acknowledge why: the analysis arrived before any view had formed.

The grammar for this is the set of stance reporting verbs, and the choice among them is itself part of the explanation. Maintain places a position as held under challenge; concede reports something against your own interest; acknowledge accepts a point without conceding the argument; claim and contend report what another party says without endorsing it. A document that uses said throughout has discarded exactly the information a reader is looking for.

A separate and sharper problem arises from where the analysis is run. In many organisations an analysis of this kind is produced only when somebody has already queried a case, which means every analysis in existence concerns a case about which a person already had a doubt. Its apparent agreement rate with those doubts is then uninterpretable: a high rate is equally consistent with a good analysis and with a worthless analysis attached to usually correct doubts.

The remedy is to run it on the cases nobody queried and to report the disagreements separately, since those are the only informative cases. This is usually cheap and almost never done, because the question is not formulated until somebody outside the organisation asks it, and the specification that limits the analysis to queried cases was written by somebody answering a request rather than designing a test.

> **DATA POINT · Explanation of assisted decisions** — Work on accountability for assisted decisions generally finds that explanations omit either the system’s contribution or the human’s, and that selective triggering of an analysis makes its observed agreement rate uninterpretable.  
> Source: general literature on decision support, 2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Tolerance for an outage is a window, not a duration, and almost no plan says so**

**Figure 8.5** — One number in the plan, four in reality, and the smallest is the one that matters.

An outage is almost never costly in proportion to its length, and organisations that measure their exposure in hours of downtime are measuring the wrong quantity. What determines the cost is what was in progress, which depends on the date, the stage of a cycle, and which external deadlines fall inside the period. The same nineteen hours can cost one telephone call or force a choice between missing a statutory deadline and performing work without the checks you contracted to perform.

The useful exercise is therefore counterfactual and should be conducted in writing, because the figures are what make it an argument rather than an anxiety. Had the outage begun on the Monday of the previous week, how many items would have been in progress, how many due, and what would the choice have been? Had it lasted forty hours rather than nineteen, in which weeks would that choice have arisen? These questions take an afternoon and produce a table with four numbers in it.

What such a table shows is a tolerance window rather than a tolerance period, and the difference changes what can be done about it. A duration can only be addressed by reducing recovery time, which is usually expensive and often a supplier’s decision. A window can be addressed by moving work: spreading a filing schedule across the month, freezing non-urgent changes in the narrow period, and deciding in advance which way the unavoidable choice would go.

Continuity plans routinely miss this, and the reason is in how they were commissioned. Somebody asked to write a continuity plan produces an excellent document listing systems, recovery commitments and restoration order. Somebody asked what the organisation cannot do produces a different document, usually shorter, containing one sentence that nobody has written down before. The second question is the one worth asking and it is rarely the one put.

Finally, a plan that has never been exercised is a hypothesis. Running deliberately in degraded mode for a stated number of hours each quarter is the only way to establish that the fallback works, and it carries an awkwardness worth naming openly: exercised in the safe part of the window it least resembles the emergency, and exercised in the narrow part the exercise is itself a risk. There is no formulation that escapes this, and the honest course is to put both sentences in front of whoever decides.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Where the effort of the approach is part of the message, a cheaper channel sends a different message**

**Figure 9.5** — Identical on the measures a process can see, and opposite on the one it cannot.

A great many commercial exchanges are improved by being faster, cheaper and more consistent, and a small class is destroyed by exactly those improvements. The class is not defined by difficulty or by importance, both of which are poor guides, but by whether the effort of making the approach forms part of what is being communicated. Where it does, reducing the effort does not produce a cheaper version of the message; it produces a different message.

Credit control supplies the clearest example because the ordinary process is genuinely good. A system notices an unusual payment pattern on the nineteenth day, drafts a letter that is courteous, accurate and correctly timed, and refers appropriately to a long relationship. That letter will often be answered within a day, courteously and uninformatively, because the thing the sender actually needs to know is embarrassing and embarrassing facts are not disclosed to a correctly formatted enquiry.

What obtains such a fact is a demonstration that somebody has incurred a cost in order to hear it. Four hours and the price of a train ticket is not overhead attached to the message; it is the part of the message that cannot be written. We regret to note that the account remains outstanding conveys information accurately. I came because I was worried conveys that somebody travelled, and the recipient reads both the words and the cost.

The test needs a second limb or it licenses unlimited expense, and the second limb is that the effort must be unusual for that relationship. A visit to a client seen four times in nine years is a departure and is read as one; a visit to a client seen monthly communicates nothing beyond its content. Unusual effort, and effort that forms part of the message: both conditions, and the class is then small enough to be written into guidance.

The honest limits of the argument should be stated with it. Nobody can demonstrate that a telephone call would have failed where a visit succeeded, and a manager who claims the necessity of presence is making an unfalsifiable claim that happens also to be pleasant to act on. The most that can be said is that the class exists, that it is narrow, and that an organisation which cannot name its members has probably automated something it should not have.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Everything that can be written down eventually is; what is left is where somebody has paid**

**Figure 10.5** — Three items moved out of one person in three years; three did not move at all.

A manager asked what part of a business is theirs will usually name three things: their judgement in some domain, their knowledge of particular customers, and the standard of work the organisation holds to. In a firm that has done the work described in this series, all three answers are wrong, and the fact that they are wrong is the measure of the work having succeeded. The judgement is in a rule derived from past decisions; the knowledge is in a library and a set of records; the standard is in a handbook somebody else maintains better.

This is not a diminishment, although it is experienced as one. An organisation in which the senior person’s judgement exists only in that person’s head has a ceiling, a single point of failure and no way of training anybody, and the correct response to discovering that your judgement has been written down is relief. The uncomfortable part is that the question then has to be asked again, and the second answer is shorter.

What tends to survive is of one kind. It is the record of occasions on which the person accepted a cost for something they believed, together with the consequences of having done so: people bring difficult and embarrassing matters to somebody who has demonstrably paid for a principle, and that disposition cannot be delegated because it is not a capability but a history. A rule can reproduce what a judgement did; nothing can reproduce having borne a loss.

Such a history is dangerous without a third element, and the third element is usually omitted from accounts of this kind because it sounds like modesty. A person with convictions and no appetite for being told they are wrong is the worst thing a mid-sized firm can have at the top of it, and the arrangements they will build are indistinguishable from good ones until the first occasion on which the conviction is mistaken. Expecting correction, and arranging for it, is what makes the rest safe.

The practical conclusion is uncomfortable and small. Write down the occasions, with what each cost and whether you were certain, and keep the page. A principle nobody can point to is indistinguishable, to everybody but its holder, from a mood; and a principle nobody has ever paid for is indistinguishable from a preference. The page is not a policy and does not need reviewing. It is evidence, and it is the only part of the business that is you.
