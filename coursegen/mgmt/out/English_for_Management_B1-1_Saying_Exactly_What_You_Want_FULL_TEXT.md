### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: imperatives and infinitives of purpose**

- An instruction is the bare verb, with no subject: Send the list. Check the dates.
- Say why with to + verb: Send the list to show what is still outstanding.
- The negative is Do not + verb. For a standing rule, use Never + verb.
- An instruction with no purpose can be obeyed exactly and still be wrong.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Four things, and almost everything is one of them**

**Figure 1.5** — Four operations. Most office jobs are one of them, or a short chain.

People who are new to these systems usually ask what they can do, which is the wrong question. It is too big to answer. A better question is narrower: what kind of operation is this piece of work? Almost all routine office work turns out to be one of four things, or a short chain of them, and naming the operation first makes the instruction easier to write and much easier to check.

The first is to generate. You have nothing, and you want something: a first draft of a letter, ten subject lines, a list of questions to ask a new supplier. Generation is cheap and fast, and it is the operation people notice first. It is also the one where the output is hardest to check, because there was nothing there before to compare it with.

The second is to transform. You have something already, and you want it in another form. Notes become a letter. A recorded call becomes a summary. A long report becomes six bullet points. Transformation is where most of the quiet value sits in a small company, because the input exists, so the output can be checked against it.

The third is to extract. A document contains facts you need, and you want them pulled out and put in order: the amount, the date, the account number, the name of the person who signed. The fourth is to classify, which is to decide which group a thing belongs to. Is this an invoice or a receipt? Urgent or not? A complaint or a question?

The reason this matters is practical. Extraction and classification can be checked almost mechanically, because there is a right answer sitting in the source document. Generation cannot. So a company that starts with extraction and classification gets reliable value quickly, and a company that starts by generating a great deal of text often gets volume it then has to read.

> **DATA POINT · Where small firms start** — Surveys of small-business adoption generally report document handling and routine correspondence as the first uses, ahead of analysis or decision support.  
> Source: general small-business technology adoption research, 2026. Varies widely by sector and by country.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Naming the operation before you ask

**Figure 1.7** — Name the operation first. It tells you immediately how hard the checking will be.

Before you write a brief, say out loud which of the four operations you are asking for. It takes two seconds and it changes the instruction. If you are asking it to extract, you name the source document and the fields. If you are asking it to classify, you name the groups and what to do with anything that fits none of them. If you are asking it to generate, you name the standard the output has to meet, because nothing else will.

Most failed instructions are failed because two operations were hidden inside one word. Sort out the invoices is really classify them, then extract the amounts, then generate a letter for each. Three operations, three sets of criteria, three places to go wrong. Written as one word, there is nowhere to put the criteria at all.

When you split the job into its operations, something useful happens to the cost of mistakes. You find out which step failed. A letter that chased the wrong client is not a writing failure; it is a classification failure, and classification is the cheapest of the four to fix. You cannot repair a step you have not named.

*Say it like this*
- Name the operation: **I need you to classify these, not summarise them.**
- Name the source: **Extract the amount and the due date from the 14 March export.**
- Name the standard: **Generate a draft that a client could read without editing.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Naming the operation does not make the output correct. It tells you how the output can be checked, and nothing more. A classification can be named perfectly and still be wrong on every row, and no amount of precise wording will tell you that. Only looking at the rows will.

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: word order and the placement of only, also, even · AI: transforming what you already have*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is the difference between writing something and rewriting something?
- Where does the sentence "I only asked for the figures" put its limit?
- What records does your workplace keep that nobody can read quickly?

Fatima Zahra takes about eleven client calls on a Monday, and she writes notes while she is talking. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The quiet value is in changing the shape, not making something new**

**Figure 2.5** — Four transformations and one generation. Notice which row cannot be checked.

If you ask people which uses of these systems have been most valuable to them, the answers that sound impressive are usually about writing something new, and the answers that are actually worth money are usually about changing the shape of something that already existed. Notes become a letter. A long contract becomes a summary with the obligations pulled to the front. A recorded conversation becomes minutes with a list of actions at the bottom.

The reason transformation is worth more than it sounds is that the checking problem is already solved. When you generate something new, you can judge whether you like it, but you cannot compare it with anything. When you transform a source document, every claim in the output has an address in the input, and anybody can go and look.

That gives a small company something it rarely gets, which is a cheap and honest quality test. Take the finished letter and the notes it came from, and read them side by side. Anything in the letter that is not in the notes is either an error or an addition, and both of those are worth knowing about before a client sees them.

The failures in transformation work are nearly always additions rather than mistakes. The output is fluent, the facts are mostly right, and somewhere in the third paragraph a sentence has appeared that nobody said. It is usually reasonable. It is usually the kind of thing the client probably does think. It is also not in the source, and if it later matters, that is the sentence that will matter.

So the useful instruction for transformation names three things rather than one: the source, the finished form, and the limit. Turn these notes into a client letter. Use only what is in the notes. Where the notes are unclear, write a question instead of a guess. That third sentence is the one people leave out, and it is the one that does the work.

> **DATA POINT · What people use it for** — Workplace surveys consistently place summarising, rewriting and drafting from existing material above analysis or decision-making in reported frequency of use.  
> Source: general workplace technology surveys, 2026. Self-reported, and varies by role.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Transforming what you already have

**Figure 2.7** — The right-hand column takes about twenty seconds longer to write.

Transformation is the operation most worth learning first, because the input already exists and so the output can be checked by anybody with two documents and ten minutes. The whole skill is in writing an instruction that names the source precisely, names the finished form precisely, and then says the thing almost nobody says, which is what must not appear in the output that was not in the input.

Give the source a name and a date. The notes is not a source; the call notes dated 3 March is a source. Give the finished form a shape and a length. A letter is not a shape; a one-page client letter using template 4 is a shape. These sound pedantic until the day you have to work out which of three versions came back.

Then handle the unclear case on purpose. Any real source has gaps in it, and the question is not whether the gap will be filled but by what. Left alone, a gap is filled with something plausible. Told to flag it, the gap comes back as a question, and a question is something a person can answer in four seconds.

*Say it like this*
- Name the source: **Turn the call notes dated 3 March into a client letter.**
- State the limit: **Use only what is in the notes. Add nothing that is not there.**
- Handle the gap: **Where the notes are unclear, write a question, not a guess.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Transformation does not verify the source. If the call notes are wrong, the letter will be wrong in exactly the same way, written beautifully, and the error will now look official. Checking the output against the input tells you nothing about whether the input was true.

## Unit 3 · How Many, How Much

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: quantifiers: all, most, some, each, every, any · AI: drafting at volume, to one standard*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- If somebody says "most of them", how many is that?
- What is the difference between doing something twice and doing it four hundred times?
- Where in your work does the same document get written again and again?

In its first six months Arclight sent about three hundred client letters, and every one of them was written by either Dalia or Fatima. By March of the second year it could produce four hundred letters before lunch. The interesting question was never whether it could produce them, because that answer was obvious within a week. The interesting question was whether all four hundred would be the same letter.

They were not, at first. Some used the client’s first name and some used the surname. Most closed with "kind regards" and a few closed with "best". Every letter was correct and polite, and together they read like eleven different companies writing on the same morning, which is roughly what a reader concludes when the signature changes shape.

What fixed it was not better instructions about tone. It was writing down the standard, in a single page, with the decisions already made. Surname unless the client has written to you using their first name. Kind regards always. Amount, due date and reference number in the first two lines, never later.

The page took Fatima an afternoon and it is the most valuable document Arclight owns. Before it existed, each letter needed a judgement, and judgements made four hundred times by anything at all will not come out the same way. After it existed, the judgements had been made once, in advance, by somebody who knew the clients.

Dalia describes this as the real lesson of working at volume. Volume does not create quality problems; it exposes the ones you already had and were absorbing by hand. If three people each write letters slightly differently, nobody notices at thirty letters a month. At four hundred a morning, the difference is the first thing a client sees.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Volume does not create the problem; it stops you absorbing it**

**Figure 3.5** — The same work at two volumes. Only one column really changed.

There is a pattern that repeats in almost every small company that starts producing work in bulk, and it catches people by surprise because it looks like a new problem when it is really an old one becoming visible. The quality of the individual item does not usually fall. What falls away is the quiet correction that a human being was performing without ever describing it as work.

When Fatima wrote thirty letters a month, she made perhaps fifteen small decisions in each one: whether to use a first name, how much detail to give about a disputed item, whether to mention the holiday. She did not experience these as decisions. She experienced them as writing a letter. The decisions were real, they were consistent, and they were invisible even to her.

Multiply the work by a hundred and those fifteen decisions have to come from somewhere else. If they are not written down, they will be made anyway, differently each time, by whatever is producing the letters. Nothing has gone wrong. Nobody has made an error. The output is simply no longer the product of one consistent judgement, and readers notice inconsistency long before they notice quality.

This is why writing the standard down is not administrative tidiness but the actual work of scaling. A standard is a set of decisions made once, in advance, by the person who knows most, so that they do not have to be remade badly four hundred times by somebody or something that knows less. The document is cheap. Discovering what belongs in it is not.

The test of a standard is whether somebody who has never met the clients can apply it and produce something you would sign. That test is harsh, and most first drafts of a house style fail it in about four places. Those four places are exactly the judgements the author did not know they were making, which is why they are worth finding.

> **DATA POINT · Consistency and trust** — Research into business communication generally finds that perceived inconsistency between a company’s messages reduces trust, independently of the accuracy of each message.  
> Source: general business-communication research, 2026. Effect size varies by context and sector.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Drafting at volume, to one standard

**Figure 3.7** — Five decisions. Written down, they take an afternoon; left open, they take forever.

Drafting at volume is the first thing most people try and the first thing most people get wrong, because the obvious instruction is about the output and the useful instruction is about the standard. Write four hundred reminder letters produces four hundred reasonable letters that disagree with each other. Write four hundred letters to this one-page standard produces something a company can put its name on.

The work, therefore, is nearly all in advance. Before you multiply anything, write down the decisions that are currently being made silently by whoever does the job. Not the obvious ones. The ones that feel too small to mention: how to address somebody you have met once, what to do when the amount is under five euros, whether to mention an item that is in dispute.

There is a useful trick for finding them. Take ten pieces of work the best person has already done, put them side by side, and look for every place where they are consistent with each other and the instruction never required them to be. Each one of those is an undocumented decision, and each one of them is about to become a coin toss.

*Say it like this*
- Name the standard: **Write to house style version 2, not to your own judgement.**
- Put a cap on it: **Keep every letter under 180 words.**
- Decide in advance: **Surname unless the client has written to you using a first name.**

It applies whatever rule it finds, including no rule, four hundred times without tiring and without noticing. It will not tell you that your house style contradicts itself, and it cannot invent the decision you forgot to make.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The facts are already there; what is missing is the shape**

**Figure 4.5** — Extraction is easy. Agreeing what counts as the total is the work.

Most of the documents a small company handles are unstructured, which is a technical way of saying that the information in them is not labelled. An invoice contains a total, but nothing in the file says this is the total; a human being knows it because of where it sits, what word is next to it, and what the other numbers add up to. Extraction is the work of turning that into something a machine can hold.

The useful thing about extraction, compared with almost everything else in this book, is that it has right answers. The total on an invoice is a fact. Either the figure pulled out matches the figure on the page or it does not, and checking is a matter of looking. That makes extraction the cheapest place for a small company to start, and the easiest place to prove to a doubtful colleague that the work is real.

The difficulty is never the pulling out. The difficulty is in the four or five cases a month where the document does not behave, and those cases are where the value of the conversation lies. Two dates on one invoice. A credit note that looks exactly like an invoice except for a minus sign. An amount in a currency nobody expected. A tax line that is missing because the supplier is not registered for tax.

Every one of those has always been decided by somebody, usually without noticing. The person who does the job has a rule, the rule is consistent, and the rule has never been said out loud. Extraction work forces it into the open, and that is most of why the first conversation takes forty minutes rather than four.

This is also why the flag column matters more than the six data columns. A run that returns sixty rows and no flags is telling you that nothing was difficult, which is usually false. A run that returns sixty rows and seven flags is telling you where to look, and seven things to look at on a Sunday evening is not a job at all.

> **DATA POINT · Where the time goes** — Studies of document-handling work in small firms typically find that exception cases, though a small share of volume, account for a disproportionate share of the time spent.  
> Source: general document-process research, 2026. Proportions differ widely by document type.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Extracting structure from unstructured documents

**Figure 4.7** — The person does not disappear. The person moves to the seven cases that need them.

An extraction brief is a list, and the discipline is to write the list out in full rather than gesturing at it. The usual details is not a list. Supplier name, invoice number, invoice date, net amount, tax amount, total is a list, and it takes eleven seconds longer to write. Every field you leave off the list is a field somebody will have to add by hand later, one row at a time.

Then write the awkward cases down, because they are the whole of the difficulty and they are already decided. Ask the person who does the job what they do when the document is strange. They will say it is obvious, and then they will describe a rule they have been applying consistently for three years without ever saying it aloud. Write that sentence down exactly as they say it.

- Use who for people, which for things, and that for either.
- Commas change the meaning: the clients, who pay late means all of them do.
- In a brief, a defining clause is how you draw the edge of a set.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The pile you cannot define is the one worth reading**

**Figure 5.5** — Six rules, one default, and a last row that nobody expected to matter.

Classification is the operation most worth automating and the one most often done badly, and the reason is almost always the same: the categories were written by somebody describing the work they remember rather than the work that actually arrives. Six categories feel complete when you write them on a Tuesday afternoon. They are never complete, because the world does not know they exist.

The fix is not more categories. More categories make the rules harder to apply and do not solve the problem, because the next unexpected thing is unexpected in a new way. The fix is a default: one named place where everything that matches no rule goes, and a person who reads it. Without a default, items that fit nothing get forced into the nearest category, and that is how a cancellation becomes a delivery query.

What surprises people is how large the default turns out to be and how useful. In a healthy operation it is rarely under five per cent, and the contents are not junk. They are the new things: a product nobody has had to explain before, a question four customers have suddenly started asking, a word a supplier has started using differently.

There is a good argument, which not everybody accepts, that the default should be kept deliberately wide. A narrow default is efficient and silent. A wide one costs somebody twenty minutes a day and is the only part of the system that tells you something you did not already know. Which of those two you want depends on whether your business is stable, and most small businesses are not.

What classification cannot do is tell you that your categories are the wrong categories. It will sort faithfully into whatever boxes it is given, including boxes that stopped describing the business eighteen months ago. That judgement stays with whoever reads the default, which is the real reason the default needs a reader and not just a name.

> **DATA POINT · The size of the default** — Operational studies of inbound classification generally report unmatched or "other" categories in the range of several per cent, rising sharply when a product or process changes.  
> Source: general operations research, 2026. Highly dependent on how rules are written.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Classifying and routing: which pile does this go in

**Figure 5.7** — Every category in a rule set should be in the right-hand column.

Classification is the operation where grammar does the most visible work, because a category is a set and a set is defined by a clause. The urgent messages is not a category; it is an opinion. Messages which mention cancelling, a legal threat or the word today is a category, and two different people reading it will draw the same line.

Write the rules in a fixed order and say so, because sooner or later an item will match two of them. A message saying I am cancelling because my invoice is wrong belongs in Urgent and in Invoice copy, and if nothing says which wins, it will go into whichever rule happened to run first, which may change next month for reasons nobody can see.

Then name the default and give it an owner. Unsorted with nobody reading it is a bin; Unsorted that Thomas reads at half past eight is an early warning system. The difference between those two is one sentence in the brief, and it is the sentence that decides whether the whole arrangement teaches you anything.

*Say it like this*
- Define, do not describe: **Urgent means any message which mentions cancelling or a legal threat.**
- Fix the order: **Apply these six rules in order; the first match wins.**
- Own the default: **Anything matching no rule goes to Unsorted, which Thomas reads at 08:30.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Classification does not know that your categories are out of date. It will sort faithfully into boxes that stopped describing the business a year ago, and it will never report that nothing fits well any more. Only a person reading the default will notice that, and only if somebody is actually reading it.

Jonas asked a simple question one Thursday: produce a one-page summary of the position for the meeting. Everybody in the room knew what he meant. The position means the cash position, because that is the only position anybody at Arclight discusses on a Thursday, and the meeting means the Friday client review, because there is only one meeting with a capital letter in it.

Every word of it was reasonable. Not one word of it was what he wanted, and the fault was entirely in the word "the".

English uses "the" to mean we both already know which one. It is a signal between people who share a history. When Dalia says the ledger, four people know she means the client ledger and not the supplier one, because they have all been in the room for the two years in which that became obvious.

Anybody outside the room is in a different position, and so is any system. It does not have the two years. It has whatever is in front of it, which on that Thursday was one sentence containing three definite articles and no nouns that would mean anything to a stranger.

Dalia now has a rule she applies when she is writing anything that will be read by somebody who was not there. Read it back and stop at every "the". For each one, ask: have I told this reader which one? If not, either tell them, or change it to "a". It takes about twenty seconds and it has saved her more rework than any other habit she has.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**What is in front of it, and nothing else**

**Figure 6.5** — Every row has the same answer in the third column.

The single most common reason a reasonable question produces an unreasonable answer has nothing to do with capability. It is that the question was framed by somebody standing inside a situation, and answered by something standing outside it. The information that would have made the question clear was never in the message, because to the person writing it, that information was not information. It was just how things are.

This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to overcorrect. A system is not ignorant in general. It will often know a great deal about invoices, logistics, contract law and the usual shape of a reminder letter. What it cannot know is anything particular to you: which ledger you mean, what you agreed with Nora in March, that Thursday means cash and Friday means clients.

The practical consequence is that the useful part of a brief is usually the part that feels unnecessary. Nobody enjoys writing down that the client ledger is the one in the shared folder and the supplier ledger is the one Kenji keeps. It feels like explaining something everybody knows. Everybody in the room knows it. The room is not where the work is being done any more.

There is a cheap test for this and it is worth doing once on anything important. Read your own request as though you had joined the company last week. Every time you hit a word that would make you ask a question, you have found a piece of context that has to be either supplied or removed. Most people find between three and six on their first attempt.

The deeper point is that a confident answer tells you nothing about whether the picture was complete. An answer built on a missing phone call sounds exactly like an answer built on everything. There is no tone of voice for I did not know about Monday, which is why checking what was supplied has to happen before the answer arrives, not after.

> **DATA POINT · Where rework comes from** — Studies of knowledge work commonly attribute a substantial share of rework to incomplete or ambiguous initial requests rather than to execution errors.  
> Source: general knowledge-work and project research, 2026. Proportions vary by discipline.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Context: what it can see, and what it cannot

**Figure 6.7** — The left column comes free. The right column is entirely your responsibility.

The most useful mental model is a simple one. Assume that whatever you are asking carries no context about you at all, and has read a great deal about the world in general and has never heard of your company. It knows what an invoice is. It does not know that your invoices come in two folders and only one of them counts. Those two facts are not on the same shelf, and treating them as though they were is where most of the trouble comes from.

In practice this means writing a background line, which feels redundant and is not. One or two sentences, before the instruction, saying what the terms mean in your company and which of several possible things is being referred to. The test of a good background line is that a competent stranger could use it, and the test of a bad one is that it says things only you would think to say.

The second habit is naming your inputs exactly. Not the ledger but the client ledger in the shared folder dated 2 April. Not the usual template but follow-up template version 4. This is tedious to write and it removes an entire category of failure, which is the one where everything works perfectly against the wrong document.

*Say it like this*
- Define the term: **In this company, the cash position means the client account only.**
- Name the file: **Work only from the client ledger dated 2 April, not the supplier one.**
- Supply the history: **This client has been in dispute since February, which is why the account is frozen.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Supplying context does not make the answer correct; it only removes one reason for it to be wrong. A full and accurate background will not stop a confident answer built on a misreading of the data you supplied, and nothing in the reply will tell you which kind of failure you are looking at.

What cannot be checked that way is whether the result sounds like a company somebody would want to work with, and that is a judgement that has to be made by a person who knows the market.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Register is measurable; sounding right is not**

**Figure 7.5** — The second and third columns are what make the first column usable by anybody.

Shifting register is one of the few things in this book that works almost perfectly out of the box, and it is also one of the easiest places to deceive yourself. The mechanical part is genuinely solved: ask for the same content in shorter sentences with simpler vocabulary and you will get it, consistently, and you can check the result by counting. The part that is not solved is knowing which register the reader wanted.

That is why naming registers is worth the afternoon it takes. Formal, Standard, Plain, Direct is not an elegant taxonomy and it does not have to be. What it has to do is let one person say a word to another person and have them both mean the same thing, which is exactly what better fails to do. Attach a measure to each name and the word becomes an instruction rather than a hope.

Translation behaves similarly, with one extra trap. A translated document can be checked against its source by anybody who reads both languages, and that check is real and worth doing. What it cannot tell you is whether the result reads like something written by a company in that market or like something written somewhere else and carried across.

The difference matters commercially, because fluency is persuasive. A text that reads smoothly is trusted more than one that does not, independently of whether it is right. A fluent translation with a wrong register will be read carefully and believed, and the reader will form a view of your company from it that you never intended to offer.

The practical answer is unglamorous. Use the mechanical part for everything, because it is cheap and reliable. Reserve a human reader for anything that will be read by somebody deciding whether to do business with you, and accept that this is slower, costs money and cannot be reduced to a rule. Not every part of this work becomes a procedure.

> **DATA POINT · Fluency and trust** — Research in reading and persuasion consistently finds that texts which are easier to process are judged more credible, independently of their accuracy.  
> Source: general processing-fluency research, 2026. Effect sizes vary with reader expertise.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Translation and shifting register

**Figure 7.7** — The first three boxes are cheap and reliable. The fourth cannot be removed.

Begin by refusing the word better. It is the single most expensive word in this subject, because it feels like an instruction and carries no information at all. Replace it with a named register and a number: Direct, no sentence over fifteen words. Now two people can disagree about whether the output met the brief, which is the whole point of a brief.

Then write the second list, which almost nobody writes. A register change is permission to alter how something is said, and it is not permission to alter what is said. List the facts that must come through unchanged: the amount, the date, the reference, anything that could be read as a commitment. Without that list, the shortest version is always the one that dropped something.

For translation, do the same and then add the honest line. The content can be checked against the source by anybody who reads both languages, so ask for that check and specify it. Whether it sounds like a company worth dealing with is a different question, it requires somebody who sells in that market, and no amount of specification will replace them.

*Say it like this*
- Name the register: **Rewrite this in Direct: no sentence over 15 words.**
- Protect the facts: **The amount, the due date and the reference must appear unchanged.**
- Be honest about the limit: **A speaker who sells in that market must read it before it goes.**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Why the same question does not give the same answer**

**Figure 8.5** — The third column is the only one that decides whether you need to care.

One of the first things that genuinely surprises people is that the same request, made twice, does not produce the same result. This feels like a fault, and in a certain sense it is not: these systems are built to produce one of many acceptable outputs rather than the single correct one, because for most of what they are asked to do there is no single correct one. Ten ways of writing a reminder are all fine.

The difficulty arrives when a piece of work sits in a process. A process is a thing that runs repeatedly and whose output feeds something else, and processes depend on shape. Samuel does not read the reconciliation; he looks at three places on it. If those three places move, the document has stopped being a tool and started being a text that must be read, which takes four minutes and makes him cross.

The practical consequence is that you should specify shape wherever the output has a reader with a habit. Name the columns and their order. Say where the summary goes. Fix the length. This feels fussy, it takes two extra sentences in the brief, and it converts an unpredictable text into a predictable form.

It also has a quieter benefit, which is that a fixed shape makes errors visible. If the total has always been bottom right, a month when it is missing is obvious at a glance. If the layout moves every month, a missing total looks exactly like another layout change, and nobody notices until the quarter is reconciled.

Where variation genuinely does not matter, leave it alone. A one-off letter, a set of options to choose from, a first draft that a person will rewrite anyway: these lose nothing by being different each time, and specifying their shape is effort spent for no return. The skill is telling the two cases apart, and the test is whether anybody has a habit built on the output.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Why the same request gives two different answers

**Figure 8.7** — Everything you do not specify is re-decided every single time.

The behaviour to understand is this: for most requests there is no single correct output, so one acceptable output is produced, and the next time a different acceptable output is produced. Nothing has gone wrong. The variation is not noise in a system that should be deterministic; it is the normal behaviour of a thing asked an under-specified question.

This means you have a choice, and the choice is yours to make rather than something to complain about. If you want the same shape every time, say what the shape is. Columns and their order, sections and their order, length, where the total sits. Two sentences in the brief convert a variable text into a stable form, and the stable form is what a process can be built on.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**There is no tone of voice for "I do not know"**

**Figure 9.5** — The third column is what the whole problem looks like in one table.

When somebody we work with does not know something, we usually find out without either party saying so. They take a moment longer. They say I think, or probably, or you would want to check that. Their answer gets shorter rather than longer. We have spent our whole lives reading these signals and we are good enough at it that we do not notice we are doing it.

None of those signals is produced here, and the reason is worth stating precisely because it is often stated wrongly. It is not that the system is deceiving anybody. It is that generating a fluent, well-formed answer and establishing whether that answer corresponds to reality are separate operations, and the second one is not happening unless something in the process makes it happen.

The consequence is specific rather than general: the failure mode is not that answers are often wrong, because they are often right. It is that the wrong ones are indistinguishable from the right ones at the point of reading. A ten per cent error rate with visible uncertainty is a manageable thing. A two per cent error rate with no visible uncertainty is harder to handle, because there is nowhere to point the attention.

What works in practice is not asking harder questions but changing what you ask for. Do not ask what the notice period is; ask for the clause that states it and where it sits. A quotation can be checked in four seconds by opening the document. An assertion cannot be checked at all without redoing the work.

This has a secondary effect that people report more often than the first. Once answers have to arrive with their sources attached, the cases where no source exists become visible, and those cases turn out to be interesting. The question that has no clause behind it is usually a question about something the contract does not actually address, which is a thing worth knowing a month before it matters.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Why it does not say "I do not know"

**Figure 9.7** — Both columns ask the same four questions. Only one can be verified at a glance.

The habit to build is simple, and it exists because you cannot tell from the answer whether anybody knows anything. It has to be built deliberately, because it runs against how we normally ask things. Stop asking for conclusions and start asking for the evidence a conclusion would rest on. Not what is the notice period, but quote the clause that sets the notice period and tell me where it is. The second question is harder to answer plausibly without actually having the document.

Then make "not addressed" a permitted answer, in writing, in the brief. This matters more than it sounds. If every question must produce an answer, every question will produce one, including the questions whose honest answer is that the contract says nothing about this. Naming the empty answer as acceptable is what makes it available.

Finally, use must rather than should for anything that leaves the building. Should is read as a preference at five o’clock on a Friday, and the moment when somebody is tired and in a hurry is precisely the moment the rule exists for. A rule that is only followed when it is convenient is not a rule; it is a description of what people were going to do anyway.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for evidence: **Quote the clause that sets the notice period, with its number.**
- Permit the empty answer: **Where no clause covers this, return "not addressed".**
- Make it binding: **An answer that does not quote its source does not count.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Requiring a source does not guarantee the source is real. A quotation can be produced that does not appear in the document, as it was in March, and the only thing that establishes otherwise is somebody opening the file. The rule makes checking possible; it does not do the checking.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Three tests, and none of them is about how clever it is**

**Figure 10.5** — The last three rows fail at least two tests each. That is the whole criterion.

The question of which jobs to hand over is usually discussed as though it were a question about capability, and framed that way it has no stable answer, because capability moves and nobody can see how far. A more useful frame asks about consequences instead, and consequences do not move. A mistake that cannot be taken back could not be taken back ten years ago either, and will not be taken back in five.

The first test is reversibility. If the worst case is a draft somebody deletes, the stakes are low whatever the error rate. If the worst case is a document on another company’s books, an email in a client’s inbox or money in somebody else’s account, the error rate has to be judged against a cost you cannot refund.

The second is whether a mistake will be noticed in time, and by whom. This is the test people forget, and it is the one that caught Arclight in the near miss. An error that surfaces inside an hour is a different thing from an identical error that surfaces when a client finds it in June. The question is not whether somebody could notice but whether anybody is actually looking, which is a question about staffing rather than about systems.

The third is who carries the cost. A company is entitled to take risks with its own time and money, and it is in a different position when the cost of being wrong lands on a customer who did not choose it. This is not a legal point, although it sometimes becomes one. It is the difference between a decision you can make alone and one you ought to discuss with the person who will pay for it.

Taken together, the three tests have a quietly useful property: they tell you what to build rather than only what to avoid. A job that fails the second test can often be rescued by a hold and a reviewer. A job that fails the first usually cannot be rescued at all, and belongs on the list until something about the work itself changes.

These take four minutes and settle most arguments, because they move the conversation off capability, where nobody can be proved right, and onto consequences, where everybody is looking at the same facts.

Then write the fallback, and write it about something that actually happens. The useful if-sentence is not the dramatic one. It is the dull one you can predict: if the export is more than two days old, if an account has no email address, if two due dates are recorded, if the total cannot be read. Each of these arrives about once a month, and each of them has already cost somebody something.

Finally, put the word Yet in the heading and mean it. A list of jobs kept with a person should name what would have to change for each one to come off, because otherwise it becomes a monument rather than a decision. In most cases what would have to change is a hold and a reviewer, which is a cost rather than a mystery, and a cost can be discussed.

*Say it like this*
- Run the three tests: **Can it be undone, will anybody see it, and who pays?**
- Name the dull case: **If the export is more than two days old, stop and tell Dalia.**
- Say what would change it: **This comes off the list when there is a two-hour hold somebody watches.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — None of this makes the handed-over work correct. The three tests tell you what a mistake would cost, not how likely it is, and a fallback only covers the case you thought of. A job that passes all three tests can still fail every week, and nothing here will tell you that it is failing.
