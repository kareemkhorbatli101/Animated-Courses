Arclight now employs sixty-one people and works for two hundred and thirty clients, having been a firm of nine when Ines Carvalho and Dalia Rahman started it, and the change that has caused the most trouble is not the headcount but the onboarding volume: four hundred and twelve new client files were opened last year, against thirty-one in the firm’s fourth year of trading.

Every one of those files passes through a step called the format survey, in which somebody opens a sample of the client’s documents, records how each field is laid out, and writes the result into a mapping sheet that the checking work then depends on. It takes a little under thirty-eight minutes when nothing is unusual, and it has been done by hand, by a person, every single time.

Three years ago Dalia proposed standardising the format survey and building something that would read the sample and draft the mapping sheet, leaving a person to confirm it. The proposal was declined, not for any bad reason, but because the same quarter offered a competing project: rebuilding the client-facing portal, which clients could see, complained about, and mentioned in renewal conversations.

What makes the episode instructive rather than merely regrettable is that the portal was a good project and was correctly delivered. Nobody made a mistake of execution. The mistake, insofar as there was one, lay in choosing between two projects on the basis of which one the clients would notice, when one of them was performed four hundred times a year and the other once.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the third conditional**

- If + past perfect, then would have + past participle: a completed, unreal past.
- Use it to state a counterfactual, not to apportion blame; the grammar is neutral.
- Inversion replaces if in formal use: had we built it, we would have recovered the time.
- Could have and might have soften the consequence where the outcome was not certain.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Leverage lives in frequency, and frequency is almost never the thing being discussed**

**Figure 1.5** — No client has ever mentioned any of these six, and together they are twelve per cent of the firm.

The question of where to apply a new capability first is usually answered badly, and it is answered badly for a reason that has nothing to do with technology. Organisations select targets by salience: the thing a customer complains about, the thing a board member has read about, the thing that went wrong most recently and most publicly. Salience and frequency are only weakly correlated, and leverage follows frequency.

The arithmetic is trivial and that is what makes its neglect remarkable. A task that takes forty minutes and is performed four hundred times a year consumes two hundred and sixty hours; a task that takes two days and is performed twice consumes thirty-two. Removing half of the first is worth eight times removing all of the second, and the second is almost always the one that gets discussed, because it is large enough to be visible as an event.

Building the frequency list is therefore the first analytical act, and it is harder than it sounds, because high-volume work tends to be invisible in exactly the way that matters: it is distributed across many people in small pieces, it rarely appears as a line in any system, and nobody owns it in a way that would cause them to raise it. The work is drudgery, drudgery is unmentioned, and what is unmentioned does not get counted.

There is a second-order effect that the list alone will not show, and it is the one that defeats most firms at this point. Accelerating the tasks that feed a review step does not increase the output of the process; it lengthens the queue in front of whoever reviews. Leverage in a production step is only real leverage if the step immediately downstream has the capacity to absorb it, and in professional firms that step is almost always a senior person reading things.

The practical sequence that follows is unglamorous. Count the tasks, multiply by their minutes, rank by the aggregate, and then ask of the top six which of them end on the same desk. Attack the highest-frequency task whose output does not land in a queue, and deal with the queue itself as a separate and more difficult problem, which the eighth unit of this book is about.

> **DATA POINT · Where process improvement is applied** — Operations research on process improvement programmes generally finds that selected targets correlate more strongly with visibility to customers or management than with total time consumed, and that gains are frequently absorbed by an unchanged downstream review step.  
> Source: general operations-management literature, 2026. Figures vary by sector.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Where the leverage is: the task you do four hundred times

**Figure 1.7** — Eight times the leverage in the right-hand column, and none of the attention.

Before you choose anything to build, buy or delegate to a system, count. Write down every task the organisation performs more than a hundred times a year, attach the minutes it takes from real observation rather than from recollection, and rank by the product of the two. The exercise takes a fortnight and it is the single highest-value fortnight available to a manager in this position, because it routinely reorders the list of candidate projects from top to bottom.

Expect the ranking to be unflattering to your instincts. The tasks at the top will be ones nobody has complained about, nobody owns, and nobody has mentioned in a meeting, precisely because they are small enough individually to pass unnoticed and are spread across too many people to appear as anybody’s problem. The task you do four hundred times is invisible for structural reasons, not because the organisation is inattentive.

Then apply the one test that keeps the exercise honest, which is to ask where each task’s output lands. A task whose output arrives in a queue in front of a single reviewer offers no leverage at all until that queue is dealt with, however high it ranks on hours, and accelerating it will produce a visible improvement in one department and no improvement whatever in the firm. Rank by aggregate; choose by aggregate and destination together.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for the count: **How many times a year do we do this, exactly?**
- Ask for the minutes: **From how many observations is that figure drawn?**
- Ask for the destination: **Whose desk does the output of this task reach?**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A frequency ranking tells you where the hours are. It does not tell you which tasks can be done differently, which carry judgement that nothing should be asked to exercise, or which are high-volume only because the process around them is wrong. A task that should not exist does not belong at the top of a list of things to automate.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A falling cost per unit is a margin gift or a revenue cliff, depending entirely on what you bill in**

**Figure 2.5** — Nothing went wrong. The firm improved and was paid less for improving.

When the cost of producing a unit of work falls, the effect on a business depends almost entirely on the unit in which that business invoices, and this is a point that organisations routinely discover late because the two numbers live in different conversations. A fall in production cost is an operations matter; the unit of sale is a commercial matter; and in most firms nobody holds both at once.

Where the unit of sale is the output, a falling cost per unit is a straightforward margin improvement, and the only question is how much of it competition will eventually take. Where the unit of sale is the input, usually time, the same fall is a revenue reduction that arrives disguised as an efficiency gain, because the work gets better and faster while the invoice gets smaller, and everybody involved feels that something good has happened.

The disguise is strengthened considerably by growth. A firm adding clients rapidly will see aggregate revenue rise throughout the period in which revenue per client is falling, and the aggregate is the number that appears on the first page of every report. Only the like-for-like figure, computed on the book that existed before the growth began, shows the underlying movement, and computing it requires somebody to want to know.

The remedy is not a price rise, which treats a structural matter as an arithmetic one. The remedy is a change in the unit of sale, from the input to something that does not shrink: the outcome, the access, the capacity held available, the risk carried. Each of these is a different product with different obligations, and none of them can be sold honestly before it has been built, which is the part most firms underestimate.

The sequencing therefore matters more than the choice. A firm that announces a subscription for access to judgement before it has codified any judgement is selling a promise it cannot inspect, and the gap will be found by a client at the worst possible moment. The decision that actually faces a management team in this position is usually not which model to move to but how long to carry a deteriorating one while the new product is made real.

> **DATA POINT · Unit of sale and productivity gains** — Studies of professional-services firms generally find that productivity improvements accrue to clients rather than to the firm wherever billing is tied to time, and that firms converting to fixed-fee or subscription models retain a larger share of the gain.  
> Source: general professional-services research, 2026. Outcomes depend on contract mix.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Cost per unit, and what it does to a business model

**Figure 2.7** — One operational fact, two opposite commercial outcomes, decided years earlier.

Write down, in one sentence and without euphemism, what your organisation is paid for, and then write down what has happened to that unit over three years. If the unit is an input and the input is shrinking, you do not have a pricing problem that a rate rise would address; you have a business whose product is evaporating while its reports show growth, and the two facts will not stay compatible indefinitely.

Compute the like-for-like figure before you do anything else, because it is the only number that shows the movement and it is almost never in the management pack. Take the customers who were customers before the growth began, exclude those whose scope has materially changed, name the exclusions, and look at revenue per customer per year. A firm can be adding customers at speed and shrinking underneath, and most people in it will not know.

Then be slow about the remedy, because the remedy is a new product rather than a new price. Selling access, outcomes or carried risk commits you to obligations you may not yet be able to meet, and announcing such a model before the thing behind it exists is the single fastest way to convert a revenue problem into a credibility problem. Decide how long you will carry the old model deliberately, say so internally, and build.

*Say it like this*
- Name the unit: **We are paid for hours, and the hours are falling.**
- Ask for like-for-like: **What is revenue per client on the book we had three years ago?**
- Separate the questions: **That is a product decision, not a pricing decision.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — Cost-per-unit analysis tells you what is happening to your margin and your invoice. It says nothing about whether a client will accept a different unit of sale, what obligations the new unit creates, or whether you can meet them. Those are answered by building the thing, not by modelling it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Build what carries your judgement; buy what carries none; and price the exit before either**

**Figure 3.5** — The useful answer is almost never build or buy; it is which parts of the task fall on each side.

The build-or-buy question is answered badly whenever it is treated as a question about a whole task, because almost no task is uniform. A survey of a client’s documents is eight parts transcription and one part judgement about what a field is, and the honest answer is to buy the eight and build the one, which requires separating them first and is therefore the answer that rarely gets reached.

The usual rule, build what you are good at, is sound and is applied at the wrong level of abstraction. A firm good at knowing which discrepancies matter is not thereby good at reading field positions, and building the reading because it sits next to the judgement is how organisations acquire large internal systems whose valuable component is a tenth of the code and the whole of the maintenance.

What the two routes actually cost is misunderstood symmetrically. Building is costed as a project and lived as a maintenance burden: the build is four months and the eleven years afterwards are a standing claim on whoever understands it, which is a claim on a person rather than on a budget, and the person cannot be in two places. Buying is costed as a licence fee and lived as a dependency: the fee is predictable and the loss of freedom is not visible until the day it matters.

The question that exposes both is about leaving, and it should be asked before any other. How does the data get out, in what format, at what notice, and at what price; and for an internal build, what happens the week the person who wrote it resigns. Organisations that ask this question at the outset make better decisions in both directions, not because the answer is decisive but because it makes the real cost visible while there is still a choice.

One further asymmetry deserves naming. A supplier can be persuaded to build the part you need, and will put it on a roadmap, which converts your plan into their schedule. That may be perfectly acceptable, but it should be recognised as what it is: a dependency not on a system but on another organisation’s priorities, which are set by their largest customer and not by you.

> **DATA POINT · Internal systems and maintenance** — Surveys of internally developed business systems generally find that lifetime maintenance effort substantially exceeds initial build effort, and that the effort concentrates on a small number of individuals who become difficult to redeploy.  
> Source: general software-engineering literature, 2026. Ratios vary widely.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — What to build and what to buy

**Figure 3.7** — Neither column is worse. Both are regularly entered with only the first line costed.

Split the task before you decide anything. Count how many of its instances involve a genuine decision and how many are transcription, and treat the two as separate procurements, because the part that carries your judgement is the part that must be yours and the rest is work you happen to be doing. Organisations that skip this step end up building something whose valuable tenth is buried inside nine tenths of maintenance they did not want.

Then cost each route the way it will actually be experienced. A build is a project followed by a decade-long claim on one person, so express its maintenance in person-days a year and name the person, which makes the claim visible while it is still hypothetical. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A capacity figure without its constraint is not a forecast, it is a slogan**

**Figure 4.5** — Both bars are real. Only the shorter one is the capacity of the process.

Claims of the form four people now do the work of forty are usually true and almost always incomplete, and the incompleteness is systematic rather than accidental. The figure describes throughput at one stage, measured where the improvement happened, and the capacity of a process is set not by its fastest stage but by its slowest, which is ordinarily the one that was not improved because it could not be.

In professional work the unimproved stage is nearly always a person exercising judgement that rests on knowing something particular: a client’s habits, a market’s conventions, a history of what has gone wrong before. That knowledge does not compress, cannot be transferred by documentation alone, and is held by a small number of people whose working hours are the real ceiling on the firm. Improving everything upstream of them raises the rate at which work arrives at a door that opens at the same speed.

The projection that follows has a characteristic and dangerous property: it is arithmetically sound and practically impossible, and nobody can fault the arithmetic. Volumes multiply, exception rates hold, and the resulting figure quietly commits one named individual to a working day of a length nobody would accept if it were offered as a job. Because the error is not in the calculation, review processes that check calculations do not catch it.

Two remedies exist and they are not interchangeable. The exception rate can be reduced, which means the systems upstream must get better at the cases they currently refer; or the exception work can be spread, which means somebody must write down the rule that distinguishes the cases requiring particular knowledge from the cases merely requiring care. The second is almost always available and almost always undone, because it is weeks of unglamorous work by the busiest person in the firm.

The discipline that prevents the whole failure is small and procedural. Any capacity figure quoted in any document must appear in the same sentence as the condition on which it depends, joined by provided that or as long as. Four people can do the work of forty provided the exception rate falls below a stated figure; without the second clause the first clause is a slogan, and slogans get into board papers and then into commitments.

> **DATA POINT · Constraints and process improvement** — Operations research consistently finds that improvements at non-constraint stages do not raise system output, and that unrecognised human-judgement stages are a common constraint in professional services.  
> Source: general operations-management literature, 2026. Context-dependent.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Four people with the capacity of forty

**Figure 4.7** — The right-hand column is harder to say and is the only version that survives a year.

Establish the constraint before you quote any capacity figure at all. Walk the process stage by stage, ask of each stage what sets its pace, and keep going until you reach the one that has not got faster. In professional work that stage is almost always a person whose judgement rests on knowing something particular, and everything you do upstream of them raises the rate at which work arrives at a door that opens at an unchanged speed.

Then compute what your projection commits that person to, in hours of their day, and read the figure aloud. A projection that puts a named individual at six hours a day of a single activity is not a forecast but an undertaking nobody has given, and because its arithmetic is correct it will pass every review it is submitted to. Never let a capacity figure appear without the clause it depends on: four people can do the work of forty provided the exception rate falls below four per cent. ### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The apprenticeship was a by-product of the drudgery, and removing the drudgery removed it**

**Figure 5.5** — The last row is why nobody noticed for two years.

When a system takes over the execution of a task, it generally takes over the only mechanism by which the organisation was teaching that task, and this second effect is invisible for as long as the first effect is working. The people who learned the work by doing it remain in post and continue to perform at their previous standard, so no measurement moves; the people who arrive afterwards perform at the same standard on the same measurements, because confirming a correct draft and producing a correct draft yield the same output.

The divergence appears only in the cases the system handles wrongly without signalling it, which are by construction rare. A firm will therefore discover the loss at a rate of perhaps five or ten instances a year, which is far too slow to correct a cohort, and will discover it in the form of individual lapses attributed to individual people rather than as a pattern. The pattern is only visible to somebody who thinks to ask how many of the team could do the work unaided.

What is lost is not procedure, which can be written down and in most firms has been. What is lost is the capacity to notice that something is wrong before being able to say what, and that capacity is produced by having done the task imperfectly a few hundred times. A handbook records the answers that past difficulties produced; it does not reproduce the experience of having been puzzled, which is what generates the instinct.

Two partial remedies exist and both have costs that must be accepted openly. The first is a case library: not the procedure, but every instance in which the system was wrong in an unsignalled way, with what seemed odd and how it was resolved, which is cheap to keep and preserves the answers. The second is deliberate manual practice, performing the task by hand for no operational reason, which is expensive, feels wasteful, and is the only mechanism that preserves the instinct rather than the answers.

The honest position for a manager is that neither remedy is known to be sufficient, and that this should be written down rather than resolved rhetorically. A firm that requires ten manual exercises where the previous generation performed two hundred has made a judgement about an unmeasured quantity, and the only defensible thing to do with such a judgement is to record it as one and revisit it when the rare cases arrive.

> **DATA POINT · Automation and skill maintenance** — Research in aviation, medicine and process industries generally finds that operators who monitor automated systems lose manual proficiency over time, and that recovery of that proficiency requires deliberate practice rather than exposure.  
> Source: general human-factors literature, 2026. Transfer across domains is uncertain.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Automating an unstandardised process does not fix the variation; it hardens it**

**Figure 6.5** — Twelve routes rather than one, because eleven of the differences were real.

The instruction to standardise before automating is repeated so often that it has become inert, and the reason it is worth restating precisely is that the failure it predicts does not look like a failure. A system built for a process that in fact exists in forty versions does not collapse; it accommodates. Each accommodation is small, justified and documented, and the aggregate is a configuration nobody can hold in their head and nobody can safely change.

The variation itself is almost never evidence of carelessness, which is why counting it is the diagnostic step rather than deploring it. Each variant was typically introduced by a competent person in response to a real requirement and then inherited by successors who had no occasion to re-examine the requirement. The result is a population of local optima, all defensible at birth and most of them orphaned, and the only way to tell which is which is to ask.

That asking is where the exercise becomes uncomfortable and where its value sits. Of the differences a firm cannot account for, most will indeed turn out to be inherited habit; but the same investigation routinely surfaces one or two that exist for reasons nobody currently employed remembers, including regulatory ones, and a standardisation that retires those without asking has converted an untidy process into a liability. The protection is procedural: ask the client, or the regulator, before retiring any variation whose cause is unknown.

Nominalisation plays a surprisingly large part in how the problem persists. A handbook that says the intake process asserts a singular where a plural exists, and asserts it in a form that cannot be questioned, because a noun carries no count. The same grammar then obscures the remedy: the retirement of twenty-nine routes names no actor, so nobody is assigned, and the plan reads as complete while being unexecutable.

The practical sequence is therefore to count, classify, ask and only then build; and to write the resulting plan with people in the sentences. The counting is cheap, the classification is tedious, the asking is slow, and the building is the part everybody wants to start with. A firm that reverses the order pays for all four stages anyway, in a worse currency and after having told its clients a date.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**New roles get job descriptions; disappearing roles get nothing, because nobody decides them**

**Figure 7.5** — Five roles created by decision; one removed by nobody, and it was the only entry point.

The roles a capability creates are usually noticed and the roles it removes usually are not, and the asymmetry has an administrative cause rather than a political one. A new role requires a job description, a grade, an approval and a budget line, so it passes through every mechanism an organisation has for thinking; a disappearing role requires none of those, because it disappears by not being advertised, which is an absence of events rather than an event.

The roles that appear tend to share a shape. They are concerned with specifying, curating, supervising and explaining: writing the rule that distinguishes two kinds of case, keeping a body of reference material correct, deciding what must never be delegated, accounting afterwards for a decision a system contributed to. These are genuinely valuable and they are, almost without exception, graded above the entry level, because they presuppose somebody who already understands the work.

The roles that go are the ones whose content was volume rather than judgement, and it is worth stating plainly that this is usually a relief to the people who held them. The difficulty is second-order: those roles were where the firm taught, and the teaching was incidental to the volume. Removing the drudgery removes the apprenticeship, and the senior roles that appear in its place all assume the apprenticeship happened somewhere.

At the level of a single firm this problem is deferrable indefinitely, because experienced people can be recruited from firms that still have the junior work. At the level of a market it is not deferrable at all, because those firms are making the same change on the same timetable, and the pool everybody is recruiting from is being refilled by nobody. The absence does not produce a crisis in any particular quarter, which is precisely why it is not managed.

A firm that wants to act has one honest option and should cost it openly: create an entry role whose first year consists substantially of work the systems could do, performed by hand, for the stated purpose of producing people who have done it. This is expensive, it has no client paying for it, and it competes directly with the roles created to preserve the knowledge the entry role used to generate, which is an irony worth naming rather than resolving with rhetoric.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The last bottleneck is usually the person who removed all the others**

**Figure 8.5** — The shorter bar is a diary, and no amount of upstream improvement lengthens it.

In most organisations that have improved their production stages substantially, the remaining constraint is a senior person who reviews or signs, and it is frequently the same person who drove the improvements. The position is uncomfortable to occupy and almost impossible to see from inside, because the symptoms present as personal busyness rather than as a structural limit on the enterprise.

The arithmetic that makes it structural is simple and worth doing explicitly. Count the items that cannot proceed without you, multiply by the minutes each takes, and express the result as a proportion of your week; then divide your available hours by the minutes to obtain the number of items the organisation can process in a year. That figure is a ceiling, it has your name on it, and it does not move when anything else is improved.

The obstacle to delegating is almost never unwillingness, and treating it as such wastes the conversation. The genuine obstacle is that the reviewer cannot state their own test: they read something, form a view, and find on inspection that the view does not reduce to conditions they can articulate. This is usually accurate, and it is not the end of the matter, because the task is not to describe the judgement but to describe what the judgement has actually done.

That is an empirical exercise and it is dull in a specific, productive way. Take two or three hundred past decisions, record for each one the feature that caused the outcome, and look for the conditions that account for most of them. The result is typically a rule of three or four sentences in the form you may proceed unless, provided that, failing which, and it typically reveals that the reviewer’s stated test was not their operative one: size turns out to be a proxy for familiarity, lateness a proxy for client type.

Two warnings belong with the method. The first is that the person who writes a rule cannot see its ambiguities, so the rule must be applied by two people to the same twenty cases before it goes live, which costs a day and prevents the commonest failure. The second is that where the volume of cases is genuinely too small for a pattern, the honest conclusion is that the work cannot be delegated and the organisation has a ceiling with a name in it, which should then be stated in the plan rather than discovered in October.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Use produces improvement only where somebody decided to keep the use**

**Figure 9.5** — The gain is concentrated, the plateau is real, and one row reverses a firm belief.

The claim that systems improve because they are used is true in a narrow and conditional sense that is usually lost in the retelling. Use generates evidence about what the system got wrong; evidence improves the system; but the evidence exists only if somebody recorded it, and the natural behaviour of a competent worker who finds an error is to correct it and proceed, which destroys the evidence in the act of using it.

This is why the discipline is a logging requirement rather than an aspiration, and why its wording matters more than its sentiment. A correction must be logged with the field, the change and a reason describing the cause rather than the remedy, because a log of remedies records what was done and a log of causes records what was learned. The difference is invisible in the first month and decisive in the first year.

Where the requirement is adopted, three findings recur with some regularity. The gain concentrates almost entirely in the commonest cases, because those are the ones that generate enough instances to generalise from; the fields that were already correct learn nothing, which is obvious in retrospect and never predicted; and a proportion of the corrections turn out to be the reviewer introducing an error while adjusting something they merely disliked, which only a log can reveal and which is uncomfortable to publish.

The compounding itself is real and bounded, and business cases written on its first quarter are reliably wrong. A learning curve on a fixed volume of work exhausts the frequent cases in a matter of months and then flattens, because what remains is rare, heterogeneous and specific to individual customers. There is a legitimate argument that greater volume makes the rare cases frequent and restarts the curve, and it should be labelled as what it is: a proposition that cannot be tested without first incurring the cost.

The practical instruction is therefore to require the log at once, to word the requirement formally so that it does not decay into good practice, to measure the learning rate monthly rather than quarterly, and to forecast with the last three months extended flat. That forecast will look pessimistic beside the first quarter and will be the only figure anybody can defend when the curve turns over.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Compounding: systems that improve because they were used

**Figure 9.7** — The same four corrections, and only the right-hand column is evidence.

Require the log before you require anything else, and require it in the formal register, because a logging instruction phrased as good practice has a half-life of about four weeks. We require that every correction be logged with the field, the change and a reason of not fewer than five words; we require that no corrected output be released before the entry exists. The register is not pomposity, it is the difference between a rule and a suggestion.

Then insist that the reason describe the cause and not the remedy, and make cause unknown an explicitly permitted entry so that the alternative to honesty is not invention. A log full of corrected and adjusted records a thousand events and teaches nothing; a log saying this client writes dates the other way round teaches once and applies for years. The twenty seconds per entry is the entire cost and it is the cheapest asset your organisation can build.

Finally, measure the learning rate monthly and forecast it flat. Improvement on a fixed volume of work concentrates in the frequent cases, exhausts them within months, and then flattens hard, and every business case written on a first quarter is wrong in the same direction. Take the last three months, extend them flat, and say openly that the case for a second rise rests on volume you do not yet have and cannot test without spending the money.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**When the tools are identical, what is left is the record of having been wrong and having written it down**

**Figure 10.5** — Six items on the left, four on the right, and ninety-four per cent of the money on the left.

The arrival of a competitor with identical tools is the ordinary end state of any widely available capability, and it is worth treating as a certainty rather than a risk. Convergence happens quickly, because the acquiring is a purchase rather than a project, and the firm that spent three years integrating something has no claim on the firm that bought it in five weeks. What that three years bought is a separate question and usually a better one.

The diagnostic exercise is two lists and takes an afternoon. First, everything the organisation has that a competitor of similar size could acquire within three months: systems, suppliers, published terms, pricing, the customer-facing surface, and most written procedure. Second, everything that would take them more than two years. The second list is always shorter than people expect, and the proportion of historical spending that went onto the first list is usually the most uncomfortable number in the exercise.

What appears on the second list has a consistent character across firms. It consists of accumulated records of having been wrong: the cases where something failed and the reason was written down, the classifications of variation that took months to establish, the decision rules derived from hundreds of real past judgements, and people who learned the work in a way the organisation deliberately paid for. None of these can be purchased, because the only route to them is to have made the same mistakes and kept the evidence.

Each of those items also shares a political character worth naming. They are cheap, they have no customer paying for them, they are justified in language that sounds defensive, and they lose budget rounds to anything with a start date. A firm that finds its second list short is usually not a firm that was unlucky; it is a firm whose approval processes systematically preferred the first list, which is what approval processes do.

Above all, the second list is a rate rather than a possession. A competitor who adopts the same logging and training disciplines today will hold an equivalent record in two or three years, so the lead is finite and measured in the time it would take somebody else to accumulate it. Finally, and this is the practical conclusion, the advantage is extended only by continuing to be wrong in new ways and continuing to write it down, which is not a strategy anybody enjoys presenting.

> **DATA POINT · Sources of durable advantage** — Strategy research generally finds that advantages resting on purchasable technology erode within one to three years, while advantages resting on accumulated organisational learning, specialised knowledge and trained people persist considerably longer.  
> Source: general strategy literature, 2026. Durations vary widely by industry.
