### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who reads your daily report?
- Which figure in it is estimated?
- Does the report say so?

A daily production report is one page that reaches a reader before nine o’clock and leads to a decision.

Use rose to and fell to with the figure it reached, held at for a figure that did not move, and averaged with the hours it covers.

Give the basis of every figure: measured from a named instrument, estimated by a named person, or calculated from a stated input.

Where a figure is missing, say that it is missing, because a gap marked as a gap can be filled tomorrow and a gap filled today cannot be found again.

Mark a provisional figure as provisional, with the date on which it will be confirmed and the name of the person who will confirm it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A daily report in which every figure carries its basis**

**Figure 1.5** — Six figures and six bases. One of them is a gap, and it says so.

A daily report in which every figure carries its basis is the page a superintendent can act on before nine o’clock without ringing anybody.

Give the system the readings taken, the instrument tags they came from, the hours each figure covers, the estimates and who made them, and the gaps.

Ask it to mark each figure as measured, estimated or calculated, because a reader deciding something needs to know which of the three he is holding.

Ask it to leave every gap as a gap, with the reason, since a carried-forward figure looks exactly like a measured one and cannot be found again afterwards.

Ask it to carry the movement in words as well as in the table, because a reader reads the sentence and a seventeen per cent fall should not be called slight.

What it must not do is reconcile the figures. Reconciliation is measurement work with its own people and its own rules, and a daily report only reports.

**Grammar: cause and consequence in a note: because, therefore, which means**

- Use because plus the cause, and put the cause after the effect for a reader in a hurry. The seal failed because the shaft is out of line.
- Use therefore plus the consequence, in a sentence of its own. The shaft is out of line; therefore the seal will fail again.
- Use which means for a reader who is not an engineer. It is out of line, which means it will fail again within weeks.
- Do not use thus, hence or accordingly. They add nothing and they cost you readers.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A scope of work with its exclusions listed first**

**Figure 3.5** — Three exclusions, two assumptions, and then the work.

A scope of work with its exclusions listed first is the page a bidder prices accurately, because he reads the front of a document and prices the rest.

Give the system the work to be done, the materials each party will provide, the access the operator can give, the permits and who obtains them, and the claims that arose on similar jobs.

Ask it to put every exclusion on the first page with the name of whoever does that work instead, since by others is a phrase that has started more claims than it has prevented.

Ask it to write every assumption as a numbered line, because an assumption written down can be priced and an assumption relied on becomes a claim.

Ask it to use shall with a named party for every obligation, and to refuse will be provided and should be arranged, which leave the obligation with nobody.

What it must not do is decide what is included. What the operator is buying is a commercial decision taken by the person who signs the contract, and a scope only writes it down.

**A variation written with its cause and its evidence**

A variation written with its cause and its evidence is the page that answers in March the question nobody asked in October.

**An inspection report that states what was not inspected**

**Figure 8.5** — Four joints in ten looked at, and a report that says so.

An inspection report that states what was not inspected is the page that stops a superintendent reading forty joints as a hundred.

Give the system the work the inspector was asked to do, what was actually examined and by what method, what was prevented and why, and the total the examined items are part of.

Ask it to fence the work with within the scope of this inspection, because a reader treats everything outside the fence as having been looked at and found sound.

Ask it to state the extent as a figure against a total, since forty joints means nothing until the reader knows there are a hundred.

Ask it to put the limits in the body of the report and never in an appendix, and to end with a sentence saying what the report does not state.

What it must not do is draw a conclusion about fitness for service. That conclusion belongs to a competent person applying a standard, and a report of what was seen is not it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A one-page paper for an operations meeting**

**Figure 10.5** — The ask, the authority, both costs, and what happens if the answer is no.

A one-page paper for an operations meeting is the paper that gets four minutes and a decision instead of twenty minutes and a deferral.

Give the system what you want the meeting to do, who holds the authority to do it, the cost in money and in hours, and the options you considered and rejected.

Ask it to put one ask at the top, using recommends, requests or seeks approval, and to refuse members may wish to consider, which asks for nothing at all.

Ask it to give both costs, since a meeting trades money against hours and a paper with one of the two leaves the trade to be guessed at in the room.

Ask it to say what happens if the meeting declines, because a paper without that line is a paper that will be deferred to the next meeting.

What it must not do is decide anything. A paper asks and a meeting answers, and a paper that assumes the answer will be read as presumption by the people who have to give it.
