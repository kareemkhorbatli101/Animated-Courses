### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A board paper reduced to two pages**

**Figure 1.5** — The decision first, the figure once, and the assumption in writing.

A board paper reduced to two pages is the paper that gives a board twelve minutes of decision instead of thirty-four minutes of searching for the question.

Give the system the decision you want taken, the position with its figure and source, every option with its cost, the risk of deferral, and each assumption behind the forecast.

Ask it to put the decision asked for in the first line, in the words you would want to read in the minute, because that line is what a director votes on.

Ask it to carry the position once, with one figure, one period and one source, and to move every page of history into an annex that nobody has to read.

Ask it to set out the option you do not recommend with the same care as the one you do, and to write each assumption as a sentence on the page itself.

What it must not do is make the recommendation. A page can arrange what you know; the judgement, and the name under it, belong to the person tabling the paper.

> **DATA POINT · Papers to a board** — Guidance on board papers commonly recommends that a paper state the decision sought at the start, distinguish fact from recommendation, and show the assumptions behind any forecast. What a company’s board must consider, and in what form, is set by its own governance documents and by local company law.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: so that a board spends its time deciding rather than looking for the question. Scope: one paper for one meeting; it makes no recommendation of its own and it decides nothing. Inputs: the decision to be taken and the date it takes effect, the position with one figure, one period and one named source, each option with its cost and the person who would own it, what happens if nothing is decided at this meeting and from which date, each assumption behind any forecast, and anything the board decided previously that bears on this. Criteria: the first line naming the decision asked for in the words the minute would use; the position stated once, with its period and source; every option set out with its cost and with the same care, including any option not recommended; the risk of deferral given as a consequence and a date; each assumption written as a sentence of its own; the whole paper fitting on one page with history in an annex; and no recommendation and no figure without a source. Done: the paper runs to one page, the first line names the decision, every figure has a source, every assumption is on the page, and the author’s name stands under the recommendation separately. Fallback: where the decision is not the board’s to take, the paper says whose it is and asks the board only for what lies inside its own authority.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Forty-six papers, nineteen with no decision

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

Mazyad Petroleum Company timed every item at its board meetings for two years and compared the papers with the minutes that followed them.

A manager argues that a board paper should set out everything management knows and leave the board to decide what matters, on the grounds that naming a decision leads directors towards one answer.

Of 46 papers, 19 named no decision anywhere and took an average of 34 minutes of meeting time each. The 27 that named the decision in the first line took an average of 12 minutes. Eleven of the 19 returned to a later meeting still undecided, against 2 of the 27. In 4 cases the board decided something the paper had not asked for, and 2 of those 4 were still being disputed a year later by people who had been in the room.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An agenda built so that a decision has to be taken**

**Figure 2.5** — Three decisions, one report, and ten minutes against each item.

An agenda built so that a decision has to be taken is the page that stops ninety-six items in a year from reaching a minute with nothing decided in them.

Give the system every item for the meeting, the decision sought on each, the papers behind them, the time available, and who must be in the room for each decision.

Ask it to write each item as a decision in one sentence, phrased so that the answer is yes, no, or a choice between two options that are both named.

Ask it to mark any item that seeks no decision as a report, because an item that looks like a decision and is not will take the time of one that is.

Ask it to divide the time across the items and to put the figure against each one, so that a chair who overspends on the first can see it at the time.

What it must not do is take the decision. An agenda can force a question to be answered; only the people in the room can answer it, and only a chair can close it.
