### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: consequence: could, may, would, is likely to**

- Use could for a consequence that is possible but not yet supported by evidence. The gap could take a man through.
- Use is likely to where you have exposure figures or a previous event. With four men a shift, somebody is likely to step into it.
- Use would for what follows once the event happens. A man who stepped into it would fall four metres.
- Use may where the operator has to accept or reject the risk. The walkway may have to be closed until it is plated.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A hazard paired with the consequence it could produce**

**Figure 1.5** — The hazard, what it would do, to how many, and what we already know.

A hazard paired with the consequence it could produce is the entry a superintendent reads out of a register of seven hundred.

Give the system the hazard as it was reported, the number of people who pass that position in a shift, and the near misses already recorded there.

Ask it to write the consequence as what would happen to one named kind of person, with a distance, a height or a weight in it.

Ask it to take the worst credible outcome and not the worst imaginable one. Multiple fatalities on a handrail costs a register its reader.

Ask it to carry the evidence, because a hazard that has already nearly injured somebody is a different entry from one that has not.

What it must not do is rank the hazard against the others. Ranking is a judgement about a whole plant, and the person who owns the plant makes it.

> **DATA POINT · Hazard and consequence** — Many operators require a reported hazard to be recorded with the harm it could cause and the number of people exposed to it, so that reports can be ranked. What must be recorded is set by the operator’s own written system.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Credible outcomes** — Risk registers lose their usefulness where entries state outcomes that readers judge implausible. How outcomes must be described is set by the operator’s own written system.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

**A risk assessment rewritten so that a crew will read it**

**Figure 2.5** — What stays is what is on the plant. What goes is what belongs elsewhere.

**A job broken into steps with the hazard at each one**

**A stop-work request drafted for a contractor**

**Figure 4.5** — Four words, one reason, and four lines that follow them.

A stop-work request drafted for a contractor is the page that turns four words said on a walkway into something a company can act on.

Give the system what the person saw, the position, the permit the work is under, the contractor’s own reporting line, and who the area authority is that day.

Ask it to keep the spoken stop to the four words and the reason, because every extra clause is a second in which a load keeps moving.

Ask it to name who may lift the stop and on what condition, so that the condition is a thing anybody can see rather than a judgement.

Ask it to name the contractor’s own supervisor, because a stop that reaches only the man holding the tool is a stop his company never hears about.

What it must not do is judge whether the stop was justified. That is decided afterwards by the person who owns the area, and the page records what was seen.

**A pre-job brief that names what has changed since the plan**

**Figure 6.5** — Three changes, one crew action, one man and one contingency.

A pre-job brief that names what has changed since the plan is the page that keeps a brief to four minutes and makes those four minutes worth having.

**A concern recorded as what was seen and what is asked for**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A dissenting view minuted so that it survives the meeting**

**Figure 10.5** — A decision, a name, a dissent in its own words, and one dated action.

A dissenting view minuted so that it survives the meeting is the paragraph that stops agreed by consensus from meaning that nobody saw it coming.

Give the system the decision taken, the name of the person who took it, what the dissenter actually said, and the actions with their owners and dates.

Ask it to record the dissent in the dissenter’s own words, because a summary of a disagreement is always shorter and always weaker than the disagreement.

Ask it to record the acceptance beside the dissent, since a person can argue against a decision and carry it out, and the record should show both.

Ask it to give every action one named person and one date, because an action owned by a department is an action owned by nobody in particular.

What it must not do is weigh the dissent against the decision. The minute records that two views were held, and which of them was right is settled by what happens next.

> **DATA POINT · Minutes and dissent** — Where minutes record only an agreed outcome, a view held against it leaves no trace. What minutes must record is set by the operator’s own written system.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Actions and owners** — Actions recorded against a department rather than a named person are less likely to be completed. How actions must be recorded is set by the operator’s own written system.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: so that a view argued in a meeting can still be found in the record a year later. Scope: one item on one agenda; it weighs no view and it takes no decision. Inputs: the decision as taken, the name and role of the person who took it, what the dissenter said in their own words, whether they accepted the decision, the actions arising with their owners and dates, and the names of everybody in the room and anybody attending by telephone.
