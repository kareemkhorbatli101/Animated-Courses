### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who writes the first report where you work?
- How long does it take?
- What does it say about the cause?

A first situation report is written while the event is still happening, which is why every verb in it belongs in the present continuous.

Say what is happening, where it is happening, what the plant is currently doing, and how many people are accounted for.

Then say what is not yet known, which is the line that stops a reader filling the gap with something worse or something better.

Give the time of the report to the minute and the time at which the next report will be issued, because a report with no time on it is useless within the hour.

Leave the cause out. At twelve minutes nobody knows the cause, and a first report that speculates forces a correction that will be remembered longer than the event.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the present continuous in an emergency: is, are, currently**

- Use the present continuous for anything still happening. A release is continuing at the second stage.
- Use is currently for a state that may have changed by the time the reader reads it. The unit is currently shut down.
- Use are being for people. Forty-one people are being accounted for at the muster points.
- Resist the past simple until the event is over, because the past tense tells a reader that something has finished when it has not.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A first situation report written inside twelve minutes**

**Figure 1.5** — Four facts, one gap and two times. No cause anywhere on the page.

A first situation report written inside twelve minutes is the page that stops other people writing your report for you.

Give the system what is happening, where, the state of the plant, the headcount and the time it was taken, and the time at which the next report is due.

Ask it to keep every verb in the present continuous, because an event described in the past tense sounds finished to a reader and this one is not.

Ask it to write one line saying what is not yet known, since a gap you name is a gap people wait on and a gap you leave is a gap they fill.

Ask it to refuse any statement of cause. At twelve minutes the cause is a guess, and a guess that reaches three companies is a correction that outlives the event.

What it must not do is decide who is told. The order in which people are notified is set by the operator’s system and by law, and a page cannot change it.

Scope: one event, one report, issued within twelve minutes; it states no cause and it decides nothing. ### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Nineteen events, nine reports an hour late

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

Mazyad Petroleum Company reviewed how quickly it had issued a first report on nineteen serious events over four years, and what had happened in the interval.

Ten first reports were issued within twelve minutes of the event. Nine were issued more than an hour afterwards, in most cases while the company waited for the facts to be established.

In five of the nine late cases, a description of the event circulated outside the company before the company issued anything, and in four of those five the outside description was wrong: one described a fire that did not occur and two described injuries that did not occur. ### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who interviews people after an event where you work?
- What is the first question asked?
- Is the record read back?

An investigation interview is built on four open questions, and almost everything that goes wrong in one comes from asking a fifth.

Ask what did you see, what did you do, what did you expect and what did you understand, in that order, and let the silence after each one run.

A leading question manufactures the answer the asker already had, and a record built on manufactured answers will be read as a record of an opinion.

Keep what the person saw strictly apart from what they assumed, and where they assumed something, ask where the assumption came from.

Say at the start that the interview is not a disciplinary matter, read the record back at the end, and write in any correction the person makes rather than arguing with it.

**Grammar: interview questions: what did you see, do, expect, understand**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An interview record that separates what was seen from what was assumed**

**Figure 6.5** — What he saw, what he did, what he expected, and from whom he understood it.

An interview record that separates what was seen from what was assumed is the page that turns a man into a handover finding.

Give the system the four questions, the answers as the person gave them, the clock times from the logbook, and the accounts other people have already given.

Ask it to keep observations and assumptions in separate columns, because an investigation that treats them alike will find a person where it should find a system.

Ask it to trace every assumption to its source, since an assumption that came from a handover is a finding and an assumption that came from nowhere is only a habit.

Ask it to mark every point on which two accounts differ rather than reconciling them, because the difference between two honest accounts is often where the cause sits.

What it must not do is draw the conclusion. The record is what people said, and the finding belongs to the investigation that reads it alongside everything else.

**A causal chain written without naming a culprit**

A causal chain written without naming a culprit is the page that turns one man on a walkway into four things somebody can change tomorrow.

Give the system the sequence of events from the clock, the controls that were present and absent, the decisions taken earlier and the information they were taken on, and the authority each change would need.

- announce an unpopular decision in three sentences
- say what a decision does not mean, and kill the rumour
- separate what is decided from what is still open
- take anger without arguing and without promising
- write a six-line tasking for an announcement

*Foundations to build on*
- Announce an unpopular decision inside one working day.
- Say we have decided, and name who decided and when.
- Say this means, and give each change its date.
- Say it does not mean, and name the fear.

**Figure 10.13** — Of seventeen unpopular decisions announced to a workforce over five years, nine reached it as a rumour before any message was issued, at an average of eleven days from the decision; supervisors recorded thirty-one separate beliefs about those nine of which twenty-two were wrong, two employees resigned over a version that had never been true, and in six of the nine the eventual message said nothing at all about what the decision did not mean.
