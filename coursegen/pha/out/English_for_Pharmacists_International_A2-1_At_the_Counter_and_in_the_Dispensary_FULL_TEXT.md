### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: roles in a pharmacy: who does, who may, who must not**

- Use who does for the action. Who does the ordering? Who does the fridge check? The answer is a person.
- Use who may for permission. Who may sign this register? May is about the role, not about how busy somebody is.
- Say what you must not do. I must not sign that. This is useful and it is not rude.
- Ask for the role and the name together. Not "can somebody do this?" but "who is the pharmacist on duty?"

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A list of who may do what in a pharmacy, checked against the rota**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the fifth one is the one people forget.

A list of who may do what, checked against the rota, is the first thing a new pharmacist needs. It is also the last thing anybody gives her.

Give the system the tasks you have met this week, the roles you have been told about and the rota for the next four weeks.

Ask for one role against each task, and then one name for today. A role is not a person and a person is not always in.

Ask it to mark every task where no trained person is on the rota, because that task is already waiting.

Ask for an hours column, because the answer at half past five is often not the answer at eleven.

What it cannot do is tell you which tasks the law keeps to a pharmacist. That is in local law and in the procedures, and you must read them yourself.

> **DATA POINT · Roles** — Professional guidance commonly separates tasks that a pharmacist must carry out personally from tasks that a trained colleague may carry out. Which tasks fall where is set by local law and by the employer’s own written procedures.  
> Source: general professional guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Written procedures** — Pharmacy guidance commonly recommends that each task be written against a role, with the hours it may be carried out and the training required. The content of those procedures is set by the employer and by local law.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do you ask when somebody hands you a prescription?
- How do you know it is for the person in front of you?
- What do you check before the patient leaves the counter?

A prescription arrives at the counter. It takes fifteen seconds to look at it. It takes forty minutes to fix it later.

So the first look is at the counter, while the person is still there.

Four questions. Is this for you? Is this your address? When was it written? Have you had this before?

The answers are not for the record only. They tell you whether the paper and the person agree.

If the paper says one name and the person says another, stop. Ask who the medicine is for.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A handover script drafted as take, keep and come back if**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines, and the fourth one cannot be answered with a nod.

A handover script drafted as take, keep and come back if is three sentences and a question. Its value is that the order never changes.

Give the system the instruction exactly as the label prints it, the storage line from the leaflet, and the date the supply should last to.

Ask for three sentences in that order, each with one fact in it, and each short enough to say to somebody who is in a hurry.

Ask for the check question in a form that cannot be answered yes, because a question that can be answered yes will be.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An end-of-day note sorted into done, waiting and tell tomorrow**

**Figure 10.5** — Six lines, and the last one is the one that decides whether any of it works.

An end-of-day note sorted into done, waiting and tell tomorrow is the smallest useful document in a pharmacy and the one most often written badly.

Give the system the day’s tasks and which of them are finished. Add what each unfinished one is waiting for, with the names and the numbers.

Ask for three short lists in that order. Every line in the waiting list must carry a name, a telephone number and the thing awaited.

Ask for the age of each waiting line in days. A line that has waited three days should not look like one written this afternoon.

Ask for one first call, named, to be made before the door opens, because nine o’clock is the hour at which good intentions fail.

What it must not do is make the note longer in order to be complete. A note that cannot be read at nine in the morning is not a handover; it is an archive.
