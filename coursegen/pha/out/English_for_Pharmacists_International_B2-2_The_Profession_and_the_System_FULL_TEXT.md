### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What was the last board paper you read, and what did it ask for?
- How far into it was the recommendation?

A board has an hour, eleven items and a chair who is watching the clock. A paper that arranges itself as a story has misunderstood the room.

So the recommendation goes in the first line. The board is asked to approve, to note or to decide between two named options, and the paper says which of those three it wants before anything else.

Assurance comes second. The figures, the causes, the comparison and the risk, in that order, and every figure with a cause beside it rather than in a paragraph four pages later.

The cost of doing nothing is the line most papers leave out, and it is the line that most often carries a decision. Spend rises by this much over the year if nothing changes.

And risk is stated in money or in a number of patients. Significant risk and serious concern are adjectives, and a board that has read eleven papers of adjectives cannot tell which of them to be worried about.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A board paper drafted with the recommendation in the first line**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and a board that reads only the first still knows what you want.

A board paper drafted with the recommendation in the first line is one page written for eleven people with four minutes.

Give the system the decision you are seeking, the figures with their causes, the cost of inaction, the options you rejected and the date of the next report.

Ask for the first line to name the decision, because a board that has not been told what is wanted spends its four minutes working it out.

Ask for every figure to carry its cause in the same sentence, since a figure whose explanation is on another page will be queried before it is read.

Ask for every risk to be converted into money or into a number of patients, because a board ranks eleven items and cannot rank adjectives against each other.

What it must not do is argue. A paper that persuades rather than informs has taken the board’s decision for it, and the strongest paper is the one that gives a board the figure it could use to disagree.

> **DATA POINT · Board papers** — Governance literature commonly recommends that papers to a governing body state at the outset what decision is sought, distinguish between items for decision and items for noting, and quantify risk where it can be quantified. The composition and duties of a board are set by local law and the organisation’s own constitution.  
> Source: general governance literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Papers for decision** — Governance literature commonly reports that governing bodies spend disproportionate time on items whose required decision is unclear, and recommends that each paper state on its first page whether it is for decision, for assurance or for noting.  
> Source: general governance literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: so that a board of eleven people with four minutes knows what it is being asked to decide and can decide it. Scope: the decision sought, the figures that bear on it and the risk; it does not rehearse the history of the department and does not argue for the recommendation beyond stating its basis. Inputs: the decision we are seeking, each figure with the cause of its movement, the cost of doing nothing, the options we rejected with one reason each, and the date of the next report. Criteria: the first line names approval, noting or a choice between two named options; every figure carries its cause in the same sentence; the cost of inaction appears in the first paragraph; every risk is money or patients and never an adjective; and nothing on page one needs page two to be understood. Done: the paper is one page, circulated five working days before the meeting. Fallback: where a figure cannot be explained, the paper says that the cause is not established rather than offering one that is not evidenced.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Eleven items and sixty-five minutes

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A hospital board reviewed how it had spent its time over 24 meetings, against what each paper had asked of it.

Of 198 papers, 61 stated on the first page what decision was sought. In the other 137 the chair or a member asked what the paper wanted, which took a mean of four minutes before any discussion of the substance began.

### Part 10 · The Question You Have to Ask — The Question You Have to Ask · Putting in the figure that lets them disagree

Ruth has two costed options and a third figure, the cost of doing nothing, which makes her preferred option look obviously right.

Including it strengthens her case. It also gives the board the number it would need to reject her reasoning.

**Figure 1.12** — Two options, three figures, and the one that cuts both ways.

*Work in pairs. Student B reads each line as it was said; Student A repairs it out loud.*

**Turn 1 · Ruth:** The cost of doing nothing is £310,000 in the first year, and it goes in the first paragraph.

**Turn 2 · Jan:** That figure is contested, and a member will say the shortages may end in the spring.

**Turn 3 · Ruth:** Then they will say it with the figure in front of them, which is how a board is supposed to work.

*Repair moves you can use without correcting anybody*
- If I leave it out, the paper is weaker and also less honest.
- If I put it in, a member can argue the shortages end and the figure halves.
- That argument is one the board is entitled to have, and I would rather it happened in March.
- I will state the assumption beside the figure, which is that both shortages continue for twelve months.
- And I will say what the figure becomes if only one of them continues.
- A paper that cannot survive a member testing its assumption was not worth circulating.

> **WHAT DOES NOT SURVIVE TRANSLATION** — Never leave out the figure that would let a board disagree with your recommendation, and always state the assumption beside it.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What did your last committee decide, in one sentence?

A committee meets for two hours and produces, on a good day, four decisions. On a bad day it produces a set of minutes in which nothing can be found.

The difference is in the agenda. An item written as a heading invites an update. An item written as a question invites an answer, and the question is what the chair reads out.

Then the options, which the paper has already set out and the chair does not re-explain. Two or three, named, with the one the paper recommends identified as such.

Then the resolution, which the chair says aloud before the item closes. The committee agrees to do this, from this date, and Marisol will do it. If nobody can repeat that sentence, the item has not closed.

An item for noting is announced as one. Members are told at the start that nothing is being asked of them, which saves the ten minutes a committee spends discovering that there is nothing to decide.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A committee agenda built around decisions rather than updates**

**Figure 2.5** — Six elements, and the second is the one that saves ten minutes an item.

A committee agenda built around decisions rather than updates is a short document that tells eleven people what they are there to settle.

Give the system the list of items, which of them require a decision, the options in each paper and the time the committee has in total.

Ask for every decision item to be written as a question beginning with whether, because a subject heading invites an update and a question invites an answer.

Ask for the noting items to be grouped and marked, and for members to be told that nothing is asked of them, with the right to pull any one out.

Ask for each decision item to carry the minutes allowed, since four decisions in two hours is twenty minutes each and eleven headings is nothing each.

What it must not do is decide. An agenda that recommends in its own wording has pre-empted the committee, and the recommendation belongs in the paper where a member can see who made it.

> **DATA POINT · Committee governance** — Governance literature commonly recommends that committee agendas distinguish items for decision from items for noting, that papers be circulated in advance of the meeting, and that minutes record decisions with a named owner and a date. The committee’s terms of reference are set by the organisation.  
> Source: general governance literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Agendas** — Committee literature commonly reports that meetings whose agendas distinguish decision items from information items, and state the time allowed for each, reach more decisions per meeting and defer fewer items, and that items without a named owner recur most frequently.  
> Source: general governance literature, 2025–2026.

## Unit 3 · A Policy That Says What Will Not Be Supplied

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: a policy of refusal: will not be supplied, except where · AI: a policy drafted as what will not be supplied as well as what will*

**Figure 3.0** — A POLICY THAT ONLY SAYS WHAT IS SUPPLIED HAS LEFT EVERY REFUSAL TO WHOEVER IS ON DUTY.: Two halves of one policy, and the second half is the one that gets written badly.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does your pharmacy refuse to supply, and where is that written down?
- Who can authorise an exception, and how would you reach them at midnight?
- What policy in your building needs its author present to be applied?

Most policies describe what will happen, and are read by somebody at midnight who needs to know what will not. That half is usually missing, or is implied in a way that leaves a decision to whoever is standing there.

So a policy of refusal states the negative explicitly. These will not be supplied against a request of this kind, and the sentence appears in the policy rather than in the practice of the people who have worked there longest.

Each exception has a name against it, not a role in the abstract. The on-call pharmacist may authorise this one; the chief pharmacist or the deputy may authorise that one; nobody may authorise the third.

An appeal route goes inside the policy, in the paragraph that creates the refusal rather than at the end under a heading nobody reaches. A refusal with no appeal in sight is a refusal somebody will take to the complaints procedure instead.

And the test of the whole document is whether a locum, on a Sunday, with nobody to telephone, can apply it and reach the same answer that you would have reached. A policy that needs its author present is a policy that exists only during office hours.

**A policy drafted as what will not be supplied as well as what will**

**Figure 3.5** — Six elements, and the fourth one is the one a tired reader needs most.

A policy drafted as what will not be supplied as well as what will is a document written for a stranger working alone at two in the morning.

## Unit 5 · A Regulator’s Question, Answered Exactly

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: answering a regulator within the exact scope of the question · AI: a regulator’s question answered within its exact scope*

**Figure 5.0** — A REGULATOR ASKED YOU FOUR QUESTIONS. ANSWER FOUR.: Two kinds of answer, and the second one opens a second file.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What was the last formal question your organisation was asked in writing?
- Who read the answer before it went?
- What did the answer volunteer that nobody had asked for?

A regulator writes with questions that have been drafted carefully, and the first discipline is to read them as drafted rather than as you expected them to be.

So you answer within the exact scope. If the question asks about the twelve months to March, the answer covers the twelve months to March, and the period before it is not described however relevant it feels.

Then you separate what you can evidence from what you believe. We hold a signed record for each of the 41 authorisations is one kind of sentence. We believe the practice was consistent before that is another, and it is labelled as a belief.

An admission is made once, in the sentence where it belongs, and is not repeated in three places with increasing contrition. A fact admitted three times reads as a fact somebody is circling.

And where you cannot answer a part, you say which part and why. We cannot establish how many of the 41 were authorised by telephone, because the day book did not record the method until June.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: answering a regulator within the exact scope of the question**

- Restate the scope before answering it. Your question concerns the twelve months to the thirty-first of March, and this answer covers that period only.
- Use We hold for what you can evidence, and We believe for what you cannot. We hold a signed record for each of the 41 authorisations. We believe, but cannot evidence, that the practice was the same earlier.
- Make the admission once, plainly. Thirty-two of the 41 authorisations were not recorded in writing at the time.
- Name the unanswerable part. We cannot establish how many were authorised by telephone, because the day book did not record the method until the second of June.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A regulator’s question answered within its exact scope**

**Figure 5.5** — Six elements, and the sixth is the one people skip at seven in the evening.

A regulator’s question answered within its exact scope is a letter whose discipline is almost entirely about what it leaves out.

Give the system the exact words of each question, the documents you hold, the figures you can evidence, the beliefs you cannot and the deadline in the letter.

Ask for each answer to restate the scope it is answering and to stay inside it, because a period you describe unasked is a period the regulator now holds.

Ask for every sentence to be marked as evidenced or believed, since a belief presented as a fact is the single most expensive sentence in a regulatory reply.

Ask for each admission to appear once, with its number, its reason and the action already taken with its date, and for no apology inside the factual body of the letter.

What it must not do is volunteer. A draft that raises a subject nobody asked about has widened the enquiry on your own initiative, and the fastest way to turn four questions into forty is to answer seven.

> **DATA POINT · Regulatory enquiries** — Regulators in most jurisdictions may require information in writing within a stated period, and in many cases a response is itself a regulated act. The scope of the duty to respond, the consequences of an incomplete answer and any right to seek an extension are set by local law and the regulator concerned.  
> Source: general regulatory overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Scope of reply** — Regulatory practice literature commonly reports that responses which address matters outside the questions asked frequently lead to further enquiries on those matters, and recommends that organisations answer the questions put and address other matters through their own improvement processes.  
> Source: general regulatory literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A public explanation drafted in plain words with a stated limit**

**Figure 6.5** — Six elements in forty words, and the third one is the one that keeps it honest.

A public explanation drafted in plain words with a stated limit is forty words read standing up by somebody who is worried and in a hurry.

Give the system the fact as you know it, what you do not know, what the public can usefully do, who will answer questions and the date the notice will be reviewed.

Ask for every abstract noun to be turned back into a verb, because an interruption to supply is a phrase that tells a worried person only that you are protecting yourself.

Ask for the limit in the first paragraph, since a reader who learns in the last line that nothing can be done has spent the whole notice planning an effort that will fail.

Ask for one named spokesperson and a review date on the notice itself, because four accounts of one shortage in a week becomes a story about the organisation rather than the shortage.

What it must not do is predict. Normal service will resume shortly is a sentence that will be on a door in three weeks being read by the same people, and it costs more trust than the shortage ever did.

> **DATA POINT · Public communication** — Communications literature commonly recommends that public statements about service disruption use plain language, state what is not known as well as what is, name the action the public can usefully take, and carry a date for review. Any duty to notify patients or the public is set by local law.  
> Source: general communications literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Uncertainty** — Risk communication literature commonly reports that statements which acknowledge what is not known are trusted more than those which offer reassurance later contradicted, and that repeated revision of a predicted resolution date reduces confidence in subsequent statements.  
> Source: general communications literature, 2025–2026.

A workforce plan covers five years, and the things it depends on will change in two. That is not a flaw in planning; it is the condition of it, and the plan has to be written in a way that admits it.

So the sentences are conditional. If three of the six retirements happen as expected and the training commission holds at two places a year, the department is four posts short in year three.

Then the assumptions are named, in a list, with the one the plan depends on most marked as such. Our plan rests chiefly on the assumption that the two training places continue; everything else is adjustment.

Milestones carry dates rather than seasons. The first of September in year two, not early in the second year, because a season is a thing nobody is accountable for.

And there is a trigger: the fact that, if it occurs, changes the plan rather than merely worrying the people who notice it. If either training place is lost, this plan is rewritten within eight weeks.

**A five-year plan drafted in conditionals with its assumptions named**

**Figure 8.5** — Six elements, and the fifth is the one that stops a wrong plan being defended.

A five-year plan drafted in conditionals with its assumptions named is a document written in the knowledge that two of its foundations will move.

Give the system the establishment, the retirements you can forecast, the training places you receive, the vacancy rates you have observed and the funding horizon of each post.

> Source: general workforce literature, 2025–2026.

**An unwelcome figure prepared as lead, cause and action**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**What no system can do for a pharmacist’s judgement**

**Figure 10.5** — Six things a system does well, and six it cannot do at all.

What no system can do for a pharmacist’s judgement is the one section of this book that is about the limits of everything else in it.

Give the system the procedure, the checks, the flags and the policy, and expect it to catch the nine cases in ten that can be described in advance.

Ask it to tell you to stop, which is the most valuable thing a procedure does, and do not ask it to tell you what is wrong, because that sentence has to come from you.

Ask it to record what you decided, when, and on what basis, because the record is the part of judgement that can be delegated to a document and the only part that can.

Ask it never to carry accountability, since a procedure that appears to take responsibility for a registrant’s decision has misled that registrant about the only thing they cannot share.

What it must not do is decide. Whether this prescription, for this person, on this day, should be supplied is a judgement that belongs to whoever is standing at the bench, and no system written in these six books or anywhere else can stand there instead.

> **DATA POINT · Professional judgement** — Professional standards in most jurisdictions place responsibility for the exercise of professional judgement on the individual registrant, and provide that compliance with an employer’s procedure does not of itself discharge that responsibility. The scope of professional accountability is set by local law and the regulator.  
> Source: general professional guidance, 2025–2026.
