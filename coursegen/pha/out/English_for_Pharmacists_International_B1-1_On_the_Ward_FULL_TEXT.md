### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Where did the last medicines history you read come from?
- Which question finds the items nobody has written down?
- What do you write when you cannot establish a line?

A medicines history arrives on the ward in one of two conditions. Either it has sources against each line, or it does not.

A history with sources can be used. A history without them is a list that somebody once typed, and the next person will assume it was checked.

So the first discipline is the question, not the document. Have you been taking anything that is not on this list? Did you ever stop anything, and who told you to?

The second discipline is the attribution. Beside each line goes where it came from: the patient, a box the patient brought, the surgery record, or a relative speaking for them.

And the third is honesty about the gap. A line you could not establish is written as not established, with what you tried, rather than quietly omitted.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: a medicines history: have you been, did you ever, who told you**

- Use Have you been for the present stretch of time. Have you been taking anything that is not on this list? The present perfect continuous keeps the question open.
- Use Did you ever for the closed past. Did you ever stop anything? The simple past invites a specific memory rather than a general answer.
- Use Who told you to for the attribution. Who told you to stop it? The answer tells you which record to look at next.
- Use I could not establish for the gap, in the past tense, with what you tried. I could not establish the second line; the surgery was closed and the family had not arrived.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A medicines history assembled into a list with its sources**

**Figure 1.5** — Six columns, and the fifth one is the one that is usually deleted.

A medicines history assembled into a list with its sources does one thing that a printed list cannot. It tells the next reader how much to trust each line.

Give the system what the patient told you, what the containers they brought say, what the surgery record holds, and what a relative added.

Ask for one row for each item with a source column, and ask it to show a row twice where two sources disagree rather than choosing between them.

Ask it to list separately the lines nobody could confirm, with what was tried and at what time, because an unconfirmed line that looks confirmed is worse than a blank.

Ask for the date each source was consulted, since a surgery record from March is a different kind of fact from a conversation this morning.

What it must not do is reconcile the disagreements. Which version is correct is a question for the prescriber with the patient, and a tidy list is the commonest way of hiding that question.

> **DATA POINT · Medicines history** — Professional guidance commonly recommends taking a medicines history from the patient where possible, recording the source of each item, and using more than one source. What must be recorded is set by local law and the employer’s procedures, and every therapeutic decision remains the prescriber’s.  
> Source: general professional guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Multiple sources** — Pharmacy literature commonly reports that histories taken from more than one source identify more discrepancies than those taken from a single record, and that the source of each item is worth recording. Local procedures set the format.  
> Source: general pharmacy literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: so that the next person to read this history knows where every line came from and which lines are not established. Scope: the items and their sources; it makes no therapeutic judgement and chooses between no two versions, which is for the prescriber with the patient. Inputs: what the patient told me and when, the labels on any containers brought in, the surgery record with its date, anything a relative added with their relationship, and the calls I made with their times. Criteria: one row an item; a named source and a date against every row; a row shown twice where two sources disagree; the unestablished lines listed separately with what was tried; and nothing omitted for tidiness. Done: the list has been read back to the patient and the unestablished lines are in the notes. ### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Ten months of the same list

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A hospital took medicines histories by copying the most recent list held on the system, on the view that the list was itself taken from a history and that re-asking the patient duplicated work.

Over six months 1,840 admissions were reviewed. In 1,402 the history was copied without a source column. In 438 a history was taken from the patient with sources recorded.

Discrepancies were found in 61 of the 1,402 and in 319 of the 438. Of the 319, 148 were items the patient had stopped or run out of, and 44 were items bought without a prescription.

**STAGE 5 · The Decision**

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say from whom a history is taken.
- Say what every line carries.
- Say what happens where two sources disagree.
- Say what happens to a line that cannot be confirmed.

*Language bank for the decision*
- A medicines history is taken from the patient where the patient can give one, and from a named relative where they cannot.
- Every line carries a named source and the date that source was consulted, and a printed list is recorded as one source among others.
- Where two sources disagree, both versions are recorded with their sources, and the question is put to the prescriber rather than resolved by the pharmacy.
- A line that cannot be confirmed is written as not established, with what was tried and at what time, and is never omitted to make the list look complete.

A chart is read in two ways. At a glance, which confirms what you already expect, and line by line, which does not.

Reading line by line is slower and finds a different class of problem: not the wrong entry, but the absent one.

There are two kinds of absence. A field with nothing in it, which anybody can see, and a field that could be read two ways, which looks complete.

The second kind is the dangerous one, because the next reader will resolve it silently and will not know that they have.

So you write what the chart does not state, and where it is not clear which reading applies. You add a note; you do not alter somebody else’s entry.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A chart read for what it does not state**

**Figure 2.5** — Six lines, and the last one is the only one that prevents anything.

A chart read for what it does not state works against the grain of reading, which is to supply what is missing without noticing.

Give the system the fields your procedures require on a chart, the entries actually present, the notes for the same period, and what the patient has told you.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A question to a prescriber drafted as one sentence with one ask**

**Figure 3.5** — Six lines, and the fifth one is the one people leave out of politeness.

A question to a prescriber drafted as one sentence with one ask is the smallest document in this book and the hardest to send.

Give the system the entry in question, what the chart does and does not state, the patient as your procedures identify them, and the time you need an answer by.

Ask for an opening of four words with your name and your role, because a question from an unnamed pharmacy is a question somebody else can answer.

Ask for exactly one question, phrased as did you intend rather than as a doubt, since a question that assumes competence is answered and a question that implies error is defended.

Ask for the escalation line with a name and a time, and then keep to it exactly. An escalation named and not followed is worse than none.

What it must not do is propose the answer. A pharmacist who writes the decision into the question has asked the prescriber to agree rather than to decide.

Escalation routes are set by the employer, and the therapeutic decision remains the prescriber’s.  
> Source: general professional guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Phrasing of queries** — Communication literature commonly reports that queries phrased as enquiries into intention are answered more fully than those phrased as doubts, and that a single question obtains a more complete answer than several. Escalation routes are set locally.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A going-home explanation drafted as changes, unchanged and watch for**

**Figure 6.5** — Six lines, and the second one is the line that prevents readmissions.

A going-home explanation drafted as changes, unchanged and watch for is three headings and two warnings.

Give the system what the discharge letter says has started, stopped and changed, what the patient was taking at home, and the two signs that require action.

Ask for the unchanged list in full, by name, because a patient who hears only about changes will stop whatever was not mentioned.

Ask for one telephone warning and one return warning, with a number and a place, and ask it to refuse a third.

Ask for the check as a question the patient must answer in sentences: what will you do when you get home.

What it must not do is add a warning sign of its own, or explain why a change was made. The reason belongs to the prescriber, and a reason offered loosely becomes an instruction the patient acts on.

> **DATA POINT · Discharge counselling** — Counselling guidance commonly recommends separating what has changed from what continues unchanged, limiting the warning signs given to the few that require action, and checking understanding by asking the patient to restate. The content of any instruction is set by the prescriber and the discharge letter.  
> Source: general counselling guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Unchanged lists** — Counselling literature commonly reports that patients given only a list of changes sometimes discontinue unmentioned items, and that stating the unchanged list explicitly reduces this. Content remains the prescriber’s.  
> Source: general counselling literature, 2025–2026.

**A discharge letter’s medicines section drafted as started, stopped, changed**

**Figure 7.5** — Six blocks, and the fourth one is the one most often assumed.

A discharge letter’s medicines section drafted as started, stopped, changed turns prose into something three different readers can use.

Give the system what the notes record as begun, discontinued and altered during the admission, what the patient was taking at home, and what they hold at home now.

Ask for four separate lists and never a paragraph, because a change in the middle of a sentence is a change the next prescriber will read past.

A discrepancy is a disagreement between two statements about the same thing, and it has three parts.

The source of each version. The difference between them, stated as a difference. The resolution, when somebody with the authority to resolve it does so.
