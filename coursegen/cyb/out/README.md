# English for Cybersecurity
## New Syria Edition · Government and Private Sector

Six coursebooks, A2.1 to B2.2, for security professionals who have to write and
speak in English: analysts, security managers, IT directors, information
governance leads and chief information security officers, in ministries,
banks, hospitals, utilities and telecoms operators.

The spine of the series is one sentence: **a defender has to speak before the
evidence is complete.** The window closes while you are still gathering the
proof, and the professional question is not how to be certain but how to be
honest at speed — what to assert, what to label as unknown, what to refuse to
promise, and what to write down before you sleep.

### What is in every unit

Twelve parts, the same shape in all sixty units:

1. **Hero statement** — the unit in one line a tired person can quote
2. **Warm-up** — three questions about the reader’s own organisation
3. **Reading** — five sentences carrying the unit’s argument, with a data point
4. **Comprehension** — six question types, including a four-way classification
5. **Vocabulary** — twenty terms, each glossed in working language
6. **Grammar** — the point, four rules, and a two-column before-and-after figure
7. **Two dialogues** — twelve lines each; the second always ends on an open objection
8. **Speaking** — three paired tasks, six turns each
9. **The AI capability** — six paragraphs on what a system can draft and what it cannot
10. **The Sentence You Must Say Before You Are Sure** — the series signature
11. **The Decision Room** — a case with figures, three voices, and a four-line rule to write
12. **Brief Lab** — a real six-line draft, diagnosed against the precision-fault ladder

### The two ladders that run across all sixty units

**The AI ladder.** Sixty distinct capabilities, from *a first-line script for an
account that will not open* to *what no tool can do for a defender’s
judgement*. Every one of the sixty is paired with a boundary line saying what
the capability cannot do, and the last unit of Book 6 makes the division
explicit: a system arranges what is written down; it cannot reach what nobody
wrote down, and it cannot be the person who will be asked in two years.

**The precision-fault ladder.** Eight named faults — unscoped set, unbounded
quantity, unstated criterion, undefined done, ambiguous referent, missing
condition, unstated exception, hidden assumption — introduced progressively and
diagnosed in sixty Brief Labs. Each lab takes a real six-line draft (an
escalation, a policy paragraph, a notification, a board item) and names three
fault types, twice each.

### Four standing rules, held across all sixty units

1. **No offensive content.** No exploit, payload, attack tool, evasion or bypass
   technique, malicious code or working indicator of compromise appears
   anywhere in the series. Every technical decision is attributed to a named
   engineer. The subject is defence as communication.
2. **Nothing is ever described as secure.** The books teach what was checked,
   when, by whom, and what was not checked. "We have found no evidence of
   access" is taught in preference to "no access occurred", and the reason is
   given.
3. **No politics, no real organisation, no vendor named.** Cities are real —
   Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama, Latakia, Tartus, Deir ez-Zor, Raqqa, Idlib,
   Daraa, As-Suwayda, Qamishli, Al-Hasakah, Jableh, Baniyas. Institutions are
   invented: Barada Bank, Qasioun Telecom, Ministry of Public Works, Orontes
   Water Authority, Zenobia Health Group, National Cyber Incident Response
   Centre, Afamia Systems, Cham Logistics, Jableh Port Authority, Amrit
   Insurance.
4. **Law and procedure are always attributed.** Every data point panel cites
   local law, the regulator’s own process, or the organisation’s written
   arrangements. No sum of money and no legal requirement is presented as a
   fact about any jurisdiction. All figures are illustrative.

### Calibration

Mean sentence length is measured per unit and held inside a band: A2 8.5–12.5
words, B1 11.5–16.5, B2 15.0–21.0. Sentence caps are 26 words at A2, 34 at B1,
44 at B2, with over-cap sentences held below one per cent. No vocabulary term
is taught twice anywhere in the 1,200. No grammar point and no AI capability
repeats across the sixty units.
