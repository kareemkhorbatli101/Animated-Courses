## Unit 1 · Declaring an Incident Before You Are Sure

**Figure 1.0** — I AM DECLARING AN INCIDENT AT TEN PAST FOUR. I MAY BE WRONG.: Declare on what you have, and say plainly what you do not have.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is the threshold for declaring an incident where you work?
- Who may declare one at three in the morning?
- Who may stand it down again?

The decision to declare an incident is almost always taken on information that would not satisfy anybody in daylight, and the people who take it well are the people who say so in the declaration itself.

A declaration is not a finding about what has happened; it is a decision to bring people together, to stop certain kinds of work and to start keeping a record of what everybody does from that moment onwards.

So write it in three parts. What we know at this hour, which should be short and purely factual. What we have done at this hour, which should be shorter still. And what is not yet known, which will be the longest of the three and is the part that most declarations leave out.

Put the time of the declaration in the first line and your own name beside it, because the question of who declared an incident, and on what basis, is asked in every review that follows.

And say who may stand it down, since an incident that nobody is authorised to close remains open for a fortnight and consumes the attention that the next one will need.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: declaring on partial information: I, we, and what we know**

- Use I for the act of declaring, because the decision belongs to a person. I am declaring an incident at ten past four on the fourteenth of June.
- Use we for what the organisation has done, since the actions are collective. We have isolated the reporting server and suspended the overnight run.
- Use what we know and what is not yet known as headings, so that a reader at six in the morning can see immediately which is which.
- Use may for authority, not will. Only the duty director may stand this declaration down, and the standing-down is recorded with its time.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An incident declaration sorted into facts, actions and what is not yet known**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines. The fourth is the one a review looks for first.

An incident declaration sorted into facts, actions and what is not yet known is the document that lets a team at six in the morning see in twenty seconds what it is dealing with and what it is still guessing about.

Give the system the observations you have, the times on them and the systems they came from, the actions already taken with their hours, and the questions you cannot answer yet.

Ask it to keep what is known short and purely factual, because a declaration that begins with an interpretation fixes that interpretation in everybody’s mind for the rest of the week, however carefully it is qualified afterwards.

Ask it to make what is not yet known the longest section and to express each item as a question rather than a reassurance, since a declaration that reads as settled stops people looking and that is precisely what the first hour must not do.

Ask it to carry the hour of the declaration and the name of the person declaring in the first line, together with the role authorised to stand the declaration down. An incident nobody may close consumes a fortnight of attention that the next one will need.

What it must not do is characterise the event. Whether this is an intrusion, a fault or a misunderstanding is a finding that belongs to the people who will be accountable for it, and no declaration drafted at four in the morning should pre-empt them.

> **DATA POINT · Declaration thresholds** — Many organisations set a threshold at which an incident must be declared and name the roles that may declare and stand down. The threshold and the roles are set by the organisation’s own written procedures.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Thirty-one declarations and the hour they were made

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A health group examined 31 security incidents over four years and compared the hour at which each was declared with the hour at which the first observation had been made.

**A refusal to restore drafted with its reason and the route that remains**

**Figure 4.5** — Six lines. The third is the one that stops it being a wall.

A refusal to restore drafted with its reason and the route that remains is the message that turns an argument about whether into a conversation about what can be done by six.

Give the system what is being asked for, what you have not yet established, what the business actually needs to do in the next three hours, and what test would let you restore safely.

Ask it to refuse the specific thing with its hour rather than the general wish, because a refusal of restoration as a concept invites a debate you cannot win and does not help anybody at six o’clock.

Ask it to state the reason as a gap in knowledge rather than a risk in the abstract, since what has not been established can be established and a stated gap gives everybody something to work on.

**An urgent reply drafted with what is possible today**

A complaint about a control you designed arrives with a particular difficulty attached, which is that you are both the person who must answer it and the person least able to see it clearly.

So answer from the record. The access review was carried out on the ninth of May and covered eleven of the fifty-one privileged accounts, and the minute says so.

Then separate what the complainant has got right from what they have got wrong, and concede the first part before you correct the second, in that order and in that proportion.

Know the difference between a defence and an excuse. That the control was approved for eleven accounts is a fact about its scope; that nobody told you about the other forty is an explanation of your conduct and does not answer the complaint.

And where the complaint improves the control, say so and make the change, because a complainant who sees their point taken makes the next complaint earlier and in better faith.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A complaint about your own control answered from the record**

**Figure 6.5** — Six lines. The sixth is a deletion rather than an addition.

A complaint about your own control answered from the record is the letter that is about the control rather than about the person who designed it, which is harder to write than it sounds.

Give the system the complaint, the records of what the control covered and when it was approved, the minutes of any review, and your own honest assessment of which points are correct.

Ask it to concede what the complainant has got right before correcting anything, because a reader who meets a correction first reads everything afterwards as an argument and stops weighing it.

Ask it to cite a record for every correction, since a correction offered from memory in a formal answer is the single most common way in which an answer to a complaint becomes a second complaint.

Ask it to remove every sentence that explains your own conduct, resources or awareness, because those sentences are about you and the complaint is about the control, however much better they make you feel.

What it must not do is decide what the control should have covered. That is a judgement for whoever approved the scope, and an answer that quietly concedes a wider scope commits an organisation that has not agreed to it.

## Unit 7 · A Colleague Whose Practice Is Not Safe

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: raising a concern: what I saw, what I did, what I am asking for · AI: a concern about practice recorded and routed without a verdict in it*

**Figure 7.0** — WHAT I SAW. WHAT I DID. WHAT I AM ASKING FOR.: Report the practice. Someone else decides what it means.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When did you last worry about how a colleague was working?
- Did you say anything?
- What stopped you, or what made you?

Raising a concern about a colleague is the part of this work that people avoid most successfully, usually by telling themselves they are not certain enough yet.

Write what you saw yourself, in the plainest terms available, with the date and the time. On the ninth of June I saw a shared administrator password written on a card in the second drawer of the duty desk.

Keep what you were told in a separate sentence and label it as such, because a concern that mixes observation with corridor report is discounted entirely the moment one part of it turns out to be wrong.

**A concern about practice recorded and routed without a verdict in it**

**Figure 7.5** — Six lines. The sixth protects everybody, including you.

A concern about practice recorded and routed without a verdict in it is the document that lets somebody with fuller information decide what the practice means, which is the only honest thing a single observer can produce.

Give the system what you saw with the date and time, what you were told and by whom, what you did yourself, the request you are making, and whatever you know about the pressures the practice sits inside.

Ask it to separate observation from report and to mark the second as unconfirmed, because a concern in which one hearsay element proves false is discounted in its entirety, including the part you watched with your own eyes.

Ask it to state a specific request about the practice rather than about the person, since a concern with no request attached is processed as a complaint against an individual and a concern about a drawer becomes a concern about a man.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When was your organisation last inspected?

An inspection is a short period in which somebody with no stake in your organisation reads its records, and the organisations that come through it well are the ones that answer the question in front of them.

Answer what was asked. If the inspector asks when the access review was carried out, the answer is the ninth of May, and not a paragraph about the organisation’s approach to access management.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Has your organisation ever received a formal notification?

There is a moment when an incident stops being a technical matter and becomes a case, and it is usually the arrival of a letter with a reference number and a date by which something must be done.

Acknowledge it within the period stated, in three sentences. We acknowledge your notification of the fourteenth. We accept that it concerns the eleven thousand records described. We shall reply substantively by the fourth of September.

State the scope you accept and, separately, the scope you do not, because an acknowledgement that is silent about a disputed element is read as accepting it, and that reading will be put to you later.

Ask for an extension in writing if you need one, with the reason and the date you propose, since an extension requested on the telephone has no existence when somebody reconstructs the file in a year.

And from this point write everything as though it will be read aloud by somebody else in a room, because a good deal of it will be.

**A notification acknowledged with its scope and its timescale stated**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A decision taken alone reconstructed into a record with its basis**

**Figure 10.5** — Six lines written at a quarter past four, not on Tuesday.

A decision taken alone reconstructed into a record with its basis is the page that lets the decision be judged by what was known when it was taken, which is the only fair standard and the hardest one to preserve.

Give the system the hour of the decision, what you knew and did not know at that hour, what you actually relied on, whom you tried to reach, and what you have learned since.

Ask it to keep what you knew then and what you know now in separate paragraphs, because a record in which they are mingled cannot be used to defend the decision and will be read as a reconstruction.

Ask it to record the basis you actually used even where that basis was cruder than you would wish, since a polished justification is detectable against the timeline and contaminates every true sentence around it.

Ask it to name the person you would have consulted and why you could not, because the absence of a reachable second opinion at three in the morning is a finding about the rota and not about the person who was awake.

What it must not do is assess the decision. Whether it was right is a question for people who will have information you did not, and a record that argues its own case invites them to start by disbelieving it.
