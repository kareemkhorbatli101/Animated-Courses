# English for Cybersecurity — New Syria Edition · the spine

## Why this series exists

A defender has to speak before the evidence is complete.

That single fact reorganises everything about the language of the job. In
almost every other profession the professional waits until they know, and then
says it. In security the window in which a sentence still changes the outcome
closes while you are collecting the proof that would have made the sentence
comfortable to say. The attacker is already inside your timeline.

So the series is built around sentences that have to be said early, in writing,
on partial information:

- an escalation that asks one question rather than forwarding a file;
- a declaration made at four in the morning on three facts and one inference;
- a refusal to restore a system that is not yet known to be clean;
- a notification to the people whose data it was, in words they can repeat;
- an approval recorded as what the manager actually accepted;
- a record of what was checked, when, and by whom — never a claim that
  something is secure;
- and, at the end, the judgement no tool contains.

## Why government and private sector together

Because the work crosses that line a dozen times a week and the language
crosses with it. A ministry depends on a private hosting company. A bank
reports to a public regulator and buys its monitoring from a supplier who
cannot be telephoned at eight in the evening. A water authority and a
telecommunications operator share one fibre route and one set of consequences.
An analyst who can write to one of those readers and not the other is only half
trained.

So every book moves between the two. Books 1 and 2 are a private bank's first
line. Book 3 is a bank manager explaining risk upwards. Book 4 is a ministry
writing to suppliers and to a regulator. Book 5 is a private hospital group in
the worst week of its history, talking to a national centre. Book 6 is a
telecommunications operator answering a board, a regulator and the public.

## The series signature

Part 10 of every unit is **The Sentence You Must Say Before You Are Sure**: the
sentence that is awkward, necessary, and only useful while it can still change
what happens. It has a ladder, three turns, six holds, a redline and a score
sheet. In Book 1 it is as small as telling a senior manager you cannot reset his
password until you know who he is. By Book 5 it is telling a chief executive at
four in the morning that the patient records have to be treated as taken.

## Four standing rules

These hold in all sixty units and are enforced by the quality gates.

**1. No offensive content.** The series teaches defence as communication. No
exploit, no payload, no attack tooling, no evasion or bypass technique, no
malicious code, no working indicator of compromise and no configuration that
would function as an attack recipe appears anywhere. Where an incident is
described, it is described in terms of what was reported, what was observed and
what was decided — never in terms of how it was done. Every technical decision
is attributed to a named engineer who owns it.

**2. No claim that anything is secure.** A defender's sentence is what was
checked, when, by whom, and what was found — not an assurance. The books treat
"the system is secure" as a category error and teach the replacement.

**3. No politics, no real organisation.** Banks, ministries, operators,
authorities, suppliers, regulators and committees are invented. Cities are
real. Nothing in these pages describes the policy, capability or conduct of any
actual government or official, and no vendor, product or platform is named.

**4. Law and procedure are local.** Every unit that touches data protection,
breach notification, lawful access, procurement, audit, inspection or
professional duty says in its own text that local law and the organisation's
own written procedures govern it, and that the defender's job is to use the
route rather than to invent one. All sums of money are illustrative.
