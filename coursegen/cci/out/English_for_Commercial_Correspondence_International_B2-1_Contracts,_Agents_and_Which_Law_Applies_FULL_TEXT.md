### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many issues does your side put in one letter when negotiating?
- What do you ask for in return for a concession?
- How does a conditional sentence protect a position?

A negotiation conducted in writing between two parties whose first language is neither English nor each other’s is slower, clearer and far less forgiving than the same negotiation held in a room, and it rewards a different set of habits.

The first habit is sequencing. A position is not one letter but a planned series of them, each dealing with one issue, so that a concession on packing is not read as a concession on price merely because the two appeared in the same paragraph.

The second is the conditional. “We would be prepared to extend the warranty to twenty-four months if the order were increased to one hundred and twenty sets” offers something and withdraws nothing, and it can be repeated in the same words in six weeks.

The third is linkage; every concession is expressed as conditional upon something, because a concession given freely in a letter is read as a position that was never real, and it devalues everything that follows it.

Aurora writes one warm letter conceding price, payment terms, packing and delivery together, and the buyer, reading in his third language, takes the four as settled and opens on the warranty. Four positions have gone and the negotiation has not begun.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: register and distance: the conditional in a written negotiation**

- Would is the central verb of a written negotiation. It offers without committing: we would be prepared to extend the warranty; we would not wish to reopen the delivery term.
- Might lowers the commitment further and is useful for an exploratory point: it might be possible to review the packing specification.
- Should be glad to is a formal offer of willingness rather than of agreement: we should be glad to consider a longer credit period against a bank guarantee.
- Put the condition in the past subjunctive to mark it as hypothetical: if the order were increased to one hundred and twenty sets. That sentence can be written again, unchanged, six weeks later.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A position is a sequence, and every concession is linked**

**Figure 1.5** — Five exchanges, five requests, and the alternative is one letter and no requests.

A negotiating position written as a sequence of letters, not one letter, is a planning task before it is a drafting task, and the plan is what a system can hold in a way a busy negotiator cannot.

Give it your opening position, your reservation position on each issue, and the other side’s last letter; ask for a sequence of exchanges, one issue in each, in the order you should concede them.

Ask for each issue to be paired with what you would ask for in return, so that no exchange leaves your side holding nothing the other side wants — an unlinked concession is a position that was never real.

Ask for every offer to be drafted in the conditional, with the condition in the past subjunctive, so that the same sentence remains available if the exchange has to be repeated.

Ask it to check your draft for unconditional sentences, and to list them, and a single will or are in a letter otherwise written in would is the sentence the other side will quote back to you in August.

What no system can know is the other side’s authority limit, or whether the person writing to you can agree anything at all. That is a question to ask early, politely, and in writing, because a negotiation with somebody who cannot decide is a sequence of letters to nowhere.

> **DATA POINT · Written negotiation** — Negotiation practice commonly advises dealing with one issue in each written exchange, and expressing concessions as conditional upon a reciprocal movement, since written concessions are difficult to withdraw.  
> Source: general commercial negotiation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Concession linkage** — Negotiation research commonly finds that unreciprocated concessions reduce the perceived value of a negotiator’s remaining positions, and that written concessions are harder to recover than oral ones.  
> Source: general negotiation research literature, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · An Agency Agreement and What It Obliges

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: obligation, permission and prohibition: shall, may, shall not · AI: an agency agreement read for what it obliges you to do*

**Figure 2.0** — WE READ WHAT THE AGENT MUST DO AND SIGNED IT: An agency agreement for North Africa, divided into his obligations and ours.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who reads an agency agreement in your company before it is signed?
- Which obligations does your side take on?
- What would it cost to end the agreement in two years?

A principal reading an agency agreement reads the agent’s obligations, because that is what he is buying: a territory covered, a minimum achieved, reports delivered, a market developed.

The obligations that matter to him, however, are his own, and there are usually more of them than he expects. The duty to supply information, the duty to pay commission within a stated period, the duty to account, and in several legal systems a duty to compensate the agent on termination.

Two of these are easy to miss; the first is commission on direct sales: in an exclusive or sole agency, commission may be payable on orders the principal receives from the territory without the agent’s involvement at all.

The second is termination, and in a number of jurisdictions an agent who has built a market has a statutory entitlement on termination, calculated from past commission, and a clause saying otherwise does not always remove it.

Aurora signs an agency agreement for North Africa after a careful reading of the eleven obligations it imposes on Fatima Benali, and a quicker reading of the nine it imposes on Aurora. Two years later it discovers what the ninth of those nine is worth.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: obligation, permission and prohibition: shall, may, shall not**

- Shall creates an obligation on the party named as its subject: the principal shall pay commission within thirty days of receipt of payment.
- May creates a permission or a discretion: the agent may appoint a sub-agent with the principal’s written consent; either party may terminate on six months’ notice.
- Shall not creates a prohibition: the agent shall not sell competing products within the territory; the agent shall not hold himself out as able to bind the principal.
- Read every shall in an agreement twice, once for what it requires and once for who its subject is. A clause that reads naturally as an obligation on the agent sometimes has the principal as its subject.

**Read your own half of the agreement**

An agency agreement read for what it obliges you to do is a mirror-image reading, and it is the one reading almost nobody performs, because the natural question when appointing an agent is what the agent will do.

## Unit 3 · A Territory Two Agents Must Never Meet In

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: defining scope: in respect of, in relation to, for the purposes of · AI: a territory defined so that two agents never meet in it*

**Figure 3.0** — ONE BUYER, TWO AGENTS, BOTH ENTITLED: A re-exporter in a free zone, and the two agency agreements that both covered him.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How are your agents’ territories defined?
- What happens when a buyer in one territory ships into another?
- Who decides whose sale it is?

A territory described as North Africa, or as the Gulf, or as Central Europe is not a definition; it is a direction, and two agents given directions will eventually meet in the same account.

A territory is defined by a closed list of named countries, or by named administrative regions, and by one further thing: the test by which a particular sale is allocated.

There are four possible tests, and an agreement that does not choose between them has not defined anything — where is the buyer established? Where are the goods delivered? Where are they used? And who introduced him?

A buyer established in a free zone, taking delivery there and shipping the goods to a plant in another agent’s country, satisfies different tests for different agents, and both will invoice commission and both will be right.

Aurora defines two territories by region and signs both agreements in the same month. Eleven months later a re-exporter in a free zone produces a claim for commission from each of two agents on the same order, and the agreement provides no way to decide between them.

**A. Comprehension.**
0. Why is “North Africa” not a definition?  — *it is a direction, not a closed list*
1. What two things define a territory?

**Grammar: defining scope: in respect of, in relation to, for the purposes of**

- In respect of introduces the subject matter to which a provision applies: commission is payable in respect of sales to buyers established in the territory.
- In relation to is broader and is used for a connection rather than an identity: this clause applies in relation to orders received after 1 January.
- For the purposes of introduces a definition that governs only part of a document: for the purposes of this agreement, a buyer is established where he is registered.
- These three phrases are how scope is narrowed in a contract. A clause without one of them applies to everything its words can be read as covering, which is almost always more than was intended.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A direction is not a definition**

**Figure 3.5** — Four tests, four answers, and an agreement that chooses none of them.

A territory defined so that two agents never meet in it is a drafting problem with a short solution, and the solution is unpopular because it requires a decision in advance.

Give it both draft agreements, the list of countries each is meant to cover, and the house accounts you intend to keep; ask for each territory as a closed list of named jurisdictions, with nothing described by compass direction.

Then ask for one allocation test to be stated in both agreements, in the same words, and for the other three to be expressly excluded. A test chosen in one agreement and not the other is worse than no test at all.

Ask for a rule for overlaps: a split in stated proportions, and a named arbiter who decides when the proportions are disputed; the arbiter is a person in your own company, not a court.

Ask also for two cases that every agency agreement meets and few provide for: a buyer who approaches you directly through your own website, and a re-exporter whose place of use is unknown to anybody at the time of the order.

What no system can do is choose the test for you. Establishment favours agents with offices; use favours agents with engineers; introduction favours whoever moves first, and the choice shapes what kind of agent you will attract, and that is a commercial decision about your whole network.

> **DATA POINT · Territory definition** — Agency practice commonly recommends defining a territory by a closed list of named jurisdictions and specifying the connecting factor by which a sale is allocated, since several factors may point to different territories.  
> Source: general commercial agency guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Overlaps** — Agency practice commonly provides a stated split and a named decision-maker for sales connected with more than one territory, since a silent agreement leaves the principal exposed to both agents.

## Unit 4 · Standard Terms Against the Terms You Are Sent

**Figure 4.0** — HIS ORDER CARRIED HIS TERMS AND WE SHIPPED: Two sets of standard terms, never compared, and a shipment that chose between them.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Whose standard terms govern your export sales?
- What happens when a buyer’s order arrives with his own terms on the back?
- Who compares the two sets before you ship?

A sale between two companies is usually made on two sets of standard terms, each of which claims to govern it, and the buyer’s terms arrive with his order; the seller’s are printed on the confirmation. Neither party reads the other’s.

In several legal systems the question of which set governs turns on the last document sent and not objected to, and in others on the first. In nearly all of them, performing the contract without comment weakens a claim that your own terms applied.

The practical answer is not to win the battle of forms but to find out, before shipping, where the two sets differ and whether any difference matters.

Differences fall into three kinds. Conflicting clauses, where both sets deal with the same subject and say different things. Additional clauses, where one set covers something the other does not; silent subjects, where neither set says anything at all.

Aurora receives an order with the buyer’s terms on the reverse, sends its own confirmation, and ships — nobody compares the two. The buyer’s terms give him a right of rejection for eighteen months; Aurora’s give twelve, and the goods are rejected in month fifteen.

**Grammar: parallel drafting of clauses and sub-clauses**

- Parallel drafting means that clauses dealing with the same kind of thing take the same shape: the seller shall deliver; the buyer shall pay; either party may terminate.
- Keep the subject in the same position and the verb in the same form across a clause and its neighbours. A reader comparing two sets of terms compares shapes before meanings.
- Divide a clause into sub-clauses when it covers more than one case, and make the sub-clauses parallel too: sub-clause (a) covers damage; sub-clause (b) covers shortage.
- Use a defined term once and then everywhere. A document that says the Goods in clause 4 and the goods in clause 9 invites an argument about whether they are the same thing.

## Unit 5 · A Contract in Two Languages: Which One Prevails

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: equivalence and precedence: shall prevail, for the avoidance of doubt · AI: a bilingual contract checked clause against clause for the same meaning*

**Figure 5.0** — TWO VERSIONS, BOTH SIGNED, AND ONE WORD APART: A bilingual agency agreement, and the clause that means two different things.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Have you ever signed a contract in two languages?
- Which version prevailed?
- Who checked that the two said the same thing?

A contract signed in two languages is two contracts until something tells you which of them governs, and the clause that does so is one sentence long and it is the most important sentence in the document.

Without it, a court or a tribunal must reconcile two authentic texts, and it will do so by its own rules rather than by anybody’s intention; with it, one version governs and the other is a courtesy.

Naming a prevailing version does not remove the need to check the other. A buyer who signs a version he can read, and who is told afterwards that it does not govern, is a buyer with a grievance and a case about how the agreement was made.

The check is clause against clause, and the test is not whether the words are plausible but whether the obligation is the same. “Shall use reasonable endeavours” and a phrase meaning “is obliged” are both honest translations of each other to somebody who is not a lawyer.

Aurora signs a bilingual agency agreement in which the English says the agent shall endeavour to achieve the target and the other version says he is obliged to. The English prevails, and the agent has signed something he believes to be different.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: equivalence and precedence: shall prevail, for the avoidance of doubt**

- Shall prevail establishes precedence between two provisions or two texts: in the event of any discrepancy, the English version shall prevail.
- Say what it prevails over and in what circumstances. A clause saying merely that English prevails leaves open whether it prevails over the schedules and over later amendments.
- For the avoidance of doubt introduces a clarification of something already stated, and never a new obligation. If the words that follow it add a duty, the clause is drafted wrongly.
- Save as provided and subject to are the two ordinary ways of qualifying a statement of precedence: both texts are authentic, save as provided in clause 1.3.

## Unit 6 · Which Law, Which Court, Which Language

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: jurisdiction and procedure: governed by, submitted to, in the English language · AI: a dispute clause read for where, under what law, in which language*

**Figure 6.0** — A CLAUSE THAT CHOSE A LAW AND FORGOT THE COURT: A dispute clause in four parts, with two of the four left blank.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does your standard dispute clause say?
- Where would an award against your buyer have to be enforced?
- In what language would the proceedings be conducted?

A dispute clause answers four separate questions, and a clause that answers fewer than four is not a clause but a hope; which law governs the contract. Who decides a dispute, and where they sit. And in what language they work.

The four are independent; a contract may be governed by the law of one country, submitted to arbitration seated in a second, administered by an institution based in a third, and conducted in a language belonging to none of them.

The question that decides whether any of it is worth having is enforcement — a judgment or an award is a piece of paper until a court where the other party keeps its assets is willing to give effect to it.

Arbitral awards travel better than court judgments between most pairs of countries, because a widely adhered convention provides for their recognition. That is the practical reason arbitration appears in international contracts so often, and it has nothing to do with privacy.

And some rules survive any choice. A mandatory local rule protecting an agent on termination, or a local registration requirement, may apply whatever law the contract chooses, which is why the clause must be read together with the law of the place where the work is done.

## Unit 7 · Force Majeure Along a Supply Chain

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Does your standard contract contain a force majeure clause?
- How long does it give you to give notice?
- Who writes the notice while the event is still happening?

A force majeure clause does not excuse a failure; it suspends an obligation, on conditions, for as long as a stated kind of event prevents performance, and almost all of those conditions are procedural.

The first condition is a notice, given within a stated period of the party becoming aware of the event — a notice given after the event has passed is usually ineffective, however genuine the event was.

The second is a causal link, and the party relying on the clause must show that the event prevented performance rather than merely coinciding with a failure that had other causes.

The third is mitigation; a clause almost always requires reasonable efforts to overcome the event, and a party that made none has a weaker claim than one that tried and failed in writing.

And along a chain the clause must work twice. An upstream failure is not automatically force majeure downstream, unless the contracts are written back to back, so a supplier’s notice to you does not by itself excuse your notice to your own buyer.

**Grammar: exceptions and provisos: notwithstanding, save as provided, except where**

- Notwithstanding overrides what has gone before: notwithstanding clause 14, the buyer shall remain liable for sums already due. It is the strongest of the three and should be used sparingly.
- Save as provided introduces a qualification by cross-reference: save as provided in clause 14.3, obligations are suspended for the duration of the event.
- Except where introduces a condition in which a provision does not apply: relief is available except where the event was foreseeable at the date of the contract.
- In a force majeure clause these three carry almost all the commercial weight, because the clause works by excluding things: foreseeable events, market conditions, and failures with another cause.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The notice is the one thing that cannot be done late**

**Figure 7.5** — Four things that must exist while the event runs, and two that can follow.

A force majeure notice drafted while the event is still running is a short document that almost nobody writes at the right time, because the right time is the week in which everybody is busy with the event itself.

Give it the clause, the date on which you first knew of the event, the obligations affected and the mitigation steps so far; ask for a notice of four short paragraphs, dated today.

Ask it to quote the clause’s own notice period and to state in the notice itself the date on which you became aware. A notice that does not say when you knew invites an argument about whether it was in time.

Then ask for the running record: a dated line for each day of the event, naming what was tried and what it would cost, and that record is the mitigation evidence, and it cannot be made afterwards.

Ask also for the chain question. Does our clause treat a supplier’s failure as a relieving event, and is the supplier’s clause back to back with ours? Where the answer is no, a supplier’s notice is information and not relief.

What no system can decide is whether to buy the alternative at forty per cent more. That is a commercial choice between a cost now and a claim later, and it belongs to somebody who knows what the customer is worth.

> **DATA POINT · Force majeure** — Contract practice commonly conditions relief under a force majeure clause on timely notice, proof of causation and reasonable efforts to mitigate, and treats upstream failure as relief only where the contracts are written back to back.  
> Source: general commercial contract commentary, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Notice periods** — Contract commentary commonly observes that failure to give notice within a stated period is among the most frequent reasons relief under a force majeure clause is refused, irrespective of the genuineness of the event.

Two quite different restrictions govern what may leave a company as an attachment, and they are frequently confused — the first is contractual: a confidentiality obligation owed to somebody, often a third party whose drawing or specification you hold.

**Grammar: conditions precedent and permission to disclose**

- A condition precedent is expressed with until, before or it is a condition precedent that: no information shall be disclosed until the undertaking has been signed.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: summary and minute: it was agreed, it was noted, action by**

- The minuting passive removes the speaker and keeps the status: it was agreed that …, it was noted that …, it was deferred to … . The form is conventional and understood across markets, which is its value.
- Use it was agreed only for a decision both sides made. Use it was noted for something one side stated and the other recorded: Kiyomizu noted that its budget year ends in March commits nobody to anything.

Three things happen in a negotiation conducted between two second languages, and a competent record keeps them in three columns. Something is agreed; something is translated; and a great deal is merely discussed.

Only the first column binds anybody, and it binds only where the person speaking had authority to bind the party. A purchasing manager’s enthusiasm is not a board resolution, and a sales engineer cannot vary a payment term by saying it would be fine.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: distinguishing agreement from discussion in a written record**

- The grammar of non-commitment is explicit and conventional. This document is issued subject to contract and creates no obligation. This exchange is without prejudice. Nothing in this letter binds either party.
- The grammar of commitment is equally explicit and names the instrument: clause 7 of the agreement of 4 October provides that … . Not “we agreed that”, which is a memory, but “clause 7 provides”, which is a document.
- Where a term was concluded through an interpreter, record both facts: the term was agreed in English; the interpreter’s rendering into Japanese was “…”. The record states the two and resolves neither.
- Authority is stated, not assumed: signed by Dr Lindqvist, who holds authority to bind Aurora Instruments. Where authority is doubtful, say so in the record rather than in a dispute two years later.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Three columns, and the honesty is in the third**

**Figure 10.5** — Six items, three columns, and only two of them bind anybody.

A record of what was agreed, what was translated, and what was only discussed is the document a negotiation should end with, and almost never does; the file ends instead as a chronology, which answers when but not whether.

Give it the whole file: counterparts, variations, letters of intent, translated texts, emails and your own notes of meetings, and ask for every item to be placed in one of the three columns with the reason stated.

Ask for the agreed column to name the instrument and the clause for each term, and to name the signatory and state whether authority to bind was verified. An unverified signature is a fact to record, not a gap to fill.

Ask for the translated column to say which text was concluded in which language, which translations were confirmed, and where a rendering differs from the concluded text. A difference found now is a correction; found in two years it is a dispute.

Ask for the discussed column to be long and unapologetic. Letters of intent, dinners, indications and courtesies belong there however much work went into them, and a record that promotes them is not a kindness.

What no system can decide is whether a person had authority to bind a company in his own market. It can ask the question on every signature, which is most of the value, and the answer comes from the party itself.

> **DATA POINT · Three columns** — Contract practice commonly distinguishes binding terms from non-binding discussion, treats subject-to-contract and without-prejudice markings as evidence of that intention, and records the language in which a term was concluded.  
> Source: general contract and negotiation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Authority** — Commercial practice commonly treats verification of a signatory’s authority as part of contract formation, and records the language in which each text was concluded where more than one is in use.  
> Source: general contract administration guidance, 2025–2026.
