### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: quantities and packing units: cartons, pallets, cases and sets**

- Count packing units as ordinary plurals: one carton, two cartons; one pallet, three pallets; one case, four cases.
- Name what is inside a unit: a set of six gauges; a carton of twelve sets; a pallet of forty-eight cartons.
- Use per for a rate and each for one of a group: the price is per set; each carton carries the order number.
- Give a quantity in the unit the order uses. If the order says sets, your confirmation says sets, and the conversion into pieces is shown beside it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Every line where the answer differs is a new agreement**

**Figure 1.5** — Two moved lines out of thirteen, and one of them is a date in digits.

A quotation and an order compared line by line across two date formats is the first check of every order. A system does it faster than a tired reader, and more honestly.

Give it both documents. Ask for a table of three columns: the line, what the quotation said, and what the order says. Identical lines are marked as identical.

The important instruction is about dates. Tell it to treat a date in digits as unresolved, and to report both possible readings rather than choosing one.

A system that converts 06/04 into the sixth of April has made a commercial decision. One that reports “6 April or 4 June” has done the job.

Ask it also to flag any line present in one document and absent from the other. An added line is as binding as a changed one, and it is easier to miss.

What it cannot tell you is which difference matters. Sixty sets instead of forty is welcome; two months earlier is a production decision, and that is yours.

> **DATA POINT · Order checking** — Export practice commonly recommends comparing a purchase order against the quotation line by line before acknowledgement, since any difference becomes binding once the order is accepted.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Changed order lines** — Contract practice commonly treats acknowledgement of an order as acceptance of its terms, so that a difference from the quotation becomes binding unless it is raised before acknowledgement.  
> Source: general commercial contract guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Do your customers write to you in their own language or in English?
- What do you do when a sentence has two meanings?

An order arrives from Surabaya. It is nine lines long. The English is not perfect and the buyer is not apologising for that, nor should he.

Line four says: “Please send the same as before but better quality.” Three things in that sentence have no fixed meaning.

The buyer is not writing badly. He is writing in his third language, quickly, to a supplier he trusts. The reading is the supplier’s job, and the readback is how it is done.

**Grammar: imperatives and short noun phrases for labels and marks**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A refusal is a sentence, not a tone**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines, and only one of them is the no.

A refusal written so that the enquiry comes back next year is a short document with a fixed shape, and a system will hold the shape better than a nervous writer.

Give it the request, the limit and the one alternative you recommend. Ask for six lines in the order above, and ask for the refusal to be in line two.

Then give it the prohibition that matters here: no hedging words. No “difficult”, no “perhaps”, no “we shall try”. Ask for unable, regret or not in a position.

Ask for the alternative to carry a figure and a date, because an alternative without numbers is another hedge wearing a different coat.

What a system cannot judge is how a no will land with one particular buyer. Some markets refuse indirectly and expect you to as well, and only your agent knows which.

**The amount received is a different number from the amount sent**

**Figure 8.5** — Three banks, thirty-eight euros, and a shortfall letter nobody should have written.

Bank charges allocated so that the amount received is the amount invoiced is arithmetic plus one sentence of drafting, and the sentence is the part that matters.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: time expressions for chasing: still, yet, as of today, since**

- Still means a situation continues, and goes before the main verb: we have still not received your order.
- Yet is used with a negative or a question and goes at the end: we have not received your order yet. Have you decided yet?
- As of today fixes the moment of writing: as of today the quotation is still open.
- Since gives the starting point of a period: we have heard nothing since 4 April. Use a date, not “for a while”.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: adverbs of degree and warmth in thanks: particularly, especially**

- Particularly and especially strengthen one thing out of several, and go before the word or phrase they strengthen: thank you particularly for marking the pallets.
- Most and very strengthen an adjective: we are most grateful; thank you very much.
- Keep the adverb next to what it refers to. “We were especially glad to receive the advice early” praises the earliness, not the advice.
- Use one adverb at a time. Two warm adverbs in one sentence sound less sincere than none, especially to a reader in a second language.
