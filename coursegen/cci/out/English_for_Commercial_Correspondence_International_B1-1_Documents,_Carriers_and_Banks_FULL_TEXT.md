### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which trade terms does your company quote on?
- Do you name the place after the term?
- Who carries the risk between your gate and the ship?

A trade term is three letters and a place. The three letters say who arranges and pays for each stage of the journey. The place says where one party’s responsibility ends and the other’s begins.

Those two things are not the same, and the difference is where most disputes live. A seller may be obliged to pay for carriage to a port and yet carry no risk for the goods once they are on board.

Aurora quotes a buyer in Durban on a term that transfers risk when the goods are loaded on board at Rotterdam. Rain gets into a container during a four-day stop at an inland depot, before loading.

The damage is eleven thousand euros. The buyer says the goods were not yet his. He is right, because the term says risk passes on loading and loading had not happened.

A term without a place is not a term at all. “Delivered” to where? “Free on board” at which port? And which version of the rules, since they are revised and the old editions stay in use for years.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the point of transfer: prepositions of cost, risk and place**

- Use on for the moment of an event that transfers risk: risk passes on loading on board; the goods are at the buyer’s risk on delivery at the named place.
- Use for with the party who bears a cost: the terminal handling charge is for the buyer’s account; export clearance is for the seller.
- Use from and after for everything beyond the point of transfer: costs after that point are for the buyer; the goods travel at his risk from then.
- Use at with a place and to with a destination: delivery takes place at the named place; the seller pays for carriage to the named port.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Three letters, a place and an edition, on the same line**

**Figure 1.5** — Six elements, and a term missing any of the first three is an argument waiting.

A trade term explained as the point where cost and risk change hands is the most useful thing a system can do for a correspondent who is not a lawyer.

Give it the term, the named place and the edition. Ask for two separate lists: every cost element and which party bears it, and the single moment at which risk passes.

Keeping those two lists apart is the whole lesson. A seller who has paid for carriage feels that the goods are still his, and under many terms they are not.

Ask it also to name the obligations that are often assumed and not implied: who clears for export, who clears for import, who insures, and to what level of cover.

Then ask the question that catches most errors. Which of these obligations does our quotation not mention at all? A term is a shorthand, and a shorthand only works when both readers hold the same key.

What it cannot tell you is which term to quote. That is a commercial decision about price, about who can clear goods in that market, and about which party can actually insure them.

> **DATA POINT · Trade terms** — International trade practice commonly requires a trade term to be quoted with a named place and the edition of the rules relied upon, since the allocation of cost and of risk differs between terms and between editions.  
> Source: general international trade practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Editions** — Trade documentation practice commonly recommends stating the edition of the trade term rules on commercial documents, since successive editions have altered the allocation of obligations under some terms.  
> Source: general trade documentation guidance, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · Sea, Air, Road and Rail: Choosing and Saying Why

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: comparison of options: whereas, in contrast, on the other hand · AI: four modes of transport compared on cost, time and what can go wrong*

**Figure 2.0** — AIR WAS FOUR TIMES THE PRICE AND SAVED THE ORDER: Four modes compared for one consignment to Mumbai, on three measures each.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which mode does your company use most?
- When was the last time you changed mode for one order?
- What goes wrong most often with your usual mode?

A consignment of ninety sets is wanted in Mumbai. There are four ways to send it and each has a different answer on cost, on time and on what can go wrong.

Sea freight is the cheapest by a long way. It is also the slowest, and the transit time is not the whole story: a transhipment at a hub adds days that no schedule shows.

Air freight costs about four times as much. In contrast, it is measured in days rather than weeks, and the chargeable weight is calculated from volume as well as mass, which surprises people who send light, bulky goods.

Road haulage is competitive within a region and useless between continents, whereas rail freight is cheap, steady and limited to the corridors where a service actually runs.

The choice is never only about price. It is about the buyer’s own deadline, the value of the goods, the cost of being late, and which mode fails in the way you can best survive.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: comparison of options: whereas, in contrast, on the other hand**

- Whereas joins two contrasting facts inside one sentence: sea freight is cheap, whereas air freight is fast.
- In contrast begins a new sentence that opposes the one before it: Air costs four times as much. In contrast, it takes four days rather than four weeks.
- On the other hand introduces the second side of a balance, and needs a first side already stated: rail is cheap and steady. On the other hand, it runs only where there is a corridor.
- However is the general contrast marker and goes after a comma or a semicolon. Do not use two contrast markers in the same sentence.

**Compare on the same three measures, or you are not comparing**

## Unit 3 · The Bill of Lading and the Air Waybill

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: relative clauses in the description of goods and documents · AI: a transport document checked against the shipment it describes*

**Figure 3.0** — THE PAPER THAT IS THE GOODS: An original set of bills of lading, and the container they stand for.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who in your company handles the original transport documents?
- What happens if one is lost?
- Does a copy give anybody any rights?

A bill of lading does three things at once. It is a receipt for the goods, it is evidence of the contract of carriage, and it is a document of title.

The third is the one that matters in a dispute. A party who holds an original bill of lading can call for the goods, which means that the paper is, for commercial purposes, the goods themselves.

An air waybill does the first two and not the third. It is not a document of title, and the goods are released to the consignee named on it, whoever holds the paper.

So the two documents are used differently. A seller shipping against a credit keeps control of a container by controlling the original set; a seller sending goods by air has released control the moment the consignee is named.

Both documents must then agree with the shipment they describe: the container number, the seal number, the number of packages, the weights and the description of the goods. A document which contradicts the packing list is worse than no document, because a bank will reject it and a carrier will rely on it.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: relative clauses in the description of goods and documents**

- A defining relative clause says which one, and takes no commas: a bill of lading which carries a clause is not a clean bill.
- A non-defining relative clause adds information about something already identified, and takes commas: the goods, which are packed in sixty cartons, weigh 980 kilos.
- Use who for people, which or that for things, and whose for possession: the consignee whose name appears on the waybill takes delivery.
- In a document, prefer the defining clause and drop the commas. A comma in a description can change what the sentence covers, and a bank reads it literally.

**Grammar: obligation by market: is required to, must be accompanied by**

- Is required to introduces an obligation imposed from outside, followed by a verb: each consignment is required to carry a certificate of origin.
- Must be accompanied by is followed by a noun and is the standard wording for documents travelling with goods: the goods must be accompanied by a test certificate.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who in your company reads a credit when it arrives?
- What do you look for first?
- What happens if you cannot meet a condition in it?

A documentary credit is a bank’s promise to pay against documents. It is not a promise to pay against goods, and the distinction is the whole of this unit.

Read a credit once for the money and twice for the conditions. The amount, the currency and the payment basis take a minute. The conditions take an hour and decide whether you are paid.

Four dates govern every presentation: the latest date for shipment, the number of days you have to present after shipment, the expiry date of the credit, and the place where it expires. A credit expiring abroad is a credit whose last day is not the day you post the documents.

Then look for the conditions you cannot perform yourself. A credit calling for an inspection certificate signed by the applicant puts your payment inside the buyer’s diary. The banks call such a line a soft clause, and it is as dangerous as it sounds.

The remedy is never to ship and hope. It is to ask for an amendment, in writing, before the goods move, and to say plainly which line you cannot satisfy and what you propose instead.

- Must states an obligation on the beneficiary: documents must be presented within twenty-one days of shipment.

- Shall be presented is the formal passive of a credit’s own drafting, and is read as a requirement rather than a future: a full set of bills of lading shall be presented.
- None of these is a suggestion. When a credit says is to be, treat it as must, and if you cannot comply, ask for an amendment rather than arguing about the verb.

## Unit 6 · A Discrepancy, and What It Costs by the Day

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: quantified consequence: per day, per container, up to a maximum of · AI: a set of documents checked against the credit, field by field*

**Figure 6.0** — ONE WORD WRONG, AND A CONTAINER EARNING NOTHING: A refused presentation, and the charges that run while it is argued about.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What happens in your company when a bank refuses documents?
- Who decides what to do next?
- What is running while the decision is taken?

A discrepancy is a difference between what a credit asks for and what was presented. It may be a missing notation, a goods description that does not match the credit, or a date that is one day late.

The fee for a refusal is small, often under a hundred euros. The real cost is time, and time is charged by somebody else, usually per container and per day.

A container sitting at a destination port accrues demurrage after its free period. Equipment held beyond that accrues detention. Storage runs on goods out of the container. None of these stops because a bank is checking a word.

There are three routes out. Correct the documents and present them again, if there is time. Ask the buyer to accept the discrepancy and his bank to waive it. Or abandon the credit and send the documents on a collection basis, which means the bank’s promise is gone.

Each route has a cost and a number of days, and choosing between them is arithmetic. The wrong choice is the one taken on the fourth day, after three days of hoping the bank was mistaken.

A set of documents checked against the credit, field by field, is the one task in commercial correspondence where a machine is simply better than a person, because the work is comparison without interpretation.

**Grammar: indirect questions for enquiries to an authority**

- An enquiry to an authority uses an indirect question inside a statement, and ends with a full stop: we should be grateful if you would advise whether the instrument falls under heading 9026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: exclusion and limitation: except, excluding, save for**

- Except introduces an exception to something already stated: the cover is all risks except the exclusions listed below.
- Excluding introduces what is left out of a list, and sits close to the list: all perils are covered, excluding war and strikes.
- Save for is the formal equivalent of except for, and is used in policies and contracts: the goods are insured, save for loss by inherent vice.
- Place the exclusion where it cannot attach to the wrong noun. “All cartons except those repacked at destination” is clear; “all cartons repacked except at destination” is not.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The exclusions are the policy**

**Figure 9.5** — Six things to read, and the premium is not among them.

An insurance certificate read for what it excludes is a short piece of work with a large return, and the instruction is to read the document backwards.

Give it the certificate and the policy conditions. Ask for the exclusions first, in plain words, with an example of a loss each one would refuse.

Then ask for the three numbers: the deductible, the insured value basis, and the notification period in days from a named event. A notification period from arrival is not the same as one from discovery.

Ask it to compare the exclusions against your own packing specification, and to name any exclusion that your current packing could trigger. That is the one output that changes what the factory does.

What it cannot do is tell you what this underwriter expects. Ask him, in one letter, what packing he considers sufficient for the route, and keep the answer in the file with the policy.

And notify every possible loss on the day you hear of it, in three lines, before you know anything. A notification costs nothing and a late claim costs everything.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the past perfect for a chronology of events**

- Use the past perfect for an event that happened before another past event: by the time we telephoned, the booking had already been rolled.
- It is the tense of a chronology, because a chronology is always written looking back from a later point: the broker told us that an inspection had been ordered.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The chronology is the claim**

**Figure 10.5** — Nineteen days in five parts, and only three of them were ever Aurora’s.

A chronology built from emails after a container stops moving is a task a system does extremely well, because the material is messages with dates on them and the output is an ordered list.

Give it every message, in any order, from every party. Ask for one line per event with the date, the hour, the event, the party who stated it, and the message or document that proves it.

Insist that nothing without a source becomes an entry. A telephone call with no note is not an event; it is a memory, and it will be contradicted by somebody else’s memory.

Ask for gaps to be shown as gaps. “Days seven to nine: no entry” is more useful than a smooth narrative, because it tells you where to go and ask.

Then ask for the days to be grouped by cause, and for each group to be marked as ours, the carrier’s, the buyer’s or nobody’s. That grouping is the claim, and it is also the defence.

What no system can do is make the entries exist. If nobody wrote a line on day four, no amount of cleverness will recover day four, and nineteen days of charges will be settled by whoever argues best.

> **DATA POINT · Chronologies** — Claims practice commonly relies on a contemporaneous dated record of events, since a reconstruction made after the event carries less weight and may be incomplete.  
> Source: general transport claims guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Contemporaneous records** — Dispute practice commonly gives greater weight to records made at the time of an event than to later reconstructions, and treats unsourced recollection as of little evidential value.  
> Source: general commercial dispute guidance, 2025–2026.
