### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the present simple for what a company does and sells**

- Use the present simple for what is always or usually true: Aurora Instruments makes measuring instruments.
- With he, she, it and a company name, add -s: the company sells, the office opens, the factory closes.
- Make questions and negatives with do and does: do you export? the office does not open on Sunday.
- This is the tense of a company profile, a product description and the first paragraph of almost every commercial letter in the world.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An enquiry is questions, guesses, and one line nobody meant that way**

**Figure 1.5** — Four questions, two guesses and one line to ask about again.

A short enquiry can be read by a system in a second, and the list that comes back is usually right about the questions that were asked.

The second list is the useful one. Ask for the things the buyer has assumed but not asked. That you export to his country. That you hold stock. That you accept a certain payment.

Then ask for a third list, and this is the list only an international correspondent needs: the lines that could mean two things in English.

Both of you are working in a second language. “For the end of month” is not bad English; it is English written quickly by somebody whose own language says it differently.

Those lines cost orders. A supplier who chooses one meaning and ships on that day has a claim on his desk a month later, and the buyer is not wrong.

So the instruction is specific: list every question, list every assumption, and mark every line that has more than one reading. Then write one reply that covers all three lists, and keep the present simple for what your company does.

> **DATA POINT · Enquiries** — Trade practice commonly treats a first enquiry as the most valuable message a company receives, and recommends that every question in it be answered in the first reply, since a second exchange loses many buyers.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · First replies** — Export practice commonly recommends that a first reply state what the supplier does, answer every question asked, and give a date for anything that cannot be answered at once.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which part of your name is your family name?
- Does it come first or last when you write it?
- What do people abroad get wrong about your name?

A buyer writes from Osaka. He signs his email “Tanabe Hiroshi”. The signature block under it says “Hiroshi Tanabe, Purchasing Manager”.

Both are correct. In Japan the family name usually comes first. When he writes in English he turns it round, because that is what English-speaking readers expect.

Four people at Aurora Instruments enter him in the system. One writes Mr Hiroshi. One writes Mr Tanabe Hiroshi. One writes H. Tanabe-san. One writes Mr Tanabe.

Only the last one is right. Mr Hiroshi uses the given name, which is wrong. Adding a courtesy title from his language to one from ours uses two titles for one person.

The rule is short: write a person’s name the way that person writes it, and copy the spelling exactly. You do not have to understand a naming system to copy one.

**A name is not a word to be improved**

**Figure 2.5** — Four entries, one buyer, and three letters that will not be opened by the right person.

A name and a title written in the order its owner writes them is a small piece of work. A system does it well, provided you give it the signature block and nothing else.

**The sequence belongs to the country that has to deliver it**

**Figure 3.5** — Five patterns, one constant: the country is last and in English.

An address laid out the way the destination country lays out addresses is quick work for a system. The patterns are published, and there are only a few dozen of them.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A converted price without a rate and a date is a guess with a decimal point**

**Figure 5.5** — Two of these six lines can be read only one way, and both of them name a date.

A price restated in the buyer’s currency with the rate and the date named is arithmetic. A system does arithmetic without error once you have given it the right three inputs.

The three are the amount with its currency code, the rate as a number of four decimal places, and the date on which that rate was taken. Leave out the date and the conversion means nothing a month later.

Ask for the output in a fixed shape: the original amount, the converted amount, the rate, the date, and the validity period in days. Ask for the rounding rule to be stated as well.

Rounding is not a detail at scale. Four hundred units rounded up at the second decimal place is a different invoice from four hundred units rounded down.

What it cannot do is choose the rate. A bank’s selling rate, a mid-market rate and the rate your buyer’s bank will use on the day of payment are three different numbers.

So the rule for the correspondent is short. Convert if the buyer asks, name the rate and the day, say how long it holds, and keep the original currency on the same line.

> **DATA POINT · Price quotations** — Export practice commonly recommends that a quotation state the currency by its three-letter code, give the amount in words where there is any risk of misreading, and state a validity period.  
> Source: general export quotation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Currency statements** — Banking and invoicing practice commonly expects a converted amount to be accompanied by the rate used and the date of that rate, so that the calculation can be reproduced.  
> Source: general invoicing and banking guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A reply window is a promise about two clocks**

**Figure 8.5** — Two sending hours out of six reach the buyer on the day they were written.

A reply window calculated from two time zones, not one, is simple arithmetic, and it is the arithmetic almost nobody does before promising an answer.

Give it your office hours with your zone, the buyer’s office hours with his, and the date. Ask for the overlap in both local times, and for the last hour at which a same-day reply is still possible.

Then ask a second question: at what hour must a message leave here to be read before the buyer’s day ends? That hour is your real deadline, and it is usually earlier than you think.

Ask also for the two dates on which each country changes its clocks. They are not the same dates, and for two or three weeks a year the difference between you is an hour out.

What no system can tell you is whether the person is at his desk. An overlap is an office hour, not a promise about a human being.

So write the promise in his clock as well as yours. When you cannot answer inside the overlap, send a holding reply that names the hour the real answer will come.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: questions in writing: direct and indirect forms**

- A direct question inverts the verb and ends with a question mark: what is your preferred port? does the price include packing?
- An indirect question sits inside a statement and keeps normal word order: please let us know which port you prefer. We should like to know whether packing is included.
- Use whether, or if, for a yes-or-no question inside a statement. Never use a question mark at the end of an indirect question.
- Indirect forms are politer. Direct forms are harder to miss. With a reader in a third language, choose direct, and put one question in one short sentence.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the future with will and the present for fixed arrangements**

- Use will for a promise made as you write: we will send the test report today; I will confirm the port tomorrow.
- Use the present simple for a fixed arrangement, especially a timetable: the container sails on 4 May; the inspector visits on Tuesday.
- Use the present simple for standing facts about the company: our works open at eight; we hold stock in Porto.
- In a quotation, prefer the present for anything already arranged. It reads as a fact rather than an intention, and a buyer abroad reads intentions carefully.
