### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who opens a complaint in your company?
- What is the first thing you write in reply?
- How often does a complaint come back a second time?

A complaint has three parts, and they are usually tangled together in one paragraph. There are the facts the customer states. There is the loss he says he has suffered. And there is the thing he is asking you to do.

Most replies answer the first part. They explain the fault, apologise for it, and say nothing about the money or the request. The customer then writes again, and the second letter is colder than the first.

A buyer in Rotterdam writes that eleven instruments read two per cent high, that his own customer has rejected a batch worth four thousand euros, and that he would like the eleven replaced and the rejected batch credited.

Aurora’s reply explains calibration drift at length. It does not mention four thousand euros and it does not say whether eleven instruments will be replaced.

The reply is technically excellent and commercially silent, which is the commonest failure in this whole book. Answer the facts, answer the loss, answer the request, in that order, and in the first letter.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: reported complaints: states that, alleges that, maintains that**

- Use states that to report a factual assertion neutrally: the buyer states that eleven instruments read two per cent high.
- Use alleges that for an assertion you have not accepted and may dispute: he alleges that the fault arose before delivery.
- Use maintains that for a position repeated after it has been questioned: he maintains that no handling damage occurred.
- Use claims for a loss and asks for a remedy: he claims a loss of four thousand euros and asks that the batch be credited. None of these words admits or denies anything.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Answer the facts, the loss and the request, in that order**

**Figure 1.5** — Six sentences, three parts, and the sixth is the one the reply ignored.

A complaint sorted into fact, loss and what is being asked for is a short piece of reading, and the value is in being forced to see all three.

Give it the complaint and ask for every sentence to be placed in one of three columns: the facts stated, the loss claimed with its amount, and the remedy requested.

Then ask for a fourth list, which is the one that matters: anything in the letter that is neither fact, loss nor remedy, and might be the real subject. A customer’s last line often carries it.

Ask it to leave every fact in the customer’s own words and to mark each as accepted, disputed or not yet checked. Do not let a draft reply admit or deny anything before the works has looked.

Then write the reply in the order of the three columns, with the remedy first if you have decided it, and a date if you have not. An undecided claim with a date is an answer; an undecided claim in silence is a second complaint.

What no system can see is what a complaint is really about. A buyer whose own customer is reviewing his suppliers is not asking you about calibration drift, whatever his first paragraph says.

## Unit 2 · A Claim Built on Photographs, Weights and Dates

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: evidence and attribution: according to, as shown by, per the record · AI: a claim assembled with photographs, weights, dates and a survey*

**Figure 2.0** — A GOOD CLAIM AND NO PHOTOGRAPH OF THE CASE NUMBER: A claim file for a shortage at Durban, and the one photograph nobody took.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who takes the photographs when goods arrive damaged?
- What has to be visible in them?
- How long after arrival can a claim still be made?

A claim is a file, not a letter. The letter is one page at the front, and everything that decides the claim sits behind it.

Behind it there should be photographs, a weighbridge ticket, a tally sheet, the transport document, the packing list, an invoice copy, and a survey report where the value justifies one.

A photograph is evidence only if it shows three things: the damage, something giving scale, and the package number or case mark. A photograph of a crushed instrument proves that an instrument was crushed, and nothing about which consignment it came from.

Aurora’s claim at Durban has eleven photographs of damage and none showing a case mark. The carrier does not dispute the damage. It disputes that the damage happened to its container, and the file cannot answer.

Every statement in the letter should be attributed: according to the tally sheet, as shown by photograph four, per the weighbridge ticket of 14 June. A claim with no attribution is an opinion with an invoice attached.

**Grammar: evidence and attribution: according to, as shown by, per the record**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A claim is a file, and it is built on the first day**

**Figure 2.5** — Five items that can only be taken at the time, and one that can be found afterwards.

A claim assembled with photographs, weights, dates and a survey is an index and a letter, and a system is good at both once the evidence exists.

Give it everything in the file and ask for a numbered evidence index, then a letter in which every factual sentence is attributed to a numbered item.

Ask it to list what is missing, judged against the claim it is making. A shortage claim needs a tally; a damage claim needs photographs that identify the package; a weight claim needs a ticket.

That missing list is the output that matters, and it has a hard limit. Most of a claim file can only be created on the day of arrival, and no amount of later work replaces it.

Ask for the quantum to be built from documents: invoice value, freight, duty where it is not recoverable, and the repair estimate or the salvage value. Every line attributed, nothing rounded up.

And ask for the deadline. Carriers and insurers each have their own, counted from different events, and a claim filed a day late is not a weak claim but no claim at all.

Granting credit to a company you cannot visit is a problem of evidence, not of judgement. The judgement is easy once the evidence is sorted.

Some things can be verified from another country. A company’s existence and its filed accounts, where the register publishes them. Its payment history with you. And whether a credit insurer will cover it, and up to what amount.

**Grammar: hedged assertion about what cannot be verified: appears to, is understood to**

- Appears to reports something you have observed but cannot confirm: the company appears to be trading from the same address since 2019.

A credit decision built from what can be verified from another country is a sorting job before it is a judgement, and the sorting is where a system earns its place.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who writes the first reminder in your company?
- Where do the figures in it come from?
- What happens when a disputed invoice is chased as if it were late?

A first reminder is a statement of fact, and the facts come from the ledger. Every invoice number, its date, its amount, its due date, and what has been received against it.

A reminder written from memory loses the argument in its first paragraph. “Your account is considerably overdue” invites the reply “which invoice?”, and a week is gone.

The ledger also knows something a writer may not. One of the four invoices on this account is in query: the buyer wrote in May about a credit for eleven instruments, and nobody closed the item.

Chasing a disputed invoice as though it were merely late is the fastest way to turn a friendly account into a cold one. The buyer answers the mistake, not the debt, and he answers it with some satisfaction.

So a first reminder lists what is due, states what is in query and says who is dealing with it, and asks for the rest. Three paragraphs, every figure sourced, and no adjective anywhere.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Write it from the ledger, and leave the adjectives out**

**Figure 4.5** — Three invoices requested, one query named and excluded, and a total that can be paid.

A first reminder written from the ledger, not from memory, is the easiest letter in this book to get right and the one most often got wrong, because it is written in a hurry at month end.

Give it the ledger extract for the account and ask for one line per open item with five fields: the invoice number, its date, its amount, its due date and what has been received.

Then ask for the items to be divided into three groups: due and undisputed, in query, and not yet due. Only the first group is requested, and the second is named.

Ask for a total of the first group, because an accounts department pays a total. Ask for the name of the person handling each query, because that is what stops the buyer from using the query as a reason to pay nothing.

And ask for no adjectives. Not considerably, not seriously, not unacceptable. The figures are the pressure, and a firm verb carries the rest.

## Unit 5 · The Second and the Third, in the Same Words

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many reminders does your company send?
- What changes between the first and the third?
- Who signs the last one?

Escalation is not volume. A second reminder that is angrier than the first teaches a customer that your letters are about feeling, and feeling can be waited out.

What rises instead is formality. The sentences get shorter, the register gets colder, the signature gets more senior, and each letter adds exactly one new consequence.

The facts do not change at all. The same three invoice numbers, the same amounts, the same due dates, in the same order, in all three letters. A buyer who sees the figures move suspects the figures.

The consequences rise in a ladder that is known before the first letter is written: a notice period, then a copy to a director, then a suspension of further supply, then a referral.

And the intervals are fixed. Seven working days between letters is a sequence; writing when somebody remembers is a habit, and a buyer learns the difference within two months.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: escalation without aggression: formal register and shorter sentences**

- Raise the register by removing warmth, not by adding heat: we have no record of payment rather than we still have not been paid.
- Shorten the sentences. A first reminder may run to twenty words a sentence; a third should run to twelve. Short sentences read as decided.

**Figure 6.0** — “AS MUCH AS I CAN EACH MONTH” IS NOT A PLAN: A payment plan agreed by telephone, and the four things it did not contain.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Has your company ever agreed a payment plan with a customer abroad?
- How did you know whether it was being kept?
- What happened when one payment was late?

A payment plan is a contract, and a plan that cannot be monitored from your own desk is not a plan but a hope with dates attached.

Four things make it monitorable. A fixed amount for each instalment. A fixed calendar date for each. A named mechanism, such as a standing order or a credit opened for the balance. And a written confirmation from the buyer after each payment, quoting the invoice numbers.

A fifth element makes it enforceable. A default clause saying that if any instalment is not received within three working days of its date, the whole remaining balance falls due at once.

Aurora agrees a plan on the telephone with a buyer in Casablanca: four payments over four months, “as much as I can each month”. The first payment arrives, the second is half, the third does not come.

Nobody has breached anything, because nothing was promised. The lesson is not that the buyer was dishonest. It is that a plan with no figures is a plan nobody can be held to, including the buyer himself.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: commitment and schedule: undertake to, agree to, by instalments**

- Undertake to is the strongest ordinary commitment in commercial English, and binds the party who says it: the buyer undertakes to pay EUR 5,000 on the first of each month.
- Agree to records a mutual position: we agree to suspend interest while the plan is kept; both parties agree that the plan replaces the earlier terms.
- By instalments describes the manner of payment and takes a number: the balance is payable by four equal instalments of EUR 5,132.50.
- Shall be made is used for the mechanism: payment shall be made by standing order. Together these three forms make a plan a document rather than a conversation.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Five elements, or it is not a plan**

**Figure 6.5** — Six elements, and a plan missing any one of them is a conversation.

A payment plan drafted so that it can be monitored from abroad is a short document with six elements, and the test of a draft is whether a clerk in another country could check it without telephoning anybody.

Give it the balance, the buyer’s proposal and the period you are willing to allow. Ask for equal instalments to the cent, fixed calendar dates, and a named payment mechanism.

A currency exposure is three things: an amount, a date on which it is settled, and a party who carries the movement until then. Most quotations state the first and leave the other two to be discovered.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: cause and consequence: owing to, as a result of, consequently**

- Owing to and as a result of are followed by a noun phrase: owing to the movement in the rate; as a result of the trigger being reached.
- Consequently joins two sentences and goes after a semicolon or at the start of a new sentence: the rate moved by four per cent; consequently, the clause was triggered.
- Because of works like owing to and is less formal. Because is followed by a clause: because the rate moved, the price has been recalculated.
- In a letter about money, put the cause before the consequence and name a figure in each. A cause with no figure sounds like an excuse.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: making a record of failure: failed to, did not, was not received**

- Failed to is followed by a bare verb and records a non-performance without emotion: the supplier failed to deliver sixty assemblies.
- Did not is the plainest form for a specific event: two units did not pass the acceptance test; the replacement units did not arrive within the remedy period.

- Write no adjectives and no conclusions. A record that says the supplier was careless is an opinion; a record that says the goods were not received on a date is evidence.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**What can be shown is the only version that matters**

**Figure 9.5** — Eleven events, two emails, and a claim resting on the last line alone.

A supplier’s failure documented as it happens, in a usable form, is a discipline rather than a task, and a system is useful for turning a year of correspondence into a record you can use.

Give it the purchase contract, every delivery note, every acceptance test result and all the correspondence. Ask for one dated line per event: what was promised, what happened, what date.

Ask for the language to be flat: failed to, did not, was not received. Ask it to remove every adjective and every statement about whose fault something probably was.

Then ask the question that decides a claim. For each failure, was a notice of non-performance sent, and did it quote the contract and allow the remedy period? Most companies find the answer is no for almost everything.

Where a cover purchase has been made, ask for the additional cost to be built from two quotations: the contract price and the price actually paid, both attached.

And ask for the gap list: failures with no contemporaneous note. That list cannot be fixed, but it tells you how strong the claim is before you spend money pursuing it.

## Unit 10 · Collection Abroad, and When to Stop

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: settlement language: in full and final settlement, without prejudice · AI: a decision to stop chasing, written with the figures behind it*

**Figure 10.0** — THE DEBT WAS REAL AND PURSUING IT COST MORE: One foreign debt, three routes, and the arithmetic that decided between them.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Has your company ever stopped chasing a debt it was owed?
- Who decided, and was it written down?
- What would it have cost to go on?

Pursuing a debt abroad is a purchase. You buy a chance of recovery, and the price is the agency commission, the legal costs, the management time and the relationship.

The arithmetic is not complicated. An agency that recovers one debt in three and charges twenty per cent of what it recovers is worth using on a debt you would otherwise write off.

Litigation abroad is different. A judgment in one country must be enforced in another, and enforcement is a second case with a second cost. On eighteen thousand euros the costs can exceed the debt before anybody is paid.

So there are usually three honest routes. Settle at a discount now, in writing, in full and final settlement. Pass the file to an agency on commission. Or write it off, record the decision and the figures, and stop.

The fourth route is the one most companies take, which is to chase indefinitely without deciding. It costs management time every month, recovers nothing, and nobody is ever accountable for it.

**Decide, and write the arithmetic down**

A decision to stop chasing, written with the figures behind it, is a short memorandum, and its purpose is to make a decision exist rather than to drift.
