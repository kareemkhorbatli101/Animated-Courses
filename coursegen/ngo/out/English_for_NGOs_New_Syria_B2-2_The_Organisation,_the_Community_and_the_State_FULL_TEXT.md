### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: a business case: recommendation first, assurance second**

- Open with I recommend that and a specific decision with a figure and a term. I recommend that the board approve a three-year lease on a field office in Jableh at eighteen thousand pounds a year.
- Follow with assurance in descending order of what a board will test. The net annual cost is seven thousand pounds after the eleven thousand currently spent on vehicle hire.
- Cost inaction explicitly, in the same unit. Doing nothing costs eleven thousand pounds a year in vehicle hire and four hundred and ten officer-days in travel.
- Name any commitment extending beyond secured funding, in its own sentence before the recommendation is voted on. The lease runs eighteen months beyond the grant that funds it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A business case drafted with the recommendation in the first line**

**Figure 1.5** — Five parts on one page, with the recommendation in the first line.

A business case is read by people who will form a view from its first sentence and spend the remaining four minutes looking for reasons to keep it.

Give the system the decision you are seeking with its figure and term, the gross and net costs, the measured cost of the present arrangement, the options you reject with your reason for each, and the term of any commitment beyond secured funding.

Ask it to open with the recommendation and the net figure rather than the gross, since the net figure is the one the board should be holding when it reaches the budget line.

Ask it to price the option of taking no action in both money and officer-days, and to convert officer-days into something a board can picture, because a board shown no price for inaction will treat inaction as free.

Ask it to state in its own sentence, before the recommendation is voted on, any commitment that extends beyond funding already secured, with the number of months by which it does.

What it must not do is place the recommendation after the evidence. A case that builds to its recommendation invites a board to build to a different one, and the order in which you worked something out is almost never the order in which it should be read.

> **DATA POINT · Business cases** — Many organisations require a case for new expenditure to open with the recommendation, to price the option of taking no action, and to identify commitments extending beyond secured funding. What is required is set by the organisation’s own written procedures.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Commitments beyond funding** — Many organisations require a case to identify any liability extending beyond the period of secured funding, and to state the exposure in months and money. What is required is set by the organisation’s own written procedures.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Where the recommendation sits

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

An organisation reviewed 78 business cases put to its board over seven years, with the outcome of each.

In 29 the recommendation appeared in the first three lines. In 49 it appeared after the evidence, on average on the third page.

Decided at the first meeting: 24 of 29 and 18 of 49. Deferred at least once: 4 and 28. Cases pricing the option of taking no action: 22 of 29 and 11 of 49; of the 56 cases that priced it, 41 were approved, against 7 of the 22 that did not. Cases stating a commitment extending beyond secured funding: 19; in 6 of the 59 that did not, the liability was discovered later and in 2 of those the organisation had to meet it from unrestricted funds, at a combined 34,000 pounds. Mean board minutes per case: 11 for the first group and 26 for the second.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An agenda built around decisions rather than updates**

**Figure 2.5** — Five parts per item, and an item without the first does not appear.

A committee has ninety minutes and will spend them on whatever is in front of it, which makes the agenda the chair’s only real instrument.

Give the system the items submitted, the papers attached, the dates they were circulated, and your own view of which items ask the committee to decide something.

Ask it to identify every item that carries no closed question, so that each can be returned to its author with the question it appears to intend.

Ask it to attach a cost to each option in a single unit, since a committee choosing between options with no comparable figures will choose the one presented most confidently.

Ask it to draft a resolution for every decision item before the meeting, because a resolution drafted in the room is drafted by whoever speaks last and is minuted by whoever is tired.

What it must not do is leave an item on the agenda because its author will be upset. A paper that cannot be decided consumes twenty minutes and produces a deferral, and the forty-one households waiting on the item behind it pay for both.

**An authority’s question answered within its exact scope**

**Figure 5.5** — Five parts, and a copy of exactly what was sent on the file.

An answer to an authority is read by somebody drafting the next request, which is why its scope matters more than its tone.

Give the system the question as written, the figures you hold with their method and provenance, the legal basis cited in the request if any, and the authority of the person who signed it.

Ask it to answer only what was asked, and to list separately anything you were tempted to add, so that the decision to include it is a decision rather than a habit.

Ask it to draft the request for a legal basis as a question rather than an objection, since asking under which provision is a lawful step and reads as one, while declining to answer reads as a refusal to cooperate.

Ask it to state plainly what cannot be provided and to attach a date for what can, because an authority given no date will set one itself and will set it sooner.

What it must not do is attach material for context. Every attachment becomes the expected form of the next answer, and a request drafted against your most generous reply will ask for more than the law allows.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A handover plan drafted in conditionals with its assumptions named**

**Figure 8.5** — Five parts, and the fifth exists because the second one will not all hold.

A handover plan written confidently in the indicative is a plan that has hidden its conditions rather than met them, and the hiding is rarely deliberate: it is what happens when a confident document is easier to send than an honest one.

Give the system the transfer date, every step either organisation must complete first, the assets and obligations involved, and the capacities the plan depends on.

Ask it to write each step as a conditional with a date on both halves, so that no transfer is promised before the thing it depends on has happened.

Ask it to list the assumptions as a numbered set and to mark each one verified, verifiable or unverifiable, which is usually where the plan’s real risk is sitting.

Ask it to separate what moves from what stays, because the obligation to answer a complaint made before the handover does not travel with the programme.

What it cannot do is verify a single capacity. Reading audited accounts, watching a payroll run and asking a partner in plain words what they believe they have agreed to are all work that somebody has to do in a room with the other organisation.

**What no tool can do for a humanitarian judgement**

**Figure 10.5** — Five rows, and the second column is the whole of the job.

A tool is better than you at consistency, arithmetic and patience, and it is better than you at four in the afternoon in particular.

Give it the criteria, the data and the weights, and let it rank, check, flag the anomalies and draft the explanation you will have to give.

Ask it to show you the boundary cases, the overridden rows and any group that its criteria exclude entirely, because those three sets are where your judgement is actually required.
