### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: reporting on partial information: I, we, and what we know**

- Attribute every fact, with a time and a source. A volunteer told me at eleven o’clock that she had seen a man at the gate taking a child’s hand.
- Use I for your own acts and nobody else’s. I asked the volunteer to write down what she had seen, in her own words.

- Write the unknowns as questions of fact, not as doubts about a person. We do not know yet whether the man is related to the child, or whether anybody else was present.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A safeguarding report sorted into facts, actions and what is not yet known**

**Figure 1.5** — Five parts, written inside the hour, with no conclusion anywhere in them.

A concern report is written in the hour when nothing is yet established and everything is still retrievable.

Give the system the accounts you have received with their times and sources, the actions you took, the actions the organisation took, and the questions still open.

Ask it to keep facts and inferences in different parts, and to attribute every fact to the person who gave it and the time they gave it.

Ask it to write the unknowns as questions of fact rather than as doubts about anybody, since a doubt about a person is an assessment and an assessment is not yours to make.

Ask it to record the routing: to whom, at what time, and under which clause of the procedure, because the interval between the disclosure and the routing is the thing an inquiry measures first.

What it must not do is draft a question for a child or a complainant. A question asked by the wrong person at the wrong time can destroy the only account that exists, and no report is worth that.

> **DATA POINT · Reporting thresholds** — Many organisations require a safeguarding concern to be reported to a designated lead within a stated period and prohibit the reporting member of staff from investigating it. What is required is set by the organisation’s own written procedures and applicable law.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Investigation and reporting** — Many organisations separate the duty to report a concern from the authority to investigate it, and prohibit the reporting member of staff from interviewing a child or a complainant. What is prohibited is set by the organisation’s own written procedures and applicable law.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · The hour between eleven and twelve

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

An organisation reviewed 148 safeguarding concerns reported across six country programmes over three years, against the outcome of each.

Of the 148, 61 were routed to a designated lead within the period set by the procedure. Eighty-seven were routed later, with a median delay of eleven hours.

In 34 of the 87 delayed cases the reporting member of staff had made enquiries of their own before routing. 19 had spoken to the person the concern was about, 11 had asked a child or complainant a question, and 4 had telephoned a third party. Of those 34, the lead recorded that the original account had been materially altered or lost in 21. Concerns substantiated after investigation: 23 of 61 and 18 of 87. Concerns closed as unfounded: 38 and 52, with 17 of the 87 closed because no usable account remained. Two cases were referred to a statutory authority and in both the delay was the subject of a finding.

**A suspension notice prepared as act, effect and next step**

**Figure 2.5** — Five parts, said aloud first and posted afterwards in the same words.

A suspension notice is the only document a community will read word for word, and it will be read aloud by somebody who is angry.

Give the system what has been suspended with its locations and start time, whether the suspension is precautionary or a sanction, the services that continue, the households affected, and who decides on resumption.

**An irreversible harm written as what happened and what follows**

**Figure 3.5** — Five parts. The second is the one every draft tries to soften.

**A refusal to resume drafted with its reason and the route that remains**

**Figure 4.5** — Five parts, written weekly, whether or not anybody has asked.

A refusal to resume is written every week of a suspension, because the week nobody asks is the week a site quietly leaves the programme without any decision being taken at all.

**A concern about conduct recorded and routed without a verdict in it**

**Figure 7.5** — Five parts. The fourth decides whether anything happens at all.

A concern about a colleague is the hardest document in this book to write, because the person it concerns is usually somebody you respect and the facts are usually small.

Give the system the dated events you witnessed yourself, what you did at the time, what you recorded, the procedure’s routing and the role above it.

Ask it to keep every adjective and every modal verb out of any sentence about the colleague, since he may have an explanation and his behaviour seems casual are both judgements wearing the clothes of caution.

**A formal complaint acknowledged with its scope and its timescale stated**
