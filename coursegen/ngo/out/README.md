# English for NGOs
## New Syria Edition · Humanitarian and Development Organisations

Six coursebooks, A2.1 to B2.2, for people who work in aid and development and
have to write and speak in English: field assistants, registration and
distribution staff, project officers, programme managers, MEAL and safeguarding
leads, partnership and grants staff, and country directors — in national
organisations, local cooperatives and community foundations as much as in
international agencies.

The spine of the series is one sentence: **the need is always larger than the
budget.** Every criterion an organisation writes excludes somebody who meets
the need. Every list has a line, and somebody is standing just below it. The
professional question in this field is never how to help everybody; it is how
to say so, accurately and without cruelty, to the person in front of you — and
how to write down afterwards what was decided and on what basis, so that the
next person can see it was a decision rather than an accident.

### Why a New Syria edition

The work these books describe is reconstruction work. Local organisations are
taking over programmes from international ones; local councils expect to be
consulted rather than informed; communities have been assessed many times and
want to know what came of it; and the donor forms were written somewhere else.
The language of that handover — the subaward, the verification visit, the
consultation on ending a programme, the handover plan with its assumptions
named, the closure explained in plain words — is the language this course
teaches.

### What is in every unit

Twelve parts, the same shape in all sixty units:

1. **Hero statement** — the unit in one line a tired person can quote
2. **Warm-up** — three questions about the reader’s own work
3. **Reading** — five sentences carrying the unit’s argument, with a data point
4. **Comprehension** — six question types, including a four-way classification
5. **Vocabulary** — twenty terms, each glossed in working language
6. **Grammar** — the point, four rules, and a two-column before-and-after figure
7. **Two dialogues** — twelve lines each; the second always ends on an open objection
8. **Speaking** — three paired tasks, six turns each
9. **The AI capability** — six paragraphs on what a system can draft and what it cannot
10. **The Sentence You Must Say to Somebody You Cannot Help** — the series signature
11. **The Decision Room** — a case with figures, three voices, and a four-line rule to write
12. **Brief Lab** — a real six-line draft, diagnosed against the precision-fault ladder

---

### The two ladders that run across all sixty units

**The AI ladder.** Sixty distinct capabilities, from *a list of who does what,
checked against the day at the gate* to *what no tool can do for a humanitarian
judgement*. Every one of the sixty is paired with a boundary line saying what
the capability cannot do, and the last unit of Book 6 makes the division
explicit: a model can rank two hundred households in four seconds and it cannot
sign. Accountability is a relationship between a named person and somebody they
have affected, and a system that cannot be asked why has not answered.

**The precision-fault ladder.** Eight named faults — unscoped set, unbounded
quantity, unstated criterion, undefined done, ambiguous referent, missing
condition, unstated exception, hidden assumption — introduced progressively and
diagnosed in sixty Brief Labs. Each lab takes a real six-line draft (a
registration note, a selection letter, a concept note, a closure notice, a
handover note, a board paragraph) and names three fault types, twice each.

### Four standing rules, held across all sixty units

1. **No politics, no parties, no armed actors.** Cities and towns are real —
   Damascus, Aleppo, Homs, Hama, Latakia, Tartus, Idlib, Daraa, Jableh,
   Qamishli, Deir ez-Zor, Raqqa. Every organisation is invented: the Hawran
   Relief Association, the Orontes Development Foundation, the Sahl Water and
   Sanitation Trust, the Qalamoun Women’s Cooperative, the Ebla Education
   Initiative, the Barada Community Foundation, with the Northern Fund, the
   Hartley Foundation, the Coastal Donor Consortium and the Humanitarian Pooled
   Fund as funders, and the Jableh local council and the Daraa municipal
   council as authorities.
2. **Nobody is described as a beneficiary in the organisation’s own voice
   without a reason.** The books teach households, families, people, a named
   person at position ninety-one. Where the donor’s word is the word on the
   form, the form is quoted and the difference is taught.
3. **Nothing is ever promised that the organisation cannot deliver.** The series
   teaches what the criterion was, where the line fell, what remains open, and
   what the speaker will not pretend. "I cannot help you today, and this is
   why" is taught in preference to any softer formula, and the reason is given.
4. **Law and procedure are always attributed.** Every data point panel cites the
   organisation’s own written procedures, the agreement between the parties, or
   applicable law. No sum of money and no legal requirement is presented as a
   fact about any jurisdiction. All figures are illustrative.

### Calibration

Mean sentence length is measured per unit and held inside a band: A2 8.5–12.5
words, B1 11.5–16.5, B2 15.0–21.0. Sentence caps are 26 words at A2, 34 at B1,
44 at B2, with over-cap sentences held below one and a half per cent. No
vocabulary term is taught twice anywhere in the 1,200. No grammar point and no
AI capability repeats across the sixty units.
