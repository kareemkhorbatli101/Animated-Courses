# English for NGOs — New Syria Edition
## Series plan

### The spine

**The need is always larger than the budget.**

Every criterion an organisation writes excludes somebody who meets the need.
Every list has a line, and somebody is standing just below it. The professional
question in this field is never how to help everybody; it is how to say so,
accurately and without cruelty, to the person in front of you — and how to
write down afterwards what was decided and on what basis, so that the next
person can see it was a decision rather than an accident.

That is why the series signature is not a sentence said when you are certain.
It is **the sentence you must say to somebody you cannot help**: what the
criterion was, where the line fell, what remains open to them, and what you
will not pretend.

### Why a New Syria edition

The work the books describe is reconstruction work. Local organisations are
taking over from international ones; local councils expect to be consulted
rather than informed; communities have been assessed many times and want to
know what came of it; and donor forms were written somewhere else. The language
of that handover — the subaward, the verification visit, the consultation on
ending a programme, the handover plan with its assumptions named — is the
language this course teaches.

### Four standing rules, held across all sixty units

1. **No politics, no parties, no armed actors, no narration of conflict.** The
   subject is the work of delivering and accounting for assistance: the gate,
   the list, the form, the report, the committee. Where a unit needs an
   authority it is a local council or a ministry acting in its ordinary
   administrative capacity, and every technical or operational decision is
   attributed to a named member of staff.

2. **No person receiving assistance is ever described as deserving or
   undeserving.** Criteria are stated, checkable and attributed. The language
   describes what was assessed, by whom, on what date and against which
   criterion — never what somebody is like. Where a unit teaches a refusal, it
   teaches the refusal of an application against a stated line, with the route
   that remains.

3. **Protection first.** No real case is described and no example contains
   identifying detail. Every safeguarding unit routes rather than concludes:
   the language taught records what was seen, marks what was only reported,
   states what is being asked for, and leaves the finding to the person whose
   job it is.

4. **Law, mandate and procedure are always attributed** — to local law, to the
   agreement with the donor, or to the organisation’s own written procedures.
   No requirement is presented as a fact about any jurisdiction. All sums,
   caseloads and percentages are illustrative.

No real organisation, donor, government body or international agency is named
anywhere in the series.

### The two ladders

**The AI ladder.** Sixty capabilities, one per unit, each taught with an
explicit boundary line. It runs from *a first-contact script for a family with
no appointment* to *what no tool can do for a humanitarian judgement*. The last
unit makes the division explicit: a system arranges what is written down; it
cannot reach what nobody wrote down, and it cannot be the person who will be
asked in two years why a family was refused.

**The precision-fault ladder.** The eight faults — unscoped set, unbounded
quantity, unstated criterion, undefined done, ambiguous referent, missing
condition, unstated exception, hidden assumption — introduced progressively
across the levels and diagnosed in sixty Brief Labs, each on a real six-line
draft from the sector: a registration note, a not-selected letter, a donor
report, a suspension notice, a handover plan.

### Calibration

Mean sentence length per unit inside the band for its level: A2 8.5–12.5 words,
B1 11.5–16.5, B2 15.0–21.0. Sentence caps 26 / 34 / 44 words, over-cap held
below one per cent. 200 vocabulary terms per book, 1,200 across the series,
none repeated. No grammar point and no AI capability repeats.
