### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: criteria that can be checked: anyone who, provided that, except**

- Open with anyone who and a fact an applicant knows about themselves. Anyone who was displaced from their home after March 2024 may apply.
- Add the condition with provided that, carrying a measurable threshold. Provided that no member of the household is in paid work of more than twenty hours a week.
- Add the exclusion with except where, naming the thing excluded rather than the kind of person. Except where another member of the same household is already registered under this project.
- Avoid as appropriate, where necessary and in genuine need. None of them can be answered by the person reading the sheet.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Selection criteria rewritten so an applicant can check themselves**

**Figure 1.5** — Five parts. Without the last two, a criterion has no history.

Rewriting criteria is not editing. It is moving a decision out of a conversation and into a sentence an applicant can read.

Give the system the current wording, the facts a household knows about itself, the thresholds the project can measure, and ten real households from the last round.

Ask it to rebuild each criterion as population, condition and exclusion, and to mark any part it cannot build from a measurable fact.

Ask it to flag every word that requires a judgement by an officer, including vulnerable, appropriate, significant and in need, and to propose a threshold for each.

Ask it to run the rewritten set against the ten households and report which ones fall outside every category, since an accidental exclusion is the commonest fault in a criteria sheet.

What it must not do is choose the thresholds. Where the line falls is a decision about how many households a budget can reach, and that belongs to the committee and the funding agreement.

> **DATA POINT · Checkable criteria** — Many organisations require selection criteria to be expressed so that an applicant can determine their own eligibility before applying, and to be published rather than held internally. What is required is set by the organisation’s own written procedures.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Four hundred applicants and one vague line

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

An organisation compared two shelter rounds run four months apart in the same district.

In the first round the published criterion was households in genuine need of shelter support, assessed on a home visit. In the second, three measurable criteria were published before applications opened. Displacement after a stated date, more than three people to a room, and no household member in paid work above twenty hours a week.

Round one received 431 applications for 120 places, made 398 assessment visits, and recorded 61 complaints about selection, of which 22 went to the accountability committee. Round two received 196 applications for 118 places, made 140 visits, and recorded 9 complaints, 1 of which went to the committee. Of the 196 applicants in round two, 171 had read the criteria before applying. Staff time on assessment visits fell from 530 hours to 196. Eleven households in round two fell outside all three criteria and were recorded as a gap for the committee.

**A not-selected letter drafted with the cut-off stated**

A not-selected letter is the only document most applicants will ever read from your organisation. It will be kept, shown to neighbours and quoted back.

Give the system the criteria met by this applicant, the number of households funded, the cut-off position, the reserve list order, and how many places came free in the last comparable period.

Ask it to open with what the applicant met, since the first sentence of a refusal is the one that decides how the rest is read.

## Unit 3 · Saying a Need in Numbers

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: need in numbers: of every hundred households, roughly, out of · AI: a need restated as a number out of a hundred households*

**Figure 3.0** — OF EVERY HUNDRED HOUSEHOLDS, THIRTY-ONE.: A percentage without its base is a decoration, not a figure.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What figure do you quote most often?
- Where did it come from?
- When was it collected?

A need is easiest to act on as a number out of a hundred households. Of every hundred households in the district, thirty-one have no safe water point within a kilometre.

Always give the base. Thirty-one per cent of what, counted by whom, and when.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A need restated as a number out of a hundred households**

**Figure 3.5** — Five parts. The fifth is the one that keeps a figure honest.

A need statement is a sentence somebody else must be able to repeat. If they cannot repeat it, it is not evidence; it is a claim.

Give the system the raw counts, the population you drew them from, the method of collection, the dates, and what changed in the district since.

Ask it to restate each figure as a number out of a hundred households and to place the base in the same sentence, never in a footnote.

Ask it to refuse to express a count of fewer than about thirty households as a percentage, since eighty per cent of five is a sentence that destroys a proposal when anybody asks.

Ask it to attach the collection date and the collecting role to every figure, and to flag any figure older than a year for recounting.

**A project boundary written as what it does not cover**

**Figure 4.5** — Five lines. The last one is the one a committee should read.

A boundary is written for the family deciding whether to make a journey, not for the file.

Give the system the funded activities with their quantities, the funding agreement’s eligible costs, the requests received in the last three months, and the bodies responsible in this district.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Ninety-one households and a leaflet

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

An organisation reviewed requests received at four project sites over three months, against what each project was funded to provide.

Of 1,042 requests recorded, 617 were for funded activities and 425 were for things the projects did not cover: school fees (164), transport (88), medical costs (71), rent arrears (54) and cash (48).

Two of the four sites then published a six-line boundary beside the door, naming excluded items and the body responsible for each. Over the following three months those two sites recorded 388 requests, of which 61 were outside scope. The two sites that published nothing recorded 503, of which 219 were outside scope. Complaints that the project had refused help it had implied it would give: 4 at the publishing sites and 47 at the others. Nineteen excluded needs could not be routed to any body and were recorded as gaps; the committee found funding for two of them.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A community agreement recorded as what was accepted**

**Figure 5.5** — Five parts, read aloud, signed twice, copied once.

A community agreement is the document a village will quote back to you in August. It should be the document you would want quoted.

Give the system the meeting notes, the attendance by household or role, the requests made, the budget and ground constraints, and what you were able to accept.

Ask it to split everything into accepted and not accepted, and to express the second half as the boundary of the agreement rather than as a refusal of the community.

Ask it to record attendance as a number out of the whole, and to name the households or groups not represented, since an absent group has agreed to nothing.

Ask it to record every request that was declined with the ground for the decision, chosen from budget, ground conditions, law or time, so that it can be revisited when the constraint changes.

What it must not do is infer agreement from the absence of objection. Silence in a meeting room is not consent, and an agreement built on it will fail the first time it is tested.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: reassurance that is not a promise: I can, I cannot, what I will do**

- Use I can tell you that for a fact within your authority. I can tell you that absence from a distribution does not remove a name from the list.
- Use I cannot tell you for anything outside it, and put it in the middle rather than at the end. I cannot tell you which week the next distribution falls in.
- Use what I will do today is for a single action with a date. What I will do today is write your dates away beside your record.
- Never use we will make sure, you will be looked after, or do not worry. Each of them commits assistance you cannot commit.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A briefing addressed to an authority and a community together**

**Figure 7.5** — Five parts, one minute, two copies.

A joint briefing is the only document in a project that two bodies with different interests will read in the same hour.

Give the system the items for the authority, the items for the community, every figure in the project, the points on which the two have taken different positions. The next action with its owner.

Ask it to address each half by name, so that nobody has to work out which sentences are theirs, and to mark every shared figure as shared.

Ask it to place the item affecting households before the item affecting a permit, since a permit can wait four minutes and a family outside cannot wait a month.

Ask it to record a disagreement as what each body has asked for, with the next step that will settle it, and never as a view on which is correct.

What it must not do is produce two versions. A briefing tailored to each audience will be compared within the week, and the comparison will be the only thing either of them remembers.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An overruled criterion recorded without pressure and without a verdict**

**Figure 9.5** — Five lines, no adjectives, one copy to the committee.

An overruling record is written for a stranger reading the file long afterwards, and it is the only thing that will distinguish a judgement call from a favour.

Give the system the instruction as it was given, the published criterion it departs from, the decision-maker’s role and name, the date, and whether a written instruction was requested.

Ask it to state the decision in one sentence and to attribute it with you have decided or the role has decided, never with I was told to.

Ask it to strip every adjective and every statement of your own view, since the record exists to be read by people who will form their own.

Ask it to carry the request for a written instruction and its outcome, because a declined request is itself a fact the committee needs.

What it must not do is draft a complaint. A complaint is a different document with a different route, and mixing the two destroys both.

> **DATA POINT · Departures from criteria** — Many organisations require a departure from published selection criteria to be recorded with the decision-maker, the date and the criterion departed from, and to be reported to a committee. What is required is set by the organisation’s own written procedures.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Verbal instructions** — Many organisations ask staff to seek a written instruction where a decision departs from published procedure, and to record the request and its outcome where none is given. What is asked is set by the organisation’s own written procedures.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A case put to the committee as population, question and request**

**Figure 10.5** — Five parts on one page, with the recommendation named.

A committee paper is read in four minutes by people who have five other items. Everything after the first three lines is supporting material.

Give the system the population affected, the decision within the committee’s power, your recommendation, every option including the ones you would not take, and the cost of each in a single unit.

Ask it to put the population first as a number and a place, the question second as a choice, and the request third as a sentence that can be voted on.

Ask it to include every option a reasonable member might raise, with its real cost, since an omitted option turns the meeting into a discussion of the omission.

Ask it to cost deferral in the same unit as the options, because doing nothing is always available and is almost never free.

What it must not do is argue. A paper that advocates is read as advocacy and deferred; the recommendation belongs in one labelled line, and the figures do the rest.

### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Ninety minutes and six items

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

An organisation reviewed 146 papers put to its programme committees over two years, against what the committees did with them.

Of the 146, 58 opened with a stated population, a question for decision and a specific request. The other 88 opened with background.

Decisions taken at the first meeting: 49 of the 58 and 31 of the 88. Papers deferred at least once: 7 and 51. Papers deferred twice or more: 1 and 19. Mean minutes of committee time per paper: 9 for the three-line papers and 23 for the others. Of the 88, 34 omitted an option a member then raised in the room, and 29 of those were deferred. The organisation calculated that deferrals had delayed decisions affecting 2,140 households by a median of seven weeks.
