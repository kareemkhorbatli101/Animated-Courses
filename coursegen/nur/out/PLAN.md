# English for Nurses — International Edition
## Series plan

**Spine.** The nurse is the person who is there. She sees the change first, and
nothing she sees helps the patient until it has become a sentence somebody else
acts on. Every unit in these six books teaches one piece of that conversion:
noticing, saying, writing, asking, teaching, escalating, recording.

**Who it is for.** Nurses who work in English without having grown up in it:
staff nurses on international wards, nurses recruited across borders, nurses
caring for patients who share no language with them, and anyone preparing for
registration, placement or practice abroad.

**The six books**

1. **A2.1 · The Patient in Your Care** — the bedside: identity before anything is
   given, first words, pain in the patient's words, offering care rather than
   announcing it, observations said aloud, the medicine check, intake and output,
   calling for help early, explaining a procedure, the rest of the shift.
2. **A2.2 · The Shift and the Team** — the handover received and the handover
   given, the nursing note, asking a doctor to come, admitting what is undone,
   delegating, the night, a patient who will not follow the plan, a relative at
   the desk, the end of a shift.
3. **B1.1 · Watching, Writing and Escalating** — the patient who is not right,
   observations into a trend, the four-part escalation, what you had already
   done, the note that carries a shift, a fall, a wound measured, an error
   reported, questions to other professions, a discharge prepared.
4. **B1.2 · The Patient, the Family and the Words** — explaining without clinical
   words, teaching a skill, witnessing and its limits, an interpreter at the
   bedside, a frightened patient, a family that wants to stay, discharge teaching
   repeated back, a usable leaflet, a refusal recorded, a complaint answered.
5. **B2.1 · When It Goes Wrong on Your Shift** — speaking up across rank, graded
   assertiveness, refusing an unsafe instruction, disclosing a nursing error, the
   incident report, being present at bad news, the last days, a death to tell, a
   short-staffed shift documented, a concern about a colleague.
6. **B2.2 · The Nurse and the System** — the one-minute presentation, the care
   plan that is followed, a transfer across systems, a nursing audit, teaching a
   student, a policy made usable at a bedside, a third-party report, an
   application and interview, stating a staffing limit, and what no system can do
   for a nurse's word.

**Architecture.** Each of the sixty units follows the same twelve-part spine as the
rest of the Ledger list, with Part 10 as this edition's signature: **Speaking
Up**, the moment a nurse must stop writing and say something out loud to
somebody who does not want to hear it — a registrar who has already answered, a
bed manager with an admission in the corridor, a consultant proceeding anyway, a
family who want a promise, a manager who wants the shortfall left out of the
record.

**Calibration, measured not asserted.** Mean sentence length inside the band for
each level (A2 8.5–12.5, B1 11.5–16.5, B2 15.0–21.0), sentence caps of 26, 34
and 44 words, long sentences never above five per cent, 1,200 distinct field
terms across the series with no term reused, sixty distinct grammar points and
sixty distinct AI capabilities each taught with its stated boundary.

**Editorial line on clinical content.** These books teach clinical communication
and clinical writing, not nursing practice. No dose, no regimen and no
diagnostic rule appears anywhere. Clinical detail is deliberately generic and
exists only to carry the language. Every unit that touches a clinical decision
says that the decision belongs to the treating clinician; every unit that
touches a professional duty — witnessing, consent, delegation, raising a concern
— says that local law and local policy govern it, and that the forms differ
between countries and employers. Hospitals, units and homes are invented; cities
are real. No vendor, product, platform or living official appears anywhere.
