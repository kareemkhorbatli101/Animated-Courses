### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: listening and writing down: so that is, let me check, you said**

- Repeat a number with so that is. So that is eighty over fifty at six o’clock? Then write it while she confirms it.
- Ask for a repeat with let me check. Let me check: which arm is the drip in? Nobody minds a check and everybody minds an error.
- Quote her with you said. You said he had a fall last night. That is a check and not a challenge.
- Say the gap in the negative. You did not say anything about bed twelve. Then wait. The silence is where the missing patient appears.

**Figure 1.2** — So that is, let me check, you said, you did not say. Four sentences all shift.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A received handover turned into a written list for the shift**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the fifth one is the reason nine patients get seen.

A received handover turned into a written list for the shift is the first thing a nurse makes every day. The only thing she has at four in the morning.

Give the system the handover sheet, the bed list for your bay and what was said. Ask for a single line for each patient and no more.

Ask it to mark every bed on your list that nobody mentioned. That comparison takes a second and it is the one nobody does at seven in the morning.

Ask for everything due in the first hour on its own short list, with the hours. The first hour is where a shift is won or lost.

Ask it to expand every abbreviation you did not recognise, so that you can ask the night nurse before she leaves rather than guessing at two.

What no system can hear is the hesitation. A night nurse who says “he is fine, I think” has told you something that is not in any sheet.

> **DATA POINT · Structured handover** — Handover guidance commonly recommends a fixed order, a written record kept by the receiving nurse, read-back of numbers and an opportunity for questions at the end. Formats and times differ between wards.  
> Source: general nursing practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Handover failures** — Reviews of clinical handover commonly report omitted patients, unverified numbers and abbreviations understood differently by sender and receiver, and find that a written record kept by the receiver reduces omissions. Findings vary by setting.  
> Source: general patient-safety literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A handover drafted in four parts and ranked by urgency**

**Figure 2.5** — Six lines, and the last one decides who is seen first tonight.

A handover drafted in four parts and ranked by urgency is the one document a nurse writes for another nurse. It is read within ten minutes of being written.

Give the system your shift notes, the unfinished tasks, tonight’s times and your own list of who is most unwell. Ask for four parts a patient.

Ask for the order by urgency rather than by bed number, with your three sickest or most urgent patients first and a reason beside each.

Ask it to put the unfinished tasks in their own list with the reason each is unfinished. A task handed on without a reason is read as forgotten.

Ask for the family line: who is visiting, when, and what they have and have not been told. That is the sentence that saves the next nurse an hour at six o’clock.

What no system can give is your worry about the patient whose numbers are normal. Say it in your own words and let it be the last thing you hand over.

> **DATA POINT · Giving handover** — Handover guidance commonly recommends a fixed order of information, explicit statement of outstanding tasks with times, ranking by urgency rather than by bed number, and an invitation for questions. Local formats differ.  
> Source: general nursing practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Ranking and omissions** — Handover research commonly reports that information given in bed order is recalled less well than information ranked by urgency, and that outstanding tasks are frequently omitted or handed on without a reason. Findings differ by setting.  
> Source: general patient-safety literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A request to a doctor drafted with the reason in the first line**

**Figure 4.5** — Six lines, and the sixth one is what you have in March.

A request to a doctor drafted with the reason in the first line is a small piece of writing that is mostly spoken. The drafting is for your own head rather than for paper.

Give the system what you want done, the reason with its numbers, what you have already done and what time you need it by. Ask for one sentence per request.

Ask it to put the action before the journey. “Come to the ward” is a journey; “see bed four” and “prescribe pain relief” are actions.

Ask for the alternative in the same breath: or tell me and I shall ask the registrar. That is what turns a request into an answer.

Ask for the three lines of the log: the time, the name, what you asked for and what you were told. Those lines are what protects both of you.

What no system can do is decide which of your three requests is the urgent one. That is clinical judgement and it belongs to the nurse at the bedside.

> **DATA POINT · Requesting review** — Escalation guidance commonly recommends stating the request and the reason together, giving a time frame, recording the call and the response, and naming the next person to be contacted if the time frame is not met. Local routes differ.  
> Source: general acute-care guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Unanswered requests** — Reviews of escalation commonly report requests made without a stated action or time frame, unanswered pages, and responses not recorded, and recommend naming the next contact in advance. Local arrangements differ.  
> Source: general patient-safety literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A list of what remains undone, with its reason and its owner**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines, and the last two turn a shift into evidence.

A list of what remains undone, with its reason and its owner, is the most useful page a tired nurse writes. The one most often shortened to nothing.

Give the system your task list, what you finished, the hours and the staffing numbers. Ask for the undone tasks only, each with a reason and an owner.

Ask it to leave out every apology and to use because with a fact. The hoist was in use, the round was late, there were two of us for thirty patients.

What no system can do is say it out loud without apologising. The reason is the information; sorry is for the patient.

- The outstanding list names every unfinished task with the reason it is unfinished and the person or service it awaits.
- One line is left for the morning staff on the ward board, naming the single thing that must happen first the next day.

> **WHAT HAPPENED** — The ward adopts the four lines. Over three months two hundred and ten handovers are given under them. Every watch list names its patients. Falls overnight fall from nine in the previous quarter to three. In all three the patient was on the watch list and had been checked within the hour.
