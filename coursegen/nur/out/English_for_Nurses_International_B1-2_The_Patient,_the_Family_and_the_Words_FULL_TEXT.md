## Unit 1 · Explaining a Condition Without the Clinical Words

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: paraphrase: replacing a clinical term with what it does · AI: an explanation checked for every word a patient would not use*

**Figure 1.0** — A PATIENT WHO NODS HAS NOT NECESSARILY UNDERSTOOD ANYTHING.: Most patients will not say that they did not understand. They will say thank you.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which words do you use at a bedside that a patient would never use?
- How do you find out whether somebody has understood?
- What belongs to the doctor, and what can a nurse explain?

Explaining is a nursing job all day long, and most of it is paraphrase: taking a word that describes a mechanism and replacing it with what the mechanism does.

Not “your kidneys are not filtering properly” but “your kidneys are not getting rid of as much water as they should, which is why your ankles are swelling”.

The test of an explanation is not a nod. It is what the patient says to somebody else. “What will you tell your wife this evening?” is the only question worth asking.

Two sentences are usually enough, and four minutes are usually too many. A long explanation is remembered as a long explanation and nothing else.

What belongs to the treating clinician stays there: the diagnosis, the prognosis, the medicines and the plan. A nurse explains what has been decided, and says plainly who decides.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: paraphrase: replacing a clinical term with what it does**

- Build the paraphrase from a verb and a result: “your kidneys are not getting rid of enough water, so it stays in your legs”. The term disappears and the mechanism stays.
- Use which is why and so that to join the mechanism to what he has noticed. “Your heart is pumping less strongly, which is why you are breathless on the stairs.”
- Compare with something in his house, not in a textbook: a tap, a sieve, a sponge, a pump, a full bucket. One comparison, not three.
- Check with an imperative or a wh-question, never with a yes-or-no: “Tell me how you would say that to your son.” “What will you watch for at home?”

**Figure 1.2** — Verb, result, comparison, check. The term never appears.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An explanation checked for every word a patient would not use**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the first one is longer than most nurses expect.

An explanation checked for every word a patient would not use is the quickest way to find out how much of your own speech is professional habit.

Give the system the explanation as you would actually say it, the patient’s first language and roughly how he talks about his own body.

Ask it to list every word he is unlikely to use, including the ones that sound ordinary: management, monitor, fluid, acute, stable, observation, mobilise.

Ask for a replacement built from a verb and a result for each one, and for a single household comparison rather than a collection of them.

Ask for the whole thing in two sentences, and for one restatement question at the end that cannot be answered with yes.

It cannot see him nod, and it cannot hear the three seconds of silence that mean he has not understood a word. Those are why you are standing there.

> **DATA POINT · Health literacy** — Communication guidance commonly recommends plain alternatives to clinical terms, short explanations and checking understanding by asking the patient to restate rather than to confirm. What a nurse may explain, as against the treating clinician, is governed by local policy and scope of practice.  
> Source: general health-communication guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Restating and recall** — Studies of patient recall commonly report that a substantial share of information given at a bedside is not retained, and that asking patients to restate what they have been told identifies misunderstandings that confirmatory questions do not.  
> Source: general health-communication literature, 2025–2026.

**STAGE 5 · The Decision**

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say which words are avoided.
- Say what replaces them.
- Say how understanding is checked.
- Say what is written down.

*Language bank for the decision*
- Words whose everyday meaning differs from their clinical meaning, among them negative, positive, acute and stable, are not used to a patient without being explained in the same sentence.
- A clinical term is replaced by a verb and a result describing what it does, with at most one everyday comparison.
- Understanding is checked by asking the patient to restate the explanation to a named person, and never by a question that can be answered with yes.
- What the patient restated, and any misunderstanding corrected, is written in the nursing record in his words, together with what remains the treating clinician’s to explain.

> **WHAT HAPPENED** — The unit adopts the four lines. Over three months restatement checks rise from none to seventy-eight per cent of recorded explanations, and corrected misunderstandings are documented in thirty-one conversations. Mean explanation length falls from four minutes to under two. Complaints mentioning “nobody explained” fall from nine to two.

## Unit 2 · Teaching a Patient to Do It Himself

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: teaching a skill in the second person: you take, you hold, you feel · AI: a skill broken into steps a patient can carry out alone*

**Figure 2.0** — YOU DO NOT TEACH A SKILL BY DOING IT WELL IN FRONT OF SOMEBODY.: Watching a nurse do something beautifully teaches a patient that he cannot do it.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do you teach patients to do themselves?
- How do you know they can do it?
- Which step do people always forget?

A patient who must do something at home has to be taught, and teaching is not demonstrating. A nurse doing it smoothly looks like somebody doing a trick.

So the skill is broken into steps in the second person: you take, you hold, you feel, you wait, you look. Short steps, one action each.

Two steps are marked. The one people forget, which is usually a waiting step or a washing step. And the one that must never be skipped, which is usually a checking step.

Then he does it while you watch and say nothing. A return demonstration on two different days tells you more than six explanations on one.

And the record says what he can do, what he cannot yet do and who else has learnt it. Whether the task is his to do at all is a clinical decision, and it is not a nursing one.

**A. Comprehension.**

## Unit 3 · Consent, Assent and What a Nurse May Witness

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: witnessing and its limits: I witnessed, I was present, I did not · AI: a witnessing entry limited to what the nurse actually saw*

**Figure 3.0** — A WITNESS SAYS WHO SIGNED. A WITNESS DOES NOT SAY THAT ANYBODY UNDERSTOOD.: Two sentences that have saved a great many nurses, and one patient at a time.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do you sign as a witness on your ward?
- What does your signature say, and what does it not say?
- What do you do when a patient asks a question after signing?

Nurses are asked to witness signatures all day. The question nobody asks is what the signature of a witness actually claims.

In most settings it claims one narrow thing: that this person signed this document in front of me at this hour. It does not claim that the conversation happened, or that it was understood.

So the entry says both halves. “I witnessed Mr Mendes sign the form at 14.20. I was not present for the discussion with Doctor Haddad.” Nothing is hidden and nothing is overclaimed.

Where a patient asks a question after signing, that question goes in the record in his own words, and the clinician is asked to come back. A signed form does not close a conversation.

What consent requires, who may take it, how capacity is assessed and what a witness attests are all set by the law where you work and by your employer’s policy. The nurse’s part is to record exactly what she saw.

**Grammar: witnessing and its limits: I witnessed, I was present, I did not**

- Use I witnessed with a person, a document and an hour: “I witnessed Mr Mendes sign the consent form at 14.20.” That is the whole of the positive claim.

## Unit 4 · An Interpreter at a Bedside

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: short turns and the first person with an interpreter present · AI: an interpreted bedside conversation prepared as single-idea turns*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How often do you work with an interpreter?
- Who do you look at while you speak?

An interpreted conversation is not a slower version of an ordinary one. It is a different shape, and the shape is short turns.

One idea a turn. “Your ankles are swollen.” Stop. “It is water that has stayed in your legs.” Stop. Three ideas in one breath arrive as one, and usually the wrong one.

A professional interpreter is booked in advance; a relative is not an interpreter, and a child is never one. Who may interpret, and when, is set by your employer’s policy.

**Grammar: short turns and the first person with an interpreter present**

- One finite verb a turn where you can manage it. “Your ankles are swollen.” Then stop and wait. The pause is part of the sentence.
- Speak in the first person and the second: I shall, you will, I should like. Never “tell him that” or “ask him whether”, which turn the patient into a third party.

Fear at a bedside is usually specific. Not fear of treatment in general, but of a needle, of a noise, of being held still, or of what the result will mean.

The reassurance that works has two halves joined by and. “It will hurt, and it will be over in about ten seconds.” Nobody argues with that sentence, because it is true.

**Grammar: reassurance that is also honest: it will hurt, and it will be quick**

- Join the two halves with and, not but. “It will hurt, and it will be over in ten seconds.” But sets the halves against each other; and lets both be true.

- Give a duration with about: about ten seconds, about a minute, about as long as it takes to count to twenty. About is honest and it is still a number.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A ward rule explained with the reason and the exception**

**Figure 6.5** — Six lines, and the fourth one is the one that is nearly always left out.

A ward rule explained with the reason and the exception is the piece of speech that decides whether a corridor conversation at midnight ends with a blanket or with security.

Give the system the rule as your ward actually applies it, who it protects, who may authorise a departure from it and what is physically available tonight.

Ask for the rule in the we-ask-that form rather than as a prohibition, with the reason attached in the same sentence and the people it protects named.

Ask for your own limit and the route in consecutive sentences, because a limit without a route is what relatives remember as being told nothing can be done.

Ask for one compromise that is genuinely available: a chair, a blanket, a call at an hour, a promise to wake somebody before anything is done.

What it cannot do is sit down beside a frightened man and lower its voice. The sentences matter and so does the chair you pull out for him.

## Unit 7 · Discharge Teaching the Patient Can Repeat Back

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: checking teaching back: show me what you will do at home · AI: teaching checked by what the patient says back*

**Figure 7.0** — WHAT YOU SAID IS NOT THE TEACHING. WHAT HE SAYS BACK IS THE TEACHING.: Four things taught, three repeated back. The fourth one is the whole job.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When is discharge teaching usually done on your ward?
- How do you find out what has been remembered?
- Which part do patients lose most often?

Discharge teaching is usually done at the worst possible moment: in a corridor, with a bag in one hand and a taxi outside.

The test is a repeat-back. “Tell me what you will do tomorrow morning.” Whatever comes out of his mouth is what he is taking home; everything else is in your voice only.

The repeat-back finds the one part that went wrong, and usually it is a time or a quantity. He has the tablets right and the hours wrong, or the hours right and the writing-down forgotten.

Then you reteach that part only. Teaching the whole thing again punishes him for the three parts he had, and he will stop telling you what he does not know.

Teach the night before as well, sitting down, with whoever will be at home. Two teachings on two days, with the second one on the morning, is the only version that works.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: checking teaching back: show me what you will do at home**

- Use tell me and show me with a future clause: “Tell me what you will do tomorrow morning.” “Show me how you will write it down.” Both require production, not agreement.
- Use a named occasion rather than a general one: tomorrow morning, Sunday evening, the first time the bag is full. General questions get general answers.
- Praise the right parts specifically before correcting: “The four tablets were exactly right. The time is half past seven rather than eight.”
- Reteach with that part only and check it again: “Tell me the morning time once more.” Not “let us go through all of it again.”

## Unit 9 · Recording a Refusal of Care

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: recording a refusal: offered, explained, declined, recorded · AI: a refusal recorded with what was offered, said and understood*

**Figure 9.0** — A REFUSAL IS A DECISION, NOT A FAILURE OF NURSING.: The nurse who records a refusal properly has done the whole of her job, not failed at it.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do patients refuse most often on your ward?
- What do you write when somebody says no?
- When do you offer again, and when do you stop?

Patients refuse things all day: a wash, a tablet, a position, a sample, a visit from somebody they do not want to see. Most refusals are never written down.

An unrecorded refusal becomes, on paper, an omission by the nurse. The record shows a wash that did not happen and nobody who declined one.

Four parts do the work. What was offered. What was explained about it. That it was declined. And the reason, in his words: “I am too sore today.”

Then the practical half: whether you will offer again, and when; who has been told; and what has been arranged instead. A refusal usually changes a plan rather than ending it.

A patient may refuse care, and offering again is not the same as pressing. What may be done where somebody cannot decide for himself is a matter of law and policy, and it belongs to the treating clinician and the team, not to persuasion at a bedside.
