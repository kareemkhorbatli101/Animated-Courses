### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How long do you get at your multidisciplinary meeting?
- What do you bring that nobody else in the room has?
- What happens to the decisions afterwards?

A multidisciplinary meeting gives a nurse about a minute for each patient, and a minute spent describing a patient from the beginning will reach the ward round on Tuesday and stop there. The minute has a shape: the lead, the evidence, the request.

The lead is the thing you want decided, said first. “I am asking for the discharge to be moved to Friday.” Nine people now know what the next fifty seconds are for, which is the only condition under which they listen to them.

The evidence is three facts at most, chosen because they bear on the request rather than because they are interesting. Two sentences of walking distance and one of what his daughter can manage will decide a discharge; his admission bloods will not.

The reserve is the part nurses most often give away in the first ten seconds. You hold two facts back for the question you can see coming, and when the physiotherapist asks whether he can manage stairs, you have the answer and the day you tested it.

And the decision is written down as the meeting leaves it: taken, with an owner and a date, or deferred, with the reason and the week. A decision nobody recorded is a decision that will be taken again next Thursday, with the same nine people in the room.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: presenting a patient in one minute: lead, evidence, request**

- Lead with the present continuous of asking: “I am asking for the discharge to be moved to Friday.” It is shorter than I should like to request and it cannot be heard as a preliminary.
- Give evidence in the past simple with dates and figures, one fact a sentence: “He walked forty metres with a frame on Tuesday. He managed the stairs twice on Wednesday.”
- Keep the reserve in a conditional you do not say aloud, and deploy it in the past simple when asked: “Tuesday, with the physiotherapist present, and again on Thursday with me.”
- Close by repeating the request with an owner and a date attached: “So: Friday, with the district nurse informed by Thursday morning.” The repetition is what gets minuted.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A patient compressed to one minute with the nursing detail in reserve**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the fourth one is the one that wins the exchange rather than the minute.

A patient compressed to one minute with the nursing detail in reserve is an exercise in choosing what to leave out, and the material a nurse is most tempted to include is almost always the material she has worked hardest for.

Give the system everything you know about the patient, the decision you want from the meeting, who will be in the room and what each of them is likely to object to.

Ask for the lead in the asking form and for exactly three facts in the body, each with a date or a figure, and for a line beside each saying why it bears on the request.

Ask it to identify two further facts to hold in reserve, matched to the professions most likely to raise the corresponding objection, so that you are answering rather than remembering.

Ask for the close to repeat the request with an owner and a date, because the sentence that gets minuted is nearly always the last one said rather than the first.

What it cannot do is know that this patient got up alone at three in the morning on Tuesday and that nobody has written it down. That is the fact the whole meeting may turn on, and it exists only in your head until you put it somewhere.

> **DATA POINT · Multidisciplinary meetings** — Guidance on team meetings commonly recommends a structured, time-limited presentation stating the decision sought, supporting information selected for relevance, and a record of decisions with named responsibility and timescale.  
> Source: general clinical team-working guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Decision recording** — Studies of team meetings commonly report that decisions are frequently revisited where no owner and date were recorded, and that presentations stating the decision sought at the outset are more likely to produce a decision within the meeting.  
> Source: general clinical team-working literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: so that this meeting makes a decision about Mr Mendes today rather than deferring him to the twelfth. Scope: the discharge date and what has to be in place for it; his medical management and his outpatient follow-up belong to other people in the room. Inputs: everything I know about him from eight shifts, the decision I want, the nine professions who will be there, what the physiotherapist and the social worker are each likely to object to, and the figures from the record rather than from memory. ### Part 8 · The Decision Room · Eleven days and forty seconds

**STAGE 1 · The Brief**

A ward reviewed twenty-six patients presented at its weekly multidisciplinary meeting over three months. In fourteen presentations the decision sought was stated in the first sentence; eleven of those fourteen produced a decision in the meeting.

In twelve presentations the patient was described from admission onwards and the request came last or not at all; three of those twelve produced a decision, and nine were deferred to the following week.

The mean additional length of stay for the nine deferred patients was eight days.

**STAGE 5 · The Decision**

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say what the first sentence is.
- Say how much evidence follows.
- Say what is held in reserve.
- Say what is recorded.

*Language bank for the decision*
- The first sentence of any presentation to the meeting states the decision sought, in the form of a request.
- No more than three supporting facts are given, each carrying a date or a figure and selected because it bears on the decision rather than because it describes the patient.
- Further information is held in reserve and given in answer to questions, and the presenter prepares which facts are held for which likely objection.
- Every outcome is recorded as taken or deferred, with the name of the person responsible, the date by which it is to be done, and, where deferred, the reason and the week to which it is deferred.

> **WHAT HAPPENED** — The unit adopts the four lines and the chairman reads the first line aloud at the start of each meeting for a month. Over the following six months presentations leading with the request rise from fourteen in twenty-six to forty-nine in fifty-two, decisions taken within the meeting rise from fourteen to forty-four, and deferrals fall from twelve to five. The mean length of stay on the ward falls by one and a half days.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who reads the care plans on your ward?

Most care plans fail in the same three places. The goals are hopes rather than measurements, the actions belong to nobody in particular, and the review date has passed without anybody noticing, often by several weeks.

A goal is written as something that can be observed to have happened or not happened: not “maintain skin integrity”, which is an aspiration, but “no new area of non-blanching redness; heels inspected and recorded twice daily”. Somebody can tell you on Friday whether that is true.

An action carries a frequency and an owner, and the owner is a role rather than a person where the ward works shifts, because a plan that depends on Elena being on duty stops working on her days off. “Night nurse: reposition two-hourly and record the position.”

The review date is the part that distinguishes a plan from a document, and it is also the part most often treated as decorative. A date that has passed tells every reader that nobody has looked at this plan since the ninth, which is usually true and never intended.

And the evaluation is written honestly, which includes the goals that were abandoned and why. A plan in which every goal was achieved is a plan that was written to be achieved rather than to be useful, and experienced nurses can spot one across a room.

**Grammar: a care plan as goals, actions and owners**

- Write goals in the negative or the numerical, which are the two forms that can be checked: “No new area of non-blanching redness by Friday the sixteenth.” “Walking twenty metres with a frame unaided by the twentieth.”
- Write actions as a role, a colon, an imperative and a frequency: “Night nurse: reposition two-hourly and record the position.” The colon is doing the work of a whole sentence.
- Date the review in the future and in words rather than as a relative interval: “To be reviewed on the sixteenth of March”, not “review in one week”, which nobody can check against today.
- Evaluate in the past simple with the outcome and the reason: “Goal abandoned on the eleventh: he declines repositioning at night and has said so three times.” Abandoned is a respectable word in a plan.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Where do your patients go when they leave your country?

A patient transferred into another health system arrives carrying a document written by somebody who assumed a great deal. A grade of nurse that exists only here, a scale used in two countries, an abbreviation invented on one ward, and a date written in an order that means something entirely different three thousand miles away.

The register is therefore deliberately explicit, and it is longer than the summary you would write for the ward next door. Every abbreviation is spelt out at first use, every measurement carries its unit, and every date is written with the month in words.

The structure is two columns in prose: what we did, and what remains to be done. The first is in the past simple with dates; the second is in the infinitive with a name or a role attached and the date by which it matters.

Medicines are the commonest source of harm in transfer, and the commonest cause within that is a name that differs between countries. The safe practice is to state what the patient is taking with the full generic name, the strength and the frequency, and to say explicitly that local brand names may differ.

And a final paragraph says what must not be assumed: that a scale is familiar, that a grade translates, that a document has arrived, that anybody has telephoned ahead. The receiving nurse has her own system and her own law, and both are entirely hers.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: transfer register for an unfamiliar system: what we did, what remains**

- Write what was done in the past simple with a date in full: “The wound was dressed twice daily from the fourth of March.” The month in words removes the only genuinely dangerous ambiguity in a date.
- Write what remains in the infinitive with an owner and a date: “To be measured weekly: receiving nurse, from the twentieth of March.” The infinitive makes it an instruction without presuming to give one.
- Expand every abbreviation at first use and state every unit: “non-blanching redness”, “four centimetres by two”, “fifty-eight kilograms on the fourth of March”. Nothing is left to be inferred from context.
- Use may differ and should not be assumed for the limits: “Local brand names may differ from the generic names given here.” “Our nursing grades should not be assumed to correspond to yours.”

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What was the last audit done on your ward?
- What changed because of it?
- When was it repeated?

A nursing audit has five parts and it is possible to do all five badly in a morning. The standard, which must exist before the counting begins. The method, which somebody else must be able to repeat. The finding, which is a proportion with a denominator. The change, which is somebody’s to make. And the re-audit date, which is the part that distinguishes an audit from an observation.

The standard is quoted rather than invented: a sentence from a policy, a guideline or a local agreement, with its source. An audit against a standard you have made up measures your own opinion with great precision.

The method is stated in enough detail for a stranger to repeat it: how many records, chosen how, over what period, read by whom, against what question. Forty consecutive records is a method; forty records is a number.

The finding is always a proportion and always carries its denominator, because nineteen is meaningless and nineteen of forty is a fact. Where a figure surprises you, it is quoted with the surprise rather than smoothed over, since the unexpected finding is the one worth the morning.

And the change is divided honestly. What a ward can do by next Monday goes in one list, with a name against each item. What requires a decision above the ward goes in the other, with the name of the person it has been sent to and the date it was sent.

**A nursing audit written up as method, finding and change**

**Figure 4.5** — Six lines, and the fourth one is usually the only reason the morning was worth spending.

A nursing audit written up as method, finding and change is a short document that is very easy to write in a way that produces no change at all, and the commonest route to that outcome is an adjective where a denominator belongs.

Give the system the standard with its source and date, the sample and how it was chosen, the period, who read the records, the raw counts, and anything you know that might explain a figure.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who taught you at the bedside, and how?
- What do you ask a student before you show her anything?
- Where do you correct her?

Bedside teaching has four verbs and they are nearly always done in the wrong order, because the temptation is to begin by showing, which is the pleasant part, and to end by asking, which is the part that would have saved the time.

Ask first, and ask about the thing rather than about her confidence: not “have you done this before?”, which produces a yes, but “tell me the steps as you would do them at home”. Three sentences from her will tell you which of the four verbs you actually need.

Show once, at normal speed, without commentary, because a demonstration narrated while it is performed teaches two things at once and neither of them well. The commentary comes afterwards and in answer to her questions.

Then watch, and this is the part that separates teaching from company. Hands behind your back, no sounds of encouragement, no reaching in, and no finishing the step she is slow at. A student who is never allowed to be slow never finds out that she can do it.

And correct outside the bay. A correction at a bedside is heard by the patient, who then has a view about the student, which the student will carry for the rest of the placement; the only exception is anything unsafe, which is stopped at once and explained afterwards.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: teaching at the bedside: ask, show, watch, correct**

- Ask with an imperative that requires production: “Tell me the steps as you would do them at home.” “Show me what you would check first.” Both are unanswerable with yes.
- Announce the demonstration and its silence: “I shall do it once and I shall not talk while I do it. Ask me anything you like afterwards.” The announcement is what makes the silence bearable for both of you.
- Announce the watching in the same way, and then keep to it: “Now you do it and I shall say nothing at all.” Any sound you make during it becomes part of what she has learnt to need.
- Correct with a place, a number and a reason: “Outside, in a moment: the third step, and here is why it matters.” Praise with the same precision: “You did five of the six, and the one you missed was the label.”

**A bedside teaching session planned as ask, show, watch, correct**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines, and the third one includes the only thing you are allowed to interrupt for.

A bedside teaching session planned as ask, show, watch, correct takes five minutes to prepare and prevents the commonest injury in nursing education, which is a student corrected in front of a patient in her first fortnight.

Give the system the task, what the student has told you about her own experience, what the patient has consented to, and what in this task would be unsafe rather than merely wrong.

Ask for the opening question in a form that requires her to produce the steps, and for a note of which answers would tell you to skip the demonstration altogether.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which policy on your ward is most often broken?

Policies are written to be defensible and instructions are written to be obeyed, and the two purposes pull in opposite directions. Forty pages of qualification is good governance and bad nursing, because nobody at a bedside at two in the morning is going to find paragraph 7.3.2.

The translation has three moves. Find the sentence in the policy that actually requires something, quote it exactly, and then write beneath it the one line a nurse can act on, with a role, a frequency and a place to record it.

What you simplified is marked as simplified, because an instruction that silently departs from its policy is a trap for whoever follows it. “Policy requires X; on this ward that means Y, and the difference is Z.”

What the policy does not cover is written down and sent to its author, with a date. Most wards have four or five practices that no document describes at all, usually invented to solve a real problem by somebody who has since left.

And the instruction is circulated where instructions are actually read: at handover, aloud, for a fortnight, rather than laminated and attached to a wall that nobody has looked at since the card went up.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who asks you for reports about patients?
- What permission do you have, and where is it recorded?
- What have you been asked for that you should not have supplied?

Nurses are asked for reports by employers, insurers, solicitors, social services, schools, courts and occasionally by journalists. The request is almost always wider than the permission, which is why the first paragraph of a third-party report is about the report rather than about the patient.

That paragraph names the recipient, quotes the question asked, and states the permission relied upon with its date. It is tedious to write and it is the only part of the document that protects everybody in it, including the patient and including you.

Then the question is answered, and nothing else is. The second paragraph of a report is where nurses supply, unasked, an opinion about a family, a diagnosis they are not qualified to give or a judgement about somebody’s capacity, and that paragraph is read out in a tribunal eighteen months later.

Facts come from the record with their dates and are marked as coming from the record. Where an opinion is genuinely asked for, it is given as an opinion and in one sentence. The basis is stated: “In my opinion, based on six shifts in March, …”

And where the request goes beyond what you may answer, you decline that part in writing and say why, and you name who could answer it. Local law and your employer’s policy decide what may be disclosed, to whom and with whose permission, and neither of them is a matter of goodwill.

**Grammar: scope and permission in a report: what was asked, what may be said**

- Open in the passive with the recipient, the request and its date: “This report is prepared for the local authority at its request of the fourth of May.” The passive is right because the report, not you, is the subject.
- Quote the question in reported speech and mark it as the question: “The question asked was whether he can manage stairs unaided.” One question, quoted; nothing added to it.
- Attribute every fact to the record with its date: “He walked forty metres with a frame on the twelfth of May, per the nursing record.” Per the record is four words and it does a great deal.
- Give an opinion only where asked, with its basis and in one sentence: “In my opinion, based on six shifts in March, he cannot manage stairs unaided.” Then stop, and decline the rest in writing: “I shall not answer the second question, because it concerns capacity, which is not within my competence; Doctor Haddad could answer it.”

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A third-party report limited to what was asked and permitted**

**Figure 7.5** — Six lines, and the sixth one is the one that will be quoted approvingly.

A third-party report limited to what was asked and permitted is a short document in which the commonest error is generosity, because a nurse asked for help wants to be helpful and the helpful second paragraph is the one read out in a tribunal.

Give the system the request exactly as it arrived, the permission you hold with its date and form, the facts from the record with their dates, and the question of which you are competent to give an opinion.

Ask for an opening paragraph naming the recipient, quoting the request with its date, stating the purpose and identifying the permission relied upon, and tell it that nothing about the patient may appear before that paragraph.

Ask for every fact to carry its date and the words per the nursing record, and for anything you cannot source to the record to be removed rather than softened into an impression.

Ask for any opinion to be confined to one sentence with its basis stated, and for a closing paragraph declining anything outside the question or your competence, with the name of somebody who could answer it.

What it cannot do is want to be helpful, which is both its advantage here and the reason it cannot be left to decide what the permission covers. That is a matter for your policy, your law and, where there is any doubt at all, somebody whose job it is.

Caring, committed, compassionate, hard-working, a good team player: a panel reading nineteen applications has read these words seventy times before lunch and has stopped seeing them.

- What cannot be delivered on your ward when the rota has a hole in it?

Guidelines are written for a ward that is fully staffed, which is not a criticism of guidelines but a description of them. On a shift two nurses short, something in the guideline will not happen, and the only question is whether that was decided in advance by somebody with a name or at half past two by whoever was nearest.

A limit stated as a complaint produces sympathy and nothing else. A limit stated as a pair of sentences produces a decision: “We cannot deliver two-hourly repositioning for all eleven patients tonight. What we can do is two-hourly for the four at highest risk and three-hourly for the rest.”

The prioritisation is written before the shift rather than reconstructed after it, which is the difference between a professional judgement and an excuse. Four names, a frequency each, and the hour at which the decision was made.

Then somebody accepts the risk by name, and the name is the whole point. A risk accepted by the matron at seven o’clock is an organisational decision; the same risk accepted by nobody is a decision that belongs to the nurse who was on duty when it mattered.

And the pattern is kept. Four such entries in a month is the document that funds a post, and it is the only document that ever has, because an establishment is changed by evidence rather than by anybody’s exhaustion.

**Grammar: stating a limit: we cannot staff, we shall not, what we can do is**

- State the limit with cannot and a complete object: “We cannot deliver two-hourly repositioning for all eleven patients tonight.” Cannot with a vague object is a complaint; cannot with a number is a fact.
- Follow it immediately with what we can do is, which converts the limit into a proposal: “What we can do is two-hourly for the four at highest risk and three-hourly for the remaining seven.”
- Use shall not for the things you are choosing rather than failing: “We shall not accept a twelfth admission before the discharge in bay two leaves.” Shall not is a decision; cannot is a capacity.
- Ask for acceptance with a name and an hour, in the passive: “Accepted by the matron at twenty hundred.” Then write the hour you asked, and the hour you were answered.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What have you used a drafting tool for this year?
- What did you have to supply that it could not?
- What would you not let any system write for you?

Everything in these six books has been a draft, and the word is exact. A system can arrange what it is given: a trend into a sentence, a concern into an escalation, a policy into an instruction, a shift into a documented limit. None of those arrangements contains a single fact that was not supplied to it.

What cannot be delegated divides into three. The noticing, which is what a nurse does by being in a room six times in a day and finding the sixth time different. The judgement, which is deciding that this difference matters tonight on this ward with these two nurses. And the signature, which is a person taking responsibility for a sentence in a record that will outlive the shift and sometimes the patient.

The commonest error is not trusting a system too much; it is giving it the wrong work. A nurse who asks a tool to decide whether to escalate has asked the wrong question of the wrong thing. A nurse who writes out four sets of observations by hand when something would arrange them is spending on arrangement the minutes she needed for the bedside.

There is a second commonest error, which is the belief that a sentence drafted elsewhere is somehow less yours. It is entirely yours the moment you sign it. Everything in that record is read as your word, and no explanation of how it was drafted has ever been accepted by anybody who mattered.

And the last thing is the one nobody can arrange: what a patient says when there is nobody else in the room. That sentence exists only because you were there and you asked, and it is still the most valuable thing in any nursing record anywhere.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: summary, judgement and what cannot be delegated**

- Use can plus a verb of arrangement for what a system does: it can arrange, assemble, order, draft, list, check, count. None of those verbs means decide and none of them means notice.
- Use cannot plus a verb of presence or responsibility for what it does not: it cannot notice, sit, wait, ask, sign, or be accountable. The two lists are short and they do not overlap.
- Mark your own judgement explicitly in the record, because it is the part nobody else supplied: “In my judgement this mattered tonight, with two nurses on duty, and I escalated at twenty past four.”
- Sign in the first person and take the whole of it: “E. Petrova, RN, 16.25.” Nothing about how a sentence was drafted belongs beside a signature, because the signature answers for all of it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**What no system can do for a nurse’s written word**

**Figure 10.5** — Six lines, and not one of them has ever been available to anything but a person.

What no system can do for a nurse’s written word is worth stating plainly at the end of six books that have spent most of their length explaining what such systems can do.

They can arrange what they are given, and the arranging is real work that takes real minutes off a shift. Four sets of observations into a trend sentence, forty pages of policy into an instruction, a draft of a transfer summary in which every month is written out in words.

They cannot notice. The sixth visit of a day being different from the first five is produced by a person walking into a room six times, and it exists nowhere until she says it.

They cannot sit in silence for fifteen seconds while somebody decides whether to tell you what is actually frightening them, and the sentence that comes out of that silence has been the most valuable line in half the records in this book.

And they cannot be accountable. Every figure in a drafted entry is yours the moment you sign it. Is why the only unbreakable rule in this chapter is that you read every number in the output against the source, even at the end of a twelve-hour shift, even when you read nothing else twice.

That leaves the work where it has always been. The noticing, the asking, the silence, the judgement and the name at the bottom are yours, and the arranging is somebody else’s problem now, which is a good bargain and the only one on offer.
