### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: identity questions and confirmation: can you tell me your name**

- Use can you tell me for the open question. Can you tell me your name? Can you tell me your date of birth? The patient does the talking.
- Do not use are you. “Are you Mr Costa?” needs only yes. A tired or frightened patient says yes.
- Say the answer back to him. So you are Mateus Costa, born on the fourth of June. Is that right?
- Use I am for yourself, every time. I am Rosa. I am your nurse this morning. The patient has met nine people today.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An identity check prepared against the chart, the band and the patient**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the first three must agree before anything is given.

An identity check prepared against the chart, the band and the patient is a small job. A system does it well. A tired nurse at seven in the morning does it badly.

Give it the chart, the band and the name the patient says. Ask it to show the three side by side, and to mark anything that does not match.

Ask for the question in the words you will use. Can you tell me your name, please. Not: are you Mr Costa.

Ask it to list the other patients on the ward with the same family name. On a ward of forty there is often one, and nobody told you.

Ask it for the language he prefers and the name he uses for himself. A record is not always written in the name a man answers to.

What no system can do is wait. The patient has to say his own name, and you have to be quiet while he says it.

> **DATA POINT · Three-point check** — Hospital practice commonly asks staff to confirm a patient’s identity from more than one source — the patient’s own words, a wristband and the record — before any medicine, sample or procedure.  
> Source: general patient-safety guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why checks fail** — Reviews of identification errors commonly find that the check was done from one source only, that a closed question was used, or that a wristband was missing at the time. Local rules on banding and checking differ between hospitals.  
> Source: general patient-safety guidance, 2025–2026.

**A pain description recorded in the patient’s own words**

**Figure 3.5** — Six lines, and the third is the one that is usually thrown away.

A pain description recorded in the patient’s own words takes one minute to write. Everybody who sees him in the next week reads it.

**A care task written as something a patient may agree to or refuse**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Observations entered with their units and their previous values**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines, and the last one is the one a flat line hides.

Observations entered with their units and their previous values are a small piece of work. They turn a column of numbers into a line a doctor reads in three seconds.

Give the system this set, the set before and the time of each. Ask for every number with its unit and the previous value beside it.

Ask it to mark the direction of each number with a word: up from, down from, unchanged at. The words are what carry on a telephone.

Ask it to leave a gap where a reading was not taken, and to say why. An empty box is information; a copied number is not.

What no system can do is take the reading again with a bigger cuff. The number in the chart is only as good as the arm it came from.
