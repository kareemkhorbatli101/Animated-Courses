## Unit 1 · The Patient Who Is Not Right, and the Words for It

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: describing a change you cannot name yet: not himself, different from · AI: a vague concern turned into the observations that support it*

**Figure 1.0** — “HE IS NOT RIGHT” IS A FINDING. IT IS NOT YET A SENTENCE.: A nurse who has seen a patient six times today knows something a single reading does not.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you describe a patient who is not right?
- What makes you sure, and what makes you doubt yourself?
- Where do you write a worry you cannot yet explain?

A nurse often knows a patient is becoming unwell before anything measurable has changed. She has seen him six times today, and this time he is different.

The difficulty is language. “He is not right” is true, and it travels badly. The sentence has to be turned into things that can be seen by somebody who has not been in the room.

So the first step is comparison with himself. Not “he is confused”, but “he knew the day of the week this morning and he does not now”. The baseline is the same man four hours ago.

The second step is the search. What is the pulse, the pressure, the breathing, the urine, the temperature, the sugar? A worry with two findings behind it is an escalation; a worry alone is a mood.

And the third step is to write it at the time, in those words, so that a review which finds nothing still leaves a record of why somebody looked.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: describing a change you cannot name yet: not himself, different from**

- Use not quite himself and different from, with a time. He is not quite himself this afternoon. He is different from this morning.
- Make the comparison explicit with was and is. He was answering questions at eight; he is not answering them now. His colour was normal at two and is grey now.
- Use comparatives for measurable change: faster, slower, lower, higher, quieter, less. His breathing is faster than it was at two.
- Avoid the diagnostic guess. “I think it is an infection” invites an argument about infection. “He is grey and his breathing is faster” invites a visit.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A vague concern turned into the observations that support it**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the third one is the honest part most people leave out.

A vague concern turned into the observations that support it is the piece of work that gets a doctor to a bedside before a number has crossed a line.

Give the system what you have noticed in your own words, the current observations, the sets from earlier today and what is usual for this patient.

Ask for the comparison in the was and is form, with an hour on each half, and for the findings that support the worry listed with their numbers.

Ask it to say plainly which observations have not changed, because a nurse who hides the normal ones is argued with and a nurse who says them is believed.

Ask for the baseline sentence: what is usual for this man. He usually finishes his lunch. He usually reads the newspaper. He usually walks to the lavatory alone.

What no system can supply is the noticing. You have seen him six times today, and the sixth time was different; no chart holds that.

> **DATA POINT · Nursing concern** — Deterioration guidance commonly treats nursing concern as a legitimate trigger for review, and recommends describing the change by comparison with the patient’s own earlier state, with the supporting observations. Local thresholds differ.  
> Source: general acute-care guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Concern and outcome** — Studies of rapid-response systems commonly report that reviews triggered by staff concern alone identify deterioration that scores have not yet detected, and that a proportion of such reviews find no abnormality. Rates vary by setting.  
> Source: general patient-safety literature, 2025–2026.

**STAGE 5 · The Decision**

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say what a documented concern produces.
- Say what the concern must contain.
- Say what may not be the only answer.
- Say what is recorded after the review.

*Language bank for the decision*
- A nursing concern recorded with at least two supporting findings produces a medical review with a stated time, whatever the early warning score.
- The concern is recorded as a comparison with the patient’s own earlier state, with the hours, together with the observations that have and have not changed.
- Repeating the observations is never the only response to a documented concern; a review may be deferred only with a named clinician and a stated hour.
- After the review, the finding, the clinician’s name and any threshold for calling again are written in the same entry as the concern.

## Unit 2 · Observations Into a Trend, and the Sentence That Follows

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: trends and direction: has fallen from, is rising, twice in an hour · AI: observations assembled into a trend with its direction marked*

**Figure 2.0** — A SINGLE SET SAYS WHERE HE IS. FOUR SETS SAY WHERE HE IS GOING.: Every reading in range, and every reading moving the same way.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many sets of observations do you look at before you speak?
- What worries you more: one number out of range or four moving together?
- Where would somebody see a trend on your ward?

Observations are usually taken, written and left. The next nurse takes another set and writes it under the first. Nobody looks up the column.

A trend is what the column says. Over four hours a pulse may go from seventy-two to eighty-four to ninety-six to a hundred and eight, and never once leave the normal range.

Scores are built to catch the number that crosses a line. They are slower with the number that is drifting inside one, which is why the drift is a nursing job.

The sentence that follows a trend has three parts: the direction, the hours and the request. “His pulse has risen from seventy-two to a hundred and eight over four hours, and I should like him seen before six.”

And the repeat has to carry a time. “Repeat the observations” is a wish. “Repeat them at half past four and telephone me either way” is an instruction.

**Grammar: trends and direction: has fallen from, is rising, twice in an hour**

- Use the present perfect for the movement and the present for where he is now. His pulse has risen from seventy-two to a hundred and eight. It is now a hundred and eight.

Between the moment a nurse becomes worried and the moment somebody else arrives, work happens. Observations are repeated. A sample is sent. A family is telephoned.

Most falls on a ward are unwitnessed. A nurse hears something, reaches the bay and finds a man on the floor between the bed and the chair.

What she can write is exact: the hour she arrived, where he was lying, which way he was facing, what was within reach and who else came.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: measurement and position: at the base, two centimetres, since Monday**

- Give dimensions with by and the unit once: “four centimetres by two”. For depth, say at its deepest: “one centimetre deep at the deepest point”.
- Use at, on, over and under for position: at the base, at the edge, on the left heel, over the sacrum, under the dressing. Write left and right in full.
- Use since with a start and for with a duration: “dressed daily since Monday”, “present for eleven days”. Both answer a question the reader will otherwise ask.
- Compare with a named day, not with memory: “four by two today, against three by two on Monday the fourteenth”. A comparison without a date is an impression.

**Grammar: reporting an error plainly: I gave, I did not check, I have reported**
