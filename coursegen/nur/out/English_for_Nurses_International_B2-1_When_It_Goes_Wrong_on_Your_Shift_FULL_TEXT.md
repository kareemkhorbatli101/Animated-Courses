### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When did you last decide not to say something to somebody senior?
- What would you have needed in order to say it?
- What happens on your ward when a junior nurse disagrees with a consultant?

The difficulty in speaking up across rank is almost never the content, which a nurse usually knows better than the person she is speaking to, and almost always the opening, because the first sentence is the one that costs something.

So the first sentence is the one to prepare, and it should be flat, short and unapologetic: “I need to say something about bed four.” It announces that a conversation is beginning rather than asking permission for one.

What follows is divided deliberately into two halves, because they have different weights: what you can prove, which does not depend on your rank at all. What you are worried about, which does, and which should therefore be labelled as a worry rather than smuggled in as a fact.

One repetition is not insubordination. If the point has not landed, it is said again in the same words and the same tone, because changing either implies that the first attempt was a mistake, and it was not.

And then it is written down: that you spoke, at what hour, to whom, and what was decided. A nurse who has spoken and been overruled is in a wholly different position from a nurse who cannot show that she spoke at all.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: speaking up across rank: I need to say something about**

- Open with I need to say something about, I want to raise something about, or there is something I have to tell you about, and then stop talking until the other person has turned towards you, because a concern delivered to somebody’s back is not a concern.
- Divide what follows explicitly, with a signpost: “There are two things. The first I can show you; the second is a worry and I cannot prove it.” The division protects the first half from the second.
- Repeat with the same words and an explicit marker: “I shall say that again, because I do not think it has landed.” The marker is what makes it a repetition rather than a challenge.
- Close by naming the decision as theirs and the record as yours: “If you decide to wait until the morning, that is your decision, and I shall write down that I raised it at ten past four.”

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A speaking-up script prepared for a conversation across rank**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the first one is the only one most people need rehearsed.

A speaking-up script prepared for a conversation across rank is the difference between a nurse who intended to raise something and a nurse who raised it, and the difference lies almost entirely in the first eight words.

Give the system the concern as you would say it to a colleague of your own grade, the figures you hold with their hours, who you are speaking to and how that person has reacted to concerns before.

Ask for one opening sentence containing no apology, no just, no only, no probably and no obviously, and short enough to be said to somebody who is already walking away.

Ask for the body in two explicitly divided halves, so that your evidence is not weakened by standing next to your intuition, and so that the intuition is still said rather than suppressed.

Ask for a repetition sentence with its marker, and a closing sentence that gives the decision to the other person and the record to you, because those two things are not in competition.

What no script can do is take the first breath in front of somebody who outranks you by twenty years. It can only make sure that when you do, the sentence that comes out is the right one.

> **DATA POINT · Speaking up** — Safety literature commonly reports that staff who intend to raise a concern are most often stopped by the anticipated cost of the opening exchange rather than by uncertainty about the clinical content, and that rehearsed opening phrases increase the likelihood of the concern being raised.  
> Source: general patient-safety literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Hierarchy and silence** — Studies of clinical teams commonly report that a substantial proportion of staff recall a recent occasion on which they did not voice a safety concern, most often to somebody more senior, and that the stated reason is the anticipated response rather than doubt about the concern itself.  
> Source: general patient-safety literature, 2025–2026.

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say what opening phrase the ward adopts.
- Say how a concern is structured.
- Say what is recorded, whatever the answer.

*Language bank for the decision*
- The ward adopts a single opening phrase for raising a concern across rank, which any member of staff may use to any other and which is not to be treated as a challenge to the person addressed.

## Unit 2 · Graded Assertiveness at the Bedside

**Figure 2.0** — THREE SENTENCES, IN ORDER. THE THIRD ONE STOPS THE ROOM.: The third phrase works because the first two were used first, and not instead of it.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do you say when something is going wrong in front of you?
- What is the strongest thing you have ever said at a bedside?
- What happens on your ward when somebody says the word unsafe?

Graded assertiveness exists because the alternatives at a bedside are both bad: saying nothing until the thing has happened, or shouting at a colleague in front of a patient who is awake and who will remember it for the rest of his admission.

The grades are three and they are used in order. I am concerned, which invites an answer. I am uncomfortable, which says that the answer did not satisfy me. And this is unsafe, which is not a feeling at all but a statement that the procedure stops.

The order is the whole of the mechanism, because the third phrase draws its force from the two that came before it. A nurse who begins with unsafe has nothing left, and a nurse who never reaches it has only ever expressed feelings.

The tone stays level at every grade, and the volume does not rise, because a raised voice turns a safety intervention into an argument about manners, and the argument about manners is one you will lose even when you are right.

And afterwards, whatever happened, it is written down in the same three phrases, so that the record shows a graded exchange rather than a disagreement between two people whose accounts differ.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: graded assertiveness: I am concerned, I am uncomfortable, this is unsafe**

- The first two grades are first-person statements about you, which is what makes them unarguable: “I am concerned that the wristband has not been checked.” Nobody can tell you that you are not concerned.
- The third grade is not about you at all. It is a statement about the situation together with a request: “This is unsafe. I am asking you to stop.” The subject changes from I to this, and that change is the escalation.
- Add the observation, not the accusation: “…that the wristband has not been checked”, never “…that you have not checked the wristband”. The first describes a gap; the second describes a person.
- Offer the move outside in the same breath as the stop: “This is unsafe. I am asking you to stop, and we can talk about it outside the bay.” The patient has heard enough already.

## Unit 3 · Refusing an Unsafe Instruction

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: refusal with an alternative: I shall not do that, and I shall · AI: a refusal drafted with the alternative you will provide*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What have you been asked to do that you should not have done?
- What did you say, and what happened to the patient while it was settled?
- What can you point to on your ward when you refuse something?

Nurses are occasionally instructed to do things they should not do: a task outside their competence, a delegation to somebody untrained, a shortcut through a check, a medicine they have not been authorised to give. Most of these instructions are given in a hurry by somebody who has not thought about them for more than four seconds.

The refusal has two halves and they belong in the same breath, because a bare refusal leaves the patient in a gap between two professionals who are now disagreeing with each other. “I shall not give that without a second checker, and I shall find one within five minutes.”

The reason is given once, plainly, and then not argued. Repeating it in different words invites a negotiation, and a competence you do not have is not a thing that can be negotiated into existence by a senior colleague who is late for a list.

While the disagreement stands, somebody has to keep the patient covered, and that somebody is usually you. The nurse in charge is called in, not to arbitrate the principle, but to make sure that the patient does not become the place where the argument happens.

And it is recorded at the time, in the plainest possible terms: the instruction, the refusal, the alternative offered, who was told and what they decided. A refusal that nobody can find in the notes becomes, three weeks later, a difference of recollection between two people, one of whom is more senior than the other.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: refusal with an alternative: I shall not do that, and I shall**

- Join the two halves with and, in one sentence, so that the refusal cannot be quoted without the offer: “I shall not give that without a second checker, and I shall find one within five minutes.”
- Use shall not rather than cannot where the reason is authorisation rather than ability, because cannot invites somebody to tell you that of course you can, and shall not does not.
- Give the reason in one clause and then stop: “It is outside what I am trained and authorised to do.” A second clause beginning because or you see turns it into an opening bid.
- Name the protection of the patient as a separate sentence: “He will not be left while this is settled; I am staying with him and I have asked Sister Doyle to come.”

**A refusal drafted with the alternative you will provide**

## Unit 4 · Telling a Patient About a Nursing Error

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who tells the patient when a nurse makes an error on your ward?
- How soon, and with whom present?
- What is the hardest part of the sentence?

The disclosure of a nursing error has a shape, and the shape is what happened, what is being done now, and what happens next. Anything that arrives before what happened is heard as an attempt to prepare the ground, and it is usually right to be heard that way.

The first sentence is plain and it is first person: “Mr Delgado, I have made a mistake. I gave you tablets that were meant for another patient.” No preamble about how busy the ward has been, because that sentence is true and it belongs somewhere else entirely.

What is being done now is the part the patient most wants and the nurse least has: the doctor has been telephoned, she is on her way, and the observations are being taken every hour until she has seen him. Facts, with hours, rather than reassurance.

What happens next divides sharply. Whether he will be all right is a clinical question and it belongs to the treating clinician, who should be the person who answers it. What the nurse can promise is her own conduct: that she will stay, that she will write it down, and that his daughter will be telephoned if he wants her telephoned.

And then she stays. The instinct after an error is to find somewhere else to be, and the single most useful thing a nurse can do in the hour after a disclosure is to remain visible at the bedside of the person she has wronged.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the plain apology from a nurse: I am sorry, I made a mistake**

- The apology is a bare sentence with no subordinate clause attached: “I am sorry.” Anything joined to it with because, but or although converts it into the first half of a defence.
- The error is stated in the first person, active, past: “I gave you tablets that were meant for another patient.” The passive, “tablets were given”, removes the only person the patient wants to hear from.
- The present continuous and the present perfect carry “what now”: “The doctor has been telephoned. She is on her way. Your observations are being taken every hour.”
- Mark the boundary explicitly with whether: “Whether this will affect you is for her to tell you, and she will.” Whether signals a real question rather than an evasion.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An error disclosure drafted as what happened, what now, what next**

**Figure 4.5** — Six lines, and the second one is the shortest and the hardest.

An error disclosure drafted as what happened, what now, what next is a piece of writing most nurses do only two or three times in a career. Is exactly why it is worth drafting rather than improvising at a bedside with a dry mouth.

Give the system what you actually did and at what hour, who you have told and when, what is being done for the patient now, and what the patient has already said or asked.

Ask for the error in the first person, active and past, with the hour in it, and for the apology as a bare sentence with nothing joined to it by because, but or although.

Ask it to remove every reassurance about outcome, because the outcome is not yours to promise and a promise you cannot keep is the thing he will remember if it fails.

Ask for the clinical boundary marked with whether and the name of the person who will answer it, and for the next-steps line to contain only things you can personally guarantee. That you will stay, that you will write it down, that you will telephone whoever he wants telephoned.

What it cannot do is stay in the bay afterwards. The hour after the disclosure is the part that decides what this patient thinks of nurses for the rest of his life, and it is spent on a chair beside him rather than in the sluice.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When do incident reports get written on your ward?

An incident report written three days later is not the same document as one written before the shift ends, and the difference is not merely one of accuracy. What is lost first is never the big thing; it is the hour the alarm sounded, the name of the agency nurse who was in the bay, and the fact that the second trolley was broken.

The register is flat and the adjectives go. Not “the patient was found in a very distressed state on the floor”, but “found on the floor at 21.40, lying on his right side, saying that his hip hurt”. The second version is shorter, it claims less, and it survives being read by somebody who was not there.

Conditions are named and culprits are not. “The two patients have surnames differing by one letter and were in adjacent beds” is a sentence an organisation can act on. “Nurse X failed to check” is a sentence that produces a meeting about Nurse X and no change to the beds.

Most forms contain a box that invites blame, usually phrased as a question about contributory human factors, and the discipline is to answer it with conditions as well. The interruption, the equipment, the staffing figure at that hour, the agency nurse who had never worked the bay before.

Then it is filed before you go home, and the reference number is written in your own notes, because a report you cannot find afterwards is a report you cannot be shown to have made.

**Grammar: factual register for an incident: what, when, who was present**

- Begin each statement with the fact and attach the hour: “Found on the floor at 21.40.” The hour is part of the fact rather than a detail added at the end of the paragraph.
- Delete the intensifiers without exception: very, extremely, highly, completely, significantly, severely. They carry no information and they are the first words quoted back at you.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is your job while a doctor is giving bad news?
- What do patients ask you after the doctor has gone?
- What gets written down about the conversation?

A nurse present at a bad-news conversation has three jobs, and only one of them happens while anybody is talking. Before: a chair for everybody, the door closed, the bleep handed to somebody else, and a box of tissues within reach rather than across the room.

During: silence. The urge to soften, to add, to explain in simpler words while the doctor is still speaking is strong and it is almost always wrong, because two voices in a bad-news conversation leave the patient certain of nothing.

After: the real work. The doctor has gone, and what the patient now says is the only reliable record of what he has understood. “What did you hear her say?” is a better question than “did you understand what she said?”, and the answer goes into the notes in his own words.

Those words are frequently not what was said. A man told that his disease has spread may say that he has been given six months, which nobody said; a woman told that treatment is possible may say that she is going to die, which nobody said either.

Where the gap matters, the clinician is asked to come back, and the gap itself is recorded as a gap: what she said, what he has taken from it, and that she has been asked to return. That is not a criticism of anybody and it should not be written as one.

**A record of what a patient was told, and by whom, in his words**

**Figure 6.5** — Six lines, and the fifth one is the reason the whole entry exists.

A record of what a patient was told, and by whom, in his words, is the entry that prevents the same conversation being had three times by three people who each believe something different about what the first one contained.

Give the system the hour and the speaker, what was actually said as closely as you can recall it, what the patient said afterwards when you asked him, and who else was in the room.

Ask for the speaker and the hour in the first sentence, because an entry that begins with the patient having been informed names nobody and can be relied upon by nobody.

Ask for the patient’s version in quotation marks, unaltered, even where it is plainly wrong, and for the gap to be stated in one neutral sentence of the form she did not say X.

Ask for a sentence recording that you were present and added nothing, and a final sentence naming who has been asked to return, by when, and whether the patient knows they are coming.

What it cannot do is sit in the chair for the ten minutes after the door closes, which is when the only reliable information in the entire entry becomes available.

- What words are used on your ward when somebody is dying?

The language at the end of life is plain or it is useless. Deteriorating, poorly, not doing very well: a family hearing these words knows that something is being withheld and spends its last hours decoding instead of sitting down.

Dying is the word, and it is said once and then not repeated every time somebody comes in. “I think your father is dying, and I think it will be today or tomorrow.” What follows that sentence is practical, and the practical part is the nurse’s whole contribution.

What can be offered is comfort, and comfort is specific: a mouth that is wet, a position that is easier, a hand that is held, a room that is quiet, a visitor at any hour of the day or night without asking anybody’s permission.

What must be warned about is what will frighten them: the sound of breathing that changes, the long gaps, the colour of the hands, the periods when he does not respond. A family warned in advance watches; a family not warned runs for a nurse and then remembers only the panic.

And faith is asked about rather than assumed, in a question with no expectation in it: “Is there anything that should be done, or anybody who should be here, that we have not thought of?” What may or must be done. By whom, is a matter for the family and for the patient’s own wishes wherever those are known.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the bedside at the end of life: plain words, no euphemism**

- Use dying, died and dead rather than their substitutes, and put the hedge where it belongs, on the timing rather than on the fact: “I think he is dying, and I think it will be today or tomorrow.”
- Use will for the changes you are confident about and may for those you are not: “His breathing will change. There may be long gaps.” The difference between will and may is the honest part of the warning.
- Make comfort concrete with a frequency: “I shall keep his mouth wet every half hour.” “I shall turn him when he needs it and not on a timetable.” Specific comfort is believed; general comfort is not.
- Ask about faith and ritual with an open question and a negative frame, which gives permission rather than inviting agreement: “Is there anything that should be done, or anybody who should be here, that we have not thought of?”

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who tells a family on your ward, and how?
- What words are used instead of died?

The sentence is short and it has three parts: the past tense, the person’s name, and the hour. “Mrs Delgado died at twenty past four.” Families repeat that sentence to other relatives for years afterwards, which is a reason to get it right rather than a reason to decorate it.

Passed, passed away, lost, gone, slipped away and no longer with us all do the same damage, which is to leave a small doubt in a conversation where no doubt should exist. A woman told that her mother has gone has to ask a second question to be sure.

The first question is nearly always whether he suffered, and it deserves an honest answer rather than an automatic one. What can be said truthfully is what was observed and what was done: that he was not distressed in the last hour, that his breathing was quiet, that somebody was with him.

What cannot be known must be said to be unknown. Whether he could hear, whether he knew she was there, whether he was waiting for her: these are questions with no answer, and the honest sentence is that nobody knows and that people who have sat where she is sitting often believe he did.

And over the telephone the same rules apply with one addition: the family must be invited in rather than told the news and left in a house. Where somebody has to be told by telephone, they are told plainly, asked who is with them, and given a name and a number to ring back.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: announcing a death: past tense, the name, the time**

- Use the simple past with the name and the hour, and nothing else in the sentence: “Mrs Delgado died at twenty past four.” Any additional clause dilutes the only sentence they will remember exactly.
- Answer the suffering question with the past continuous and the past simple about observable things: “She was not distressed. Her breathing was quiet. Daniel was with her.” Three observations are worth more than one reassurance.
- Mark the unknowable with whether and nobody knows: “Whether she could hear you, nobody knows.” Then, if it is true, add what families often believe, attributed to them rather than asserted as fact.
- On the telephone, follow the sentence with two questions and an offer: “Who is with you at home? Can somebody bring you in? My name is Beatrice Achieng and this is the number.”

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What gets left out first on your ward when you are short?
- Where is that written down?

Every short-staffed shift involves a series of decisions about what will not happen, and those decisions are usually made correctly and recorded nowhere. The mouth care, the second turn, the walk to the lavatory with the man who can nearly manage alone: these go first, and they go silently.

A staffing shortfall documented as what was not done and why is the only honest account of such a night. A shift recorded as though it had been fully staffed is worse than no record, because it tells the organisation that two nurses are sufficient for that ward.

What is written is narrow and factual: the establishment for the shift against the number actually on duty, the care that was postponed with the hour at which it was due, the care that was prioritised instead. The hour at which help was requested and what the answer was.

It is not a complaint and it must not read like one. “Mouth care postponed from eight until eleven for four patients; two nurses on duty against an establishment of four; bank requested at 19.40, none available” is a sentence nobody can dismiss and nobody can be annoyed with.

And one such entry is an anecdote while four weeks of them is a pattern. The nurse who writes it every time she is short is doing the only thing that has ever changed an establishment anywhere.

**Grammar: documenting what could not be done, and why**

- Use the passive with a named care and an hour for what did not happen: “Mouth care postponed from 08.00 until 11.00 for four patients.” The passive is right here because the actor is the shift rather than a person.

**A staffing shortfall documented as what was not done and why**

**Figure 9.5** — Six lines, and the sixth one is the only one that ever changes an establishment.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What would make you raise a concern about a colleague?
- What route exists on your ward, and who has used it?
- What is the difference between somebody unwell and somebody unsafe?

A concern about a colleague is the hardest thing in this book to raise, and it is harder still when the colleague is somebody you like, which is usually the case, because wards are small and most people are likeable.

The content is three things and no more. The behaviour, described as you would describe an observation: what was done or not done, on which dates. The effect, on a named patient or on a specific risk. And the frequency, which is the number of times you have seen it yourself.

What you were told by somebody else is kept separate and labelled as such, and it is not left out, because a pattern frequently exists only in the accounts of four different people. But it is never presented as your own observation.

A corridor conversation is not a route. The route is the person your policy names, and using it protects the colleague as much as the patient, because it produces an account that can be answered rather than a rumour that cannot.

And there is a distinction worth holding on to: a colleague who is unwell and a colleague who is unsafe are not the same, although one may produce the other. Both are raised; the first is raised with compassion and the second with urgency, and it is not for you to decide which it is.

**Grammar: raising a concern about a person: behaviour, effect, and to whom**

- Describe the behaviour with a date and a verb, never with an adjective about the person: “On the third, the seventh and the ninth of March she gave medicines without checking the band.” Not “she is careless about identity checks.”

**A concern about a colleague drafted as behaviour, effect and route**

A concern about a colleague drafted as behaviour, effect and route is the piece of writing that decides whether what you have is an account somebody can answer or a rumour that will follow her for years without ever being tested.

Give the system the dates you noted at the time, what you saw on each, what it affected, how many of those you witnessed yourself, what others have told you, and the route your employer’s policy names.

- First-hand observation and accounts given by others are set out separately and labelled, and no account is presented as first-hand that is not.
- The concern is raised through the route named in the employer’s policy and not by informal mention; the colleague is told that it has been raised, and the person raising it is told what support they are entitled to.
