## Unit 1 · Explaining a Diagnosis Without a Single Technical Word

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: paraphrase: replacing a technical term with a description · AI: a diagnosis explanation checked for every word a patient would not use*

**Figure 1.0** — HE NODDED AT EVERY WORD HE DID NOT KNOW: A patient nods to be polite. A patient who can say it back has understood.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Which technical words do you use with patients without noticing?
- What does your patient call his illness?
- How do you check that an explanation has landed?

An explanation that contains one word a patient does not know stops being an explanation at that word. Everything after it is heard through the gap.

The remedy is paraphrase: replace the term with a description of what it does. Not “your kidneys are not filtering”, but “your kidneys are not cleaning your blood as well as they should”.

Three sentences are usually enough: what is happening in the body, what it means for him, and what happens next. More than three and the first is forgotten.

Use the word the patient uses. If he calls it his sugar, call it his sugar, and write in the notes that this is his term.

Then check in the only way that works: ask him what he will tell his daughter when he gets home.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: paraphrase: replacing a technical term with a description**

- Paraphrase describes the function rather than naming the thing: not “renal impairment”, but “your kidneys are not cleaning your blood as well as they should”.
- Build the paraphrase round a verb the patient knows: cleaning, pumping, carrying, swelling, blocking, healing. A verb is easier to picture than a noun.
- Give the term afterwards, if at all, and label it as a word for other people: there is a word for this that doctors use, and you will see it in letters.
- Use the patient’s own word in the conversation and the clinical term in the record, with his word in quotation marks beside it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An explanation with no word he does not know**

**Figure 1.5** — Six terms and six descriptions, none of them longer than the term.

A diagnosis explanation checked for every word a patient would not use is a short editing task with one rule: no word survives that he would not say himself.

Give the system the diagnosis, the patient’s age, his preferred language, and the words he has used for his own illness. Ask for an explanation in three sentences.

Ask it to list every term it has removed, with the description that replaced it, so that you can check the description says the same thing.

Ask it to build each description round a verb rather than a noun. Cleaning, pumping, swelling and blocking can be pictured; impairment and insufficiency cannot.

Ask it to propose one analogy and then to list what that analogy also implies. A heart like a battery implies recharging, and the patient will take that part too.

What no system can do is watch the patient’s face at the second sentence. The nod that means carry on and the nod that means I am lost look different, and only one person in the room can tell them apart.

> **DATA POINT · Plain explanation** — Communication guidance commonly recommends replacing clinical terms with functional descriptions, limiting an explanation to three points, and verifying understanding by asking the patient to restate it.  
> Source: general clinical communication guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Analogies** — Communication guidance commonly warns that analogies carry unintended implications, and recommends testing an analogy by asking the patient what he takes from it.  
> Source: general clinical communication guidance, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · Risk in Numbers a Patient Can Use

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: proportion and risk: one in, out of every, about · AI: a risk restated as a number a patient can picture*

**Figure 2.0** — “IT DOUBLES THE RISK” — FROM WHAT TO WHAT?: A relative figure with no baseline is not information.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you say a risk to a patient?
- Do you give the baseline first?
- When did a patient last ask you for a number?

Patients ask for numbers and are usually given adjectives. Small, rare, significant and unlikely mean different things to the person saying them and the person hearing them.

The useful form is a natural frequency with a fixed denominator: about three people in every hundred; one person in twenty; two in a thousand.

Keep the denominator the same throughout a conversation. Moving from one in twenty to five per cent to ninety-five per cent safe in three sentences is three different mental pictures.

A relative change needs an absolute one beside it. “It doubles the risk” means one thing if the baseline is one in a thousand and another if it is one in four.

When the words are not working, draw it. A hundred squares on a sheet of paper, with three shaded, is understood by almost everybody.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: proportion and risk: one in, out of every, about**

- The natural-frequency forms are: one in twenty; three in every hundred; two out of a thousand. They take a plural verb when the people are the subject: three people in a hundred have this.
- About marks an honest approximation and should be used: about three in a hundred. Exactly is almost never true of a risk figure.
- Give the baseline first and the change second, in the same denominator: about three people in a hundred have this already; after the operation it would be about four in a hundred.
- Avoid a bare relative change. “It doubles the risk” and “fifty per cent more likely” are not statements a patient can use without the baseline.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A figure a patient can hold in his head**

**Figure 2.5** — Six forms of one number, and only three of them survive a conversation.

A risk restated as a number a patient can picture is an arithmetic task with a human constraint: the form must survive four minutes of a frightening conversation.

Give the system the figure you have, its source, and the baseline it departs from. Ask for a natural frequency with one denominator for the whole conversation.

Ask it to give the figure both ways: three people in a hundred have this, and ninety-seven do not. Patients hear the two differently and they are entitled to both.

Ask it to refuse to produce a relative change without an absolute one. Doubling is the single most misleading word in risk conversations and it is the easiest to say.

Ask it to state the source and the uncertainty of the figure: from our own unit’s records, about two hundred operations in four years. A figure with no source invites the question you cannot answer.

What no system can decide is which risk this patient is actually frightened of. He may be asking about the operation and thinking about his job, and only the person in the room can hear that.

> **DATA POINT · Framing both ways** — Risk-communication guidance commonly recommends presenting a risk both as the proportion affected and as the proportion unaffected, with the source and uncertainty of the figure stated.

## Unit 3 · Consent Is the Conversation, Not the Signature

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: checking understanding: what will you do when you get home · AI: an explanation tested against the questions the patient asks back*

**Figure 3.0** — THE FORM WAS SIGNED. THE CONVERSATION WAS NOT HELD.: The form records a conversation. Without the conversation it records nothing.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who takes consent in your hospital, and when?
- What do you do when a patient asks no questions at all?
- Where is the conversation recorded, as opposed to the signature?

A consent form is a receipt. The thing it is a receipt for is a conversation in which a patient understood what is proposed, what else is possible, and what happens if nothing is done.

The commonest failure is not dishonesty. It is a conversation in which the clinician speaks for four minutes, asks “any questions?”, hears none, and takes a signature.

A patient who asks nothing has usually understood nothing, or has decided that asking is rude. So the check is a request, not a question: tell me what you will say to your wife tonight.

The alternatives must include doing nothing, with what is likely to happen then. A choice between one option and no option is not a choice.

What goes in the record is the conversation: what was explained, what he asked, what he was given to read, how long he had, and what he decided.

**Grammar: checking understanding: what will you do when you get home**

- The check is a request for a future action: tell me what you will do when you get home; tell me what you will say to your wife; show me where you will put the dressing.

**A consultation prepared as short single-idea turns**

**Figure 4.5** — Six lines that turn twenty minutes of interpreting into twelve of them.

An interpreted consultation prepared as short single-idea turns is a script you may depart from, not a script you must read.

Give the system what the consultation is for, what the patient already knows, and which three things he must leave with. Ask for the turns in order, one idea each.

Ask it to mark every term that has no simple equivalent and offer a plain version. A word you cannot say simply in English will not survive the journey.

Ask for the check as a turn of its own: tell me what you will say at home tonight. It is the only part of the consultation that tells you whether the rest arrived.

Ask for the briefing too: sixty seconds for the interpreter on the purpose, the hard words and how you will work. Most of the failures happen before anybody speaks.

What no system can prepare is the nod. Whether that nod meant yes or meant please stop talking is read from a face, by the person in the room.

## Unit 5 · A Patient Who Does Not Want to Be Told

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: permission and declining: you may decline, you do not have to · AI: a patient’s stated wish recorded in his own words*

**Figure 5.0** — HE ASKED YOU NOT TO TELL HIM. THAT IS ALSO A DECISION.: You may stop explaining. You may not stop offering.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Has a patient ever asked you not to tell him?
- What did you write in the notes?
- Who decides what his wife is told?

Some patients do not want the detail. A man may say: tell my daughter, do what you have to do, I would rather not hear the numbers. That is a decision, and it is his to make.

What he may decline is information. What he cannot hand over is the decision itself, unless he names somebody to take it and the law where you work allows that.

So the offer is made and left open: you do not have to hear any of this today, and you may ask me at any point. I shall ask you again before anything is decided.

There is a floor. A patient who is agreeing to a procedure must know what is being done to his body, even when he does not want to know what it is for.

Record the wish in his own words. “Patient does not want to know” is a clinician’s summary. “I would rather you told my daughter the numbers” is evidence.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: permission and declining: you may decline, you do not have to**

- Offer with may and do not have to: you may hear as much or as little as you like; you do not have to decide today; you may ask me at any point.
- These are not polite softeners. They are statements about what is allowed, and a patient who does not know he is allowed to decline will sit through an explanation he did not want.
- Say what is still required, in the same voice: there is one part I must tell you, because you are being asked to agree to it.
- Then ask, rather than assume: who would you like me to talk to about the detail? How much would you like me to tell her?

## Unit 6 · A Family That Wants to Decide for Him

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who speaks for the patient in your consultations?
- What do you say when a son answers a question you asked his father?
- What have you promised a family that you could not deliver?

A family that answers for a competent patient is usually trying to protect him. The answer is not to argue with them but to put the question to him again, in the same words, and wait.

Sometimes the room has to be cleared for two minutes. “I should like to ask your father something on his own, and then we shall all talk together again.”

What you can give is a plan in conditions. If the scan is clear, then we go home on Friday. If it is not, then we stay and this is what we do next.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: one action per sentence in written instructions**

- Start each line with the verb. Keep the dressing dry. Take the tablet with breakfast. Ring the ward. The patient’s eye finds the verb first.
- One action, then stop. Not: keep the dressing dry for two days and then take it off and shower as normal, unless it is still bleeding.
- Put the condition in its own sentence after the action, not inside it. Shower normally from Wednesday. Do not shower if the wound is still open.
- Use the plain imperative, not the passive and not the future. The dressing should be kept dry tells a patient what somebody else ought to do.

A complaint is a set of specific points wrapped in anger. The anger is real and the points are answerable, and the letter that answers them must keep the two apart.

Number the points as the complainant made them, in their order, and answer each one. Reordering the points to suit your account is the commonest way a reply fails.

Then say what will change, and what will not. A complaint answered with no change is a complaint that will come back, and promising a change you cannot deliver is worse.
