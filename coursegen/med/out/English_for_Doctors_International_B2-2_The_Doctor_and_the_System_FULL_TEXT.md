### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How long is your usual case presentation?
- What is the first sentence of it?
- What do you ask for at the end?

A case presented in a meeting is not a story but a request for a decision, and the structure that gets the decision is lead, evidence, question, in that order and in about sixty seconds.

The lead is one sentence containing the person, the problem and the stage: a man of sixty-two with a recurrence in the liver, eleven months after resection, now fit and asking about surgery.

The evidence is three facts chosen because they bear on the decision, with their dates; not everything true about the patient, and not the chronology of his admission.

The question is the last sentence and it asks for something: would you operate, would you refer him for the trial, or would you treat him medically and reassess in three months?

Everything else — the admission in March, the two scans that were normal, the social history — is held in reserve and produced when somebody asks for it, which is a different skill.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the one-minute presentation: lead, evidence, question**

- Build the lead as a noun phrase with three qualifiers: a man of sixty-two with a liver recurrence eleven months after resection, now fit and asking about surgery.
- Give each piece of evidence as a short sentence with its date and its source: the scan of the ninth shows two lesions; the histology of the eleventh confirms the same type.
- Make the question an alternative: would you operate, refer him for the trial, or treat medically and reassess? Three branches is the most a room can hold.
- Say explicitly that you are holding detail: I have the admission history and the social circumstances if the meeting wants them. That sentence buys you the floor back.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A case compressed to one minute with the detail held in reserve**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and the fourth is the one that makes the first three safe to shorten.

A case compressed to one minute with the detail held in reserve is a genuine act of judgement dressed up as an act of summary, and that is why doing it badly is so easy.

Give the system the whole case, the decision you want and who is in the room, and ask for the lead in one sentence, three evidence sentences and a question in branches.

Ask for the reserve as a separate ordered list, so that when somebody asks about the 2023 investigations you can answer in one sentence rather than opening a file.

Ask it to name the anchor fact: the single figure or finding that the room will carry out of the meeting whatever else is said.

Ask it to count the words, because sixty seconds of speech is about a hundred and fifty of them and almost every draft is twice that.

What no system can judge is what this particular room will ask first; that is read from the people, and it is the reason the reserve is ordered by who is sitting there.

> **DATA POINT · Case presentation** — Structured brief presentations, in which the clinical question is stated at the start and at the end and supporting detail is held for questions, are widely taught in postgraduate medical education and are associated with shorter meetings and more decisions reached. Formats differ between specialities.  
> Source: general medical education literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why cases are deferred** — Audits of multidisciplinary meetings commonly report that a substantial proportion of cases are deferred for want of a stated question or of a specific piece of information, and that stating the question at the outset reduces deferral. Rates vary with the meeting and the speciality.  
> Source: general literature on multidisciplinary working, 2025–2026.

**STAGE 5 · The Decision**

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say what the first sentence contains.
- Say how much evidence follows.
- Say what the last sentence does.
- Say what happens to the rest of the case.

*Language bank for the decision*
- The first sentence of a presentation states the patient, the problem and the stage, and contains no history earlier than the current episode.
- No more than three pieces of evidence follow, each with its date and its source, chosen because they bear on the decision being asked for.
- The last sentence asks a question in two or three branches, naming the decision the meeting is being asked to make.
- All remaining detail is held in reserve, listed in the order the room is likely to ask for it, and produced in one sentence when requested.

## Unit 2 · The Written Case Report

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: impersonal chronological narrative in a case report · AI: a case report drafted chronologically from the record*

**Figure 2.0** — A CASE REPORT WITHOUT A LEARNING POINT IS A DIARY.: The question a reviewer asks first is: what does a reader do on Monday that he would not have done?

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Have you written a case report?
- What was the learning point, in one sentence?
- Who consented to its publication?

A case report is justified by a learning point, not by rarity, and the reviewer’s question is what a reader should do differently, and “this was an interesting case” is not an answer to it.

The narrative is chronological and impersonal: the patient presented with, examination revealed, a scan performed on the fourth day showed; the clinicians disappear into the passive on purpose.

Anonymisation means more than removing a name — a rare condition, a date, a city and an occupation together identify a person as surely as a hospital number does.

Consent for publication is obtained from the patient, in writing, for the version that will be published, and the journal will ask you to confirm it. Obtain it before you write, not after.

## Unit 3 · A Research Consent Document in Plain Language

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: plain language and voluntariness in a consent document · AI: a research consent document rewritten in plain language*

**Figure 3.0** — HE AGREED TO THE TRIAL BECAUSE HE THOUGHT IT WAS HIS TREATMENT.: The commonest misunderstanding in clinical research is not about risk. It is about purpose.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How long is the consent document you use most often?
- Where in it does the word research first appear?
- What would a participant say the trial is for?

A research consent document has one job that is not shared with any clinical document: it must make clear that this is research, which may not benefit the person reading it.

The therapeutic misconception is the belief that a trial is individualised treatment; it is extremely common, it survives long documents, and it is defeated only by a plain sentence.

Voluntariness has to be unconditional and repeated: you do not have to take part; you may stop at any time; your care will not change if you say no.

Randomisation and placebo must be explained in ordinary words, and a computer will choose which of the two treatments you have, and neither you nor I will choose it.

Give the burden in hours and visits rather than in adjectives; four extra visits, about two hours each, over six months, and three blood tests more than usual.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: plain language and voluntariness in a consent document**

- Address the reader as you and use short active sentences: you do not have to take part; you may stop at any time; we shall ask you for three blood tests.
- State voluntariness with may and do not have to, and never with a condition attached. Not “you may withdraw, although this may affect the study”, which is a discouragement.

- Quantify everything a participant will feel: visits, hours, needles, nights. Adjectives such as minimal, brief and well tolerated are not information.

**A research consent document rewritten in plain language**

**Figure 3.5** — Six lines, and the sixth is what lets the first five be short.

A research consent document rewritten in plain language is the one rewriting job where you may not remove a single piece of content, which is what makes it difficult.

Give the system the full document, the committee’s required content list and the reading level you are aiming at — ask for a two-page plain summary with the full text behind it.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

A transfer summary that crosses a health system is read by somebody who has your patient and none of your context, and every assumption you leave in it becomes a telephone call or a delay.

A report for an employer, an insurer or a tribunal is answerable to two limits: the instruction you were given and the consent the patient has given.

Begin by setting out the instruction verbatim and the consent with its date. Everything that follows is measured against those two paragraphs by whoever reads it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A third-party report limited to what was asked and permitted**

**Figure 5.5** — Six lines, and the fifth and sixth are the ones that make the rest safe.

A third-party report limited to what was asked and permitted is an exercise in subtraction, and subtraction is the one editorial task on which a system is reliably better than its author.

Give it the instruction, the consent with its scope, the record and your own conclusions; ask for the questions numbered as the instruction numbered them.

Ask it to flag every sentence in your draft that answers something nobody asked, and every sentence that goes beyond the consent, as two separate lists.

Ask for each opinion to be marked as opinion and qualified by the speciality from which it comes, and for anything outside that speciality to be listed as a question to decline.

Ask for absences to be written as absences, because a report that is silent about a gap invites the reader to fill it, usually unfavourably.

What no system can do is show the report to the patient and listen to what he says about it — that conversation changes a fact about once in five reports, which is a high rate for anything.

> **DATA POINT · Third-party reports** — Professional guidance commonly requires that a clinician writing for a third party answer only the questions asked, write within their competence, distinguish fact from opinion, obtain and record the patient’s consent, and offer the patient sight of the report where permitted. Requirements vary by jurisdiction and by the body requesting it.  
> Source: general professional guidance on reports and disclosure, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Reports and consent** — Guidance on reports for third parties commonly requires that the patient’s consent be specific to the questions asked, that the patient be offered sight of the report before it is sent where the instruction allows, and that opinions outside the writer’s competence be declined. Practice varies by country and by body.  
> Source: general professional guidance on reports, 2025–2026.

## Unit 6 · Turning a Guideline Into a Local Instruction

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

A guideline says what should happen; a local instruction says who does it here, with what, by when, and what happens when the thing it assumes is unavailable.

Every recommendation must become a verb with an owner: the nurse in charge requests the scan; the resident prescribes within thirty minutes; the pharmacist checks the dose before it is given.

Give the instruction a start date and a review date, and measure whether it is followed; an unmeasured instruction becomes folklore within a year, and nobody can say when it stopped being true.

**An audit written up as method, finding and change**

**Figure 8.5** — Six lines, and the fourth one is the only part that changes a hospital.

An audit written up as method, finding and change is a document whose whole purpose is to be acted on once and then repeated, which is an unusual purpose for a piece of writing.

Give the system the standard with its source and adoption date, the sample with its exclusions, and the counts; ask for every finding as a fraction of its denominator.

Ask it to flag any finding stated as a percentage without its denominator, and any recommendation addressed to a department rather than a person.

> Source: general clinical-audit guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Closing the loop** — Surveys of clinical audit commonly report that a minority of audits proceed to re-measurement, and that audits with a named individual responsible for the change and a scheduled re-audit date are substantially more likely to complete the cycle. > Source: general clinical-audit literature, 2025–2026.

- Which of the documents in this book would you let a system write alone?

Everything in these six books can be drafted by a machine and almost nothing in them can be delegated, because a draft is a summary and what a clinician adds is a judgement.

A summary rearranges what is already recorded; a judgement commits somebody: this patient can go home; this consent is real; this is unsafe and I am asking you to stop.

The judgement is the part with a name on it, and the name is not decoration, and it is the mechanism by which a hospital can be asked why, and answered.

So the division of labour is simple and unchanging: the system prepares, the clinician decides, and the record says which is which and who did it.

The hardest part is unobserved; nobody checks whether you asked the question at the second visit, wrote the incident account on the shift, or showed the report to the patient first.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**What no system can do for a clinician’s written word**

**Figure 10.5** — Six pairs, and the right-hand column has not changed in two hundred years.

What no system can do for a clinician’s written word is the last of the sixty capabilities in these books, and it is the one that explains the other fifty-nine.

A system assembles, drafts, measures, lists and flags — each of those is real work and most of it was previously done badly by tired people at the ends of shifts.

What remains is seeing the patient, deciding the plan, reading the face, owning the uncertainty and signing the sentence, and none of it is nostalgia; each is a thing a machine cannot be asked about afterwards.

So the instruction to a system is always the same shape: prepare this, flag that, and leave the judgement to me in a separate sentence with my name on it.

And the instruction to yourself is simpler and harder: do the unobserved parts anyway; the question at the second visit, the account before handover, the report shown to the patient first.

Six books of sentences come to this; the language is how the judgement travels; the judgement is yours, and the signature at the end of the document is not a formality but the whole of it.
