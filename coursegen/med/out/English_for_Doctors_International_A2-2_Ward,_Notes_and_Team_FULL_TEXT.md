A ward round moves. Eighteen patients in ninety minutes is five minutes each, and the five minutes include the talking, the examining and the deciding.

Somebody must write. If the person who writes is also the person who examines, something is lost at every bed.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: present perfect and past simple side by side in a note**

- Use the past simple for a finished event with a time: he had a fever on Tuesday. The nurse saw him at six. We started the test yesterday.
- Use the present perfect for a change that still stands: the fever has gone. He has not eaten since Monday. We have started the second test.
- The two sit side by side in a good note: he had a fever on Tuesday; it has now gone. That sentence tells the night team both things.
- Since and yet belong to the present perfect: he has not walked since Friday. He has not passed urine yet. Yesterday and on Tuesday belong to the past simple.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Eighteen beds, eighteen notes, one list**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines per bed, and the fifth is the one the night team acts on.

A round list turned into a note for every patient seen is the most ordinary task on a ward and the one that fails most often. The round moves faster than anybody can write.

Give the system the round list, the observations for each patient and what was said at each bed. Ask for one note per patient, in the same six lines.

Ask it to separate what happened from what has changed, because those are different tenses and different information. The night team needs both.

Ask for every decision to carry the name of the person who made it and the words that person used. A decision attributed to nobody is not a decision.

Ask for every job to carry a person and a time, and for a role to be replaced by a name wherever the rota is known. “The registrar” is nobody at four in the morning.

What no system can do is ask the consultant to repeat himself. Twelve seconds at the bedside is a human act, and the note that follows is only as good as the sentence you asked for.

> **DATA POINT · Writing on the round** — Ward-round practice commonly recommends that an entry and a jobs list be completed at the bedside, with each action attributed to a named person and a time.  
> Source: general ward-round and record-keeping guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Attributed actions** — Record-keeping guidance commonly recommends that each action arising from a round be recorded with a named individual and a time, rather than a role or a team.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who reads your notes when you are asleep?
- What do they need in the first line?
- When should somebody telephone you about your patient?

A note is written for one reader. A doctor who has never met the patient, at three in the morning, with eleven other patients and no time.

That reader needs four things. Who this patient is in one line. What is wrong. What to do tonight. When to telephone somebody.

Amara ends every note with two lines. For tonight: encourage him to walk to the bathroom. Telephone the registrar if his temperature rises above the figure written on the chart.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: articles and reference in a record: a rash, the rash**

- Use a or an when you mention something for the first time: there is a rash on his left arm. We have taken a blood sample. He has a cough.
- Use the when the reader already knows which one you mean: the rash has not spread. The sample was sent at seven twenty. The cough began on Monday.
- This matters in a record because the is a claim that the reader knows. If the reader has never met the patient, the rash with no earlier a is a sentence he cannot place.
- Some words take no article when they are general: he has chest pain; there is swelling of the right ankle. Add the when you point at one thing: the pain is worse at night.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: requests and offers to colleagues: could you, would you mind, shall I**

- Could you takes a plain verb: could you repeat the observations? Could you telephone the porter? It is the ordinary form for a colleague.
- Would you mind takes an -ing form: would you mind repeating the temperature? Would you mind telephoning him? Mind means object, so the answer to “would you mind?” is “no”.

- Add the time inside the request, not after it: could you do it before eleven? A time added afterwards is heard as an apology and is often lost.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A request with the question in the first line**

**Figure 6.5** — Six elements, and the fifth is the one that protects the night staff.

A request to a colleague, with the question in the first line, is a small piece of writing. It decides whether a thing happens at all.

Give the system the tasks you want done, the people on the ward and the times they are available. Ask for one line per task, each with a person, a time and a priority.

Ask it to turn every watching instruction into three things: what to measure, how often, and the trigger for telephoning. “Keep an eye on him” must not survive.

Ask for the list to be ordered, so that a colleague who can do only half of it does the right half. An unordered list is a list you have not thought about.

Ask for every task you could do yourself to be marked, so that you can offer rather than ask. A doctor who does three of his own jobs is asked for favours differently.

What no system can know is how many patients are in that bay this morning. The refusal you get is information about the ward, and the right answer to it is usually to do the task yourself.

**A handover in order of urgency**

**Figure 8.5** — Four groups, one question, and no alphabet anywhere.

A shift handover drafted from the day’s notes and ranked by urgency is a sorting task, and the sorting is the whole value.

Give the system the ward list, the day’s notes, the outstanding results and the jobs with times. Ask for four groups, not a list.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: reporting a patient: he reports, he describes, he denies**

- Reports introduces what the patient says happened: he reports pain since Monday. He reports two episodes last week.

**Grammar: saying you do not know: we are waiting for, it is not yet clear**

- Say the doubt plainly, in the present: we do not know yet; it is not yet clear; we cannot say today.

**A note that lets another doctor see your doubt**
