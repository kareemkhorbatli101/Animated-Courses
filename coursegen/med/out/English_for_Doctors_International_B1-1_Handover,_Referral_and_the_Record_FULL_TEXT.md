## Unit 1 · A Handover in Four Parts, Said in Thirty Seconds

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: the four-part handover: situation, background, assessment, request · AI: a handover sorted into situation, background, assessment and request*

**Figure 1.0** — THE REQUEST CAME LAST, AFTER NINETY SECONDS: Four parts, in order, and the fourth is the one people leave out.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- In what order do you hand a patient over?
- Where does your request come?
- How do you know the listener has understood?

Clinical handover in English has settled into four parts, and the order of them is the whole point: the situation now, the background that matters, your assessment, and what you are asking for.

The situation is one sentence with a name, an age, a place and the problem now. “This is Mr Costa, sixty-eight, in bed nine, whose pulse has risen all afternoon.”

The background is the part that tempts everybody to go on too long. It is the history that changes what the listener does, and nothing else.

The assessment is what you think, with the word you mean: I think this is a chest problem; I am not sure; I am worried and I cannot tell you why.

The request is the sentence that is most often missing, and it is the only sentence the listener needs in order to act: I need you to see him within the hour.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the four-part handover: situation, background, assessment, request**

- Begin with a single sentence that identifies the patient and the problem now: this is Mr Costa, sixty-eight, in bed nine, whose pulse has risen at every reading since six o’clock.
- Give the background in the past simple and the present perfect: he was admitted on Monday with a cough; he has not eaten since Tuesday. Choose only the facts that change the plan.
- Give the assessment in the first person, with your real degree of confidence: I think this is a chest problem. I am not certain. I am worried and I cannot tell you why.
- Make the request an action with a time: I need you to see him within the hour. Then check: can you tell me what you understand the plan to be?

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Four parts, and the request is the point**

**Figure 1.5** — Six elements, about fifty words, and nothing said twice.

A handover sorted into situation, background, assessment and request is a sorting problem before it is a speaking problem, and the sorting can be done before you pick up a telephone.

Give the system the notes, the observations and the question you want answered. Ask for the four parts, each labelled, with the background limited to what changes the plan.

Ask for the situation to be a single sentence containing a name, an age, a place and the problem now. Everything the listener does afterwards is read inside that sentence.

Ask for the assessment in the first person, with the confidence you actually have. “I am worried and I cannot tell you why” is a legitimate assessment and a common one.

Ask for the request to name an action and a time, and for a closing question that asks the listener to say the plan back. A handover with no read-back is a hope.

What no system can decide is what you are asking for. The request comes from your judgement about this patient tonight, and a handover without it is a story rather than a referral.

> **DATA POINT · Structured handover** — Handover guidance commonly recommends a structured four-part format — situation, background, assessment and request — on the grounds that an unstructured account places the request last, when the listener has already formed a plan.  
> Source: general clinical handover guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · The request** — Handover guidance commonly identifies the absence of an explicit request as the most frequent structural fault in clinical handovers, and recommends a closing read-back by the receiving clinician.  
> Source: general clinical handover guidance, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · A Referral Letter With the Question at the Top

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: nominal style and economy in a referral letter · AI: a referral letter assembled with its question at the top*

**Figure 2.0** — THE QUESTION WAS ON PAGE TWO: A referral is triaged by somebody reading the first six lines.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who reads your referral letters, and for how long?
- Where is the question in your last one?
- What do you enclose, and what do you describe?

A referral letter is read twice and never in full. A clerk reads it for a category, and a clinician reads the first six lines to decide how soon the patient is seen.

So the question goes first, before the history, before the examination and before anything courteous. It reads: I should be grateful for your opinion on whether this cough needs investigation beyond what we have done.

The style is nominal and economical: investigation of a cough of three months; a history of surgery abroad in 2016; no response to two courses of treatment.

Results are enclosed, not narrated. A sentence describing a result costs forty words and persuades nobody; a copy of the result costs one line and settles the point.

Dr Ana Ferreira, who receives these letters in primary care, reads them for three things: what is being asked, what has been done, and what she must do next.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: nominal style and economy in a referral letter**

- Nominal style puts the content in nouns: investigation of a cough of three months; assessment of a swelling in the right knee; review of treatment begun in October.
- It is shorter than the verbal equivalent and it reads as a professional register. “We would like you to investigate why he has had a cough for three months” becomes “investigation of a cough of three months’ duration”.
- Economy is a discipline, not a style. Every sentence either changes what the reader will do or comes out of the letter.
- Keep the courtesies to one line and put them at the end. “I should be grateful for your opinion” belongs at the top because it carries the question; “with many thanks for seeing him” belongs below your name.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The letter the clinic will actually read**

**Figure 2.5** — Six paragraphs, half a page, and the first line does the work.

A referral letter assembled with its question at the top is an exercise in putting the conclusion first, which is the opposite of how clinical reasoning is taught.

Give the system your notes, the investigations already done with their dates, and the clinical question you want answered. Ask for six short paragraphs in a fixed order.

Ask for the first line to carry the question in nominal style, with the duration and the failure of previous treatment in it. That line is what decides the urgency category.

An imaging request has two authors. The clinician supplies the question, and the radiologist chooses the examination that answers it.

Requests go wrong when the clinician does the second job as well. A form that names an examination but no question leaves the radiologist to guess what is feared.

The safety questions are always the same, and answering them first saves a telephone call: previous imaging, implants, contrast history and, where it applies, pregnancy.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: purpose and exclusion: in order to, to exclude, to confirm**

- A purpose clause begins with to or in order to and names the thing you want settled: to exclude a collection; in order to confirm the finding of 3 March.
- To exclude is used where a negative answer changes what you do. If a negative answer would change nothing, the word is wrong and the request may be unnecessary.
- To confirm is used where a positive answer changes what you do. To assess is used where the thing is known and its extent is the question.
- Add a consequence clause when it helps the reader: to exclude a collection, since a negative result would allow discharge today. That sentence tells a radiologist how soon to report.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: evidential verbs: shows, suggests, is consistent with**

- Shows and demonstrates assert a finding: the film shows a fracture of the left sixth rib. There is no hedge in that sentence and none is intended.
- Suggests, may represent and could be due to are hedges with different weights: suggests is stronger than could be due to, and both are weaker than shows.
- Is consistent with is the most misread phrase in medicine. It means the appearance would fit that explanation; it does not mean the explanation is established.
- Cannot be excluded is a negative hedge and belongs with the others: a small collection cannot be excluded on this examination. It tells you the limit of the answer.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: tense discipline in a summary: was, has been, is now**

- Use the past simple for the admission and for finished events with dates: he was admitted on 11 November; the film was taken on 14 November; one medicine was stopped on the fifteenth.
- Use the present perfect for what has changed and still stands: his temperature has been normal since Thursday; he has had no further pain since Monday.
- Use the present simple for the state at discharge and the plan: he is now walking unaided; he is taking two medicines; the clinic appointment is on 4 December.
- Keep the three apart in separate sentences. A summary that mixes them in one paragraph makes the reader work out which facts are still true.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you say that you are not sure, in a letter?
- When should the family doctor send the patient back to you?

A letter to the doctor who continues the care has to do something no other clinical document does: it must hand over a degree of uncertainty without pretending it is a fact.

The words are available and they have different weights. Probably is stronger than possibly. Likely is a judgement. Cannot be excluded is a limit, not an opinion.

A hedge is only useful with a reason attached: probably a chest infection, because the fever settled within two days of treatment; the alternative cannot be excluded without the result of 18 November.

The letter must also give a threshold: send him back to me if the cough has not gone by the end of January, or sooner if he loses weight.

Many of these letters are copied to the patient, which is not a reason to write less clearly. It is a reason to write two paragraphs: one for the clinician and one that the patient can read.

**Grammar: hedging with precision: probably, likely, cannot be excluded**

- Rank your hedges and use them deliberately: certainly, probably, likely, possibly, cannot be excluded. Each is weaker than the one before it, and “query” is not on the list at all.

After an unexpected event somebody has to write a chronology, usually within hours and usually the person who was there.

It is a list of times and facts: at 02.10 the pulse was recorded as 128; at 02.25 I was telephoned; by 02.40 the registrar had arrived.

## Unit 9 · Correcting a Record Without Altering It

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: addendum register: recorded in retrospect, entered at · AI: an addendum drafted that leaves the original entry intact*

**Figure 9.0** — THE ORIGINAL STAYS. THE CORRECTION IS ADDED.: A record is corrected by adding to it, never by changing what it said.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How are corrections made in your records?
- Who is told when a correction matters?
- What happens to the original entry?

Records are corrected often and for good reasons. A time was wrong, a side was wrong, an entry went into the wrong patient’s file.

A correction is an addition. The original entry stays legible, and a new entry says what is wrong with it, what the correct position is, who is writing and when.

An alteration is something else: changing the original so that the earlier version cannot be read. In a clinical record that is never acceptable, however small the change.

The register is fixed. Recorded in retrospect; entered at 09.40 on 20 November; this entry corrects the entry of 19 November at 16.40, which stated the left knee.

The part most often forgotten is the reader. If somebody has already acted on the wrong entry, the correction must reach that person and not only the file.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Adding to a record, never changing it**

**Figure 9.5** — Six parts, and the sixth is the one that reaches a person rather than a file.

An addendum drafted that leaves the original entry intact is a small discipline with a large consequence: it is the difference between a record that can be audited and one that cannot.

Give the system the original entry, the correct position, the evidence for it, and the list of people and departments that may have acted on the error. Ask for the six parts above.

Ask it to quote the original entry rather than describing it, and to state explicitly that the original has not been altered.

Ask it to produce a notification list before the addendum itself: who must be telephoned, in what order, and by when. The file can wait four minutes; a patient already in a department cannot.

Ask for a line distinguishing a typographical correction from a clinically significant one, because the second needs a telephone call and the first does not.

What no system can do is make the telephone calls. Correcting the record protects the next reader; telephoning protects the patient who is being treated on the strength of the error now.
