### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: present simple for habit, routine and symptom**

- Use the present simple for what a patient does every day: he works at night, he smokes, she lives alone, they walk to the shop.
- Use it also for a symptom he has now and often: it hurts here, my knee hurts in the morning, I get headaches.
- The third person takes an -s: he works, she lives, it hurts, she gets. The question and the negative use do or does: does it hurt? he does not smoke.
- Ask about routine in the present simple: what do you take? who do you live with? how far do you walk? These answers go in the record as the patient says them.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Three sources, and none of them is enough alone**

**Figure 1.5** — Six sources and six ways to be wrong, which is why you use three.

A patient’s identity checked against three independent sources is the first task of every shift. It is also the task most often skipped, because it feels like paperwork.

Give the system the admission record, the wristband details and what the patient has just said. Ask it to report where the three do not agree.

Ask it to compare the name letter by letter, including the order of the parts. A name written Minh Nguyen in one file and Nguyen Van Minh in another may be one patient with two files.

Ask it to look for a second file with the same date of birth. A duplicate record is dangerous because half of a patient’s history sits in a file nobody opens.

Ask it to show the preferred language and the next of kin on the same line as the name. You need both in the first minute, not in the tenth.

What no system can do is ask the patient. He is the only source who knows which name is his family name. The only one who can tell you that the band on his arm is not his.

> **DATA POINT · Three-point check** — Hospital practice commonly asks staff to confirm a patient’s identity from more than one source — the patient’s own words, a wristband and the record — before any procedure.  
> Source: general patient-safety guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Duplicate records** — Health-record practice commonly warns that a patient may hold more than one record where names are transliterated or reordered, and recommends a search on date of birth as well as name.  
> Source: general health-records guidance, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · The First Four Questions of Any History

*CEFR A2 · Grammar: open and closed questions in a history · AI: a set of history questions prepared in words a patient will recognise*

**Figure 2.0** — ONE WIDE QUESTION, THEN FOUR NARROW ONES: The first question decides how much of the story you will hear.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is your first question to a new patient?
- Do your patients answer with one word or with a story?
- When do you write down the words a patient uses?

A consultation starts with one wide question, and the wide question is always the same: what has brought you here today? Then the doctor is quiet and lets the patient talk.

Most patients talk for less than a minute. In that minute they say the thing that worries them, and that thing is often not the first thing they mentioned.

After the open question come the closed ones. Where is the pain? When did it start? Does it move anywhere? Does anything make it better? These questions have short answers.

A leading question is a question with the answer inside it. “It is worse at night, isn’t it?” A tired patient agrees, and the record is then wrong in a way nobody can see.

Amara writes the patient’s own words in the notes. The patient says “a weight on my chest”, so the note says a weight on my chest. She does not translate it into a medical word.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: open and closed questions in a history**

- An open question begins with what, how or why, and it cannot be answered with yes: what has brought you here today? how does it feel? what do you think is happening?
- A closed question begins with do, does, did, is, are, have or has, and it has a short answer: does it hurt now? have you had this before? is it worse when you walk?
- Use one open question first, then wait. Then use closed questions to fill the gaps the patient left, and keep each closed question to one idea.
- Avoid the leading question, which ends in isn’t it, doesn’t it or hasn’t it. It tells the patient which answer you want, and a polite patient gives it to you.

A set of history questions prepared in words a patient will recognise is not a script to read aloud. It is a list you check afterwards, to see what you forgot to ask.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: describing pain: adjectives, intensity and comparison**

- Pain adjectives go before the noun or after the verb: a sharp pain; the pain is sharp. Offer three or four at a time, and let the patient choose or refuse them all.
- Intensity uses a number and a reference: on a scale of one to ten, how bad is it now? And how bad was it at the worst?
- Comparison needs something to compare with: is it worse than yesterday? is it worse than the pain you had last year? A comparative without a reference says nothing.
- Use like for a comparison the patient makes himself: it feels like a weight, like a band, like a knife. Write that sentence down exactly as he says it.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: present perfect for duration: how long have you had it**

- Use the present perfect for something that started in the past and is still here: how long have you had it? I have had it for three weeks. She has had the cough since March.
- For takes a period: for three weeks, for two years, for ten minutes. Since takes a point: since March, since Monday, since my daughter’s wedding.
- The past simple is for something that is finished: I had a pain last winter. It lasted two days. It went away. Keep the two apart in the note.
- Ask frequency with how often, and ask for a number: how often does it come? Twice a week. How many times since Monday? Three times.

**Grammar: quantity and frequency: how many, how often, how much**

**Grammar: checking a negative: have you ever had, has anything ever**

- Use have you ever and has anything ever to check a negative: have you ever had a reaction to a medicine? has anything you have taken ever made you ill?

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: going to and will for what you are about to do**

- Use going to for what you have decided and are about to do: I am going to listen to your chest. I am going to press on your stomach. I am going to take a little blood.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: imperatives and softened instruction: take, please take, you should take**

- The imperative is the clearest form and the right one for an instruction a patient must follow: take one tablet in the morning. Come back on Friday. Drink water.
- Add please to soften it without losing the action: please come back on Friday. Please bring the boxes with you.
- Should is weaker and belongs to advice rather than instruction: you should rest this week. Use it when the patient has a choice, and not when he has none.
- Do not is the form for a prohibition, and it needs a reason: do not take two at the same time, because one is enough for the whole day.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: summarising back: so, if I have understood you**

- Begin a summary with so, and give the patient room to correct you: so, if I have understood you, the pain began in September and comes twice a week.
- Use three or four facts only. A summary of everything is not a summary, and the patient cannot correct a paragraph.
- End the summary with a question: is that right? Then stop talking. The silence is where the correction comes.
- Use plain future forms for the plan: we shall meet again on Friday. The nurse will telephone you with the result. I shall write to your own doctor today.
