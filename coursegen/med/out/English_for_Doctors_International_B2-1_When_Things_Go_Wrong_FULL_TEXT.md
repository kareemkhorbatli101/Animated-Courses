### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How did your last escalation call begin?
- How long was it before you said what you wanted?
- What do you do when the person you have called does not come?

An escalation is not a report; it is a request made under time pressure to somebody who will decide, in the first ten seconds, how quickly to get out of a chair.

So the concern and the request go in the first sentence: I am worried about the man in bed four and I need you to see him within fifteen minutes.

Only then come the observations that justify the urgency, and only the ones that do, and three numbers and a trend beat a chronological account of the afternoon.

Name a time and ask for agreement to it, because an unanswered “as soon as you can” has no moment at which it has been broken.

Then say what you will do if nobody comes. I shall call the medical registrar at twenty past and I shall tell you that I have done so.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: urgency and request in one sentence: I need you to come now**

- Build the first sentence out of a concern and a request joined by and: I am worried about the woman in bed nine, and I need you to see her within ten minutes.
- Use I need you to rather than could you possibly, which invites a negotiation you do not have time for and which a tired colleague will take as an opening.
- Attach a time to the request, and attach a question to the time, so that agreement or refusal has to be spoken: can you be here by half past, or shall I call somebody else?
- Keep the evidence in the second sentence, in numbers and trends, and keep the narrative out of it entirely unless you are asked for it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An escalation message drafted with the concern in the first line**

**Figure 1.5** — Six lines, and a colleague who reads only the first still knows what to do.

An escalation message drafted with the concern in the first line is the one piece of writing in this book that may be read on a screen, in a corridor, by somebody walking.

Give the system the observations with their times, what you have already done, and what you are afraid of; ask for the concern and the request in the first line and nowhere else.

Ask it to keep the message under eighty words and to put the figures in a single line with the direction of travel marked, rather than as a list the reader has to interpret.

Ask for the fallback to carry its own time, so that the message states when you will escalate past the person you are writing to, and to whom.

Ask it to leave out the history, the apology and the hedging — a message that opens with regret for disturbing somebody has spent its first line on manners.

What no system can judge is how frightened you are, which is usually the most accurate instrument on the ward, and say the worst case in your own words and let it stand.

> **DATA POINT · Structured escalation** — Structured communication tools for escalation, which place the concern and the request before the narrative, are widely recommended in acute care and are associated with shorter times to review in several reported series. Local protocols vary considerably between institutions and systems.  
> Source: general acute-care communication guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why escalations fail** — Reviews of deterioration incidents commonly find that a request for review was made but that the degree of concern was not conveyed, and that the requester often did not state a time or a fallback. Findings vary by setting and by reporting method.  
> Source: general patient-safety literature, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · Graded Assertiveness: Concerned, Uncomfortable, Unsafe

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: graded assertiveness: I am concerned, I am uncomfortable, this is unsafe · AI: a graded assertiveness script prepared for a call that may go badly*

**Figure 2.0** — THERE ARE THREE SENTENCES. MOST PEOPLE ONLY EVER USE THE FIRST.: The third sentence works because the first two were used first and were heard.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What words does your department use to stop something?
- Have you ever said that something was unsafe, out loud?
- What happened when you did?

Graded assertiveness gives a junior clinician three sentences of rising force, so that the strongest one is available without being the only one.

The first is a concern: I am concerned that the dose is twice what we usually give; it is information offered, and most of the time it is all that is needed.

The second is discomfort: I am uncomfortable proceeding until somebody checks the weight; it names your own state and asks for a pause rather than a decision.

The third is the stop: this is unsafe, and I am asking you to stop, and it is a sentence nobody uses twice in a year, and the whole point of the first two is to keep it credible.

The levels only work if everybody knows them; a department where “I am uncomfortable” means nothing in particular has three ordinary sentences and no ladder at all.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: graded assertiveness: I am concerned, I am uncomfortable, this is unsafe**

- Level one is I am concerned that, followed by a fact: I am concerned that the prescription says 108 milligrams and the chart says 54 kilograms.
- Level two is I am uncomfortable, followed by a gerund and a condition: I am uncomfortable going on until somebody has checked the weight with me.
- Level three is a declaration and a request, in two short clauses: this is unsafe, and I am asking you to stop. No adverbs, no hedges, no explanation inside the sentence.
- Never attribute a motive at any level. You have not checked is a fact; you are not listening is an accusation, and it converts a clinical disagreement into a personal one.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

A clinical disagreement is not a contest between two people but a difference between two readings of the same patient, and the language should make that visible from the first clause.

When it goes in writing, write it as though it will be read by somebody who likes neither of you, which is the only register that is safe in a record.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A written disagreement drafted without accusation**

**Figure 3.5** — Six lines, and not one adjective about another clinician in any of them.

A written disagreement drafted without accusation is the hardest short document in clinical practice, because every sentence that feels satisfying to write is one that should come out.

Give the system the decision as it stands, the facts with their dates, your own reading and the points on which you accept the other clinician is right.

Ask it to remove every word that attributes a state of mind: failed, ignored, refused, declined, did not bother, chose not to; each of them turns a record into an allegation.

Ask for the concessions to be stated as prominently as the disagreement, and for the decision to be recorded as remaining where it lies.

Ask for a version you can show the colleague before it goes into the record, because a note shown in advance is a disagreement and a note discovered later is an ambush.

What no system can decide is whether this is the hill — some disagreements are worth a note, some are worth a telephone call to a consultant, and most are worth a conversation and nothing else.

> **DATA POINT · Professional disagreement** — Most health systems expect a clinician who disagrees with a colleague’s plan to raise it directly, to record the disagreement factually where it is not resolved, and to escalate through a named route. The responsibility for the decision normally remains with the treating senior clinician under local arrangements.  
> Source: general professional guidance on working with colleagues, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Records of disagreement** — Guidance on clinical records commonly advises that differences of opinion be recorded factually, without comment on colleagues’ competence or motives, and that the clinician responsible for the decision be identified. Local policies differ on what is copied to whom.  
> Source: general record-keeping guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Have you ever had to tell a patient about an error?
- Who told you how to do it?

When something has gone wrong in a patient’s care, the patient is the person it happened to, and he is entitled to be told by somebody who was involved and who can answer questions.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the plain apology: I am sorry, this was our mistake**

- The apology is one short sentence with a subject and no condition: I am sorry. We made a mistake. This was our error and not yours.
- Avoid the passive and the impersonal. Mistakes were made, an error occurred, the dose was administered incorrectly: each of them removes the person who did it.
- Avoid the conditional of feelings: I am sorry if you feel that, I am sorry you are upset. They apologise for his reaction rather than for your act.
- Keep the apology separate from the explanation. An apology with a because attached is heard as a defence: I am sorry, because the ward was very short-staffed that night.

**An error disclosure drafted as what happened, what now, what next**

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When did you last write an incident report?

- What did you have to guess at by then?

An incident report is a timed sequence of facts written by somebody who was there, and its value depends almost entirely on how soon after the event it is written.

Memory for times and order degrades within hours, and it degrades towards coherence: the account becomes a better story and a worse record every time you tell it.

So write it on the shift, before handover, even in rough form; a rough contemporaneous account beats an elegant one composed on Sunday from what colleagues have since told you.

Keep interpretation out, and the patient became unwell at 03.20 is a fact; the patient was clearly deteriorating for some time before that is a conclusion, and it belongs to the review.

Name the people who were present, because the report is a map for whoever investigates, and a name is not an accusation; what they did goes in; why they did it does not.

## Unit 6 · Breaking Bad News Without Hiding It

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

**Grammar: the warning shot and the plain sentence**

- The warning shot uses I am afraid or I have, with a comparative: I am afraid the news is worse than we hoped; I have some difficult news about the biopsy.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: possibility and limit: this may, this will not, this cannot**

- Use may for what the treatment might achieve, with a quantity attached: this may give you three or four months; this may keep the pain at the level it is now.
- Use will not for what it is not designed to do: it will not shrink the tumour; it will not get you back to work; it will not make next week easier.
- Use cannot for the categorical limit: it cannot cure this. Cannot is stronger than will not and should be kept for the things that are impossible, not merely unlikely.

**A family meeting recorded as decisions, questions and what was deferred**

A family meeting recorded as decisions, questions and what was deferred is the document that stops a bereaved family being asked the same question twice by three different people.

Give the system who was there with their relationships, what you said, what they asked, what you answered, and what you promised to find out.

## Unit 9 · Asking for a Second Opinion Without Insult

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: requesting review: I should value another opinion on · AI: a second-opinion request assembled with the question and the file*

**Figure 9.0** — A SECOND OPINION WITH NO QUESTION IN IT IS A SECOND DELAY.: “Grateful for your opinion” gets you an opinion on whatever the reader finds interesting.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When did you last ask for a second opinion?
- What was the question, in one sentence?
- Did the first clinician know before it went?

A second opinion is a question, not a gesture. “Grateful for your opinion on this complex gentleman” produces a letter about whatever the reader found interesting, four weeks later.

Ask one answerable question: would you operate on this man at this stage, or would you treat him medically and reassess in three months?

Send what makes it answerable, listed so that the reader knows nothing is missing: the imaging with dates, the histology, the previous opinions and the patient’s own stated priorities.

Tell the first clinician before the request goes, in person where you can; a second opinion discovered in a copy letter is an insult; one mentioned the day before is ordinary practice.

And plan for disagreement, because two opinions that differ leave the patient with a decision nobody has helped him make — say in advance who will sit down with him and reconcile them.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A note about another team’s record written as fact alone**

**Figure 10.5** — Six lines, and the sixth is the one that makes the other five safe.

A note about another team’s record written as fact alone is the clearest case in clinical writing where the useful instruction to a system is a list of words it may not use.

Give it the entries you are describing with their dates, the figures that differ, the window in which you found nothing, and your own actions with their times.

Ask for the record itself as the subject of every sentence about the other team: the entry records, the chart shows, the two entries differ.

Ask it to refuse the words failed, omitted, neglected, overlooked, ignored, inexplicably and should have, and to list anywhere it was tempted to use one.

Ask for your own actions and your own request for clarification to be recorded with times, because those are the two things in the note that are indisputably yours.

What no system can do is make the telephone call that turns your note into their addendum — a correction written by its author ends the matter; one written about him begins something else.

> **DATA POINT · Writing about colleagues’ records** — Record-keeping guidance commonly holds that clinicians should record facts, including discrepancies and absences, without comment on colleagues’ competence or motives, and that corrections to an entry should be made by its author as a dated addendum rather than by alteration. Local rules on amendment differ.  
> Source: general record-keeping guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Disclosure** — Clinical records are routinely disclosed to patients, to investigations and to courts in most systems, and guidance generally assumes that any entry may be read by the colleague it describes. Rules on access and redaction differ by jurisdiction.  
> Source: general guidance on clinical records and disclosure, 2025–2026.
