# English for Doctors — International Edition · plan

## Who the series is for

Doctors and other clinicians who work in English without having grown up in it: residents
and registrars in international hospitals, clinicians treating patients with whom they
share no language, doctors preparing for registration or a placement abroad, and anybody
who must hand over, refer, document, explain and consent in a second language.

## The spine

When the doctor, the nurse, the patient and the family are all working in a second
language, **the sentence is part of the treatment**. Each of the six books takes one place
where that is true:

1. **A2.1 The Patient in Front of You** — the consultation between two people who share no
   first language.
2. **A2.2 Ward, Notes and Team** — the working day on a ward of eleven nationalities.
3. **B1.1 Handover, Referral and the Record** — the machinery that carries a patient from
   one clinician to the next.
4. **B1.2 Explaining, Consent and the Family** — the patient's side of the desk.
5. **B2.1 When Things Go Wrong** — escalation, disagreement, error, bad news, death.
6. **B2.2 The Doctor and the System** — writing for institutions, across borders.

## What distinguishes this edition

* **Nobody's first language.** A history misheard, a handover that dropped the one fact
  that mattered, a consent conversation the patient did not follow: each is treated as a
  drafting or speaking fault with a clinical consequence, not as the patient's failure to
  understand.
* **The patient's words are data.** Book 1 teaches the doctor to keep what the patient
  actually said, in his own words, and to check identity three ways before anything else.
* **Written for the reader who acts.** Every document in the series is taught from the point
  of view of the person who must do something with it: the night registrar, the family
  doctor, the radiologist, the nurse at 03.00, the patient at home with a bag of boxes.
* **Interpreters are part of the method,** not an obstacle: short single-idea turns, the
  first person, speaking to the patient and not to the interpreter.
* **The hardest conversations are taught, not avoided.** Disclosure of an error, a ceiling
  of care, a death to tell a family, a disagreement with a senior colleague.
* **Three systems of everything.** Dates, units, reference ranges, drug names that differ
  between markets, and a discharge summary that will be read in a country whose services
  are organised differently.

## Architecture (unchanged from the rest of the LEDGER series)

Twelve parts per unit; ten units per book; sixty units in all. Twenty new vocabulary terms
per unit (1,200 across the series, no repeats), one grammar point per unit (60 distinct),
one AI capability per unit with its boundary line (60 distinct), one Brief Lab diagnosing
exactly three of the eight named precision faults, one five-stage Decision Room in which
the senior person is often wrong, and **Part 10, The Difficult Conversation** — this
edition's signature, replacing the Money Conversation of the commercial series.

## The editorial line on clinical content

The books teach clinical **communication and writing**. They do not teach medicine, give
clinical guidance, or recommend any treatment: no dose, no regimen and no diagnostic rule
appears anywhere in the series. Clinical detail is deliberately generic and exists only to
carry the language. Every unit that touches a clinical decision says in its own text that
the decision belongs to the treating clinician, and every AI capability is paired with a
boundary line that keeps examination, diagnosis and decision with the person who is
answerable for them.

Hospitals and clinics are invented; cities are real. No vendor, product, model, platform,
living official or political claim appears anywhere.
