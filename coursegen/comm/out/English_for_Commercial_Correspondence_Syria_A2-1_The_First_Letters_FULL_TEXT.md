### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the present simple for what a company does**

- Use the present simple for what is always or usually true: Barada Textiles makes cotton fabric.
- With he, she, it and a company name, add -s: the company sells, the office opens, the factory closes.
- Make questions and negatives with do and does: do you export? the office does not open on Friday.
- This is the tense of a company profile, a product description and the first paragraph of almost every commercial letter.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An enquiry is a list of questions and a list of guesses**

**Figure 1.5** — Four questions and two guesses, and the reply has to deal with all six.

A short enquiry can be read by a system in a second, and the list that comes back is usually right about the questions.

The useful part is the second list. Ask for the things the buyer has assumed but not asked. That you export. That you have stock. That you can deliver in a certain month, and that you accept a certain payment.

Those guesses cost orders. A buyer who assumes you export and discovers in the third message that you do not has lost a week, and he will not come back.

So the instruction is simple and specific: list every question, list every assumption, and mark any question you cannot answer today.

Then write one reply that covers all of them. A reply that answers three questions and promises the fourth later is a reply that gets no second message.

And keep the present simple for what your company does. We make, we export, we hold stock, we accept. The tense is small and it is the whole of a company profile.

> **DATA POINT · Enquiries** — Trade practice commonly treats a first enquiry as the most valuable message a company receives, and recommends that every question in it be answered in the first reply, since a second exchange loses many buyers.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · First replies** — Export practice commonly recommends that a first reply state what the supplier does, answer every question asked, and give a date for anything that cannot be answered at once.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A name is a fact about a person, not a guess from an address**

**Figure 2.5** — Four sources to use and two to ignore, and the mistake comes from the last two.

A system is good at this and is used badly. Given an email, it will extract a name, a title and a company in a second. It will be right as often as its source is right.

So name the source in the instruction: the signature block of the buyer’s own message, the letterhead, the card. Never the email address, which is made by an administrator, and never a telephone list, which is two years old.

Ask for the name exactly as its owner writes it, including the order of the parts. In many markets the family name comes first and in many it comes last. A list that reorders names to look tidy has destroyed the only information you needed.

Ask for the title as printed, and keep it. A general manager is not a manager; a commercial director is not a salesman; and a buyer whose title is shortened reads it as carelessness.

Then build one contact record for each person. Name as written, title as printed, company in full with its legal form, and the source and date of each. A record without a date is a record you will doubt in a year.

The whole discipline costs a minute per contact. The alternative is a first letter that begins with somebody else’s given name.

> **DATA POINT · Names in correspondence** — Business practice commonly takes the signature block, the letterhead or the business card as the authority for how a person’s name and title are written, in preference to an email address or a telephone list.  
> Source: general business correspondence guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Name order** — Correspondence practice commonly reproduces a name in the order its owner uses, and treats reordering into a local convention as an error rather than a tidying.  
> Source: general business correspondence guidance, 2025–2026.

**A company publishes facts about itself, and they can be read**

**Figure 3.5** — Six facts, four of them public, and two that must be asked for.

Checking a company against what it publishes is a good use of twenty minutes. It is also a good use of a system, which reads a website, a licence and a letterhead faster than we do.

Ask for the check as a table of differences rather than a report. Registered name, registered address, delivery address, licence or register number, telephone with its country code, year of establishment, and the source of each.

And record four addresses, not one: invoice to, deliver to, papers to, and the name of the person who signs for goods. The fourth is never on a website and is the one you need at six o’clock on a Thursday.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The description that sells is the one in the buyer’s own words**

**Figure 4.5** — Six true technical facts, and six sentences a buyer can act on.

Turning a technical specification into a buyer’s description is a task a system does well. It does it badly when it is asked to make the product sound good instead.

Say who the buyer is and what they make. A shirt maker, a hotel laundry and a furniture workshop need three different descriptions of the same cloth, because they use it in three different ways.

Give it the technical sheet and the test report, and require every sentence to rest on a figure that is in them. No adjective may be added that cannot be traced to a number.

Forbid the words that mean nothing: high quality, excellent, superior, best, wide range. They are the same in every catalogue in the world and they carry no information at all.

Require the honest line as well: what you do not have. Four weights and eleven shades is a fact a buyer can work with; a wide range is a sentence he has read four times this morning.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A figure without a currency, a unit and a date is not a price**

**Figure 5.5** — Four parts that make a price, and two more that decide what it covers.

Converting a price list into one currency and one unit is simple arithmetic, and a system does it accurately in seconds.

What it cannot do is know which unit your buyer thinks in, and that is the whole of the risk. A market that buys by the yard, by the piece or by the kilogram will read your metres as its own unit. The unit has to stand beside every figure.

So the instruction names the target. Convert to this currency and this unit, show the rate used and its date, and repeat the unit beside every figure.

Rounding must be stated as well. A price rounded up by half a unit is a price a buyer can recalculate and query. A quotation that cannot be reproduced from its own numbers starts an argument about arithmetic.

Add validity. A price without a date is a price the buyer holds for as long as it suits him. The yarn market does not wait for anybody.

And say what the price covers: ex works, packed in rolls, exclusive of carriage. Could you give me your best price is a question. A price with six parts is an answer nobody has to telephone about.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the past simple for what has already happened**

- Use the past simple for a finished action with a finished time: we shipped the goods on 5 May.
- Regular verbs add -ed: telephoned, confirmed, delayed. Many common verbs are irregular: send became sent, leave became left, take became took.
- Questions and negatives use did and the base form: did the goods arrive? we did not know about the holiday.
- In correspondence the past simple reports what happened. It is the tense of a record, and a record settles arguments.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Count backwards from the buyer’s day, not forwards from your own**

**Figure 7.5** — The same two weeks, counted from two ends, three days apart.

Counting a delivery date is arithmetic, and a system does arithmetic well. The mistake is never in the sum.

A delivery date calculated backwards from the buyer’s week needs four calendars, and three of them are not yours. Your production days, your holidays, his holidays and his week.

So give it all four. The day the buyer must have the goods, your production days, your holidays, the transit time and the buyer’s weekend. Then ask for the dispatch date.

Ask for the buffer separately. One spare day in fifteen is a buffer; one spare day written into the promise is a date you have already spent.

Then write the date so that nobody can misread it: the day in figures, the month in letters, the year in full. 5/6 means two different days in two markets, and 5 June 2026 means one day everywhere.

And mark every date as confirmed or estimated. We shipped on the fifth is a fact. We shall ship on the fifth is a promise, and the difference between them is what a buyer plans his own factory around.

> **DATA POINT · Dates** — Trade practice commonly expresses a delivery date as the date goods must be available to the buyer, and requires production and transit time to be calculated backwards from it.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Date format** — International practice commonly recommends writing the month in letters in commercial documents, since the numeric order of day and month differs between markets.  
> Source: general documentary practice guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A call is a conversation until somebody writes it down**

**Figure 8.5** — Four lines, one hour, and no argument in March about a Tuesday in January.

A telephone call turned into four lines of confirmation is a small task and a large protection, and it is one of the few places where a drafting tool is useful immediately after an event rather than before it.

Dictate the call while you remember it and ask for four lines in a fixed order: what was agreed, what was discussed but not agreed, what you must check with a date, and one question back.

The second line matters most. Buyers and sellers remember agreement; nobody remembers that something was only discussed, and the gap between the two is where claims begin.

Require figures to be repeated with their conditions attached. Six twenty at two thousand metres is one fact. Six twenty is half a fact, and the half that travels is the one that suits the reader.

Send it within the hour, with the time of the call in the first line. A confirmation that arrives on Thursday about a Tuesday call invites a correction; one that arrives at four o’clock about a three o’clock call invites a reply.

And end with a question. A confirmation nobody answers is still your own memory with a date on it; a confirmation that is answered is a record both sides made.

> **DATA POINT · Confirming calls** — Commercial practice commonly treats a written confirmation sent the same day as the record of a telephone agreement, in preference to either party’s later recollection.  
> Source: general business correspondence guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Silence** — Commercial practice commonly treats a written confirmation that is not corrected as evidence of what was agreed, which is why confirmations are sent promptly and invite a reply.

**Grammar: negative and polite forms: we are unable to, we regret that**

- We are unable to + verb is the neutral commercial negative: we are unable to supply this weight.
- We regret that + clause adds the writer’s regret: we regret that we cannot meet your date.
- We are not in a position to is slightly softer and is used where the reason is temporary.
- Express regret once. Two apologies in a short letter sound anxious, and three sound as though something is wrong.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A refusal costs nothing today and decides the enquiries of next year**

**Figure 9.5** — Six differences, about forty words, and one buyer who writes again.

Writing a polite refusal is easy for a system and the easiness is the trap: what comes back is warm, grammatical and empty.

A refusal written so that the enquiry comes back next year has four parts, and a general instruction produces only the first. The regret, the reason, the alternative and the date.

The reason must be specific and true: our minimum is five hundred metres; we do not make that weight; our capacity is full until August. A buyer can act on any of those, and can act on nothing in we are unable to supply.

The alternative is where the order is saved or lost. Many buyers ask for four hundred metres because they are guessing at your minimum, and will take five hundred if they are told the number.

The date keeps the relationship. September, with an offer to quote now, converts a refusal into a diary entry on both sides.

And express regret once. A short letter with three apologies in it reads as a company that is sorry about itself, and buyers do not write back to that.

> **DATA POINT · Refusals** — Commercial practice commonly recommends that a refusal state its reason, offer an alternative where one exists, and invite the enquirer to return at a stated future point.  
> Source: general business correspondence guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Minimum quantities** — Trade practice commonly finds that buyers frequently enquire below a supplier’s minimum because the minimum is unpublished, and that stating the figure converts a proportion of such enquiries into orders.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the future with will and the present for fixed arrangements**

- Will makes a promise at the moment of writing: we will send the sample on Monday; we will hold this price until 31 March.
- The present simple states a fixed timetable: the ship sails on the fourteenth; our office closes on Friday afternoon.
- The present continuous states an arrangement already made: we are shipping your order on the fifth.
- In a quotation, use will for what your company undertakes and the present for what the calendar does. Mixing them makes a timetable look like a promise.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A quotation is an offer, and every line of it can be accepted**

**Figure 10.5** — Six decisions in eight lines, and all of them become binding on acceptance.

Assembling a quotation from a price list and an enquiry is a job a system does accurately, and it is worth giving it, because the arithmetic and the layout take an afternoon by hand.

Give it the eight parts as a list and require each one to come from a named source: the price list, the production plan, the standard terms. Nothing may be inferred.

The danger is the opposite of the one in a letter. Here the output is too confident: it will produce a complete, handsome quotation with a delivery date it has deduced and a payment term it has assumed.

So require the sources and then check the two lines that cost money: the date against the dyeing schedule and the stock, and the payment terms against what credit control has agreed for this buyer.

Leave out protective formulas. Without engagement tells a buyer that nothing on the page is firm, which is the opposite of what a first quotation is for. Where something genuinely depends on a condition, write the condition on that line.

And sign it. A quotation that commits the company carries the name and the title of the person who may commit it, and a buyer who sees that reads a firm offer rather than a price idea.

> **DATA POINT · Quotations** — Commercial practice commonly treats a quotation as a firm offer capable of acceptance, and recommends that its validity, its delivery term and its payment terms appear on the same page as the price.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Firmness** — Commercial practice commonly distinguishes a firm offer, which binds the seller for its stated validity, from an indication without engagement, and treats the wording used as determining which has been made.  
> Source: general export practice guidance, 2025–2026.
