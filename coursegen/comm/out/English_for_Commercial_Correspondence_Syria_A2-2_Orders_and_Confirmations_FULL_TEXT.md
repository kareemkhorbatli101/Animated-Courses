### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An order is the buyer’s document, and it says what the buyer wants**

**Figure 1.5** — Three lines different out of eight, and all three are about money or time.

Comparing a quotation with an order is simple work that is easy to skip when an order arrives, because an order is good news and nobody reads good news carefully.

A quotation and an order compared line by line is exactly the task to hand over. Give it both documents and ask for a table with three columns: the line, what we offered, what has been ordered.

Require every line to appear, including the identical ones. A comparison that shows only the differences cannot be checked, because you cannot see what has been looked at.

Ask for the differences to be marked by kind: quantity, price, date, payment, packing. A change of quantity usually changes a price; a change of date usually changes a cost; and a change of payment is the one nobody notices until the money is late.

What it cannot do is tell you whether a difference matters. Eighteen hundred metres instead of two thousand may be a rounding to the buyer and a price break to you.

So the reply is yours. Two facts and a question for each line, with no blame in any of them: our quotation offered this, your order says that, which would you like?

> **DATA POINT · Order and offer** — Commercial practice commonly treats an order that varies the terms of a quotation as a counter-offer, which the seller may accept, decline or confirm with amendments.  
> Source: general commercial practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Checking orders** — Order-handling practice commonly requires every received order to be checked against the offer it answers before production is planned or materials are committed.  
> Source: general commercial practice guidance, 2025–2026.

**A change is not a favour; it is a price, a date and a new document**

Buyers change their minds, and they are entitled to ask. What they are not entitled to is an answer that pretends nothing has happened in the factory.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: modals of certainty and promise: will, shall, can confirm**

- Use will for a promise or a prediction you stand behind: we will ship on Thursday.
- Use shall with I and we for an undertaking in formal writing: I shall send the receipt today.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Silence usually means absence, and absence has a telephone number**

**Figure 9.5** — Six reasons for silence, and only one of them is being ignored.

When a customer goes quiet, the natural response is to write again, and then again, with the tone hardening each time.

A chase sequence drafted for a customer who has gone quiet is useful work to hand over, because the shape is fixed and the dates can be calculated.

Give it the last letter sent, the date of the last reply and the amount or document at stake. Ask for three letters with stated intervals, a telephone call before the third, and a line naming an alternative contact.

So make the telephone call yourself, early, and record what you learn. Nine days of sharpening prose is worth less than thirty seconds with a switchboard.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A letter that records what somebody did is worth more to them than praise**

**Figure 10.5** — Six empty phrases and the facts that would have replaced them.

A closing thank-you is the cheapest letter in commercial correspondence and the one most often wasted on a phrase that names nothing.

A thank-you that records what was done well is simple work to hand over, because the material is in the file: the dates, the names, the acts and what they saved.

Give it the correspondence on the order and ask for three facts: who did what, when, and what it prevented or saved. Ask for the names exactly as they are signed.

Forbid adjectives about the person. Excellent, wonderful and invaluable say nothing a manager can act on, while a date and an act can go in a personnel file.

What it cannot know is who should receive a copy. Copying a manager helps in most companies and embarrasses in a few, and only you know which this is.

So the letter goes out short: the act, the date, what it saved, one line of warmth, and a closing question that gives the reader something to answer.

> **DATA POINT · Supplier relations** — Relationship practice in trade commonly recommends naming the individual and the specific act when thanking a counterpart, and copying their line manager where the act went beyond routine.  
> Source: general business relationship guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Repeat business** — Commercial practice commonly finds that the cost of winning a new customer exceeds the cost of retaining an existing one, which makes post-shipment correspondence a commercial activity rather than a courtesy.  
> Source: general commercial practice guidance, 2025–2026.
