## Unit 1 · A Complaint That Deserves an Answer

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: reported complaints — states that, alleges that, maintains that · AI: a complaint sorted into fact, loss and what is being asked for*

**Figure 1.0** — A COMPLAINT IS THREE THINGS, AND MOST PEOPLE ANSWER ONLY THE THIRD: Eleven lines of complaint, holding four facts, one figure and one request.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does a complaint usually contain?
- Which part do people answer first?
- What should be acknowledged, and how soon?

A complaint contains three different kinds of statement, and treating them as one is the commonest error in answering it.

The first kind is fact: the goods arrived on the ninth, two cartons were open, the colour differs from the sample. Facts can be checked.

The second is loss: he has lost a customer, he has had to buy locally at a higher price, he has paid for storage. Loss is a figure, and a figure can be tested.

The third is the remedy: he wants a credit, or a replacement, or an apology. The remedy is what he is asking for and it is often larger than his loss.

So the first letter back acknowledges, states a date for an answer, and promises nothing. The second letter answers the facts, the loss and the remedy in that order.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: reported complaints: states that, alleges that, maintains that**

- States that reports a neutral assertion: he states that the goods arrived on the ninth.
- Alleges that reports a claim you have not accepted: he alleges that two cartons were open on arrival.
- Maintains that reports a position held against contradiction: he maintains that the colour differs from the sample.
- Avoid claims that for a customer, which reads as disbelief, and avoid admits that, which is for your own side.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A complaint is answered in the order the complainant did not write it**

**Figure 1.5** — Three facts, two losses and one request, which is the order to answer them in.

A complaint arrives as a single piece of unhappiness, and the instinct is to answer the part that is loudest, which is almost always the request.

A complaint sorted into fact, loss and what is being asked for is good work to hand over, because the sorting is mechanical and the complainant has done the writing.

Give it the complaint and ask for every sentence to be classified as a statement of fact, an alleged loss, or a requested remedy, with a note of what evidence would settle each fact.

Require any loss without a figure to be listed as unquantified. A lost customer is a loss of something, and until it has a number it cannot be answered.

What it cannot judge is the relationship. A complaint from a buyer of eleven years is answered in the same structure and a different voice, and sometimes with a gesture that the facts do not require.

So the acknowledgement goes out today with a date on it, the facts are checked against evidence, and the answer takes the facts, then the loss, then the remedy, in that order and in separate paragraphs.

> **DATA POINT · Complaint handling** — Complaint-handling guidance commonly recommends acknowledging a complaint within one working day, giving a date for a substantive reply, and separating factual investigation from any discussion of remedy.  
> Source: general business practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Acknowledgements** — Service guidance commonly treats a prompt acknowledgement with a stated date for a full reply as reducing the escalation of complaints, independently of the eventual outcome.  
> Source: general business practice guidance, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · The Claim with Photographs

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: evidence and attribution — according to, as shown by, per the record · AI: a claim assembled with photographs, dates and weights*

**Figure 2.0** — A CLAIM IS AS STRONG AS ITS WEAKEST PHOTOGRAPH: Eleven photographs in a claim file, and not one of them taken at the doors.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What evidence does a claim need?
- When must the first photograph be taken?
- How is a loss turned into a figure?

A claim is a file, not a letter. The letter is the last two pages of it, and nobody reads them until the evidence has been looked at.

The evidence has three parts: images with dates, weights and tallies, and documents showing the condition on arrival.

Timing decides everything. A photograph taken at the doors, before anything is moved, shows the seal, the stow and the first sight of damage; one taken later shows a warehouse.

The loss must be quantified and the arithmetic shown. Forty metres at 6.20 is 248 dollars, and a claim for the full roll is a claim nobody will pay.

A protest to the carrier goes within the time bar stated in the transport document, usually measured in days. A claim notified late is usually refused whatever its merits.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: evidence and attribution: according to, as shown by, per the record**

- According to attributes a statement to a source: according to the tally, fifty-eight cartons were landed.
- As shown by introduces visual or documentary proof of what you have just asserted: two cartons were wet, as shown by images 3 and 4.
- Per the record is a compact attribution to a file or a document: per the delivery receipt, the driver noted the damage.
- Do not use according to for your own direct knowledge, and never attribute a statement to a document that does not contain it.

**A claim is read as evidence first and as grievance never**

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is a credit limit for?
- What evidence supports one?
- Is a slow payer the same as a risky one?

A credit limit is the most the company is willing to be owed by one customer at one time. A credit period is how long he may take to pay.

The two are separate decisions. A customer may be given a high limit and a short period, or a low limit and sixty days.

The evidence is his own history with us: the average days to pay, the highest balance he has ever owed, and whether any invoice has gone more than ninety days.

A slow payer is not necessarily a risk. A customer who has paid at fifty-two days for nine years is predictable, and predictability can be priced.

The dangerous customer is the one who pays on time for two years and then stops, because nothing in his history warns you, and the limit is the only protection you have.

**Grammar: cause and consequence: owing to, as a result of, consequently**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A credit decision is an argument from a customer’s own record**

**Figure 3.5** — Six customers, six different decisions, and all of them from two columns.

Credit decisions are made in the memory of whoever is asked, and memory is generous to customers who telephone pleasantly and harsh to those who do not.

A credit decision supported by what a customer has paid before is good work to hand over, because the ledger holds the answer and nobody has to be judged.

Give it the ledger for that customer, the current balance and the orders in hand. Ask for the average days to pay, the highest balance ever owed, the worst single invoice, and the trend over the last twelve months.

Require the figures to be given with the number of invoices behind them. An average of thirty days over four invoices is not evidence of anything.

So the limit comes from you: a little above the highest balance for a predictable payer, well below the request for a customer with no history of pressure, and reviewed at a stated interval in both cases.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When does a first reminder go out?
- What should it contain?
- What should it never contain?

A first reminder goes out on a fixed day, usually the day after an invoice falls due, and it goes out whether or not anybody remembers the customer.

It contains four things: the invoice number, the sum, the number of days it is overdue, and a request for the date on which it will be paid.

It does not contain an apology for writing, a reference to the relationship, or any suggestion that the customer may have had a reason.

Asking for a date rather than for payment changes the reply. A customer cannot usefully answer please pay; he can answer when will you pay.

If the customer raises a query, the query is answered and the reminder cycle pauses only for the amount in question. The rest of the invoice remains due.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A reminder written from the ledger goes out whether anybody remembers or not**

**Figure 4.5** — Six fields, all of them in the accounts, none of them in anybody’s memory.

Reminders are written when somebody notices, which means they are written for customers who are remembered and not for those who are not.

A first reminder written from the ledger, not from memory, is good work to hand over, because the ledger knows exactly who is overdue and by how much.

Give it the aged debt list and the standing letter. Ask for one letter per overdue invoice, carrying the invoice number, the amount after credits, the due date, the days overdue and a request for a payment date.

Require every credit note and payment on account to be deducted before the letter is drafted. A reminder for an amount the customer has already paid in part destroys the next three.

What it cannot know is which customer telephoned yesterday with a genuine difficulty. A reminder sent to a man who spoke to you an hour ago reads as a system, and systems are easy to ignore.

So the list comes back and you take out the two or three you know about, and the rest go on the fixed day, four lines each, asking for a date.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How is a second reminder different from a first?
- What changes, and what must not change?
- What should a final letter never contain?

A sequence of reminders escalates by becoming shorter, more formal and more specific, and not by becoming angrier.

The facts are identical in every letter: the same invoice number, the same amount, the same due date, with only the days overdue changing.

A customer who finds a different figure in the second letter stops reading all of them, because one of them must be wrong.

The third letter states what will happen and when: supply suspended, credit withdrawn, the matter referred. Each of those must be something the company will actually do.

A consequence stated and not carried out teaches the customer that the sequence is a form of words, and the next account that goes overdue will test it again.

## Unit 6 · A Payment Plan

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- When is a payment plan the right answer?
- What must a plan contain to be monitored?
- What happens when an instalment is missed?

A payment plan is for a customer who cannot pay now and will be able to pay later. For a customer who will not pay, it is a delay dressed as an agreement.

A plan that can be monitored has four elements: named amounts, named dates, a first payment made before anything is conceded, and a stated consequence if an instalment is missed.

The total never changes. A plan reschedules a debt; it does not reduce one, and any reduction is a separate decision taken for a separate reason.

The first instalment comes before the plan is confirmed. A customer who cannot pay the first instalment today has not agreed to a plan; he has agreed to a hope.

Plans are renegotiated once at most. A second renegotiation tells the customer that dates are opinions, and the account will then run for a year.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A plan is a schedule somebody can check without asking anybody**

**Figure 6.5** — The same six elements, one version checkable by anybody and one by nobody.

A payment plan is usually agreed on the telephone by somebody relieved that the customer has answered at all, and it is written down afterwards, loosely.

A payment plan drafted so that it can be monitored is good work to hand over, because monitorability is a property you can test line by line.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

Refusing credit is not refusing to trade. The customer wants goods; the credit is only the method of paying for them.

**The order is the objective; the credit is only one way of getting it**

**Figure 7.5** — Six refusals, six alternatives, and every one of them keeps the business.

Refusals are written as if the credit were the point. The customer wanted cloth, and we answer with a paragraph about our credit policy.

A refusal of credit that keeps the customer and the order is good work to hand over, because the alternatives are known and the reasoning is a policy.

Give it the request, the credit assessment and our list of alternative bases. Ask for a letter of five lines: the regret, the policy reason, the alternative with its benefit, the review date, and the close.

So telephone first where the relationship matters, send the five lines the same day, and put the review date in the diary so that the promise is kept without being asked for.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How much notice should a price increase carry?
- What reason will a buyer accept?
- What happens to orders already placed?

A price increase is announced, not negotiated. The letter gives a figure, a date from which it applies, and a reason the buyer can check for himself.

The reason must be a cost the buyer recognises: cotton, energy, freight, or a currency movement he reads about in his own newspaper.

A reason he cannot verify sounds like an excuse, and an increase that sounds like an excuse invites a conversation about discounts.

Orders already placed must be protected. An increase that reaches into orders a buyer has confirmed is a broken confirmation, whatever the covering letter says.

Notice should be long enough to let him order at the old price. A month is generous and usually brings forward business, which is a benefit and not a loss.

**Grammar: making a record of failure: failed to, did not, was not received**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A supplier file is written on ordinary days and used on a bad one**

**Figure 9.5** — Four two-minute notes, which together are a case nobody can argue with.

Supplier failures are discussed in corridors and not written down, because writing them down feels like an accusation and nobody wants to accuse a supplier they depend on.

A supplier’s failure documented as it happens is good work to hand over, because the four facts are always the same and the language is flat.

Give it the order, the promise and what actually happened. Ask for a note of the undertaking, the date promised, the date and nature of the outcome, and the cost to us in figures.

Require the language to be free of adjectives and the cost to be left blank where there was none. A note that says the cost was serious is a note you cannot use.

What it cannot judge is whether to act. A supplier who has failed four times in five months may still be the only mill that dyes that shade, and the file informs that decision rather than making it.

So the notes are written on the day, the costs are quantified when they arise, and a performance review with the supplier is held on the file rather than on anybody’s memory of a bad week.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does a settlement letter have to do?
- What is the scope of a settlement?
- Why does a settlement need a signature from both sides?

A settlement letter ends a matter. To end it, the letter must say what the matter was, what is being paid, what is being given up, and by when.

The scope is the difficult part. A settlement of the claim on shipment 4418 is not a settlement of every claim that customer may have, and a letter that confuses the two reopens in six months.

In full and final settlement is the phrase that closes the matter. It should name the matter it closes: in full and final settlement of all claims arising from shipment 4418.

Without prejudice marks a letter written in the course of negotiation that is not meant to be used as evidence. It is not a magic phrase and it does not make a bad offer safe.

A settlement is complete when both sides have signed it and the money has been paid. A signed letter with no payment is an agreement about an intention.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A settlement is a scope, a figure, a date and two signatures**

**Figure 10.5** — Six elements, and the absence of any one of them turns a settlement into a letter.

Settlements are written in relief. The argument is over, everybody wants to move on, and the letter reflects that mood rather than the matter.

A settlement letter that ends a matter in writing is good work to hand over, because the elements are fixed and the omissions are predictable.

Give it the correspondence, the sum agreed and the list of open matters on that account. Ask for the sum, the scope named by shipment or invoice, the exclusions by number, the payment date, the exclusion of any admission, and the counter-signature.

Require every open matter on the account to appear either inside the scope or in the exclusions. A matter mentioned nowhere is a matter somebody will later say was included.

What it cannot judge is how much to offer and what to insist on. A settlement is a commercial decision, and the letter is only the instrument that records it.

So you decide the figure, the letter states the scope exactly, and the file is closed only when the counter-signed copy and the money have both arrived.

> **DATA POINT · Settlements** — Commercial settlement practice commonly requires the scope of the matters released to be stated expressly, with payment and signature by both parties as conditions of the release taking effect.  
> Source: general commercial practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Conditional settlements** — Settlement correspondence commonly makes the release conditional upon payment by a stated date, so that the original claim revives in full if payment is not made.  
> Source: general commercial practice guidance, 2025–2026.
