### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What happens to a bid that is non-responsive?
- What should be read first in a tender dossier?
- Who answers a question about an ambiguous requirement?

A tender is decided in two stages that have nothing to do with each other. The first asks whether the bid is admissible at all, and the second asks whether it is any good.

Bids are rejected at the first stage for reasons that have nothing to do with competence: a bond of the wrong value, a page unsigned, a technical document placed in the financial envelope, a submission delivered eleven minutes late.

So the dossier is read in reverse. The requirements that disqualify are extracted first, listed with the clause that imposes them, and turned into a matrix that somebody checks against the bundle before it is sealed.

Where a requirement is ambiguous, a clarification question is put to the procuring entity within the period the dossier allows, and the answer, which is usually issued to every bidder as an addendum, becomes part of the rules.

The prose itself is impersonal and nominal, because that is the register submissions are read in: the provision of training is included rather than we will train your staff, and the bidder confirms rather than we promise.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: impersonal and nominal style in formal submissions**

- Nominal style turns a verb into a noun and removes the actor: the provision of training is included, rather than we will train your staff.
- The impersonal passive states what is done without naming who does it: the carriage of the goods will be effected by sea.
- Where a commitment must be attributed, the bidder is the subject: the bidder confirms that all documents are enclosed.
- This register is not decoration. Evaluators read many bids against a matrix, and a sentence in the expected form is found in seconds.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**The first reading of a dossier is a search for the ways you can be thrown out**

**Figure 1.5** — Four failures, three clauses, and not one of them about the work itself.

A tender dossier is read from the front, which means the requirements that disqualify are reached in the fourth hour, by which time somebody has already begun drafting the prose.

A tender dossier reduced to what disqualifies you is excellent work to hand over, because the mandatory requirements are stated in the document and can be extracted without judgement.

Give it the dossier and every addendum. Ask for one row per mandatory requirement, with the clause number, the words that impose it, the evidence required, and the consequence of failure as the dossier states it.

Require every requirement about form, signature, envelope, bond, deadline and language to appear, because those are the clauses that reject bids, and they are scattered through the document rather than collected.

What it cannot do is write the bid. The matrix tells you what must be in the bundle; whether your company can actually deliver in eight weeks is a different question and a harder one.

So the matrix is built in the first hour, the clarification questions go out within the period, and somebody who did not write the bid checks the bundle against the matrix before it is sealed.

> **DATA POINT · Tender admissibility** — Public and institutional procurement commonly assesses bids first for responsiveness to mandatory requirements, and a bid failing a mandatory requirement is commonly rejected without evaluation of its substance.  
> Source: general procurement practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Clarifications** — Procurement practice commonly provides a period for clarification questions, with answers issued to all bidders as addenda forming part of the tender documents.  
> Source: general procurement practice guidance, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · A Proposal Read in Twenty Minutes

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: structure signals in a long document — first, accordingly, in conclusion · AI: a proposal structured for a reader with twenty minutes*

**Figure 2.0** — THE READER WHO DECIDES WILL GIVE YOUR THIRTY-PAGE PROPOSAL TWENTY MINUTES, AND HE WILL SPEND TWELVE OF THEM ON THE FIRST PAGE: Thirty pages, of which the deciding reader sees four and remembers one.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How long will a decision-maker spend on a long proposal?
- What does he read, and in what order?
- What should a heading do?

A long proposal has two kinds of reader. One will read every page, usually a technical specialist checking whether the work can be done, and one will read for twenty minutes before deciding whether to do it.

The twenty-minute reader starts with the summary, looks at the headings, and turns to the price. He reads a middle page only if a heading or the summary has given him a reason to.

So the first page carries the recommendation, the reason for it, and the figure, in that order. A summary that describes the proposal rather than recommending something has wasted the only page that is certain to be read.

Headings state conclusions rather than topics. Delivery can be completed in eight weeks is a heading; the question of delivery is a label on a box whose contents nobody will open.

Everything that supports a claim without being necessary to it goes into an annex, and the claim and its support appear on the same page, because a reader who must turn to page twenty-two will instead decide without it.

**Grammar: structure signals in a long document: first, accordingly, in conclusion**

- First, secondly and thirdly mark the parts of an argument and must be used in order and completely: a first with no second confuses a skimming reader.
- Accordingly and therefore introduce a consequence of what has just been established: the capacity is insufficient; accordingly, we propose two units.
- In conclusion introduces the end of the argument and appears once, at the end: in conclusion, the investment is recovered in fourteen months.
- These signals are read by somebody scanning, which means they must be true: a paragraph introduced by accordingly must follow from the one above it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A long document is two documents, and the shorter one decides**

**Figure 2.5** — Four pages against thirty, and the four decide whether the thirty are believed.

A proposal is written from the front in the order the writer thinks, which produces a document that explains the background for nine pages before it says what it wants.

A proposal structured for a reader with twenty minutes is good work to hand over, because the structural test is mechanical: can the recommendation, its reason, the price and the timing be found on the first page?

Give it the draft and the question you were asked to answer. Ask for the first page to be checked for those four elements, for every heading to be tested as a conclusion rather than a label, and for every claim to be matched with support on its own page.

**A profile is read by somebody comparing it with forty others**

A profile is written by somebody who knows the company and is read by somebody who does not, which means that every sentence carrying familiarity rather than information is wasted.

## Unit 4 · A Trade Fair and the Letters Before It

**Figure 4.0** — A FAIR IS NOT A PLACE WHERE BUYERS FIND YOU; IT IS A PLACE WHERE THE MEETINGS YOU ARRANGED SIX WEEKS EARLIER HAPPEN TO BE HELD: One stand, two outcomes, and the difference was made entirely by letter.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What decides the value of a trade fair?
- When should the letters go out?
- What should a request for a meeting contain?

A stand costs the same whether twenty-two buyers come to it by appointment or four wander past it. The cost per meeting is therefore decided six weeks before the fair opens, by correspondence.

The exhibitor and visitor lists are published in advance. From them a list of buyers worth meeting is extracted, and each one receives a letter that does not ask whether he would like to meet.

A letter that asks for a meeting invites a decision; a letter that offers a time invites a confirmation. We should be pleased to see you at eleven o’clock on the Wednesday is answered yes or no, and either answer is useful.

At the fair itself a note is made at the stand, on the card or beside the name, of what was discussed and what was promised. Nothing written down on the third evening is worth having.

The follow-up goes within three days and refers to something specific: the shade you asked about, the sixty-day terms we discussed. A follow-up that could have been sent to anybody is read as a circular.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A fair is three days of meetings that were arranged in six weeks of letters**

**Figure 4.5** — Five of the six stages happen before or after the fair, and three are letters.

A fair is prepared by booking a stand, arranging travel and printing a price list, which are the three activities that have no effect whatever on how many buyers you meet.

A trade fair prepared by letter, meeting by meeting, is good work to hand over, because the exhibitor and visitor lists are published and the letters are a fixed form.

Give it the published lists, your market priorities and the three days available. Ask for a list of buyers worth meeting, with the reason in one line each, and a letter to each one offering a specific hour within the three days.

Require every letter to offer a time rather than request a meeting, and require a reserve hour for each buyer so that a refusal of the first can be answered immediately with a second.

What it cannot do is sit at the stand. Three words written on a card while a buyer is still standing there are worth more than the entire pre-fair mailing, because they make the follow-up possible.

So the letters go six weeks out, the diary fills to twenty-two, every card is annotated at the time, the follow-ups go within three days naming the shade and the city, and the cost per meeting is calculated afterwards and used to decide about next year.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Why does a bank ask a company about its owners?
- What makes an answer responsive?
- What should be said about a document you do not hold?

A bank that handles a payment for you is required to know who you are, who owns you, and what the money is for. The questions it asks are not curiosity and cannot be negotiated.

An answer is accepted when it is exactly responsive and accompanied by the document that supports it. An answer that explains, justifies or adds context produces a further question about the explanation.

Each incomplete round costs about a fortnight, and a payment held for a fortnight is a shipment that misses a sailing. The questionnaire is therefore worth an afternoon of exact work.

## Unit 7 · An Announcement for the Trade Press

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: news register for the trade press — the active voice and a named source · AI: an announcement written for a trade journal’s own readers*

**Figure 7.0** — AN EDITOR DOES NOT PUBLISH YOUR ANNOUNCEMENT; HE PUBLISHES THE PART OF IT THAT IS NEWS TO HIS OWN READERS, AND HE CUTS FROM THE BOTTOM: Eleven paragraphs submitted, two printed, and the two were the first two.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who are the readers of a trade journal?
- What does an editor do with a long announcement?
- What makes a quotation worth printing?

A trade journal is written for people in the trade, which means that its readers already know what shirting is and do not need to be told that quality matters.

An editor receives more material than he can print, and he cuts from the bottom. An announcement whose news is in the fourth paragraph is an announcement whose news is not printed.

So the first sentence carries the news in the active voice, with the company as the subject: Barada Textiles has opened a finishing plant in Homs, employing forty people.

A quotation must say something that the facts do not. We are delighted with this development is not a quotation; the plant lets us finish cloth in Syria that was previously finished in Turkey is a quotation, because it tells the reader what has changed in his trade.

Everything an editor cannot check will be cut. Figures, dates, places and named people survive; leading, significant and state-of-the-art do not, and their removal makes the announcement shorter and better.

**Grammar: news register for the trade press: the active voice and a named source**

- News register uses the active voice with the company as the subject: Barada Textiles has opened a finishing plant in Homs.
- A quotation is attributed to a named person with a title: Dr Hiba Rizk, managing director, said that the plant doubles finishing capacity.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What transfers easily in a handover, and what does not?
- What exists only in one person’s head?
- How should a customer learn that his contact has changed?

A handover transfers the file easily and the knowledge badly. The ledger, the correspondence and the open orders move in an afternoon; what the predecessor knew about each buyer does not move at all unless somebody writes it down.

Four kinds of thing exist only in one head. Promises made on the telephone, preferences that were never requirements, the person who actually decides as distinct from the person who formally decides, and the history of a quarrel that was settled.

The note for each customer is therefore short and specific: the position as at the date of transfer, what is currently outstanding, what has been promised and by whom, and what the successor must do first.

Customers are told jointly and in writing, by both the predecessor and the successor, with a date of transfer and an assurance that terms are unchanged. A customer who hears of a change from a stranger’s email has been told two things.

A joint visit or a joint telephone call to the eight or ten largest customers is worth more than the whole written handover, and it is the part that is cut when the predecessor’s notice period is short.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A company is judged by the decisions in its letters, and decisions are taken by people**

**Figure 10.5** — Five columns of mechanical work handed over, and six things that were not.

Six books have described a single method: identify the mechanical part of a task, hand it over with a six-line instruction, and spend the time saved on the part that requires a person.

What a machine cannot do for a company’s written word is therefore the last question, and it has a short answer: it cannot decide, it cannot remember a conversation, it cannot keep a habit, and it cannot be responsible.

Give it everything that can be stated as a rule. The comparison, the checklist, the classification, the chronology, the structure, the draft: all of these are better done by a system than by a tired person at six o’clock.

Keep the figure, the refusal, the concession and the signature. Each of them commits the company, and a commitment made by nobody is a commitment nobody will honour.
