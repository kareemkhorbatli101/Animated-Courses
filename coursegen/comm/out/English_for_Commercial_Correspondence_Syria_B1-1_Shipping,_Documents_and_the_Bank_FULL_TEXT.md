### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does a trade term decide?
- Who pays for carriage, and who carries the risk?
- Why must a place be named beside the term?

A trade term is a short answer to two long questions: who pays for what, and from which point the risk belongs to the buyer.

Cost and risk are not the same thing, and they do not always pass at the same place, which is where most arguments begin.

Under a cost and freight term, the seller pays the freight to the destination port. The risk, however, passes when the goods are loaded on board at the port of shipment.

So a cargo may be damaged at sea on a voyage the seller has paid for, and the loss may still be the buyer’s.

Every term must be written with a place: cost and freight Aqaba, free carrier Latakia. A term with no place names nothing and decides nothing.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the passive in shipping and banking procedure**

- Procedure is written in the passive because the actor is defined elsewhere: the goods are loaded on board; the documents are presented to the bank.
- Name the actor with by only where the term makes it the point: export clearance is arranged by the seller.
- Use is to be and must be for what a procedure requires: the credit is to be advised through our bank; documents must be presented within 21 days.
- Keep the agent out where the duty follows from the term itself, and put it in where two readings are possible.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A term is a division of responsibility, not an abbreviation**

**Figure 1.5** — The two columns differ in four of the six rows, which is the whole lesson.

Trade terms are learned as abbreviations and used as abbreviations, which is why two parties can agree a term and disagree about everything it governs.

A trade term explained in terms of who pays and who risks is a useful task to hand over, because the output is two columns rather than a paragraph of theory.

Give it the term, the named place and the route. Ask for a table with one row for each cost item and one for risk, and a column saying which party bears it.

Require the risk row to state a physical point: the ship’s rail at Latakia, the carrier’s depot, the buyer’s yard. A date is not a point and will not settle a claim.

What it cannot know is what your buyer believes. A term is a legal allocation, and a customer who thinks the seller covers everything to his door will be surprised by a correct answer.

So the explanation goes to the buyer in plain words before the order, not after a loss, and the term is written with its place in every document that follows.

> **DATA POINT · Trade terms** — International trade terms are commonly published as a set of rules that allocate cost, risk and the duty to clear goods, and each term is intended to be used with a named place.  
> Source: general international trade practice guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Named places** — Trade-term guidance commonly stresses that each term must be followed by a named place, and that the place determines where delivery occurs and where risk passes.  
> Source: general international trade practice guidance, 2025–2026.

## Unit 2 · The Bill of Lading

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: relative clauses in the description of goods and documents · AI: a bill of lading checked against the shipment it describes*

**Figure 2.0** — THE DOCUMENT THAT IS THE GOODS: Three originals issued, and the cargo belongs to whoever presents one.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is a bill of lading for?
- Who can claim the goods at the far end?
- What happens if the description does not match the invoice?

A bill of lading does three things at once. It is a receipt for goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, and a document of title.

Being a document of title is what makes it unlike every other paper in the file. Whoever presents an original at the destination can take the cargo.

The description on it must repeat the description on the invoice, which must repeat the description in the credit. A document that describes different goods describes a different shipment.

A clean bill carries no clause about damaged or defective packing. A notation that cartons were stained makes the bill unclean, and an unclean bill is usually refused by a bank.

The notation shipped on board, with a date, is what proves the goods were loaded rather than merely received for shipment.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A bill of lading is checked against the shipment, not against hope**

**Figure 2.5** — Six fields, six sources, and the same consequence for every mismatch.

A bill of lading arrives as a draft from the forwarder, and the natural response is to read it for sense. Reading it for sense is exactly what a bank will not do.

A bill of lading checked against the shipment it describes is work worth handing over, because it is a field-by-field comparison with a stated source for each field.

Give it the draft bill, the credit, the invoice and the packing list. Ask for one row per field, the value on the draft, the value in the source, and a verdict of match or mismatch.

Require character-level comparison on names and ports. Yarmuk and Yarmouk are a mismatch, and so are Lattakia and Latakia, however obvious the intention.

What it cannot judge is the remedy. A wrong consignee is corrected before sailing; an unclean notation may mean opening a container and losing a vessel.

So the mismatches come to you ranked by what they cost, and the telephone call to the forwarder is made the same day, because a draft is cheap to change and an issued original is not.

> Source: general transport documentation guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Document checking** — Banks examining documents under a credit commonly compare data field by field for consistency, and a difference in spelling of a named party may be treated as a discrepancy.

## Unit 3 · The Certificate of Origin

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: conditional obligations — provided that, subject to, unless · AI: a certificate of origin prepared for a particular market*

**Figure 3.0** — MADE IN SYRIA IS A CLAIM, AND A CLAIM NEEDS A CRITERION: One garment, four countries of material, and a single declared origin.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does a certificate of origin prove?
- Who issues one in your country?
- Why does origin change the duty a buyer pays?

A certificate of origin states where goods were produced. It is issued by an authority, commonly a chamber of commerce, on the strength of the exporter’s declaration.

Origin is not the same as the address on the invoice. Goods may be invoiced from Damascus and originate elsewhere, and customs are interested only in the second fact.

Where everything comes from one country, origin is simple: the product is wholly obtained there. Where materials are imported, a rule decides whether the work done amounts to a substantial transformation.

Duty rates often depend on origin, which is why the certificate is checked at the border and not merely filed. A wrong statement is a customs matter, not a clerical one.

Markets want different wording. One buyer’s authority requires the invoice number on the certificate; another requires a consular legalisation before the goods will be released.

**Grammar: conditional obligations: provided that, subject to, unless**

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is a discrepancy?
- Who gains when documents are refused?
- What choices does a seller have after a refusal?

A discrepancy is any difference between what the credit requires and what the documents show. The size of the difference does not change its effect.

A complying presentation obliges the bank to pay. A discrepant one does not, and the decision passes to the buyer, who may waive the discrepancy or decline.

That shift is the whole cost. The seller’s security becomes a request, and the request is made to the person whose money is at stake.

The remedies are three. Cure the discrepancy and present again within the time left; ask the buyer for a waiver; or send the documents on a collection basis and wait for payment.

Each remedy has a price in days and in fees, and the choice depends on how many days remain before the credit expires.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: comparison of documents: whereas, in contrast, on the other hand**

- Whereas joins two clauses in one sentence and marks a difference: the invoice shows 2,000 metres, whereas the credit says 1,800.
- In contrast begins a second sentence or clause and is followed by a comma: In contrast, the packing list notes damage.
- On the other hand introduces a balancing consideration rather than a flat difference: a waiver is quick; on the other hand, it depends on the buyer.
- Keep the two compared items parallel: compare a date with a date, not a date with a document.

A forwarder is an agent who arranges carriage. He books space, moves containers, lodges declarations and issues documents on instructions.

He is not a partner in your commercial judgement. Where an instruction is silent, he will choose whatever is normal for his office, and he will be entitled to.

Instructions are numbered, one action to a line, and sent in writing. A telephone call is a warning that an instruction is coming, not an instruction.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An instruction is written for a man who will not telephone you**

**Figure 6.5** — Six silences, six reasonable defaults, and not one of them chosen by you.

Instructions to a forwarder are written in a hurry, late in the day, by somebody who has the whole shipment in mind and assumes the reader does too.

Shipping instructions written for a forwarder who will not ask is good work to hand over, because completeness can be tested against a list of decisions.

Give it the order, the credit, the route and the terminal cut-off. Ask for numbered steps, one action to a line, each with a time and a place, in the order they must happen.

Require a line for every point on which a default would cost money: container type, routing, transhipment, vessel nomination, weight declaration, seal number, draft bill of lading.

What it cannot know is your forwarder. One office telephones when something looks wrong; another books what the paper says and sends an invoice, and both are within their rights.

A customs declaration states what the goods are, what they are worth, and where they come from. The classification is a code, and the code carries a duty rate.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A classification question is nine lines of description and one question**

**Figure 7.5** — Five facts and an offer; without them the enquiry comes back unanswered.

Classification is treated as a broker’s job, and the broker is usually right. When he is wrong, the penalty and the duty fall on the trader who signed the declaration.

A customs classification question put to somebody who can answer it is useful work to hand over, because the hard part is a complete description rather than an opinion.

Give it the product specification, the sample details and the headings that might apply. Ask for a description in tariff language, followed by one indirect question.

Require the description to cover composition by weight, construction, weight per square metre, finish, width and intended use. Forbid any statement of which heading we prefer.

What it cannot do is choose the body to ask. A broker’s opinion, a chamber’s view and a binding ruling from the authority are three different kinds of safety.

So the letter goes to the authority that will decide, a sample is offered rather than sent, and the reply is kept in the file with the declaration it supports.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does marine insurance cover?
- What does all risks not cover?
- How soon must a claim be notified?

Marine insurance covers loss or damage to goods in transit. The words all risks describe the breadth of the cover and not the absence of exclusions.

Four exclusions catch exporters repeatedly: insufficient packing, inherent vice of the goods, delay, and loss the insured could have prevented.

Insufficiency of packing is the commonest. If cartons collapse because they were too light for the stow, the cause is the packing and not the voyage.

A claim must be notified within the period the policy states, with a survey carried out before the goods are moved. A claim notified late is usually a claim refused.

**Grammar: exclusion and limitation: except, excluding, save for**

- Except introduces what is left out of a general statement: all risks except insufficiency of packing.

**Cover is defined by its exclusions, and exclusions are read before the voyage**

An insurance certificate is read for the sum insured and filed. The sum is the least interesting thing on it, because it is the exclusions that decide whether it is ever paid.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What charges run while a container stands still?
- Who notices first when a box stops moving?
- Why is a chronology worth building?

When a container stops moving, money starts running. Demurrage is charged for a box left at a terminal, and detention for a box kept outside it.

Both run daily and both are usually free for a short initial period, after which they accumulate without anybody sending a reminder.

The first task is not to argue. It is to establish what happened, in order, with dates: when the vessel discharged, when the documents arrived, when the broker lodged the declaration, when the question was asked.

A chronology shows the day on which the problem could have been solved for nothing. That day is almost always earlier than anybody remembers.

The second task is to separate the charges that are still running from the ones that have stopped, because a daily charge is an argument about speed and a fixed charge is an argument about liability.

**Grammar: the past perfect for a chronology of events**

- Use the past perfect for the earlier of two past events: by the time we asked, the free period had already expired.
- Use it with by the time, before, after, when and already: the vessel had discharged before the documents arrived.
- The later event stays in the past simple: nobody had told us that the box had stopped, so we telephoned on Friday.
- Do not use the past perfect for a simple sequence of equal steps; use it to mark what was already true when something else happened.

**The first hour of a hold is spent on dates, not on opinions**

When a container is held, the correspondence doubles and the facts get worse. Everybody remembers telling somebody, and the charges continue regardless.

A chronology built from emails after a container stops moving is excellent work to hand over, because it is a sorting task with a strict rule about evidence.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who reads a commercial document besides its addressee?
- What should never appear in one?
- Where does a difficult message belong instead?

Commercial documents travel. An invoice goes to a bank, a customs authority, an insurer and sometimes a court, and it is read by people who know nothing of the conversation behind it.

Four things never belong in one. A speculation about the goods or the buyer; an admission of fault; a value that is not the real value; and a comment intended for a colleague.

Difficult messages belong in a conversation or a letter of their own, not in a document that will be read beside an invoice for the next six years.

**Grammar: prohibition and absolute rules: never, under no circumstances, at no time**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A document is a durable record, and durability is the whole difficulty**

**Figure 10.5** — Five messages with a proper home, and one with no home at all.

Documents are written quickly and read slowly, often years later, by people who have no idea what was happening in the office on the day they were typed.

What must never be written into a document that travels is a question worth putting systematically, because the categories are few and stable.

Give it the draft documents for a shipment and ask for every sentence to be classified: required by the credit or the authority, harmless, or one of the four kinds that must come out.
