### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do you think happens in an embassy building every morning?

Nour Ajami arrives in Calmar on a Sunday. It rains. Her suitcase is heavy and the taxi driver speaks a language she is still learning. On Monday she walks to the Mission. She arrives at ten to eight. The door is locked.

The Mission opens at eight. A man opens the door at two minutes past. He is Firas Obeid. He runs the administration and he holds every key in the building. He looks at her and asks one question. Are you the new third secretary? She says yes. He says good, because the desk is empty and the post has been empty for five months.

The building has four floors. The chancery is on the first two floors. The consular section is on the ground floor, with its own door to the street. The Ambassador works on the third floor. The residence is in another street, twenty minutes away. Nobody explains this to Nour. She learns it by walking.

At nine o’clock she meets Dr Suhair Kanaan, the deputy head of mission. Dr Kanaan is direct. She says that the Mission has nine people. Four are posted from home. Five are locally engaged, and they know the host system better than anybody else in the building. She says Nour should learn their names today, before she learns anything else.

At eleven Nour has her first task. A letter arrives from the host ministry. It is in the local language. Nour puts it into a translation system and reads the English. The English is clear. It is also wrong in one place, and she does not know which place.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the present simple for routine and fact**

- Use the present simple for what is always true: The Mission has nine staff.
- Use it for routine: The door opens at eight. The pouch leaves on Thursdays.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Nine people, five languages, and one door that opens at eight**

**Figure 1.5** — Six fixed hours. Everything else in the day moves.

A small mission is nine people and a timetable. Four officers are posted from home. Five colleagues are engaged locally. The posted officers change every three or four years. The local colleagues often stay for twenty.

This matters more than it sounds. The memory of a mission lives with the people who do not leave. They know which office answers, which form is refused, and which number actually rings at night. A new officer who ignores them repeats every old mistake.

The day has fixed points. The building opens at eight. The consular window opens at half past eight. There is a morning meeting at nine. The post from the host ministry arrives at eleven. At five the duty phone starts, and it belongs to one named person.

Between the fixed points the day is not fixed at all. A citizen arrives without an appointment. A letter arrives in a language nobody in the room reads well. The capital asks for something by the end of the day, and the end of the day in the capital is not the end of the day here.

So the first week is not about diplomacy. It is about the building, the people and the clock. An officer who knows these three things can be useful in week two. An officer who does not is still asking where the forms are in month two.

There is one more thing the first week teaches. A letter arrives in a language you do not read, and a translation system gives you clear English in four seconds. Some of that translation you can check, because you hold the original or a colleague reads both languages. Some of it you cannot check at all, and that difference decides what you may put in writing.

> **DATA POINT · What services do first** — Published accounts of foreign services describe arrival briefings that cover the building, the people and the local legal position in the first week, because an officer who learns these by accident learns them late.  
> Source: reported practice in several foreign services, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why services brief on the building first** — Services that give new officers a written orientation in the first week report fewer procedural errors in the first quarter than services that leave arrival to chance.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic staff training, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — A translation you can check, and one you cannot

**Figure 1.7** — The left column is safe to use today. The right column needs a person.

A translation system is fast and it is often right. It reads a letter in four seconds and gives you clear English. The danger is not the speed. The danger is that the English is always clear, even when it is wrong.

So divide every translation into two kinds. The first kind you can check: you have the original, or a colleague reads both languages, or the word is a name, a date or a number. Use that translation today. The second kind you cannot check. Use it to understand, never to act, and never to quote.

In a mission this rule has a simple shape. A translation you cannot check never goes into a report, a note or a reply. It goes into your head, so that you know what to ask a person about. That is a real use, and it is not the same as trusting it.

*Say it like this*
- Name the kind: **Is this a translation I can check, or one I cannot?**
- State the check: **I have the original and Rim reads both languages.**
- State the limit: **I understand the letter. I will not quote it until it is checked.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A translation system does not know that a polite form in one language is a refusal in another, and it cannot tell you that it has guessed. It gives a clear sentence for a doubtful one, and it never says that it is unsure.

## Unit 2 · Who Does What in the Building

*CEFR A2 · Grammar: there is, there are, and prepositions of place · AI: learning a building: asking for a plain explanation*

**Figure 2.0** — AN ORGANIGRAM ON THE WALL, AND NOBODY IN THE BOXES: The corridor outside the registry, where the chart has hung since the last handover.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Is there a chart of your organisation on a wall? Is it correct?
- How do people in your office find out who signs something?
- Who in your building knows everything, and has no title at all?

There is a chart on the wall outside the registry. It is a square of eleven boxes with names under them. Nour reads it on her second morning. Two of the names are wrong. One officer left the post in March. Another is on leave until November.

There are four sections in the Mission. The chancery does the political and economic work. The consular section deals with citizens and with visas. The administration section holds the money, the keys and the building. The press and culture desk is one person, and that person is also the website.

The registry is a small room behind the chancery. There is no window in it. Every incoming letter goes through the registry first, and every outgoing letter goes through it last. Firas says that if a document is lost, it is lost in a person, not in the registry.

There is one meeting room on the first floor. There are two telephones at the front desk. There is a strong room on the ground floor, and there are three people in the building who may open it. Nour is not one of them, and she will not be one of them this year.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A chart tells you the shape of a mission; a person tells you how it actually works**

**Figure 2.5** — Four of the six rows are wrong, and the chart has hung there for two years.

Every mission has two structures. The first is on paper. It has boxes, titles and a line from each box to the box above it. The second is the one that works. It is made of people who answer the telephone, people who know the host system, and people who sign.

The paper structure is not useless. It tells you the shape of the thing and the language for it. A new officer who cannot name the sections cannot ask a sensible question. But a chart is a photograph, and it was taken some time ago.

The working structure changes with every posting. An officer leaves and a task moves. A post is frozen and the work is shared. A local colleague has done the same job for twelve years and knows a procedure that is written down nowhere at all.

A system can give you the paper structure of a mission in seconds. It is good at this, because the paper structure of missions is published in many places and is broadly the same everywhere. That is exactly why it cannot give you the second one.

So use the general answer as a set of questions, not as a set of facts. Ask the system what sections a mission usually has. Then walk down the corridor and ask a person which of them exists here, who is in it this month, and who actually signs.

Learning a building is therefore two jobs. A plain explanation from a system gives you the vocabulary and the shape of the place in four seconds, which is a real saving on a second morning. Asking a person gives you the building itself, and only the second job produces a fact you can act on this month.

> **DATA POINT · Orientation that names people, not boxes** — Services that give a new officer a current list of who signs what, rather than an organisation chart alone, report fewer misdirected approvals in an officer’s first months.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic administration, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Where new officers lose time** — Administrative reviews report that new officers lose more time to approval sent to the wrong person than to any other procedural error in the first three months at a post.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic administration, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A name is a record, and a record is only useful if it never moves**

**Figure 3.5** — Six records, two people, one officer, and four hours of somebody’s week.

A diplomatic name is not a personal matter. It is an index. Tax offices, customs, banks, security passes and the diplomatic list all use it to connect one person to one set of entitlements, and each of them copies the spelling it was first given.

This is why protocol officers are strict about something that looks trivial. The cost of a second spelling is not embarrassment. It is a duplicate record, and a duplicate record has to be unmade by a human being in an office, one system at a time.

The same is true of rank. A rank is a position in a service, and it maps to entitlements, seating and precedence. A form of address is a social act. Officers who confuse the two either sound rude or claim something they do not hold.

Systems are good at transliteration in general and bad at your name in particular. They produce the common form, which is usually reasonable and sometimes beautiful, and they have no way of knowing which form a host ministry typed into a file last March.

So the instruction is the other way round. Do not ask a system what your name is. Tell it your name, exactly as accredited, and ask it to place that spelling correctly in a sentence, a letter or a form of address in the host language.

> **DATA POINT · One spelling, for the whole posting** — Protocol offices commonly advise that an officer fix one transliteration of a name at accreditation and use it unchanged for the whole tour, because duplicate entries on a diplomatic list create duplicate records in tax, customs and bank systems.  
> Source: reported protocol practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why duplicates are expensive** — Administrative reports describe duplicate personal records as among the slowest errors to correct at a post, because each downstream system must be amended separately and by hand.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic administration, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A form is a set of questions somebody else wrote, and every field is a decision**

**Figure 4.5** — Nineteen fields can be drafted in seconds. Three decide what the file is.

An application form looks like typing. It is not. A form is a list of questions written by an office that will use the answers to put your file into one category rather than another, and the categories carry different rights.

Most fields are simply copying. A passport number, a date of birth, an address, a post, a rank: these exist already in documents. Copying them is slow, boring work, and it is exactly the kind of work a system does quickly and accurately.

A few fields are not copying at all. Purpose of stay, category of permit, and the description of a dependant are legal questions. The answer decides which rules apply. Getting one of them wrong does not produce a small error; it produces the wrong file.

This is the useful division. A system may fill any field whose answer is already written in a document you have given it. A system may not choose a category, because the right category depends on law and on practice at this ministry this year.

So ask for the nineteen and refuse the three. Give the documents, ask the system to fill only from them, and ask it to list every field it could not fill from a document. That list is short, and it is the part of the form that actually needs you.

> **DATA POINT · Why category fields are kept human** — Services that allow drafting assistance on administrative forms commonly restrict it to fields copied from source documents, and require an officer to complete category and status fields personally.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Local rules are the fastest thing to ask about and the easiest thing to get wrong**

**Figure 5.5** — Six answers, four of them honest, and only two of them worth acting on.

A new officer needs a hundred small local facts in the first month. How much is a deposit? Is a guarantor normal? What notice must you give? How is a utility account transferred? These are not diplomatic questions, but they cost real money and real weeks.

A system answers all of them immediately, and the answers are usually of the right shape. That shape is the useful part. It tells you what the normal range is, so that you can notice when somebody is asking for something outside it.

What a system cannot give you is the date. Local administrative rules change often and quietly, and a confident answer may describe the position two years ago. Nothing in the reply marks it as old, because the reply has no idea that it is old.

So treat the answer as a reason to ask, never as a basis to sign. Ask one person who lives under the rule, and ask for the thing a system cannot produce: an article number, a form name, a page, a dated notice on an office wall.

This habit has a second benefit. It gives you something to say at the counter that is not an argument. Asking which article applies is polite, specific and hard to refuse, and it turns a disagreement about confidence into a question about a document.

> **DATA POINT · Where new arrivals lose money** — Administrative reports from missions commonly identify housing deposits, notice periods and utility transfer charges as the three places where arriving officers most often pay more than the local rule requires.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic administration, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why dates matter more than answers** — Guidance to officers posted abroad commonly stresses that a general answer about a local rule should be verified against a dated source before any payment or signature, because administrative rules are amended frequently and without publicity.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic administration, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Practical local information, and how to check it

**Figure 5.7** — The left column acts on an answer. The right column uses it to make a question.

Local administrative information is the easiest thing to ask a system for and the hardest kind of answer to rely on. It is easy because the question is short and common. It is hard because the right answer depends on where you are and on what month it is.

So take the answer as a shape, not as a fact. If a system says the usual deposit is two months, you have learned that three is above the normal range. That is genuinely useful at a kitchen table with an agent, and it costs four seconds.

Then check it against something with a date on it. An article number, a form, a notice on an office wall, a colleague who pays this rule every month. Ask for the dated thing before you sign, and ask for it politely, because you will meet the same agent again.

Most calls need no record. Hours, addresses, which day the window opens: these are answered and forgotten. The calls that need a record are the ones where somebody will act later, and those are the calls where a missing detail costs hours.

A message slip has five parts. Who called. When. A number that works. What they want. What was promised. The fifth part is the one officers skip, and it is the one that decides whether the Mission has kept its word.

Systems can now listen to a recorded call and write a clear summary in seconds, in good English, better organised than most people write by hand at a busy counter. For the substance of an enquiry this is genuinely useful and saves real time.

The weakness is exactly where the risk is. Digits, spellings of names and place names in an unfamiliar accent are the items a system is most likely to get wrong, and it writes a guessed digit in the same confident type as a certain one.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — A telephone message written down correctly

**Figure 6.7** — The system is better at the paragraph. You are better at the seven digits.

A recorded call and a short written summary is a real improvement on a hand-written slip at a busy desk. The summary is clear, it is in order, and it does not lose the third thing the caller mentioned while you were writing down the first.

But a message has two different kinds of content. There is the substance, which can be said in several ways and still be right. And there are the exact items — digits, names, addresses — where one wrong character makes the whole message useless.

So split the work. Let the system write the substance. Take the exact items yourself, out loud, by reading them back to the caller while they are still on the line. Then type them into the slip with your own fingers, and mark anything you could not confirm.

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A transcription system does not know when it has misheard a digit, and it cannot ask the caller to repeat anything. It writes an uncertain number in exactly the same confident form as a certain one, so nothing on the page shows the difference.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A capital triages by the first line, and a draft always sounds more certain than a corridor**

**Figure 7.5** — Six subject lines, and ten hours of difference in when they were read.

A message from a post competes with ninety others. The desk officer who receives it has to decide in four seconds whether it needs attention this morning or this week. That decision is made on the subject line, and nothing else.

So a subject line is not a title. It is an instruction about priority. The best ones say three things: whether action is needed, by whom, and by when. No action is a perfectly good thing to say, and it is a kindness to say it.

The body has a second problem. Reporting a conversation means separating three things: what you saw, what you were told, and what you concluded. These get mixed together very easily, and a reader in a capital cannot unmix them.

A drafting system makes this harder, in a way that is easy to miss. It writes fluent, confident prose. Fluent, confident prose smooths over hedges. Hoped becomes expected. Suggested becomes said. A corridor remark becomes a departmental position.

The remedy is small and dull. Read the draft against your notes, not against your memory of the meeting, and check every verb that carries certainty. If you took no notes, say so in the message, because that is information too.

> **DATA POINT · What a capital reads first** — Reporting guidance in several services asks officers to put the decision needed, the date and the source of a claim in the first two lines of any message, because desks triage by opening line rather than by length.  
> Source: reported reporting practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why hedges matter in reporting** — Reporting guidance commonly requires officers to distinguish observed fact, reported statement and personal assessment in separate sentences, because a reader in a capital has no way to recover the distinction once it is lost.  
> Source: reported reporting practice, 2025–2026.

**A diary is a map of two countries, and a scheduling system has only seen the clock**

A mission lives between two working weeks. The capital and the host country rarely rest on the same days, and the overlap can be as little as three days. Add a time difference and the overlap becomes a handful of hours in the middle of each of those days.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Two sources that agree may be one source, copied twice**

**Figure 9.5** — Six entries, two still correct, and nobody had ever written a date beside any of them.

A new officer needs the city quickly: where things are, how long they take, which door to use. A map system answers all of this instantly, and most of the time it is right, which is exactly what makes the occasional wrong answer dangerous.

The weakness is not the map. It is the age of the information behind it. Public registers are updated slowly. A police station that closed, an office that moved one street, a clinic that no longer treats foreign patients: these change quietly and nothing announces it.

There is a second trap. When two sources agree, people treat that as confirmation. Often the two sources are the same source twice, because one copied the other some years ago. Agreement proves common ancestry, not truth.

For an officer this matters most in the lists a mission publishes. A citizen in trouble at midnight does not test an address. He walks to it, and if it is an empty building he is alone, in the dark, in a city he does not know.

So every public list carries a date and a method. Verified by telephone on 4 November. Verified by visit on 12 January. An entry with no date beside it is not information; it is a rumour that somebody printed.

> **DATA POINT · Why address lists are checked on a date** — Consular guidance commonly requires any public list of local emergency addresses to carry the date on which it was last verified, because a closed police station or clinic on a published list sends a person in difficulty to an empty building.  
> Source: reported consular practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Agreement is not verification** — Open-source verification guidance commonly warns that independent-looking sources frequently share a single origin, so agreement between them is not evidence unless the origins are known to differ.  
> Source: reported open-source verification practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Directions, maps and information that is out of date

**Figure 9.7** — Streets change slowly. Institutions change quietly, and nothing announces it.

A map system is excellent for geography and poor for institutions. Streets, distances and journey times are stable, well recorded and checked by millions of people. Which door of a government building is open at night is none of those things.

The same is true of any general answer about a service: opening hours, whether a station still operates, whether a clinic takes foreign patients. These are the facts that change without publicity, and they are precisely the facts a citizen in trouble needs.

So use it for directions and for maps, and check the destination with a human being. Any piece of local information that sends a person to a door may be out of date. The test is simple: if the answer sends somebody to a door, telephone the door. If you are going to print the answer and pin it on a wall, walk to the door yourself.

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A map system does not know when an office moved or closed, because that information often reaches public registers months late or not at all. It will give a confident route to an empty building and nothing in the answer shows its age.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A first report is worth having because it is early, and dangerous for exactly the same reason**

**Figure 10.5** — Eleven real conversations, and four empty rows that are the most informative part.

A first report has a quality that no later report has. The writer still notices things. After a year the strange has become normal and cannot be seen at all, so a service that does not ask for a first report loses that information permanently.

It is also the least reliable report an officer will ever write. Everything in it rests on a handful of conversations, and at a new post those conversations are almost always arranged by somebody else, for reasons of their own.

The fix is not to write less. It is to show the shape of the sample. Eleven people. Nine through one introduction. None outside the capital. None who disagree with the department. A reader can then weigh the judgement properly.

The same applies to a press summary. A system can read forty articles in two minutes and group them honestly by subject. What it will not volunteer is that the forty come from three publishers, two of which belong to one group.

So ask it the second question every time. How many sources? Who owns them? What did you not read? The answers take four seconds, and they turn a confident summary into evidence with a known shape.

> **DATA POINT · First reports and their limits** — Reporting guidance commonly asks a newly arrived officer to state, in a first assessment, how many interlocutors the judgement rests on and how they were met, because early access at a post is usually arranged by one channel.  
> Source: reported reporting practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Sample before conclusion** — Analytical guidance commonly requires an assessment to state the number and the variety of sources it rests on before it states the conclusion, so that a reader can discount a narrow base without discarding the work.  
> Source: reported analytical practice, 2025–2026.
