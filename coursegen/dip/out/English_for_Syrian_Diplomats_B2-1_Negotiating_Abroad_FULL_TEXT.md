### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is the difference between being told what to achieve and being told what to say?
- How do you work out what your own capital actually wants?
- When is it right to ask for a new instruction rather than interpret an old one?

An instruction is the shortest document in diplomacy and the one on which everything rests. It states what the capital wants, what it will not accept, and occasionally, if the desk officer has had time, why. The third of these is worth more than the other two together, and it is the one most often missing.

Hala Darwish has eleven lines for a negotiation that will run for three days. Four of the eleven concern tone. Two state outcomes that must be secured. Three state outcomes that cannot be accepted. The remaining two say that she should use her judgement, which is either generous or evasive depending on how the week goes.

An instruction is not a position. A position is what the delegation says in the room, in its own words, at a level of detail the instruction never reaches. Converting the one into the other is the work, and it cannot be done by expanding the words.

On Sunday she gives the instruction to a system and asks for a full negotiating position. Ninety lines come back in four minutes, organised by article, fluent and internally consistent, covering every clause of the draft text including eleven the instruction never mentions.

Every one of the ninety lines is plausible. Perhaps sixty follow from the instruction. The rest are reasonable inventions, written in the same confident register, and in a room at ten the next morning there is no way to tell the two apart.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: modals of deduction: must, may, might, cannot**

- must + infinitive: you are nearly certain. They must have seen the draft.
- may and might + infinitive: it is possible; might is slightly weaker.
- cannot + infinitive: you are nearly certain it is false. That cannot be what they meant.
- For the past, add have + past participle: must have known, cannot have known.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An instruction gives you an outcome; only the reason behind it lets you defend it when the text moves**

**Figure 1.5** — Two instructed elements, one sensible inference, and a great deal of fluent invention.

The instruction is the hinge of the whole system. A capital that cannot write a clear one sends a delegation into a room with nothing to hold, and a delegation that cannot read one carefully converts ambiguity into commitment at a speed nobody intended.

What an instruction gives is an outcome: secure this, resist that, do not accept the other. What it usually fails to give is the interest behind the outcome, and the interest is what a delegate actually needs, because the text will not stay where it was when the instruction was written.

Turning an instruction into a position is therefore interpretation, and a drafting system is very good at it in the way that is most dangerous. Given eleven lines and a draft treaty it produces ninety lines of coherent, well-ordered, fluent position in minutes.

Roughly two-thirds of those lines will follow from the instruction. The remaining third will be reasonable extensions of it, invented to fill clauses the capital never considered, and written in exactly the same confident register as the instructed parts.

The remedy is mechanical and takes twenty minutes. Mark every line as instructed, inferred or invented. Send the inferred list to the capital and ask for confirmation. Keep the invented list for yourself, and never say any of it in a room as though it were the position of a government.

> **DATA POINT · Why instructions carry a reason** — Negotiating practice commonly requires that an instruction state the interest behind the position as well as the position itself, because a delegate who knows the interest can protect it when the text moves and a delegate who knows only the words cannot.  
> Source: reported negotiating practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Marking the provenance of a position** — Delegation practice commonly requires each element of a negotiating position to be traceable to an instruction, with inferred elements identified and referred for confirmation before they are stated in a negotiation.  
> Source: reported negotiating practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Turning an instruction into a position you can hold

**Figure 1.7** — The generation is four minutes. The marking is twenty, and it is the position.

Ask for the draft, and ask explicitly for the list of clauses on which the instruction is silent. That second list is the valuable one: it is the map of everything you will be asked about in the room and have no authority to answer, and it can be sent to the capital on Sunday night rather than discovered on Wednesday afternoon.

Then mark the draft line by line. Instructed, where a paragraph of the instruction can be cited. Inferred, where it follows but is not stated. Invented, where it is a reasonable position that nobody has authorised. The three categories look identical on the page, which is exactly why they must be written on it.

And ask the capital one question that instructions rarely answer: what is this for? A delegate who knows the interest can accept a different article, a protocol or a declaration and still protect it. A delegate who knows only the words will defend the words, win them, and lose the thing the words were there to secure.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for the gaps: **List every clause on which my instruction is silent.**
- Mark the provenance: **Instructed, inferred, invented — line by line.**
- Ask for the interest: **What is the reference in article 4 intended to protect?**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A drafting system cannot distinguish what your capital has authorised from what follows plausibly from it, because both are consistent with the same eleven lines. It writes authority and invention in one register, and the page does not show which is which.

## Unit 2 · The Bilateral Meeting

*CEFR B2 · Grammar: conditionals in negotiation: unless, provided that, failing that · AI: preparing the other side’s case against your own*

**Figure 2.0** — THE ARGUMENT THEY ACTUALLY MADE WAS NOT ON ANY OF THE ELEVEN PAGES: A bilateral room in Rhene, where two delegations of four sit down for ninety minutes.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you prepare for a meeting with somebody whose position you dislike?
- What is the strongest argument against your own case?
- Where do the real objections in a negotiation usually come from?

A bilateral meeting is ninety minutes long and produces, at best, one agreed sentence; at worst it produces a cordial exchange of positions that both sides already knew, after which everybody says that the atmosphere was good, which is what delegations say when nothing has happened.

**Grammar: conditionals in negotiation: unless, provided that, failing that**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**You can generate every published argument in four minutes, and the real objection is rarely published**

**Figure 2.5** — Eleven anticipated arguments, two of them used, and the one that mattered was not among them.

Preparing the other side’s case is the oldest discipline in negotiation and the one most often skipped, because it is uncomfortable, time-consuming and produces a document that argues against your own government, which nobody enjoys drafting on a Friday afternoon.

A system removes the discomfort and the time. Given your position and everything the other party has published, it will produce eleven pages of well-constructed opposing argument in four minutes, ranked by how damaging each would be, and some of it will be better than what they will actually say.

That is genuinely valuable, and it changes what a delegate can do: an opening statement can be written to answer the strongest objection before it is made, which is worth more in a ninety-minute meeting than any amount of fluency later.

What such a document cannot contain is anything that has not been written down, and the decisive obstacle in a bilateral relationship is very often exactly that: a meeting handled badly, a letter unanswered for eleven months, a minister kept waiting in 2019.

So prepare the published case with a system and the unpublished one with a telephone. Ask a long-serving local colleague, a predecessor, or the interpreter who has sat in every meeting for a decade, one question: what are they still annoyed about, and when did it happen?

> **DATA POINT · Why the real objection is often unpublished** — Negotiating practice commonly distinguishes between stated positions, which are documented and can be anticipated, and underlying grievances, which are frequently unrecorded and which determine how readily a counterpart will move.  
> Source: reported negotiating practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · The unwritten obstacle** — Practitioners commonly report that the principal resistance in a bilateral file is more often a historical grievance about process than a disagreement of substance, and that such grievances are seldom recorded in either side’s documents.  
> Source: reported negotiating practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Preparing the other side’s case against your own

**Figure 2.7** — Four minutes for the published case; one telephone call for the one that is not.

Ask for the case against yourself, explicitly and without softening the instruction: the strongest arguments the other party could make, drawn from what they have published, ranked by the damage each would do if it were made well, with the weakest point of your own text identified separately.

**Grammar: nominalisation in a written position**

- A nominalisation turns a verb into a noun: decide becomes the decision, consult becomes consultation, withdraw becomes withdrawal.
- Written positions use it to compress several clauses into one formal sentence.
- The danger is that the actor disappears: a decision was taken says nothing about who took it.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A shortlist from a delegation list is fast and well reasoned, and it cannot know who answers his own post**

**Figure 3.5** — Five names from a published list, four of them right, and the useful sixth found by a colleague.

A published delegation list is exactly the kind of document a system handles well: two hundred and eleven names with titles and delegations, which can be cross-referenced against your own live files and ranked by relevance in a few minutes, with a stated reason for every name.

That is a real gain in a two-day conference, because the alternative is a week of reading or, more commonly, meeting whoever happens to be standing in the coffee queue, which is how most margins programmes are actually assembled.

The limit is not the reasoning but the record. A list gives titles, and a title is a claim about authority that is often untrue: the head of delegation who delegates everything, the deputy who decides, the adviser who drafts the text everybody then argues about.

None of that is published anywhere, and it is known instead by the people who have sat in the room: a long-serving colleague, an interpreter, a predecessor, the officer at another Mission who dealt with the same delegation last year.

So take the ranked shortlist, which is good, and put one question to a person before the requests go in: of these five, who answers his own correspondence, and who should I be asking for instead?

> **DATA POINT · Why the margins matter** — Conference practice commonly holds that the formal outcome of a large multilateral meeting is largely settled in advance, and that the incremental value of attendance therefore lies in bilateral contact at the margins.  
> Source: reported conference practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Title and authority** — Diplomatic practice commonly notes that the formal seniority shown on a delegation list is an unreliable guide to who holds decision-making authority on a particular file, and that this information is transmitted informally rather than published.  
> Source: reported conference practice, 2025–2026.

A memorandum of understanding is not a treaty, which is precisely why it is agreed so often: it records what two administrations intend to do, it binds nobody in law, and it can be signed by officials without the procedures that a binding instrument requires.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A comparison finds every difference between two texts, and cannot tell you which one removes the obligation**

**Figure 4.5** — Complete, accurate, and ordered by where the words sit rather than by what they do.

Comparing two versions of a long text is work that a system does better than a tired official at six in the evening: it will find every insertion, deletion and substitution in eleven pages in under a minute, and it will not miss the comma, the capital letter or the word removed from a title.

That completeness is the point. The traditional method is to read both texts twice and hope, and the traditional result is that one change in twenty is missed, which over four months of exchanges is how a text drifts away from what either side thought it had agreed.

What a comparison cannot do is weigh. It reports a substitution in paragraph 6 and a comma in paragraph 2 in the same register and in the order they appear, because significance is not a property of the text: it is a property of the relationship, the file and the law.

So the list is the beginning of the task. An official reads all twenty-three items, marks the three that change what either side must do, and writes those three up with the paragraph numbers and the exact words, flat, without adjectives.

It is the ranking that is the diplomatic work, and it is the ranking that nobody else can do for you, because it requires knowing what your administration can concede and what it cannot.

> **DATA POINT · What a memorandum of understanding is** — A memorandum of understanding is commonly described as an instrument recording the intentions of the parties without creating obligations enforceable in law, which makes the precise wording of its commitments a matter of practical rather than legal weight.  
> Source: general treaty practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Shall, will and may** — Drafting practice commonly treats shall and will as expressing commitment and may as expressing permission or possibility, so that substituting may for shall in an operative paragraph removes the commitment while leaving the sentence grammatically intact.  
> Source: general drafting practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An estimate built from published positions is honest about what it does not know, and it still cannot hear a corridor**

**Figure 5.5** — Nine votes, thirty-two expectations and ten blanks, presented in one table of fifty-one.

A vote estimate is the clearest case in this book of a system doing a useful thing well and labelling its own limits honestly: fifty-one delegations assessed against every published statement and comparable vote, with the reasoning shown and the ten unknowns marked as unknown rather than filled in.

That last behaviour is worth noticing, because the temptation it resists is exactly the one a delegate under pressure feels: to produce a full table of fifty-one, because a full table looks like knowledge and a table with blanks looks like failure.

While the estimate is sound in its method, it is built from one kind of material only. Everything in it is public, and a voting intention is not public: it is communicated privately, conditionally, late, and frequently with an if attached that nobody writes down.

The two delegations that would abstain if the funding amendment carried had said so in a corridor, to a third delegation, which mentioned it to a desk officer in another capital. No corpus contains that sentence, and no reasoning could have recovered it.

So keep two columns on your tally sheet, and never let them merge: what we have been told, with the name and date of who told us, and what we expect, with the reason. The first column is a count. The second is a hypothesis.

> **DATA POINT · Inferred and declared positions** — Multilateral practice commonly distinguishes between a declared voting intention, communicated by a delegation to another, and an inferred position derived from public statements or past voting behaviour, the latter being substantially less reliable in a contested vote.  
> Source: reported multilateral practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · The conditional vote** — Negotiating accounts commonly observe that voting commitments in contested multilateral proceedings are frequently conditional on the fate of prior amendments, and that such conditions are usually communicated orally rather than in writing.  
> Source: reported multilateral practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Counting votes without pretending to know

A demarche is a formal representation made on instruction: a government decides what is to be said, writes it down, and sends it to a Mission with a direction to deliver it, at a stated level, by a stated time.

The officer who delivers it is not the author and is not invited to be. The words are the instruction, the instruction is the position of a state, and an officer who varies the words has, in a small but entirely real way, made foreign policy without authority.

The instruction that reaches Bassel Tannous is nine sentences long, written in the flat and slightly archaic register that such cables use, and he has forty minutes before his appointment at the ministry.

He asks a system to turn it into talking points he can deliver naturally, and what comes back is genuinely better English: six sentences instead of nine, warmer, clearer, and arranged so that the main point arrives first.

Two things have changed. We expect the authorities to respond by 30 April has become we hope to hear from the authorities soon, and the sentence recording that this is the third such representation since January is not there at all.

- Is to issues an instruction, usually from above: the Mission is to deliver at ministerial level.
- May grants a permission that can be used or not: either Party may propose an amendment.
- These three are not interchangeable, and in a cable or a text each tells the reader exactly how much freedom they have.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A system will make an instruction read better, and reading better is not what an instruction is for**

**Figure 6.5** — Four changes of substance in a rewrite nobody asked for and everybody would call an improvement.

Asking a system to turn a stiff cable into speakable talking points is an obvious and reasonable thing to do, and it works: the output is shorter, warmer, better ordered and easier to deliver without reading from a page, which matters in a room.

The difficulty is that the instruction was not badly written. It was written flatly on purpose, by somebody who chose expect rather than hope, who put a date in, and who included a sentence whose only function is to tell the other government that a tally is being kept.

Every one of those choices is a choice about policy rather than style, and a model asked to improve the English has no way of knowing which flat sentence is flat by accident and which is flat because a legal adviser spent an hour on it.

So the rule is narrow and absolute: a system may help you deliver a demarche, and it may not draft one. Use it to make a layout you can speak from, to check your pronunciation of a name, to anticipate the four questions you will be asked, and to draft your record afterwards.

What it must never do is produce the sentences you say. Those are nine, they came from a capital, they are to be delivered in that order, and the only thing you contribute is the tone in which you say them and the accuracy of what you write down afterwards.

> **DATA POINT · What a demarche is** — Diplomatic practice commonly describes a demarche as a representation delivered on instruction, in which the delivering officer is expected to convey the substance as instructed and to report the response, rather than to negotiate or reformulate.  
> Source: general diplomatic practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Reporting a response** — Practice commonly requires the delivering officer to report the counterpart’s response in the counterpart’s own terms, since characterisations such as positive or receptive introduce the officer’s assessment into what is intended as a record.  
> Source: general diplomatic practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — A demarche delivered exactly as instructed

**Figure 6.7** — Everything on the right changes a state’s position. Everything on the left helps you deliver it.

Use a system for the preparation around a demarche, which is substantial: a readable layout of the instructed sentences, the pronunciation of two names, a list of the questions a counterpart is likely to ask, and a draft of the delivery record once you have your notes.

Do not use it on the sentences themselves, and if you have already pasted the cable in and read a smoother version, discard that version completely rather than trying to decide which of its changes were harmless. The comparison is work, and the instructed text already exists.

Afterwards, write the response in the counterpart’s own words, with the words he used, before you write anything about his manner. If he said the matter is under consideration, that is the record. He was receptive is your opinion, and it is to be kept out of a document the capital will act on.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A system will tell you which premises have failed, and then offer you an authority it does not have**

**Figure 7.5** — Four premises, two of them dead by breakfast, and one suggestion nobody in the capital has read.

Checking an instruction against a new document is one of the most useful things a system does, and it is useful precisely because it is mechanical: set out the premises, read the new text, and say which premises the new text contradicts, with paragraph references for each.

Done by hand under time pressure, this is the step most often skipped. An officer reads a new decree, forms a general impression that the situation has changed, and walks into the room with a vague unease rather than a list of two failed premises and their paragraph numbers.

The list is reportable. A flash telegram saying premises 3 and 4 of the instruction of 12 May no longer hold, see paragraphs 2 and 7 of the decree, is something a desk officer can act on in ten minutes, whereas the situation has changed is something nobody can act on at all.

What follows the list is where the trouble begins, because a system asked what to do next will answer, and it will answer well. It will propose a reformulated line that accounts for the decree, and the proposal will be intelligent, proportionate and entirely without authority.

Such a suggestion is not an instruction, and in a negotiation the distinction is the whole of the officer’s discipline: the analysis goes in the telegram, the suggestion goes nowhere, and in the room the officer takes note, reserves the position and reverts.

> **DATA POINT · Premises and instructions** — Practice commonly recognises that an instruction rests on an assessment of circumstances at the time of drafting, and that an officer who finds those circumstances materially changed is expected to report and seek further direction rather than to adapt the instruction locally.  
> Source: general diplomatic practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Reserving a position** — Negotiating practice commonly treats an express reservation of position, accompanied by an undertaking to revert, as the standard means by which a delegation avoids being bound while awaiting instructions.  
> Source: reported negotiating practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A change log tells you when a phrase entered a text, and the text does not know who asked for it**

**Figure 8.5** — Every change correctly dated and placed, and every attribution the same and useless.

Comparing fourteen versions of eleven paragraphs by hand is a day’s work and nobody does it, which is why disputes about when a phrase entered a text are usually settled by whoever remembers most confidently rather than by evidence.

A system settles them properly. Give it all fourteen files and it will tell you that where practicable appeared in version 7 along with two other insertions in the same paragraph, and that shall became should in version 4 and was restored in version 5.

That is genuinely new information in a drafting group, and it changes an argument: a delegation claiming a phrase was there from the beginning can be shown, with version numbers, that it arrived on the third day.

Attribution is a different matter entirely. Versions are circulated by a secretariat, so a log built from the files attributes every change to whoever sent the file, and the real proposer spoke for forty seconds in a room and is recorded nowhere.

Worse, up to a third of what a drafting group settles is settled outside the text: over lunch, in a corridor, between four of nine delegations. None of that is in any version, and the only record is the note an officer makes the same evening, with the names.

> **DATA POINT · Rolling texts and attribution** — Multilateral drafting practice commonly works through successive versions of a rolling text, in which proposals may be made orally and incorporated by a chair or secretariat, with the result that the text itself does not record which delegation proposed a given change.  
> Source: reported multilateral drafting practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · The unwritten understanding** — Accounts of drafting practice commonly note that significant parts of a negotiated outcome may be agreed informally among a subset of delegations and incorporated later, so that the written record of versions does not reflect when or by whom agreement was reached.  
> Source: reported multilateral drafting practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A draft written from your notes can only flatter you, because your notes are what you did**

**Figure 9.5** — Six elements, all of them true in both columns, and only one column is of any use.

Drafting a reporting telegram at nine in the evening after eleven hours in a room is work that a system genuinely improves. The sequence comes out clean, the chronology is right, the English is better than tired English, and forty minutes is enough.

The flattery is not deliberate and it is not a fault of the system. It follows from the inputs: a draft produced from your dictated notes describes a day as you experienced it, and you experienced yourself pressing firmly, because you felt firm at the time.

The same mechanism produces the passive. It was accepted that the period would be four months is a natural way to narrate your own concession, and it is the single most common way a report conceals a concession from the capital that will have to live with it.

The structural gap is larger. A draft from your notes cannot contain what you did not say. The point you forgot, the question you did not ask, the reservation you meant to enter at six o’clock: none of it exists in any input, and all of it is what your capital needs.

So draft with a system and then edit against three questions: who is the agent of every sentence, which words describe my feelings rather than my acts, and what is missing because I failed to do it. The third question is the one that makes a report worth sending.

> **DATA POINT · What a report is for** — Reporting practice commonly holds that the value of a negotiation report lies in its usefulness to those who must decide next steps, which requires an explicit account of positions not achieved and of commitments given, separated from the officer’s own assessment.  
> Source: general diplomatic reporting practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Omission and acquiescence** — Negotiating practice commonly treats a position not stated and not reserved as liable to be read by the other party as acquiescence, which is why reports are expected to record points that were not raised as well as those that were.  
> Source: reported negotiating practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A list of agreed points is only as good as its quotation marks**

**Figure 10.5** — Four points that exist in two records, and two that exist in one notebook.

Producing a list of agreed conclusions from two sets of notes is useful, and it is useful in a very specific way: it shows you quickly which of your points appear in the other side’s account and which appear only in yours, which is the distinction that matters.

The trap is the writing. A system asked for a list of agreed points will produce good prose, and good prose paraphrases: it tidies will consult, where practicable into agreed to consult, drops a qualifier that was fought over for two days, and does so in a register that sounds more official than the original.

In a negotiation a paraphrase of an agreement is a new agreement, and it is always a new agreement in your own favour, because the qualifiers that get tidied away are the ones the other side insisted on and you resented.

So instruct the opposite: every agreed point is to be quoted exactly from the text or the summary in which it appears, with its source named, and nothing is to be reworded for readability. A list of nine awkward quotations is worth more than a page of elegant prose.

Then write the confirming letter the same night, quoting the agreed words, and put anything that exists only in your own record in a separate paragraph with a request that it be confirmed. What comes back, or does not, tells you what you actually have.

> **DATA POINT · Agreed conclusions** — Practice commonly treats a point as agreed only where both parties hold the same form of words, which is why delegations exchange agreed minutes or confirming letters rather than relying on each side’s own record of a discussion.  
> Source: general diplomatic practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Confirming letters** — It is common practice for a delegation to send a confirming letter shortly after a round, quoting the wording it understands to have been agreed, so that any divergence of recollection emerges immediately rather than months later.  
> Source: general diplomatic practice literature, 2025–2026.
