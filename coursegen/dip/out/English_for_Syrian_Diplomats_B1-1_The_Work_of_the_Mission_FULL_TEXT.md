### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What happens in your country when more people arrive than an office can serve?
- How do you decide which of two waiting people is dealt with first?

The consular section in Ostport has been open for nineteen years and has never once begun a Monday without a queue. Four officers and two locally engaged colleagues deal with about eighty-one people a day, and with three hundred messages a week.

Most of the work is routine and important. Passports, documents, certificates, questions about residence. These are not emergencies, but each of them matters enormously to the person asking, and an officer who forgets that stops being any use within a month.

A small number of cases are different. A citizen in hospital. A citizen arrested. A missing person. A family that has run out of money and cannot go home. These must reach an officer within minutes, not within the normal order of the queue.

Bassel Tannous has run the section for two years. He says the whole job is triage: deciding, in seconds and with very little information, which of the eighty-one people in front of you is the one who cannot wait until Thursday.

Since March the section has used a system to sort incoming messages. It reads three hundred a week, groups them by subject and marks the ones that look urgent. It is fast, consistent and never tired, and it has been right about the routine cases every time.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: the present perfect for a situation that continues**

- Use the present perfect with for and since for something still true: He has run the section for two years.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Sorting by words is fast and reliable, and distress does not always arrive in words**

**Figure 1.5** — Two hundred and eighty-three sorted perfectly. The fourteen that mattered were sorted worst.

Triage is the oldest idea in consular work. Deal with the person whose problem will get worse before you deal with the person whose problem will not. It sounds obvious and it is very hard to do at a counter with eighty people waiting and a service standard to meet.

Sorting incoming messages is exactly the kind of repetitive, high-volume work that a system does better than a tired human being on a Friday. Three hundred messages a week, grouped by subject, with the apparently urgent ones marked, in seconds and without fatigue.

On routine traffic the accuracy is excellent. Passport, certificate, registration, appointment: these arrive in predictable language and are sorted correctly almost every time, which frees several hours a week for the cases that need judgement.

The weakness appears exactly where the stakes are highest. A detention message written calmly by a relative in another country, in good English, at midday, does not look urgent in its words. A frightened person often writes less, not more.

So add a rule the system cannot infer. Any message mentioning a police station, a hospital, a court or a child is read by an officer the same day, whatever the sorting says. That is about two minutes a day, and it is the whole value of the section.

> **DATA POINT · What triage is for** — Consular services commonly operate a formal triage step at first contact, on the basis that the cost of delaying a case involving detention, hospitalisation or a missing person is categorically different from the cost of delaying a document application.  
> Source: reported consular practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why sorting is kept under review** — Services that use automated sorting for incoming consular traffic commonly retain a rule that any message containing defined high-risk terms is read by an officer regardless of how it has been classified.  
> Source: reported consular practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Triage at the window: sorting enquiries in seconds

**Figure 1.7** — The left column is volume. The right column is why the section exists.

Give the sorting away and keep the reading. A system groups three hundred messages a week by subject in seconds, marks the obvious duplicates and leaves the officers with an ordered pile instead of an inbox. On routine traffic it is more consistent than people are.

Then write the override rule yourself, in words a system can apply without judgement. Any message that names a police station, a hospital, a court or a child is read by an officer the same day, whatever category it has been given.

The reason is simple and it is the heart of consular work. Sorting works on language, and the people in the worst trouble often write the calmest messages. A brother writing from another country at midday does not sound like an emergency, and may be one.

*Say it like this*
- Give it the volume: **Group these by subject and mark the duplicates.**
- Write the override: **Any message naming a police station or a hospital comes to me.**
- Keep the judgement: **I decide what is urgent; you decide what is similar.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A sorting system classifies by the words in a message, so it cannot see distress that is not in the words. A calm message about a detention looks routine, and nothing in the classification shows how close the decision was.

## Unit 2 · A Citizen in Trouble

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: defining relative clauses · AI: the case that must reach a person immediately*

**Figure 2.0** — THE CASE THAT CANNOT WAIT UNTIL THURSDAY: The interview room behind the counter, where the first eleven minutes of a case are decided.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What can a consulate actually do for a citizen who has been arrested?
- What would you most want somebody to find out first on your behalf?
- How do you take information from a person who is frightened?

Consular assistance is often misunderstood. A consulate cannot get a citizen out of prison, cannot pay a fine, cannot act as a lawyer and cannot ask for treatment that local nationals do not receive. What it can do is narrow, and it matters enormously.

It can visit. It can tell a family. It can give a list of local lawyers. It can make sure that the person knows their rights in a language they understand, and that the authorities know a foreign state is watching the case.

The first eleven minutes decide a detention case. There are six facts that an officer must leave the room with: the full name, the date of birth, where the person is held, when they were taken, whether a charge has been made, and who may be told.

The last of the six is the one that is forgotten. A consulate may not tell a family anything without the detained person’s written authority, and a frightened relative on the telephone will not understand why.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Assistance is narrow, and the first hour is where the whole case is decided**

**Figure 2.5** — Four things a consulate may do, and the two it may not are the two most often asked for.

The first hour of a consular case is worth more than the next three weeks. In that hour the facts are either captured or lost, the family is either told correctly or told something that must later be withdrawn, and the authorities either learn that a foreign state is watching, or do not.

The six facts are not a formality. A name without a date of birth matches four people. A police station without a town matches eleven. A case recorded without the question of who may be told produces a second crisis three days later, inside the family.

Escalation is the other half. A section that serves eighty people a day cannot escalate everything, and it must escalate some things immediately. The only workable rule is one written in words that need no interpretation at three in the morning.

A sorting system can apply such a rule perfectly, and this is one of its best uses. Given four trigger words and a time limit, it will never be tired, never be busy and never decide that a case can probably wait until the morning.

What it cannot do is notice the case that uses none of the four words. A message about a man who has not come home, written by somebody who does not know the word detention, is exactly the case the rule was written for and exactly the case it will miss.

> **DATA POINT · What consular assistance covers** — Consular services commonly publish an explicit list of what assistance can and cannot include, because unmanaged expectation is the largest single source of complaint in consular work.  
> Source: reported consular practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Rules that survive the night shift** — Operational guidance commonly requires escalation criteria to be expressed as named triggers and fixed time limits rather than as judgements of seriousness, so that they can be applied consistently outside working hours.  
> Source: reported operational practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — The case that must reach a person immediately

An escalation rule is the clearest case in this course for giving work to a system. Four named words, a fixed time limit and an order of people to try. A system applies that perfectly at three in the morning, which is exactly when a human being applies it worst.

## Unit 3 · Passports, Powers and Proof

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: the passive in procedure · AI: checking a document against the signs of a forgery*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How many documents did you need the last time you proved who you were?
- What would you do if an official said your certificate did not look right?
- Who decides whether a document is genuine?

Documents are checked, copied and returned. A passport is examined for its security features. A certificate is compared with the register that issued it. A signature is checked against a specimen. Nothing is decided by one officer alone, and nothing is kept without a receipt.

The language of this work is passive for a reason. The applications are received, the documents are examined, the originals are returned. The procedure is the same whoever is behind the glass, and the passive says so.

Forgery exists and is sometimes very good. The usual signs are small: a font that is almost right, a seal that is slightly too round, a date written in a format the issuing office never used, paper of the wrong weight.

Since January the section has used a document-checking tool. It compares a scanned document against thousands of genuine examples and raises a flag where something differs. It has found two real forgeries in nine months, which is two more than were found before.

In March it flagged a death certificate from a northern district. The paper was lighter than the national standard. The district had used lighter paper until 1996, as every registrar in that province knows and no database recorded.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A similarity check finds difference; only a register finds falsehood**

**Figure 3.5** — Thirty-three flags, two forgeries, and thirty-one people who had to wait.

Document fraud is real, it is sometimes very good, and a consular section is where it arrives. The usual signs are small and they are the kind of thing a machine notices faster than a person: a font that is nearly right, a seal a fraction too round.

A checking tool compares a scanned document with thousands of genuine examples and raises a flag where something differs. In nine months this one found two forgeries that would almost certainly not have been found by eye.

It also raised thirty-one flags on genuine documents. Old formats, regional variations, water damage, paper from a district that changed its supplier in 1996. The tool was accurate every time: all thirty-three were different from the standard.

Difference is not falsehood, and this is the distinction the whole procedure turns on. A flag says look harder. Only the issuing register can say genuine or false, and asking it takes days rather than seconds.

So the rule is procedural. A flag is never a refusal, never a finding and never told to the applicant as an accusation. The register is asked the same day, the applicant is told what is being done in a sentence she can repeat, and the file records both.

> **DATA POINT · How document checks are used** — Document examination guidance commonly treats an automated similarity check as an indicator that requires human examination, never as a determination, because genuine regional and historical variation is routinely scored as anomalous.  
> Source: reported document examination practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why flags are not findings** — Services using automated document checks commonly require that an anomaly be recorded as an indicator only, that verification with the issuing authority be initiated the same day, and that the applicant be informed of the delay in neutral terms.  
> Source: reported document examination practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Checking a document against the signs of a forgery

**Figure 3.7** — The left column is measurement. The right column is the only answer that matters.

A checking tool is a very good second pair of eyes. It compares a document against thousands of genuine examples and notices differences no officer would see at a counter with eighty people waiting. Two forgeries in nine months is a real result.

What it produces is an indicator. The sentence it can honestly say is this differs from the examples I hold. The sentence it cannot say is this is false, because falsehood is a fact about an issuing office, not about a scan.

So treat every flag as an instruction to ask the register, and keep the applicant out of it. Tell them the document is being verified with the office that issued it, which is true, neutral and repeatable. Never tell them that a system found something wrong.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Short transactional sentences translate well; facts that decide cases do not**

**Figure 4.5** — Eight hundred and fifty-nine exchanges were safe. The forty-one that decide cases were not.

Live translation at a counter has made a real difference to queues. A short sentence about opening hours or fees is rendered accurately almost every time, in both directions, and the applicant leaves knowing what to do instead of leaving confused.

The reason is structural rather than technical. Transactional language is short, repetitive and low in consequence. The vocabulary is small, the grammar is simple, and a small error produces a small problem that is noticed and corrected the same day.

Case-deciding language is the opposite. It is about history, family and circumstance, it uses words that carry legal weight, and a small error produces a sentence that sits in a file for years and is read as the applicant’s own account.

Refused a visa and refused entry differ by one word and by a great deal of law. Lived with and married to are not the same. Was asked to leave and was removed are not the same. No rendering marks which of these distinctions it has guessed at.

So the line is drawn by consequence, not by difficulty. If a sentence could appear in a decision, it is taken through an interpreter and read back to the applicant in their own language, and they say yes or no to the words that will be recorded.

> **DATA POINT · Where live translation is used** — Services that allow machine translation at public counters commonly restrict it to routine transactional exchanges and require a qualified interpreter for any conversation that may affect a decision on a case.  
> Source: reported consular practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Reading back is the safeguard** — Interview practice commonly requires that any account recorded through interpretation be read back to the person and confirmed, because the person is the only participant who can know whether the record matches what they meant.  
> Source: reported interview practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Live translation at a counter, and where it breaks

**Figure 4.7** — The line is drawn by consequence. A fee error is corrected; a history error is permanent.

Use live translation for the business of the counter and it will shorten the queue without harming anybody. Hours, fees, forms, windows, appointments: these are short, repeated sentences with small consequences, and the rendering is reliable.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Fluent English is written for the writer; plain English is written for the reader**

**Figure 5.5** — Nobody misread it. Six readers produced three honest readings of one fluent clause.

Half the people in most consular sections are locally engaged. They do not rotate, they hold the memory of the post, and they usually know more about how the host country works than any officer who will ever be posted there.

They work in a language that is not their first. That is not a weakness to be managed; it is a fact to be designed around. The written procedures of a mission should be built for the person who will follow them at two in the morning in their third language.

A drafting system produces fluent English very quickly, and fluency is exactly the wrong target. Fluent writing uses idiom, abstraction and subordinate clauses, and each of those adds a place where two honest readings can appear.

Ambiguity in a team is not resolved by questions. It is resolved silently, by each person choosing the reading that seems most likely, and the first anybody learns of it is when two cases have been handled two different ways.

So ask for the opposite of elegance. Short sentences. One instruction per line. Named triggers instead of judgements. No idiom at all. Then have the procedure read by somebody who will have to follow it, before it goes on the wall.

> **DATA POINT · Plain language in multilingual teams** — Operational guidance for multilingual workplaces commonly requires internal instructions to use short sentences, named triggers and no idiom, on the basis that ambiguity is resolved silently and inconsistently rather than raised as a question.  
> Source: reported practice in multilingual public services, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Who should read a draft first** — Practice in multilingual organisations commonly requires that written procedures be tested with a member of the team who will apply them, rather than cleared only by the drafter’s own supervisor.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Drafting in English for colleagues who share no first language

**Figure 5.7** — The left column asks for English. The right column asks for a procedure.

A drafting system will write whatever register you ask for, and if you ask for nothing in particular it will write fluent professional English. That is the register of the people who wrote most of what it has read, and it is the wrong register for a back office.

So specify the constraints as hard rules. One instruction per line. No sentence over fifteen words. No idiom, no phrasal verbs, no abstract nouns where a verb will do. Name the triggers instead of describing the circumstances in which they apply.

Then test it, because a constraint followed is not the same as a text understood. Have two colleagues who will actually follow the procedure read it aloud and say, line by line, what they would do. Every place they hesitate is a line to rewrite.

*Say it like this*
- Set hard limits: **One instruction per line, nothing over 15 words, no idiom.**
- Name, do not describe: **List the 4 triggers; do not write where circumstances warrant.**
- Test it aloud: **Read line 3 and tell me exactly what you would do.**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A drafting system optimises for natural English, not for a reader in their third language at two in the morning. It cannot know which words your colleagues half know, and it will not warn you that a clause has two honest readings.

## Unit 6 · The Duty Phone at Two in the Morning

*CEFR B1 · Grammar: indirect questions and polite enquiry · AI: the duty phone: a script you can follow when half awake*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Who answers your organisation’s telephone outside working hours?
- What is the first question you would ask a stranger in trouble at night?
- How well do you think clearly at two in the morning?

Every mission keeps a duty phone. It is carried in turn, it rings rarely, and when it rings the officer holding it has about four seconds of clear thought before they are fully awake. Everything about the system is designed around that fact.

The calls divide into three kinds. Most are not emergencies at all and could have waited until Monday. A few are genuine and ordinary: a lost passport, a hospital, a traveller with no money. A very small number are serious and will be remembered for years.

The duty officer works from a script. Not because officers cannot think, but because at ten past two nobody thinks in the right order. A script asks the questions in the order that matters, and it does not forget the fourth one because the third one was upsetting.

In February the section replaced its old card with a script generated from two hundred logged calls. It was better than the card in almost every way: clearer, better ordered, and it covered six situations the card had never mentioned.

It opened with the question the logs most often began with: could you tell me what has happened? That is a good question and it is not the first one. The first one is where you are now, because a call can end at any moment.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A script built from what you do will polish your habits, including the bad ones**

**Figure 6.5** — The habit and the right order are almost exactly opposite.

A duty script is one of the clearest wins available to a small mission. It costs nothing, it is used a few times a month, and it protects the one moment when an officer is least able to protect themselves: the first ninety seconds after being woken.

Building one from the section’s own logged calls is a good idea and produces a better script than most officers would write from memory. It captures the situations that actually arise, in the words the section actually uses, including several nobody had thought to write down.

The danger is subtle. A script learned from what a section does will reproduce what the section does, polished and better organised. If the section has always opened with what has happened, the script will open with what has happened, and it will do so confidently.

Habits are not evidence. The right first question on an emergency call is where are you now, followed by a number that reaches the caller directly, because a call can end at any moment and those two facts allow everything else to be done without the caller.

So use the logs for coverage and a person for order. Ask what situations appear, what questions were useful, what nobody thought to ask. Then put the two questions that survive a dropped line at the top yourself, and keep the rest.

> **DATA POINT · The first question is location** — Emergency call-handling practice commonly places the caller’s present location and a call-back number before the nature of the incident, because a call that is cut off can still be acted on if those two facts have been captured.  
> Source: reported emergency call-handling practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why order matters more than content** — Call-handling guidance commonly notes that the sequence of questions is more important than their completeness, because an interrupted call yields only the answers given before the interruption.  
> Source: reported emergency call-handling practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How much do you write down while somebody is talking?
- What is the difference between what somebody said and what they meant?
- Who reads your notes after you have left a post?

A record of conversation is the main product of a mission. Somebody met somebody, something was said, and the record is the only part of it that will exist in four years. Everything else is memory, and memory leaves with the officer.

The problem is physical. While a director is speaking, an officer is listening, thinking about the next question and writing at the same time. Nine words on a page is a normal result, and nine words is not a record.

So the notes are expanded afterwards, usually within the hour, while the memory is still warm. This is the dangerous moment. The expansion is honest and it is also reconstruction, and reconstruction smooths a conversation into something tidier than it was.

Omar was writing notes while the director was explaining the licensing changes. He wrote four words about timing. When he expanded them, he produced a sentence giving a date the director had not given, because a date was the obvious thing to have meant.

A drafting system makes this faster and the risk larger. Given nine words it will produce four fluent sentences, and the four sentences will contain things that were not in the nine words, written in the same confident voice as the things that were.

Note-taking is a physical problem before it is an intellectual one. While somebody is speaking, an officer is listening, forming the next question and writing. Something has to give, and what gives is the writing. Nine words on a page is normal.

The record is therefore always a reconstruction. That is not a scandal; it is the nature of the work, and it is why records are written within the hour and why the best officers develop a shorthand that captures numbers, names and the exact words that matter.

The risk is that reconstruction tidies. A conversation that wandered becomes an argument in three parts. A silence becomes agreement. A hesitation becomes a position. None of this is dishonest, and all of it is wrong.

A drafting system does the tidying faster and more convincingly. Given nine words it produces four well-formed sentences, and some of them will describe what usually happens in such a meeting rather than what happened in this one.

The remedy is traceability. Mark verbatim notes in quotation marks. Mark reconstruction as reconstruction. Write no date, no number and no agreement that is not in the notes, and say in the record that the point was not covered, which is itself information.

> **DATA POINT · Why records are written within the hour** — Reporting practice commonly requires a record of conversation to be written while contemporaneous notes are still intelligible, and to distinguish on its face between what was recorded at the time and what is supplied from memory.  
> Source: reported reporting practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Marking what is verbatim** — Record-keeping guidance commonly asks officers to distinguish verbatim quotation from paraphrase within the body of a record, so that a later reader can tell which sentences can be relied on as the speaker’s own words.  
> Source: reported record-keeping practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Notes into a record, without inventing what was said

**Figure 7.7** — The left column saves twenty minutes. The right column is what makes the record evidence.

Expanding notes into a record is slow, dull work that a system does quickly. Give it the notes and ask for the points in the order they were raised, expanded only from what is there, with your abbreviations preserved rather than interpreted.

Then ask for the opposite list, which is the valuable one: every point the notes do not cover. That list tells you what to go back and ask, and it is far easier to see from outside a conversation than from inside it.

What you must forbid is completion. A fluent record of a wandering meeting is a false record. Say explicitly that no date, number, agreement or reaction may appear unless it is in the notes, and that a gap must be written as a gap.

A cable is one page. Not because a page is a natural unit, but because the person reading it has forty others and eleven minutes. A two-page cable is read as far as the bottom of the first page, which means the second page was written for nobody.

Bassel now drafts the first cut with a system. He gives it the notes and the standard shape, and a page comes back in ninety seconds. It keeps the facts, the figures and the order, and it is better organised than his own first attempt.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A summary keeps what is repeated, so the thing said once is the thing at risk**

**Figure 8.5** — Ten correct cuts and one that removed the only disagreement in the room.

The one-page limit is not a stylistic preference. A desk officer receives forty cables and has a morning. A page is what gets read; a second page is written for a reader who does not exist. Everything beyond the page belongs in an annex that will be opened only if the page says it should be.

Cutting four pages to one is therefore the central skill, and it is harder than writing the four pages. Most of what is in notes is true, interesting and irrelevant to the decision. The test is not importance in general; it is whether a reader would act differently if the sentence were absent.

Summarising systems are good at this and getting better. Given notes and a fixed shape, a competent one-page draft comes back in under two minutes, with the figures right and the order sensible. For a tired officer at six in the evening this is a real gain.

The weakness is structural rather than accidental. Summarisation keeps what is central, and centrality is measured by repetition and by connection to the rest of the text. A fact stated once, in eleven words, connected to nothing else, scores low.

Unfortunately that is exactly the shape of a dissent, a caveat, a single contradicting figure or an unexpected remark at the end of a meeting. So ask for the list of what was cut, read that list first, and put back by hand whatever changes a decision.

> **DATA POINT · What a summary loses first** — Analytical guidance commonly warns that automatic summarisation preserves what is repeated and central, and that minority views, caveats and single-mention facts are therefore the first material to be lost.  
> Source: reported analytical practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Read the cut list first** — Editorial practice for automated summarisation commonly recommends reviewing the material removed before the summary itself, because a reader who has already absorbed a coherent summary is less likely to notice what is missing.  
> Source: reported editorial practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — One page from four pages of notes

**Figure 8.7** — The fourth item on the left is the one that makes the rest safe.

Ask for two things, not one. The one-page draft, and the list of every sentence that was removed. The second is produced as easily as the first and is almost never requested, which is why the risk of summarisation is so widely underestimated.

Read the cut list first. Once you have read a coherent one-page summary, your mind has a shape for the meeting, and a missing sentence does not announce itself. Read the cuts cold, and the one that matters stands out in a second.

Then restore by hand. A dissent, a caveat, a single contradictory figure, a remark made at the door on the way out: these are short, unrepeated and decision-changing, which is the exact combination a summary is built to remove.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A count answers the question it was given, which is rarely the question that was meant**

**Figure 9.5** — Two of six figures are comparable. The two that drive staffing decisions are not.

Counting is how a small section talks to a large organisation. Nobody in the capital can visit Ostport every month, so the section is known by eleven figures, and decisions about its staff, its hours and its budget are made from them.

This makes definitions more important than arithmetic. A case is whatever a post has decided a case is, and two posts with different decisions produce figures that look comparable, are reported side by side, and mean entirely different things.

A counting system removes all the arithmetic difficulty and none of this. It will produce eleven figures in two seconds, with a chart and a trend line, and every one of them will be exactly right under whatever definition it was given.

It also prefers what is easy to count. Events are easy: calls, documents, cases opened. Outcomes are hard: a family reassured, a detention noticed early, a forgery stopped. A dashboard therefore shows the half of the work that counts itself.

The remedy is two sentences, written once a month. The first states the definition behind the main figure. The second states what the figures do not cover. Both take four minutes and both outlive every chart on the page.

> **DATA POINT · Definitions before counts** — Administrative guidance commonly requires that a counting definition be recorded in writing before a figure is reported, because comparisons between posts are otherwise comparisons between definitions.  
> Source: reported administrative practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · What a dashboard shows** — Performance guidance commonly warns that measurement systems favour activity over outcome because activity is directly observable, and that organisations tend over time to resemble what their measures describe.  
> Source: reported performance management practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — The month in numbers: counting what was actually done

**Figure 9.7** — Arithmetic is free. The definition is the whole meaning of the number.

A counting system is excellent at the parts of this work that are genuinely hard by hand: matching records, avoiding double counts, finding the one month that is out of line, and producing the same eleven figures in the same shape every time.

Give it the definitions explicitly, in writing, before you ask for anything. A case is opened when an officer takes action. A document is counted once at issue. A call is counted only if it lasted more than thirty seconds. Then the figures mean something.

And write the sentence the system cannot write: what these figures do not cover. Welfare visits that reassured a family, a detention noticed early, a forgery stopped. If nobody writes that line, the section will slowly become the part of itself that is counted.

- Give the definition: **A case is opened when an officer takes action on it.**
- Ask for the checks: **List any double counts and any figure outside the usual range.**
- Write the gap line: **These figures do not cover the work of the duty phone.**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Two kinds of limit: what we may not do, and where information may never go**

**Figure 10.5** — One column is a tool. The other is a promise, and the promise is to a family.

A mission’s limits are not failures. They are the shape of the thing. A consulate that could overturn a court would not be a consulate; it would be a court. Saying what cannot be done, early and plainly, is one of the most useful services an officer provides.

The second kind of limit is about information, and it is newer. Case files hold names, dates of birth, addresses, medical facts and the location of people who are frightened. The Mission promises that this stays inside the Mission.

That promise is broken in four seconds. Not by a spy and not by carelessness in any ordinary sense, but by an officer who wants to do the work properly, needs a paragraph translated, and uses whatever is nearest.

The convenience is the whole danger. Thinking takes longer than pasting. So the rule cannot be a principle to be remembered; it has to be a short list of things that are never typed outside: a name, a number, an address, a medical fact.

And it must come with a remedy, because a rule without one is ignored. Replace the name with a letter. Ask the general question without the case. Use the approved tools freely and without embarrassment, because they exist precisely so that the promise can be kept.
