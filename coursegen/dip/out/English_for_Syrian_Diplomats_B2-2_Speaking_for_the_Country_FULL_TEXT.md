### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: question forms under pressure, and the question behind them**

- A negative question (Isn’t it true that …?) asks you to accept a premise before you answer; deal with the premise first.
- A tag question (You said that, didn’t you?) seeks agreement, not information.
- A presupposing question (When will you publish the figures?) assumes a fact; if the assumption is wrong, say so before answering.
- A multi-part question lets the interviewer choose which part you answered; answer the part you can and name the part you are leaving.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A prediction from the published record is astonishingly good, and the best question in the room was never published**

**Figure 1.5** — Twenty-four questions asked, twenty-two of them anticipated, and the two that were not were the two that mattered.

Predicting interview questions is a task a system performs better than a Mission press officer, and it is worth being plain about why: the raw material is entirely published, abundant and patterned, and interviewers are far more consistent than they would like to believe.

A year of a programme’s output shows what it asks about, in what order and with what formulations. A particular interviewer’s own pieces show which words she favours, which premises she builds into questions, and whether she opens softly or goes straight at the difficulty.

Thirty ranked questions produced in minutes, of which twenty-two are asked in some form, is preparation an Ambassador could not previously buy at any price, and it changes what twenty minutes in a studio feels like.

The two questions that were not predicted came from the two sources no corpus contains: something the interviewer heard with her own ears at a reception, and the ordinary human curiosity about the person in front of her.

So add two things to any predicted list. First, every unscripted thing you have said in public in the last year, which only you and your press officer can reconstruct. Second, the four questions about yourself that you would least like to be asked.

> **DATA POINT · Ground rules** — Media practice commonly distinguishes between on-the-record, background and non-attributable exchanges, and treats the terms of an interview — length, format, whether live or recorded, and who else appears — as matters agreed beforehand rather than discovered in the studio.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · The unpublished remark** — Press-handling guidance commonly advises that remarks made at receptions, briefings and social occasions may be used by journalists who were present, and that a speaker should assume that anything said in such a setting may be raised later in an interview.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A compression keeps what persuades and drops what qualifies, which is the wrong way round for a government**

**Figure 2.5** — Eight good decisions and one that changes the position the speaker is authorised to take.

Cutting a long position to three minutes is a genuinely difficult editorial task and a system does it well: it identifies the three arguments that carry the most weight, keeps one example for each, drops the rest without sentiment, and produces something that reads aloud at the right speed.

The cut is also better structured than most officials manage under time pressure, because it is willing to throw away six arguments that are true, and an author is not. Most three-minute openings fail by trying to contain nine points in a hundred and eighty seconds.

What a compression cannot weigh is legal and political necessity. A conditional clause contributes almost nothing to persuasion and almost nothing to length, so it is removed early and quietly, and it is frequently the clause on which the whole position was authorised.

The same applies to anything that sounds like a hedge: the delegation is prepared to consider, subject to the outcome of, without prejudice to. Each of those phrases was fought for by somebody, and each of them reads as weak prose to any editor, human or otherwise.

So cut with a system and restore by hand. Read the long text once for conditions only, list them, and put the one that matters in the first thirty seconds, where it cannot be lost to a moderator with a stopwatch.

> **DATA POINT · Compression and qualification** — Studies of summarisation commonly observe that conditional and qualifying clauses are among the first elements removed when a text is shortened, since they contribute least to the main propositional content while adding substantial length.  
> Source: general summarisation literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Interruption and structure** — Broadcast and conference practice commonly notes that speakers subject to time limits are frequently interrupted before completing a rhetorical structure, so that material placed last may never be delivered.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Holding language is easy to generate and the difficult part is whether it is true**

**Figure 3.5** — Three phrases that commit to nothing and three that assert things nobody had done.

Asking for holding language is one of the most reasonable uses of a system in a Mission, because the task is a language task: produce ten neutral, calm, repeatable sentences that answer a question without answering it, in the register of public life.

It does this very well, and the phrases it produces are better than the ones most people improvise at twenty past eight with a camera outside, which are usually either too blunt or too long to survive being quoted.

The trouble is that the register of holding language and the register of reassurance are almost the same, and reassurance makes claims. We are working closely with the authorities is grammatically a statement about the present conduct of your Mission.

A journalist can check it with one telephone call, and if the answer is that nobody has been in touch, the story is no longer about a consular case: it is about a Mission that says things that are not true, which is a far worse story.

So apply one test to every phrase before you use it: what would somebody find if they checked this sentence? Keep the phrases that would hold at six o’clock tonight, and strike the ones that describe work you have not yet done, however calm they sound.

> **DATA POINT · Holding language** — Press-handling guidance commonly distinguishes between holding language, which commits the speaker to nothing while remaining accurate, and formulations which imply action, knowledge or contact that has not taken place and which are therefore statements capable of being falsified.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Verifiable implicature** — Media guidance commonly warns that reassuring formulations about contact, cooperation or ongoing work are routinely verified with the third parties named or implied, and that a formulation found to be inaccurate becomes a separate and more damaging story.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A fluent account of an institution’s past is the most dangerous text a Mission can be given**

**Figure 4.5** — Three invented helpful facts and three documented ones, in the same calm paragraph.

Everywhere else in this course the warning has been about judgement: a system that reasons well from material it cannot see. Here the warning is simpler and harder, because a question about an institution’s past invites a kind of writing that generates facts.

The pattern is consistent and worth recognising. Asked what a named institution did in a given year, a system produces the things such institutions usually do: a statement was issued, a delegation visited, a letter was sent in reply. Each is plausible and each is specific enough to be checked.

If an Ambassador says in public that his Mission issued a statement in a year when it issued nothing, he has made a false claim about his own institution, in his own voice, on the record, and the correction will be the story rather than his answer.

The only source for an institution’s past is the institution’s own record, which means an archive search: what files exist for that year, what they contain, where the gaps are and how long they run. A gap is a finding, and it is often the honest centre of an answer.

So use a system for everything around the answer and nothing inside it: the likely questions, the structure, the ninety seconds of practice. The facts come from the files, the dates come from the register, and where there is nothing, you say that there is nothing.

> **DATA POINT · Generated accounts of the past** — It is commonly observed that systems asked to describe the history of a named institution may produce plausible but unfounded specifics, since fluent historical narrative is generated from patterns rather than from the institution’s own records.  
> Source: general practice guidance on generated text, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Regret and admission** — Public-communication guidance commonly distinguishes between expressing regret about an established fact and accepting responsibility for conduct not yet established, and advises that a speaker confine expressions of regret to what the record supports.  
> Source: general public communication guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A rebuttal is a language task, and whether to publish one is an arithmetic task**

**Figure 5.5** — The strongest document on the list is also the one that spreads the error furthest.

Drafting a correction is a task a system does well, and it does the hardest part of it well: it separates the assertions, answers each in turn, keeps the tone level and avoids the two temptations of an aggrieved press officer, which are sarcasm and length.

The draft it produced here is accurate on both factual points, and an official who published it would be publishing nothing untrue. The difficulty is not in the text at all, which is why it is easy to miss.

Whether to answer a claim depends on its reach, on who has noticed it, on whether anybody with influence believes it, and on what answering would do to all three. None of that is in the claim, and none of it is in the draft.

A system also treats every assertion as answerable, because every assertion is answerable in language. It does not distinguish a wrong number, which can be corrected, from a view about visibility, which cannot be corrected and can only be argued with, and arguing is a quarrel.

So do the arithmetic first and the drafting second. Establish the reach of the claim and the reach of every available answer, separate the facts from the interpretations, and start with the smallest instrument that works, which is very often a telephone call to an editor who has done it before.

> **DATA POINT · Amplification** — Communication practice commonly warns that a public response to a low-reach claim can increase that claim’s audience substantially, and advises that the decision whether to respond be taken on reach and salience rather than on the merits of the claim alone.  
> Source: general public communication guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Fact and comment** — Press practice commonly distinguishes between statements of fact, which are subject to correction, and expressions of opinion, which are not, and complaints procedures are generally confined to the former.  
> Source: general media practice literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Correcting a false claim without amplifying it

**Figure 5.7** — The left column is drafting. The right column is the decision, and it is arithmetic.

Ask first for a sorting rather than a draft: which assertions in this piece are statements of fact, which are expressions of opinion, and for each factual error what the correct figure is with its source. That list is the useful product, and it takes a minute.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A generic first hour is better than no plan at all, and it does not know where your door is**

**Figure 6.5** — Eighteen actions performed, two reduced to what the post can do, and one added that no list contained.

A first-hour action list is the clearest case in this book of a generated document being better than the one in the drawer, because the one in the drawer was written two years ago by an officer who has left, and nobody has read it since.

Twenty-two actions in a sensible order, produced in forty seconds while a square fills with people, is a genuine operational gain. It will not forget to open a log, and under pressure that is exactly what people forget.

Two kinds of error follow from the same cause, which is that the list describes a Mission in general. It assumes staff and arrangements a particular post may not have, and it omits the single local fact that governs everything, which here is a door.

The second kind of error is more dangerous in writing. A drafted holding statement will contain a casualty figure, because holding statements contain casualty figures, and the figure will come from whatever reporting it has seen, unattributed and unverified.

A Mission that puts an unconfirmed number into its own statement has made that number official in the only way that matters to a family reading it. So reconcile the list against your own staffing in the first five minutes, add your local fact, and take every number out of your own voice until somebody with authority has confirmed it to you.

> **DATA POINT · Generic and local plans** — Crisis-management practice commonly holds that a generic action list must be reconciled against the actual staffing, premises and local arrangements of the post concerned, since a plan assuming resources that do not exist fails at the moment it is needed.  
> Source: general crisis management literature, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Casualty information** — Crisis-communication practice commonly provides that casualty figures are issued by the responsible national authorities and not by foreign missions, which confine themselves to what they have confirmed about their own nationals and to the time of that confirmation.  
> Source: general crisis management literature, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Remarks are easy to write and the twelve questions are already in your own log**

**Figure 7.5** — Twelve questions, none of them new, and only one of them the Mission itself decides.

A system writes community remarks fluently, and the remarks are pleasant, structured and warm. They are also written in the register such remarks are usually written in, which is the register of a government addressing a public, and the register itself produces promises.

We will ensure is a natural phrase in that register and an impossible one for a Mission, because the matters most often raised at a community meeting are decided in a capital, in a ministry, or in a court, and a Mission forwards, chases and explains.

The deeper problem is that nobody in the hall came for remarks. They came with individual cases, and a draft written from nothing but a subject heading cannot know what those cases are — but your own enquiry log can, and it is on the desk.

Put the log through a system instead. Ask it to group six months of enquiries into the twelve questions that recur, with the number of times each was raised, and ask who decides each one, which your consular section already knows.

Then print the twelve answers in both languages, hand them in at the door, speak for ten minutes rather than thirty, and spend the rest of the evening taking names for individual appointments. That is what a hall of four hundred cases actually needs.

> **DATA POINT · Expectation and authority** — Consular guidance commonly warns against assurances at public meetings concerning matters determined by other authorities, since such assurances are recalled accurately by those who heard them and cannot be honoured by the post that gave them.

## Unit 8 · Speaking Before You Know

- What is wrong with saying nothing has changed?

The most useful sentence in public life is I do not know yet, and it is the hardest to say, because every instinct in a room pushes towards a form of words that sounds like an answer and costs nothing.

Dr Suhair Kanaan is asked at eleven o’clock whether a decision has been taken. She does not know. The Mission expects to be told on Thursday, which is three days away, and the person asking has been waiting since March.

The third is the most tempting, because it is kind: the matter is progressing normally. It asserts progress, it asserts a norm, and if the file has in fact been sitting untouched since April it is simply false.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**There are many ways to say you do not know, and some of them are claims**

**Figure 8.5** — Three formulations that withhold and three that quietly assert, in the same calm voice.

Asking for ways to say you do not know is a sound use of a system, and it produces more and better formulations than anybody thinks of under pressure, including several that are both short and courteous, which is a difficult combination.

The failure is specific and worth memorising, because it recurs in every language: some standard non-answers are not non-answers. The position remains as it was asserts that no change has taken place, which is knowledge about the world you do not have.

The same is true of nothing has changed at this stage, of the matter is in hand, and above all of the matter is progressing normally, which asserts both that there is progress and that there is a normal rate of it. A file untouched since April makes all three false.

The second failure is the timescale. A system asked how to reassure somebody will offer a period — within two weeks, shortly, in the coming days — because that is what such sentences contain, and no part of that period came from anybody with authority to set it.

So test each formulation by asking what it asserts, and give only one date: the date of your next contact, which is yours to keep. I will telephone you on Thursday whether or not I have an answer is a promise you can keep, and it is worth more than a guess about a decision.

> **DATA POINT · Non-answers that assert** — Communication guidance commonly notes that several standard holding formulations contain factual assertions about the current state of a matter, and that a speaker who does not know that state is making a claim rather than withholding one.  
> Source: general public communication guidance, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · The date you can keep** — Practice in handling enquiries commonly advises that an official should undertake only to make contact by a stated time, rather than to deliver an outcome by it, since the outcome is frequently determined elsewhere.  
> Source: general public communication guidance, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A generated handover note is complete on everything documented, which is not what a successor asks**

**Figure 9.5** — Six questions, no answers in fourteen accurate pages, and five of the answers inside one person.

Drafting a handover note from files, a calendar and a contact list is a task a system does quickly and thoroughly, and the result is genuinely better than most handover notes, which are written in the last two days of a posting by somebody who has already mentally left.

It will miss nothing that is documented. Every live file, every deadline in the next six months, every contact with a name and a title, in a consistent order, with the dormant files separated from the live ones.

What it cannot contain is the thing a successor actually needs, which is practice: which of those correctly named contacts answers his own telephone, which requires a call before a letter, which never replies to anybody below ambassadorial rank.

Nor can it contain the obligations you have incurred in conversation — a promise to look at something, a debt of courtesy to somebody who was helpful in March — or the fact that the only working list of a municipality is in a local colleague’s notebook.

So build the note backwards, from your successor’s first fortnight. Write the six questions she will ask in week one, answer them in two sentences each, put the fourteen generated pages behind that as an appendix, and ask any colleague you are about to describe whether they are content to be described.

> **DATA POINT · Handover and local staff** — Practice guidance commonly notes that locally engaged staff hold much of a post’s working knowledge, and recommends that their role be recorded accurately and with their knowledge rather than described in their absence.  
> Source: general organisational practice literature, 2025–2026.

**Grammar: restraint: parallelism, understatement and the short sentence**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Six books of gains, and one limit that did not move**

**Figure 10.5** — Six real gains, and six limits that are all the same limit wearing different clothes.

This course has made one argument, in sixty units, and it is worth stating plainly at the end. A system is very good at everything that has been written down, and the part of diplomatic work that decides outcomes is almost never written down.

That is not a complaint about the technology and it is not a reason to avoid it. The gains recorded in these books are real: forty seconds for a comparison that took an evening, thirty predicted questions, a first hour you cannot forget, a list of differences nobody could produce by hand.

Every one of those saves hours, and the hours matter, because they are the hours in which an officer does the part nobody can do for him: sitting in a room, telephoning somebody who was there in 2019, asking a local colleague what the other side still resents.

And there are four things that will not be delegated, however good the instruments become. A representative is present, which is why he is told things. He is trusted, which takes years and cannot be transferred. He is answerable, which is why his judgement is worth having. Use everything. Check every name, every figure and every claim about what once happened. Write your own judgement in your own words, with restraint, and sign it. That is the whole of it, and it has not changed.
