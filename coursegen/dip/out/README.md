# English for Syrian Diplomats
### Syria Edition · six books · A2.1 → B2.2

For officers serving abroad: embassies, consulates general, permanent missions
and delegations, and anyone holding a representative title that requires travel
abroad. Not for ministry staff in the capital — that is a different course.

## What is in each unit

Twelve parts, in this order:

1. **The Situation** — a figure, three questions, and the reading
2. **Comprehension** — five exercise types
3. **Vocabulary** — twenty terms, defined, with exercises
4. **Grammar** — one point, a LOOSE/PRECISE figure, fifteen items
5. **Dialogues** — two of twelve lines each
6. **Speaking** — three pair tasks
7. **The Capability** — what a system did, in figures, and where it stopped
8. **Writing** — a six-line tasking: Purpose, Scope, Inputs, Criteria, Done, Fallback
9. **The Skill** — how to use it, what to say, what it cannot do
10. **On Your Feet** — an unscripted spoken round, with holding language and a red line
11. **Review** — all six skills
12. **Can-do** — five statements to tick

Plus, in every unit: **The Decision Room**, a real case with an exhibit and a
documented outcome, and **the Brief Lab**, a six-line instruction as somebody
actually gave it, what came back, and what was wrong with the instruction.

## Teaching it

**One unit is two to three ninety-minute classes.** A workable split:

- Class A: Parts 1–4 (situation, comprehension, vocabulary, grammar)
- Class B: Parts 5–8 (dialogues, speaking, the capability, writing) plus the
  Brief Lab
- Class C: Parts 9–12 (the skill, On Your Feet, review, can-do) plus the
  Decision Room

**On Your Feet needs no preparation and must not be prepared.** Student B reads
one turn at a time; Student A answers immediately, out loud, with nothing
written down first. Then both score the answer against the five rows. It is the
only part of the course where a wrong answer out loud is the point.

**The Decision Room works as a plenary.** Read the brief, show the exhibit,
take the three voices as three readers, then work the five steps and write the
four-line rule as a class.

**Self-study** works: every exercise has an answer key at the end of its unit,
and the On Your Feet rounds can be read aloud alone, slowly, which is less
useful than a partner and better than nothing.

## What the course argues

One thing, in sixty units: a system is very good at everything that has been
written down, and the part of diplomatic work that decides outcomes is almost
never written down.

So the books teach delegation and verification together. Every unit states the
gain in figures — twenty-two of thirty questions anticipated, twenty-three
differences found in forty seconds, five names from two hundred and eleven in
six minutes — and then states exactly where it stopped, and what the officer
had to do instead: telephone somebody who was there, read the archive, ask a
local colleague what the other side still resents.

And four things the books say cannot be delegated at all: being present in a
room, being trusted by a person, being answerable for a decision, and deciding
what a government is prepared to lose.
