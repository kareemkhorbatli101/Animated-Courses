### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What does an invitation tell you besides the time and the place?
- How do you say no to an invitation without causing offence?
- Who in your organisation decides which invitations are accepted?

The Mission receives about forty invitations a month. Some are important and some are not, and the card itself rarely says which. Tarek sorts them on a Monday. He would like an hour to do it and he usually has twenty minutes.

An invitation carries four pieces of information. The date and time. The dress code. The place. And, most important of all, who exactly is invited. The fourth is the one that is read fastest and understood worst.

A card that says His Excellency the Ambassador is an invitation to one person. A card that says the Ambassador and Mrs Hourani is an invitation to two. A card that says the Mission of our country is an invitation to the institution, and anybody suitable may go.

Nour asked a system to read the month’s invitations and build a table: date, time, venue, dress code, who is invited. It produced the table in forty seconds and it was accurate about dates, times and venues, which are written plainly on every card.

It was not accurate about the fourth column. Where a card said His Excellency, it wrote the Mission, because that is what most invitations mean and the system had no way of knowing that this one did not.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: would like, shall and the language of invitation**

- would like + to + verb is a polite want: I would like to accept.
- Would you like …? is a polite offer or invitation.
- Shall I …? and Shall we …? make offers and suggestions.
- Formal replies use the third person: The Mission has the honour to accept.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Three columns a system reads perfectly, and one it has to guess**

**Figure 1.5** — Three columns are transcription. Two are judgement, and one of those is not on the card at all.

Representation is work, and it begins with a pile of cards. A medium-sized mission receives several hundred invitations a year. Somebody has to decide which are accepted, by whom, and what the Mission is saying by the choice.

An invitation read correctly gives you four things: the date, the dress code, the place and the person invited. Three of these are printed plainly and can be transcribed by anything that can read. A system builds that table in under a minute and makes no errors.

The fourth is different. Who is invited is written in a form that carries meaning: a name, a title, a couple, an institution. The words are plain. What they imply about who may attend instead is not on the card at all.

There is a fifth question that is never written anywhere. Why does this invitation matter? A card from a small cultural association may matter more this month than a card from a large ministry, and only a person at the post knows that.

So let a system do the transcription and keep the judgement. Date, time and venue in a table in forty seconds. Who is invited, read by a human being, every time, because that column is a decision about what the Mission signals by turning up.

> **DATA POINT · What a card is really saying** — Protocol guidance commonly stresses that the form of address on an invitation defines who may attend, and that substituting a junior officer for a named head of mission is read as a deliberate signal rather than an administrative convenience.  
> Source: reported protocol practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why substitution is read as a signal** — Protocol literature commonly notes that attendance at the named level is itself a form of communication, and that a last-minute substitution without explanation is interpreted by hosts as a deliberate downgrading of the occasion.  
> Source: reported protocol practice, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — An invitation read correctly: date, dress, who is invited

**Figure 1.7** — The left column is printing. The right column is who the Mission must be that evening.

Give the system the cards and ask for a table: date, time, venue, dress code, and the exact words of the addressee line, copied and not interpreted. That instruction matters: copied, not interpreted. Then the table is useful and the judgement stays with you.

An invitation read correctly tells you four things, and the system can be trusted with three of them. The date, the time and the venue are printed plainly and it transcribes them perfectly. The dress code is usually printed and occasionally implied.

The fourth is the addressee, and it is a decision disguised as a line of text. A system will normalise it to the most common case, because that is what most cards mean. Read the exact words yourself, and decide who may go instead, which is never written down.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for transcription: **Copy the addressee line exactly; do not interpret it.**
- Keep the judgement: **I will decide who attends; you give me the four facts.**
- Check the implication: **This card names one person. May anybody go instead?**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A system reading an invitation does not know that a named head of mission cannot be replaced without asking, and it will normalise an unusual addressee line to the usual one. It is transcribing text; it is not reading a signal.

## Unit 2 · Introductions in a Room of Strangers

*CEFR A2 · Grammar: adjectives and adverbs of manner · AI: a guest list you can actually use in the room*

**Figure 2.0** — A ROOM OF SIXTY PEOPLE AND A LIST OF FORTY NAMES: The first twenty minutes of a reception, when everybody is still standing near the door.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- How do you introduce yourself at an event where you know nobody?
- What do you do when you cannot remember somebody’s name?
- How do you leave a conversation politely?

A reception is not a party. It is forty minutes of work, standing up, with a glass in one hand. An officer who arrives with no purpose speaks pleasantly to three people and remembers nothing. An officer who arrives with two names in mind usually gets both.

An introduction in English is short. Your name, your rank, your mission. Then a question. Long introductions are a common mistake and they are not polite; they are a way of making the other person wait.

Nour learns to join a circle carefully. She stands at the edge, waits for a pause, and is introduced by somebody. If nobody introduces her she waits for the speaker to finish, then says her name quietly and asks a question about what was being said.

Before the event Ziad gives her a guest list. It has forty names. A system has added two lines about each: the organisation, the role, and one recent public activity. It took two minutes and it is extremely useful in the room.

One line is wrong. A guest is described as the director of a cultural agency. She left that post in March and now runs something else. Ziad greets her warmly by the old title, in front of three other guests, and the correction is hers to make.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A prepared list makes a room usable, and the preparation has a shelf life**

**Figure 2.5** — Two lines age in months. One line only exists if somebody at the post writes it.

An officer who walks into a room of sixty strangers with no preparation is being paid to drink fruit juice. An officer with a guest list, three lines about each name and two objectives will leave with something the Mission did not have that morning.

Building such a list used to take an afternoon. A system now does the dull part in two minutes: match the names, find the organisation, find the role, find one recent public activity. For forty guests, that is genuinely transformative.

The weakness is in the word recent. Roles change faster than public pages. A person who left a post in March may still be described by it in November, and the system reports what it reads, carefully and accurately.

Getting it wrong is not a small embarrassment. Calling a guest by a position they have lost, in front of others, forces them to correct you in public. The memory of that exchange will outlast anything else said that evening.

So put a date beside every role and confirm the half-dozen that matter most. And add the line no system can supply: what the Mission actually wants from this person. That line is written by somebody at the post or it does not exist.

> **DATA POINT · Why guest notes are dated** — Missions that prepare biographical notes for events commonly require each entry to carry the date of its latest confirmation, because public sources describing a person’s role are frequently several months behind an appointment or a departure.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic event preparation, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · The line a system cannot write** — Event preparation guidance commonly distinguishes between researched background, which can be assembled automatically, and the objective for each contact, which must be set locally by the officer who will be in the room.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic event preparation, 2025–2026.

## Unit 3 · Small Talk With a Purpose

*CEFR A2 · Grammar: comparatives and superlatives · AI: small talk prepared, not scripted*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What do people in your country talk about when they first meet?
- Which subjects are easy at home and difficult abroad?

Small talk has a bad name and it is the most useful thing at a reception. It is the cheapest way to find out whether a person is worth twenty more minutes. It is also the only way to begin a relationship that has no business reason to exist yet.

Good small talk is shorter than people expect. Two or three exchanges, a question that is easier to answer than to avoid, and a way out for both of you. The best openers are about the room, the host, the food or the other person’s work.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Preparation is professional; a script is a trap, because the room is not on the page**

**Figure 3.5** — Five subjects survive contact with the room. The sixth needed something no list contains.

Preparing for informal conversation is not cheating. Every experienced officer arrives with two or three things to ask about and a sense of what to avoid. The alternative is not authenticity; it is standing by a wall talking about the weather for an hour.

A system is good at the first half of this. Ask for subjects a visitor could raise politely in a given country, and ten arrive in thirty seconds. Most will be sensible: food, music, the host’s hospitality, the building, the other person’s work.

It is weaker on the second half. What a published source calls a current topic may be a live wound in one province and a dull technicality in another. The difference is rarely written down anywhere a system can read it.

There is a further danger in preparation itself. A remark that has been rehearsed sounds rehearsed. Small talk delivered as a prepared item makes the listener feel processed, which is the opposite of what the forty minutes are for.

So prepare subjects, not sentences. Know four things you could ask about and one you will not raise. Then look at who is actually in front of you, because the only reliable guide to a room is the people standing in it.

> **DATA POINT · Prepared, not scripted** — Diplomatic training commonly distinguishes preparation for informal conversation, which is encouraged, from scripted remarks, which are discouraged, because a remark delivered without local judgement can be accurate and still be badly received.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic training, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why local judgement cannot be delegated** — Guidance on intercultural communication commonly notes that sensitivity to a subject varies by region, generation and family history within a single country, so general country-level advice cannot determine what is appropriate in a particular conversation.  
> Source: reported practice in intercultural training, 2025–2026.

### Part 7 · Real-World Skills — Small talk prepared, not scripted

**Figure 3.7** — The left column is material. The right column is the evening itself.

Small talk is prepared, not scripted, and the difference lies in what you ask for. Ask for subjects and reasons, not for sentences. Ten polite subjects with one line each on why they are safe gives you something to think with. Ten ready-made remarks give you something to recite, and a recited remark is audible across a room.

Ask also for the opposite list, and ask why. Subjects that are generally avoided, with the reason given for each, is far more useful than a list of forbidden words. The reason is what lets you judge the variant that was not on the list.

Then test anything regional against a person. A country-level answer cannot know that a project is a dull technicality in the capital and a wound in the east. If no local colleague is available before the event, leave every regional subject alone.

*Say it like this*
- Ask for subjects: **Ten subjects a visitor may raise politely, with a reason for each.**
- Ask for the reason: **Which subjects are usually avoided here, and why?**
- Test it locally: **Tarek, is the canal a safe thing to mention on Thursday?**

> **WHAT THIS DOES NOT DO** — A general list of safe subjects does not know who will be standing in the circle, where they were born or what happened in their province. It gives a national answer to a question that is answered differently in each room.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Precedence is international; the exceptions are local and nobody writes them down**

**Figure 4.5** — Four rows match the manual. Two rows are why you telephone the protocol office.

A formal meal is a seating plan with food attached. The placement tells every guest how the host sees the room, and it tells them in the first ten seconds, before anybody has spoken. A mistake in it is read as a judgement, not as an accident.

Most of the rules are genuinely international, and they are written down. The guest of honour on the right. The second on the left. Alternation down the table. Spouses separated. A system reproduces all of this quickly and accurately.

The exceptions are local and almost never published. A country may rank a visiting chargé above a resident counsellor. It may seat an interpreter at the table rather than behind a chair. It may follow an order for religious guests that exists in no manual.

These exceptions are held by protocol offices and by long-serving staff. They are not secret. They are simply not written, because everybody who needs them already knows them, which is exactly the condition under which a visitor gets them wrong.

So the order is: look it up, then ask a person. The looking up gives you the structure, the words and the right questions. The asking gives you the three places where this country does it differently, and those three places are where offence lives.

> **DATA POINT · Where seating rules actually live** — Protocol offices commonly maintain their own local order of precedence that differs in places from the general international practice, and these local orders are often unpublished and transmitted between officers by direct advice.  
> Source: reported protocol practice, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why protocol offices welcome the question** — Protocol practice commonly treats a query from a foreign mission about local precedence as routine and welcome, since resolving a seating question in advance is far less costly to both sides than correcting it at the table.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What makes a short speech good, in any language?
- How long should a toast be?
- What is the worst thing a speaker can say at a friendly dinner?

A diplomatic speech at a dinner is two minutes long. Not five. Two. First you welcome, then you thank, after that you say one thing of substance, and finally you propose the toast. Everything else belongs in a meeting.

The toast itself is one sentence. Ladies and gentlemen, I ask you to rise and drink to the friendship between our two countries. People stand, drink, sit down. A toast that needs a paragraph is not a toast.

The danger in a short speech is not nervousness. It is substance. A sentence that sounds warm can also be a commitment, and twenty-two people have heard it, and one of them will write it down.

Nour is asked to draft a welcome for the Ambassador. She asks a system. The draft arrives in two minutes. It is well ordered, correctly warm, the right length, and better than her own first attempt would have been.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**Two minutes is the easiest thing to draft and the easiest thing to over-commit**

**Figure 5.5** — Three of the four parts are safe. The third part is the whole of the risk.

Speech drafting is one of the clearest uses of a system in a mission. Give it the occasion, the host, the audience, the length and the one point you want made, and a well-ordered two-minute text comes back in two minutes. The structure will be correct.

It will also be fluent, warm and confident, and that is where the problem begins. Fluent warmth produces sentences like we look forward to signing in the spring, which is exactly the kind of sentence a drafting system writes and a mission cannot say.

An audience cannot tell the difference between a courtesy and a commitment. Suppose a representative of a state says at a public dinner that something will happen in the spring. As far as the room is concerned, that is the position of the state.

So the drafting is cheap and the checking is the work. Read every sentence and ask one question of each: does this promise anything, name any date, or praise anything I have not verified? Three sentences usually fail, and they take ten minutes to fix.

Two minutes to draft and ten minutes to check is an excellent trade. What it is not is optional, because the two minutes of drafting produce precisely the kind of text that reads as finished when it is not.

> Source: reported practice in diplomatic communication, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why clearance covers short texts** — Communication guidance commonly applies the same clearance requirement to a two-minute toast as to a formal statement, on the basis that the audience makes no distinction between an informal remark and a position.

**A brief can be complete, well ordered and still carry one borrowed number**

An event brief is the single most useful document a mission produces in a month. One page, read by six people, that says what happens at what hour, who pays for what, who stands where and what the plan is if it rains.

**A programme is a promise to seven organisations, and an average is not a promise**

**Figure 7.5** — One average journey time, and every row after it is wrong.

A visit programme is not a plan for your own day. It is a promise made simultaneously to the delegation, to four ministries, to a port authority, to a university and to a hotel. When one hour fails, six organisations are inconvenienced, and only one of them is yours.

Building a programme hour by hour is genuinely hard by hand. There are fixed points, travel times, opening hours, meal breaks and a flight at the end. A system handles the arithmetic quickly and produces something dense and professional-looking.

The word to distrust is average. An average journey time is a true number about a city and a useless number about half past four on a Tuesday. Rush hours, bridges, markets and school runs are all invisible inside a single figure.

Margins are the answer, and they must be built from the worst recorded time rather than the usual one. A margin looks like waste on paper. It is the only thing standing between one late meeting and an afternoon in which nothing else happens.

Most missions already hold the right numbers. Drivers keep logs. Officers remember which bridge closes. Put six routes and their worst times on one page, give that page to the system, and the programme it builds will survive contact with the city.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A good general answer is accurate about a country and silent about a family**

**Figure 9.5** — Three answers matched. Three did not, and two of those were visible to everybody present.

Cultural questions divide into two kinds, and officers worry about the wrong one. The first kind is published: dress codes, forms of address, the hours of an office. These are written down, and a system answers them quickly and correctly.

The second kind is not published, because the people who need it are born into it. What is brought to a house of mourning, when a visit is made, what is said at a birth, how a gift is refused twice and accepted the third time. These vary by region and by family.

A general answer to a question of the second kind is not false. It describes the majority practice honestly. The trouble is that it arrives looking finished, and a finished answer stops you asking the person who would have known.

The cost is not embarrassment to you. In matters of death, faith and family, a visitor who gets it wrong makes a difficult day harder for people who are already having one, and the memory of it is long.

So keep a short list of questions that are never answered generally: condolence, faith, birth, marriage, hospitality in a private house. For every one of those, you ask a named person at the post, and you ask before you need the answer.

> **DATA POINT · Where cultural advice must be local** — Intercultural guidance commonly warns that national-level descriptions of custom conceal substantial regional, community and family variation, and that for acts of condolence or religious observance a named local adviser should be consulted in every case.  
> Source: reported practice in intercultural training, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Why a complete answer is risky** — Training in intercultural practice commonly notes that the confidence and completeness of an answer reduces the likelihood that a questioner seeks a second source, which makes fluent general advice more hazardous than an obviously partial answer.  
> Source: reported practice in intercultural training, 2025–2026.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A relationship that lives in one telephone is not a relationship the Mission has**

**Figure 10.5** — Two rows survived. The rows that mattered most were the ones in a person.

A mission has two kinds of knowledge. The first is written: reports, instructions, files. The second is personal: who answers the telephone, which brother to ask, who owes whom a favour. The second kind is more useful and it leaves the building every three years.

A contact record is the cheapest fix in diplomacy. Four lines per person, written the day you meet them. Who they are. Where and when you met. What they can actually do. What was last promised or asked, by whom, and when.

Turning rough notes into clean records is exactly the kind of work a system does well. Give it two scruffy lines and it returns a consistent entry with the name, the body, the date and the next step, in the same shape every time.

But it will dress a judgement as neatly as a fact. If your note says somebody seemed unreliable, the record will say it in careful official English, in a file that other people will read for years. The system improved the sentence, not the evidence.

So write facts, not verdicts. He arrived late and did not bring the paper. She answered the same day. A reader four years from now can weigh those. Nobody can weigh your opinion of a stranger formed at midnight on a Thursday.

> **DATA POINT · Why contact records are institutional** — Services commonly require that contacts made in an official capacity be recorded in a system belonging to the service rather than in personal devices, so that a relationship survives the departure of the officer who began it.  
> Source: reported practice in diplomatic administration, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Records are read by people you did not expect** — Records guidance commonly reminds officers that entries about individuals may be disclosed, inherited or read by colleagues years later, and should therefore record observable facts rather than personal assessments of character.  
> Source: reported records practice, 2025–2026.
