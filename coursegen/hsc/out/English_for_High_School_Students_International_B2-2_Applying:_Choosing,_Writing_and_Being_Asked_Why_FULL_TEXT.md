### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a sentence about yourself in anything you have written.
- Does it contain an adjective or a verb?
- If an adjective, what did you actually do?

At some point this year you will write about yourself, and the whole difficulty of that writing is contained in the difference between an adjective and a verb.

An adjective about yourself is a claim nobody can check. I am organised, I am hard-working, I am passionate about this subject: every one of those appears in thousands of applications and none of them carries any information, because the writer who is none of those things writes the same sentence.

A verb about yourself is a claim anybody can check. I ran a weekly problem class for eleven pupils for two years. I built the sign-up system the club now uses. I taught myself the second half of a syllabus from a library copy because the class had moved on.

And the verb does the adjective’s work for you, which is the part students find hard to believe. A reader who is told that you ran something for two years concludes that you are organised, and they conclude it on their own, which is the only way anybody believes anything about a stranger.

The method is one question asked of every sentence: what did I actually do? If the answer is nothing in particular, the sentence is a feeling and can be deleted without loss, and deleting it makes room for one of the four or five things you genuinely did.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: writing about yourself: I did, I built, I ran**

- The simple past with a first-person subject is the whole grammatical apparatus you need. I ran, I built, I organised, I taught, I wrote, I repaired. Each of them takes an object and each object is a thing that existed.
- A duration or a quantity attaches directly and does most of the persuading. I ran a weekly problem class for eleven pupils over two years.
- A result follows as its own clause and should contain a number. Attendance rose from four pupils to eleven over the two terms.
- The construction to avoid is I am plus an adjective of character, because it asserts a quality rather than reporting an action, and a reader has no way to distinguish an accurate one from an inaccurate one.

**Dialogue 2 · The thing she did not think counted**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An activity list rewritten as what you did rather than what you are**

**Figure 1.5** — Four checks, and a third of the draft turns out to be deletable.

An activity rewrite is the one task in this book where the raw material is entirely yours and the work is entirely mechanical, which makes it unusually well suited to being done in two passes with help in the middle.

Give the system your activity list exactly as you wrote it, with every adjective still in it, and say for each entry how long it lasted, how many people it involved and what happened as a result.

Ask it to list every adjective of character in your draft and to ask, for each one, what action it is standing in for, without supplying an answer.

Ask it to flag every verb that has no object or no number attached, because a verb without those is as uncheckable as an adjective and reads worse.

Ask it to identify which entries contain a problem, an action and a result, and which contain only an action, because an entry with all three is worth three with one.

What it cannot do is supply the actions. Only you and the people who were there know what you did, and the commonest thing missing from a draft is not an adjective but an entire action you did not think counted.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a course you are interested in.
- Find its module list rather than its description.
- How many modules do you actually choose?

A course name is a label agreed between institutions for the convenience of people filling in forms, and it tells you almost nothing about what three years of your life would contain.

The module list tells you. It is usually two or three clicks further in than the page you first find, and it is often a document rather than a page. It is also the only part of the material written for somebody who had already decided to look properly.

Three verbs do most of the work in a course description and they mean different things. Covers means it is taught. Requires means you must have it before you arrive. Assumes means you must have it and nobody will teach it to you.

Assumes is the dangerous one, because it appears in a sentence that looks like a description rather than a condition. A course that assumes familiarity with a topic has told you that the first six weeks will be difficult and has not told you it clearly.

And count the choice. A course in which eighteen of twenty modules are core is a different proposition from one in which eight are, and a student who wants to specialise and a student who wants breadth should be reading that number first and the name last.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: reading a course description: covers, requires, assumes**

- Covers takes the subject matter directly and reports that it is taught within the course. The course covers statistical inference in the second year.
- Requires takes a qualification or a grade and states a condition on entry, which means it will appear on your offer. The course requires mathematics at the higher level.
- Assumes takes knowledge rather than a qualification and states a condition that will not appear on your offer and will not be taught. The course assumes familiarity with elementary programming.
- The three verbs are not interchangeable and the difference between the second and the third is the difference between something an admissions office will tell you about and something you will discover in week two.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A course compared with two others on its modules**

**Figure 2.5** — Four checks across three courses, and the name stops deciding.

A course comparison is a document-handling task of exactly the kind that rewards being handed over, and the reason students do not do it is that the documents are long and dull rather than that the task is hard.

Give the system the module list of each course in full, including the optional modules and the year in which each is taught, and say which three courses you are comparing.

Ask it to produce a table of modules against courses, marking each as core, optional or absent, so that the overlap between any two of the three is countable.

Ask it to list every use of the verb assumes in all three descriptions, with the knowledge it names, because that list is the one that will govern your first term.

Ask it to report, for each course, how many modules you would choose yourself and in which year the choosing begins.

What it cannot do is tell you which course you want. A course with eighteen core modules is better for one person and worse for another, and the thing that decides is what you want your third year to look like, which is not in any of the documents.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Think of somebody who might recommend you.
- List what they actually saw you do.
- Could they name a specific piece of your work?

A recommendation is written by somebody who taught thirty of you a week for two years, and the quality of what they write depends almost entirely on what you put in front of them.

So a request is not a question; it is a small package. The course and institution you are applying for. The deadline, with the actual date. A reminder of what you did together, naming the term. One piece of your own work, attached. And a sentence making it easy to decline.

The deadline is the part students get wrong in both directions. Eleven days is not enough for a careful letter written between forty other things. Four months is too early, because it will be done in the last week anyway and by then your reminder is out of date.

Three or four weeks, with the date written out and a note of when you will send a reminder, is the shape that produces the best letters, and saying that you will send a reminder is a kindness rather than a threat.

And ask the right person rather than the most senior one. A teacher who supervised a piece of work you are proud of can write four specific sentences; a head of department who knows your name cannot, and a letter of general approval is worth less than a letter that names one thing you did.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Write one sentence about why you want to study your subject.

Every application and every interview will ask you why this subject, and the answer most people give is a feeling, which is the one kind of answer that cannot be distinguished from anybody else’s.

The test is mechanical and brutal. Write your answer, replace the name of the subject with the name of a different subject, and read it again. If it still works, you have not answered the question.

An answer that survives the test has three parts. A moment when you noticed something, which can be very small. A piece of work you did because of it, which is the part that makes the moment credible. And a question you still cannot answer, which is what shows the interest is live rather than finished.

The piece of work is the part students leave out and the part that does all the work. Anybody can say they found a topic fascinating; somebody who read a particular book and then spent a term trying to reproduce one calculation from it has said something nobody else can say.

And the hardest half of the question is why not the neighbouring subject. If you want to study physics, somebody will want to know why not engineering, and an answer that shows you know what the neighbouring subject actually involves is worth more than any amount of enthusiasm about your own.

**A course chosen by reading its modules rather than its reputation**

A why-this-university answer is checkable in a way that almost no other part of an application is, because every claim in it is either about a published document or about something you read.

Give the system your reasons for one institution, the module lists of the two courses you are comparing it with, and the name of anything you have actually read by somebody who teaches there.

## Unit 7 · Being Asked Why, Out Loud

*CEFR B2.2 · Grammar: an interview answer: claim, example, what changed · AI: an interview answer built as claim, example and what changed*

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Ask a partner why they chose their subject.
- When they answer, ask why again.
- And once more.

An interview is a conversation in which somebody asks you why until they find out whether you have thought about it, and the shape of a good answer is fixed enough to be practised.

Three parts. A claim, which is a position somebody could disagree with. An example, which is something that happened and preferably something you did. And what changed, which is how your view is different now from what it was before that example.

The third part is the one almost nobody says and the one the interview is for. A candidate who reports a changed mind has demonstrated that they are capable of changing it, which is the single most useful thing an institution can learn about somebody it is going to teach.

And you will be asked why again, which is not hostility. The second why asks for your reason, the third asks for the reason behind that one, and the person asking is finding the edge of what you have thought about, which is a real and necessary thing to find.

When you reach that edge, say so. I do not know, and then: what I would do is read the second chapter and see whether the same objection applies. That pair of sentences is worth more than any attempt to keep going, and the attempt is always visible.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: an interview answer: claim, example, what changed**

- The claim is a present-tense statement in the first person that somebody could dispute. I think the proof matters more than the application.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Write down one deadline you have this term.
- List everything that has to happen before it.
- Which of those depends on somebody else?

A deadline looks like a single date and is actually the last link in a chain, and the links you control are the easy ones.

So work backwards. The published closing date. The date you intend to submit, which is earlier. The date your last attachment has to exist. The date you have to ask somebody for something. And the date you will chase them if they have not replied.

The things somebody else owns are the only ones that can actually sink an application, because you cannot make them happen faster and you can only start them earlier. A reference, a transcript, a predicted grade, a signature: each of those is a date on somebody else’s desk.

A tracker is one row per application and six columns: the institution, the closing date, your own submission date, what is still missing, who owns it, and the date you will chase. It can be on paper. What it cannot be is in your head, because the week in which four of them land at once is the week your memory is worst.

And keep the confirmation. A portal that says submitted, an email receipt or a screenshot with a date on it proves more than your recollection of pressing the button. In the one case a year where something goes wrong it is the only thing that helps.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An application tracker built backwards from its deadlines**

**Figure 8.5** — Four checks on six rows, and the thing somebody else owns starts first.

A tracker is a table, which makes it the one piece of application work where handing over the arithmetic of dates is straightforwardly useful and carries no cost at all.

Give the system the closing date, time and time zone of each application, everything each one requires, who owns each requirement, and the dates you have already asked anybody for anything.

Ask it to build one row per application with six columns, and to compute your own submission date as one week before each published closing date.

Ask it to compute, for every requirement owned by somebody else, the date by which you must ask and the date on which you will chase, working backwards from your own submission date rather than the published one.

Ask it to list every requirement with no owner named, because a requirement with no owner is the one that arrives the day after the closing date.

What it cannot do is ask the people. Every date it computes for a dependency is a date on which you have to speak to somebody, and the computation is the easy half of a task whose hard half is a conversation.

You will decline things for the rest of your life, and the skill is almost entirely about where in the message the no appears.

Four sentences. Thanks for the offer, named. The decision, stated plainly in the second sentence and not the fourth. One sentence of reason, if you have one you are willing to give. And a closing that keeps the relationship.

The decision goes early because the person reading it is going to act on it. A reader who has to search three paragraphs for your answer is being made to do your work, which is the opposite of politeness however many apologies surround it.

You do not owe a reason, and a reason you do give should be true and brief. I have accepted a place elsewhere is complete. An invented reason is a liability in a field you are about to enter, where the same thirty people will keep appearing for forty years.

And there is a third option that students forget, which is not yet. A deferral is a request rather than a refusal, and it has to be asked for rather than assumed, with a date and a reason, and a great many institutions say yes to it more readily than anybody expects.

**A decline written so that a door stays open**

A decline is four sentences long, which makes the useful work not the writing but the placing, and placing is exactly the kind of thing that is easier to see from outside the message than inside it.

**Grammar: authorship: what is mine, what is not**

- The first person with a past verb is the whole grammar of authorship, and it is the construction you have been practising since the first unit of this book. I noticed, I built, I ran, I changed my mind.
