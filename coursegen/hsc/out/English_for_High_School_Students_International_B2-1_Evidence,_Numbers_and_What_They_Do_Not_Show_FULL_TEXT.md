### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a graph in one of your subject books.
- Read the vertical axis aloud, with its units.
- Now describe the line in one sentence.

A graph is a picture of numbers, and the picture can be made to look like almost anything by the choice of scale, which means that the words you use about the shape are claims about the scale rather than about the world.

The vocabulary of trends is small and exact. A quantity rose or fell, and it rose or fell by an amount, over a period. It levelled off, which means the rate of change fell towards zero. It remained steady, which means it did not change beyond the precision of the measurement. It fluctuated, which means it moved up and down with no single direction.

Every one of those words can be checked against the axis. Dramatic, sharp, enormous and collapsed cannot, because they describe how a line looks on a page, and how a line looks on a page is a decision somebody made about the vertical scale.

So the order of operations is fixed. Read the units. Read the range of each axis. Read the period covered. Only then describe the shape, and describe it in the units that the axis gave you rather than in adjectives.

And stop at the last point. A line that has been rising for four years tells you about those four years, and anything you say about the fifth is a prediction rather than a description, however confidently the line appears to be heading somewhere.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: describing a trend: rose, fell by, levelled off**

- Rose and fell take by for the amount and over or between for the period, and a sentence that omits either of those is a shape without a size. The figure rose by eleven units between the first and the fourth reading.
- Levelled off describes the rate of change rather than the quantity, which means that a quantity can level off while remaining very high, and a reader who misses that distinction draws the opposite conclusion.
- Remained steady is a claim about measurement precision rather than about the world, and it should be written with the precision stated: it remained steady to within one unit.
- Fluctuated takes no by, because there is no single amount of change to report, and it is the honest word for a line that students are often tempted to describe as rising.

**Dialogue 1 · Dramatic, on a range of three**

**Figure 1.3** — Oleh Danyliuk and Dr Ravi Menon, over one graph.

**Ravi:** You have described that rise as dramatic. Read me the vertical axis.
**Oleh:** Sixty-one at the bottom and sixty-four at the top.
**Ravi:** So the whole height of that page represents three units, and the line crosses most of it, which is why it looks like a cliff and why it is not one.
**Oleh:** The shape looked enormous.
**Ravi:** The shape was a decision made by whoever drew the axis, and a different decision would have made the same numbers look like a flat line with a slight wobble in it.
**Oleh:** So what am I allowed to say?
**Ravi:** What the axis gives you. It rose by two point six units over four years, and whether that is a lot is a separate sentence requiring a separate argument.
**Oleh:** That sounds much weaker.
**Ravi:** It sounds weaker and it is defensible, and in this subject an indefensible strong sentence scores below a defensible weak one every time.
**Oleh:** How do I decide whether two point six is a lot?
**Ravi:** By comparing it with something: the variation between repeats, the size of the effect people care about, or the change over the previous decade.
**Oleh:** And if I have none of those?
**Ravi:** Then you say it rose by two point six units and you stop, because a reader with no comparison can draw no conclusion and it is not your job to supply them with a feeling instead of one.

**Dialogue 2 · The fifth year nobody measured**

**Figure 1.4** — Dr Ravi Menon and Zofia Kaminska, over the end of a line.

**Ravi:** Your last sentence says the figure will continue to rise. Which point on the graph is that based on?
**Zofia:** The direction of the line at the end.
**Ravi:** The direction of a line at its last point tells you about the interval before it and nothing whatever about the interval after it.
**Zofia:** But it has risen for four years.
**Ravi:** It has, and a quantity that has risen for four years is exactly the kind of quantity that is about to level off, which is why the word trend is dangerous in a conclusion.
**Zofia:** So I can never predict?
**Ravi:** You can predict, in a sentence that says it is a prediction and gives the assumption it rests on: if the conditions of the last four years continue, the figure would be expected to reach a certain value.
**Zofia:** That is a long sentence for one guess.
**Ravi:** It is a long sentence because it is doing two jobs, and the second job is telling your reader which of your sentences they are allowed to rely on.
**Zofia:** Does anybody really separate those?
**Ravi:** Everybody whose work has consequences does, and the habit of separating description from prediction is most of what distinguishes a scientific register from an ordinary one.
**Zofia:** Is it the same in geography?
**Ravi:** Ask Mr Okoye, because I believe he would say the same thing about projections and add that the assumption is the part that gets argued about for the next twenty years, and that is a conversation I would rather you had with him than with me.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A trend described with no word the axis denies**

**Figure 1.5** — Four checks before a sentence, and no adjective survives the third.

A trend description is the point at which a student most often writes a sentence that the figure in front of them does not support, and it is also the easiest of those failures to prevent, because the check is arithmetic rather than judgement.

Give the system the figure itself together with the axis labels, the units, the start and end values of each axis, and the period covered, because a trend cannot be checked against a picture alone.

Ask it to produce a description using only the vocabulary of rose by, fell by, levelled off, remained steady and fluctuated, with an amount and a period attached to each of the first two.

Ask it to list every adjective of magnitude that your own draft description contains, and to say for each one what quantitative comparison would be needed before that adjective could be justified.

Ask it to flag any sentence of yours that refers to a point beyond the last measurement, and to restate it as a conditional prediction with its assumption named.

What it cannot do is tell you whether a change matters. Two point six units is a fact; whether two point six units is large is a question about the thing being measured and about what the people reading care about, and nothing in the figure contains that.

> **DATA POINT · Axis scaling** — A vertical axis that does not begin at zero exaggerates the apparent size of a change, and guidance on presenting data commonly requires the range of an axis to be stated when a shape is described. What is required is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Extrapolation** — Extending a trend beyond the range of measured data is commonly distinguished from description and treated as a prediction requiring its assumptions to be stated. What is required is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a percentage in a newspaper or a textbook.

- Say how many that group contains.

A proportion is a relation between a part and a whole, which means that a proportion given without its whole has had the informative half removed, and most of the proportions you will read in your life are given that way.

So the first question is always of what, and the second is of how many. Forty per cent is a different claim about forty thousand people than it is about five, and a reader who is not told which has been given a feeling rather than a figure.

One proportion can be stated three ways, and it is worth being able to move between them. Two fifths, two in five and forty per cent are the same number wearing three coats, and one of the three will usually be clearer for your reader.

A percentage and a percentage point are not the same thing, and the difference is the commonest arithmetic error in school writing about data. A rise from four per cent to six per cent is a rise of two percentage points and of fifty per cent, and both of those sentences are true.

And doubling is the weakest strong word available. A quantity that rises from two to four has doubled, and so has a quantity that rises from two thousand to four thousand, and the first of those is usually noise while the second may be a result.

**Grammar: proportions: twice as many, a third of, three in five**

- Twice as many takes as before the thing compared with, and a sentence using it is incomplete until both quantities are available to the reader. Twice as many pupils used the lit route as used the direct path.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find two quantities in your work that rose together.

- Does your verb claim a cause?

When two quantities move together, you have observed one thing, which is that they move together, and the number of sentences you can honestly write about it is smaller than it feels.

English makes this hard, because the ordinary verbs of connection are causal. Reduced, improved, led to, resulted in and caused all assert that one thing produced another, and a student reaching for a natural-sounding verb reaches for a causal one without noticing.

The verbs that state an association and stop are fewer and flatter. It is associated with. It varies with. It is followed by. It coincides with. None of them is elegant and all of them are defensible.

There are three standard explanations for an association that are not the one you want: a third factor causing both, the causation running the other way, and coincidence in a short series. Naming which of the three you have ruled out, and how, is the content of a good paragraph.

And none of this means you cannot act. A school that lights a path because the unlit path is associated with more incidents is behaving reasonably. The honest sentence is that the association is strong enough to act on while the cause remains untested, and most adults never learn to write it.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: association and cause: is associated with, is followed by**

- Is associated with takes the second quantity directly and claims nothing about direction or mechanism. The unlit route is associated with a higher number of reported incidents.

- Reduced, improved, led to, resulted in and caused all assert that one quantity produced the other, and each of them is a claim about a controlled comparison you have probably not made.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A correlation stated without the causal verb**

**Figure 3.5** — Four checks, and the sentence says what you actually saw.

A correlation statement is the sentence in which most school science quietly overclaims, and the overclaim is almost always carried by a single verb that sounded natural at the time.

Give the system your draft sentence, the two quantities, the period over which both were measured, the number of points you have, and anything else you know changed during that period.

Ask it to flag every verb in your sentence that asserts a causal relation, and to offer the association verbs that would state the same observation without that claim.

Ask it to list candidate third factors from what you have told it changed during the period, and to say for each one whether it could plausibly have produced both observations.

Ask it to state the reverse causal story explicitly, and to say what evidence about timing would be needed to rule it out.

What it cannot do is decide whether the association is strong enough to act on. That depends on what the action costs, what the harm is, and how urgent the decision is, and none of those is in your data.

> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Controlled comparison** — Establishing a causal relation is commonly described as requiring a comparison in which one factor is changed and others are held constant. What is required is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Take any experiment you have done.
- Write three columns: changed, measured, held the same.
- Put every quantity you can think of into one of them.

An experiment is a sentence about one quantity, and the sentence is only true if exactly one quantity was free to vary while you were watching another.

So every quantity involved belongs in one of three columns. The one you changed, on purpose, over a stated range. The one you measured, in stated units. And the ones you held the same, each of them named.

The third column is the one students leave half empty, and it is the one that decides whether the result means anything, because a quantity you did not list is a quantity you did not think about and therefore did not hold.

Sometimes you cannot hold something the same, and the honest move is to put it in the third column with a note saying so rather than to leave it out. An experiment with a known uncontrolled variable and a sentence admitting it is worth more than one that pretends the variable did not exist.

And a comparison needs something to compare with. A control is the same setup with the changed quantity left alone, and without one you have a measurement rather than a result, however carefully the measurement was taken.

**Grammar: variables: changed, measured, held the same**

- The three verbs take the same construction and should be used in the same order every time. The quantity I changed was the length of the wire. The quantity I measured was the current. The quantities I held the same were the supply voltage, the cross-section and the temperature of the room.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**An experiment split into changed, measured and held the same**

**Figure 4.5** — Four checks on three columns, and the leftovers are the interesting part.

A variable split is a sorting task, and the value of handing it over is that a system has no stake in your experiment having worked, which is the one thing you cannot bring to your own method.

Give the system your method exactly as written, the apparatus you used, and where and when you did it, because the room and the hour are often where the uncontrolled variable lives.

Ask it to sort every quantity mentioned anywhere in your method into changed, measured or held the same, and to say which column each one went in and why.

Ask it to list every quantity it can identify from the apparatus and the setting that your method does not mention at all, because that list is the one worth reading.

Ask it to flag any case where more than one quantity appears to have changed across your series, and to say which of your conclusions would survive if the second change were responsible.

What it cannot do is tell you which uncontrolled variables matter. Whether a two-degree drift in room temperature affects your result is a question about the physics of what you were measuring, and that judgement is the subject knowledge the task exists to assess.

## Unit 5 · Narrowing a Claim to Its Sample

*CEFR B2.1 · Grammar: narrowing a claim: within, among, between · AI: a claim narrowed to the sample it came from*

**Figure 5.0** — A CLAIM IS ONLY TRUE OF THE PEOPLE YOU ASKED.: Oleh Danyliuk surveyed thirty-one pupils from his own year group and wrote a conclusion about pupils.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a conclusion you have written from data.
- Say who was actually asked or measured.
- Put those people into the sentence.

Every number you collect comes from somebody or something in particular, and the claim it supports is a claim about those particular people or things and nothing else, which is a sentence most writing about data quietly declines to accept.

Narrowing is cheap. Within this sample, among those surveyed, between the two groups tested: a phrase of three or four words placed at the front of the sentence, and the claim becomes defensible without becoming weaker in any way that matters.

The two questions that generate the phrase are who was asked and who was not. Thirty-one pupils from one year group, in one school, on one morning, who chose to fill in a form. Each of those four facts narrows the claim, and leaving them out does not widen it; it only hides the narrowness.

Self-selection is the sharpest of the four. The people who filled in your form are the people who cared enough to fill in your form, and on a question about whether something is a problem, that is the opposite of a neutral group.

And there is a real distinction between a conclusion about this school and a conclusion about schools. The first is what your data can support and is often exactly what somebody needs. The second requires a sample you did not take, and claiming it loses you the credit for the one you did.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: narrowing a claim: within, among, between**

- Within takes a named sample and sits at the front of the sentence with a comma after it. Within this sample of thirty-one pupils, the lit route was preferred by nineteen.
- Among takes the people and suits a survey, and it carries the number of them when the number is small enough to matter. Among those surveyed, nineteen of thirty-one reported the same preference.
- Between takes exactly two things and suits a comparison, and a sentence using between for three groups is reporting a comparison it did not make.
- All three are placed before the claim rather than after it, because a reader who reaches the end of an unqualified claim has already believed it and a qualification arriving late reads as a retreat.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A claim narrowed to the sample it came from**

**Figure 5.5** — Four checks, and a wide claim becomes a defensible one.

A claim narrowing is the single highest-value edit available to a student writing from data, because it costs four words and it converts an indefensible sentence into a defensible one without changing anything you actually found.

Give the system your draft conclusion, a description of exactly who or what was measured, how they came to be included, when and where the measuring happened, and how many were not included.

Ask it to rewrite the conclusion with a narrowing phrase at the front, using within for a sample, among for people surveyed and between for exactly two groups compared.

Ask it to list every noun in your draft conclusion that refers to a group wider than the one you measured, because those nouns are where an over-general claim actually lives.

Ask it to state what would have to be true of your sample for the wider claim to follow, and to say whether anything you have told it establishes that.

What it cannot do is decide whether the narrow claim is enough for your purpose. A conclusion about thirty-one pupils may be exactly what the School Council needs or may be useless to them, and that depends on what they are deciding.

> **DATA POINT · Sampling** — Guidance on reporting survey and experimental results commonly requires the sampled group to be described and conclusions to be limited to it unless the sample is shown to represent a wider population. What is required is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Self-selection** — A sample composed of those who chose to respond is commonly described as unrepresentative of the group from which it was drawn, particularly on questions about which respondents hold strong views. What is required is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Take any figure from a newspaper or a textbook.
- List three things it does not tell you.
- Would any of the three change your reading?

Every figure is the result of a series of decisions about what to include, and the decisions that matter most are the ones about what to leave out, because an omission is invisible and a reader has to go looking for it.

The standard omissions are a short list and worth learning. No units on an axis. No baseline, so that a bar chart starts somewhere above zero. No indication of how many observations lie behind each point. No comparison group. No source. No statement of what was excluded.

Two further omissions are about time. What happened before the graph starts, and what happened after it ends. A figure that begins in the year a quantity was at its lowest will show a rise whatever happened in the decade before, and choosing a start year is the commonest way to make a true graph tell a false story.

So the order is to list the omissions before you make the claim, in writing, because an omission you noticed and did not write down is one you will have forgotten by the time you reach your conclusion.

And the useful exercise at the end of the list is to write the question you would ask the author. If you would ask how many pupils each point represents, then your claim cannot depend on that number, and you have just learnt what your claim is allowed to be.

**Grammar: what is missing: the axis, the range, the sample**

- An omission is reported with a verb of absence rather than a verb of error, because the figure is not wrong; it is silent. The figure gives no units for the vertical axis.

There are two kinds of number in your work and they behave entirely differently. A count is exact: thirty-one pupils is thirty-one pupils, and there is no uncertainty in it at all. A measurement is never exact, and reporting one without its precision is the commonest numerical fault in school writing.

The vocabulary is small. About, for a measurement whose precision you know roughly. To the nearest, followed by a unit, for one you know properly. Plus or minus, followed by a quantity, for one you have characterised. Exactly, for counts and for nothing else.

At least and at most are different in kind: they are bounds rather than estimates, and they are the honest words for a quantity you could not measure directly. At least nineteen pupils used the route, because nineteen were observed and others may have passed unseen.

Writing more digits than your instrument supports is a claim about the instrument. A ruler marked in millimetres gives you 23.4 centimetres and no more, and 23.4718 asserts a measurement nobody made.

And the reverse fault is real too. Rounding 23.4 to 23 throws away a digit you paid for, and in a subject where differences of a millimetre matter, rounding too soon loses the result as surely as rounding too late invents one.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: precision: about, exactly, at least, at most**

- Exactly precedes a count and asserts that there is no uncertainty, which is true of counts and of nothing you have measured. Exactly thirty-one pupils returned a form.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A number’s precision matched to how it was measured**

**Figure 7.5** — Four checks on one number, and three digits disappear.

A precision check is pure arithmetic and pure honesty at the same time, which makes it one of the few tasks where handing the arithmetic over costs you nothing at all.

Give the system every number you intend to report, say for each one whether it was counted or measured, name the instrument used and what it can resolve, and give the spread between repeats where you have repeats.

Ask it to state for each number how many significant figures the instrument supports, and to flag every number in your draft carrying more digits than that.

Ask it to assign each number the correct construction: exactly for a count, about for a measurement with no characterised precision, to the nearest for one with a known resolution, and plus or minus for one with a measured spread.

Ask it to flag every number you have rounded to fewer digits than your instrument supports, because that fault is less discussed and loses results just as effectively.

What it cannot do is tell you how precise you need to be. Whether a millimetre matters depends on the difference you are trying to detect, and a measurement to the nearest centimetre can be entirely adequate or entirely useless for the same apparatus.

> **DATA POINT · Significant figures** — Reporting a measurement to more digits than the measuring instrument supports is commonly treated as an error of precision, and the number of significant figures is expected to reflect the instrument. What is required is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Bounds** — Where a quantity cannot be observed completely, reporting it as a lower or upper bound rather than as a point value is commonly treated as the accurate form. What is required is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a condition in your own writing.
- Ask who would check it, and how.
- If you cannot answer, it is a hope.

A condition is a sentence that transfers a decision to the future, and the quality of a condition is measured entirely by whether somebody arriving later could apply it without asking you what you meant.

Three constructions cover almost everything. If, for a positive condition. Unless, for a negative one, which is often shorter and clearer than if not. Provided that, for a condition attached to a permission, which is where it belongs and where it does real work.

A testable condition has four parts, and students usually write one of them. A quantity. A threshold. A person who checks it. And a time at which the checking happens. If the wind speed exceeds twenty kilometres an hour, as measured by the groundsman at eight in the morning.

The fourth part is the one nobody writes and the one that causes the arguments. A condition with no stated time of checking will be checked by whoever wants a particular answer at whatever moment suits them.

And every condition needs a fifth sentence somewhere: what happens when the condition fails. A condition with no stated consequence is an invitation to improvise, and improvisation under time pressure is how a reasonable plan turns into a bad afternoon.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: conditions: if, unless, provided that**

- If introduces a positive condition and its clause takes a comma when it comes first. If the wind speed exceeds twenty kilometres an hour, the route is closed.
- Unless introduces a negative condition and replaces if not, which makes it shorter and removes a negation a reader has to hold. Unless the gate is manned, the route stays closed.
- Provided that attaches a condition to a permission and is the right construction when something is being allowed rather than described. The route may be used provided that the gate is manned.
- Only if makes the condition necessary rather than sufficient, and as long as makes it a condition that must hold throughout rather than at a moment, and those two distinctions are where conditions are most often written loosely.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A condition written so that it could actually be tested**

**Figure 8.5** — Four checks, and a hope becomes something somebody can apply.

A condition check is the clearest case in this book of a task where a system has an advantage over its author, because the author knows what they meant and the system has only what they wrote.

Give the system your condition exactly as drafted, the decision it governs, who will be applying it, and whether it has to hold at a moment or throughout a period.

Ask it to report which of the four parts is present and which absent, naming the quantity, the threshold, the checking role and the time of checking separately.

Ask it to rewrite any negative condition expressed with if not as an unless clause, because a reader holding a negation inside a condition makes mistakes a reader holding unless does not.

Ask it to flag the absence of a stated consequence of failure, which is the fifth element and the one that turns a condition into a plan.

Two small habits separate writing about data that reads as competent from writing that does not, and neither of them has anything to do with how clever the analysis was.

The first is agreement. Data is a plural in scientific writing: the data show, not the data shows. One of them is a datum, which you will rarely need. Criteria is the plural of criterion, phenomena of phenomenon, and a sentence that gets one of those wrong is read as a sentence by somebody unfamiliar with the field.

Some subjects now accept data as a singular, which means the right move is not to memorise a rule but to ask which convention the subject you are writing in uses, and then to be consistent inside one piece of work.

The second habit is units. Every number in a sentence carries its unit in the same sentence, including the ones in a comparison. The speed rose from eleven to twenty-three metres per second, not from eleven to twenty-three.

A conclusion is the last sentence a marker reads carefully, and it is the sentence in which the whole of the work is either collected or thrown away.

The requirement is simple to state and hard to meet. A conclusion must name the figure it rests on, and that figure must be larger than the uncertainty you reported in the figure it came from. A difference of two point six units with a scatter of four is not a result, however carefully it was measured.

Three constructions do the work. Therefore, for a consequence you have shown. On this evidence, for one you have shown with a sample you want to keep visible. It follows that, for a step a reader could check themselves.

A limitation sentence is not an apology and it does not weaken a conclusion. It names what would change the conclusion, which is a different and stronger thing than admitting to a weakness. If the scatter between repeats were reduced below one unit, the difference reported here would become distinguishable from noise.

And the best last sentence in a piece of school science is the next measurement. Naming the measurement that would test your own conclusion is the clearest evidence a reader can have that you understood what you did.

**Grammar: a conclusion: therefore, on this evidence, it follows that**

- Therefore introduces a consequence you have shown, and it should be followed within the same sentence by the figure the consequence rests on. Therefore, on a difference of two point six units, the lit route carries fewer reported incidents.
