## Unit 1 · A Position Somebody Could Disagree With

**Figure 1.0** — IF NOBODY COULD DISAGREE, YOU HAVE NOT SAID ANYTHING.: Mateo Alvarez opened a debate with a sentence that every single person in the room already agreed with.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- State a position about school uniform in one sentence.
- Could somebody disagree with it?
- What exactly would they be disagreeing with?

The first thing you write in any argument is a position, and the commonest mistake is writing a sentence nobody would dispute.

Pollution is a problem is not a position. Everybody in the room agrees, so there is nothing for the rest of your paragraph to do.

A position has edges. Somebody has to be able to stand outside it and say no, and say what they would say instead.

Test it with one question: what would a reasonable person who disagreed with me say? If you cannot answer, you have not made a claim yet.

And narrow it until you can defend it. Our school should ban cars from the gate at drop-off time is narrow, arguable and defensible in four sentences.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: stating a position: I think, I would argue, in my view**

- I would argue that announces a position you are about to defend, and is the strongest of the three. I would argue that the gate should be closed to cars at drop-off.
- In my view does the same work in three words, and suits a shorter piece. In my view the entrance is unsafe at half past eight.
- I think is weaker and belongs in speech rather than in a written argument. I think the second option is better.
- The evidence suggests removes you from the sentence where the evidence is doing the work. The accident figures suggest that the entrance is unsafe.

**Figure 1.2** — I would argue that. In my view. The evidence suggests. A reasonable person might.

**Dialogue 2 · Too large to defend**

**Figure 1.4** — Ms Clara Benedek and Mateo Alvarez, narrowing a claim.

**Mateo:** My position is that cars should be banned from all schools.
**Clara:** All schools. In how many sentences are you defending that?
**Mateo:** Four. It is a paragraph.
**Clara:** Then you are claiming something about every school in the country and defending it in four sentences, and the first objection will destroy it.
**Mateo:** Which objection?
**Clara:** A rural school where the nearest bus stop is three kilometres away. One example, and your all is gone.
**Mateo:** So I say most schools.
**Clara:** Most is still an empirical claim about hundreds of places you know nothing about. Narrow it to the one you do know.

**Clara:** It is a smaller claim and a much stronger argument, and the two are almost always in that relation.
**Mateo:** Does a narrow claim score less?
**Clara:** A narrow claim defended scores far more than a wide one gestured at, and that is the whole of what I am marking.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A position stated so that somebody could disagree with it**

**Figure 1.5** — Four tests, one sentence, and an argument that has somewhere to go.

Drafting a position is the one part of writing an argument where you cannot see your own mistake, because a sentence you agree with looks like a strong sentence.

Give the system your position as you wrote it, the topic, the length you have to defend it in, and what you actually know about the case.

Ask it to name who in a classroom could reasonably disagree with your sentence, and what they would say instead.

Ask it to say whether your claim is too large for the length you have, and to offer a narrower version drawn only from what you said you know.

Ask it to check that a reason is attached, because an opinion without a reason is half a sentence and a position is the whole one.

What it cannot do is hold the position. It can tell you that a sentence is arguable; it cannot tell you whether you actually believe it, and an argument you do not believe is detectable in about two paragraphs.

> **DATA POINT · Argument** — Subjects that assess argument commonly expect a stated position early in the piece and expect it to be one that could be opposed. What is expected is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Topic sentences** — Many subjects expect the position of a paragraph to appear in its first sentence, so that a reader knows what the paragraph will defend. What is expected is set by each subject.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

### Part 6 · Guided Writing

**Model**

Purpose: so that my opening sentence is a claim a reasonable classmate could oppose, and narrow enough for me to defend. Scope: one position on one topic; it tests my sentence and takes no side. Inputs: my position exactly as I wrote it, the topic, the number of sentences or words I have to defend it in, and what I actually know about the case from my own experience or from a source. **STAGE 5 · The Decision**

**Write the rule, in four lines.**

- Say how much common ground is allowed.
- Say what the second sentence must be.
- Say what the student must be able to name.
- Say what happens to a universal claim.

*Language bank for the decision*
- One sentence of common ground is allowed at the opening of an argument, and no more, because common ground is context rather than claim.

## Unit 2 · A Reason That Can Be Tested

*CEFR B1.1 · Grammar: a reason you can test: because, since, given that · AI: a reason reworded until it can be tested*

**Figure 2.0** — A REASON YOU CANNOT CHECK IS A SECOND OPINION.: Mateo Alvarez gave three reasons. All three were the claim again, in slightly different words.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Give a reason for a position you hold.
- How could somebody check it?
- Is it a reason, or the claim again?

The second sentence of an argument is a reason, and the commonest failure is to restate the claim and call it one.

The gate should be closed because it is dangerous is not two sentences. Dangerous is the claim, and closed is the claim, and nothing has been added.

A reason can be tested. Somebody could go and look, count, read, or ask. Four cars reverse at the gate between half past eight and nine can be counted.

So attach a number, a source or an observation to every reason. The number does not have to be large; it has to be checkable.

And give one strong reason rather than three weak ones. Three weak reasons invite three objections. One checkable reason invites a counting exercise, which you win.

## Unit 3 · Building the Other Side First

*CEFR B1.1 · Grammar: the other side: some people argue, it could be said · AI: the strongest version of the argument against you*

**Figure 3.0** — BUILD THE OTHER SIDE AT ITS STRONGEST. THEN ANSWER IT.: Mateo Alvarez demolished an argument nobody had made, and the one somebody had made went unanswered.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- What is the best argument against your position?
- Have you written it down?
- Could the other side agree that you stated it fairly?

Every argument has a reader who already disagrees, and that reader has one particular objection in their head while they read.

If you do not answer that objection, nothing else you write reaches them. They are waiting for it, and the waiting is louder than your reasons.

So build the other side first, at its strongest. Some people argue that the walk from a further drop-off point is unsafe in winter.

A weak version of the other side is called a straw man. Knocking it down proves nothing, and a reader who holds the strong version notices immediately.

Then concede what is true and answer what is not. The walk is longer, which is true. It is on a lit pavement away from reversing cars, which answers it.

A class discussion, on paper or on a screen, asks you to do two things: answer somebody, and add something.

Most replies do neither. They restate the writer’s own opinion in new words and could have been posted under any question.

- Make a claim from one observation.
- Which word did you use: proves, shows or suggests?

A claim has a size, and the size is set by the evidence under it, not by how strongly you feel.

Three mornings of counting cars do not prove anything. They suggest something, and suggest is a perfectly respectable word.

A hedge is honest when it names what you do not know. It is cowardly when it stops anybody disagreeing with you, which is the thing an argument needs.

**Grammar: how sure you are: may, might, tends to, probably**

- May and might say that something is possible and that you have not ruled it out. It may be that December is different.

**A hedge audit: every claim sized to what you can support**

**Figure 5.5** — Four checks, one paragraph, and every claim the size of its evidence.

A hedge audit is mechanical and nobody does it, which is why the verb between the evidence and the claim is the commonest circled word in school writing.

Give the system your paragraph, and beside it exactly what evidence you hold: how many observations, over what period, from what source.

Ask it to list every claim in the paragraph with the verb that carries it, so that you can see suggests, shows and proves in one column.

Ask it to say, for each claim, whether the evidence you described supports that verb, is weaker than it, or is stronger than it.

Ask it to count the hedges in each sentence and to flag any sentence with more than one, because two hedges in a sentence is usually hiding rather than caution.

What it cannot do is know how much evidence is enough in your subject. Three years is a long run in one subject and a pilot study in another, and the only person who can tell you is the one marking it.

> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Overclaiming** — Mark schemes commonly penalise a claim that goes beyond the evidence presented, and some also penalise a claim that falls short of it. What is penalised is set by each subject.

- Read one of your own paragraphs aloud.

The commonest punctuation error in school writing is a comma doing a job it cannot do.

A comma can separate items, add information, or hold a word apart. It cannot join two complete sentences. That job needs a full stop, a semicolon or a joining word.

The test is simple. Cover each half of the sentence in turn. If both halves could stand alone, a comma between them is wrong.

The gate is unsafe, four cars reverse there is two sentences. The gate is unsafe because four cars reverse there is one.

And read it aloud. Where you breathe is usually where a sentence wants to end, and the ninety-one-word sentence is the one you cannot read without stopping.

**Grammar: sentence boundaries: the full stop, the comma splice**

- Find two short sentences in a row in your own work.

Two short sentences in a row are not an error. A paragraph of nine of them reads like a list, and a reader cannot tell which idea matters.

Joining them is the repair. But every joining word makes a claim, and the claim can be false while both sentences are true.

And adds. But contrasts. So says the first caused the second. Because says the second caused the first. Although says the second happens despite the first.

So the join decides two things: what you claim about the relation, and which of the two ideas the reader will remember.

- And, but and so are coordinating. Both halves keep equal weight, and a comma usually comes before but and so. The route is darker, but it is lit.
- Because and although are subordinating. The half they introduce becomes the supporting one. The route is darker because two lamps are out.
- When the because or although half comes first, it takes a comma after it. Although the route is darker, more people use it.
- When it comes second, it usually takes no comma. More people use it although the route is darker.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A punctuation pass that names the job of each mark**

**Figure 8.5** — Four checks, and every mark left in the paragraph has a job.

A punctuation pass is a job audit. It does not ask whether a mark is allowed; it asks what the mark is for, and it deletes the ones with no answer.

Give the system your paragraph and ask it to list every semicolon, colon and dash in it, in order, with the words either side.

Ask it to name the job of each one in a single phrase: joining balanced clauses, announcing a list, announcing an explanation, enclosing a phrase, or ending sharply.

Ask it to mark any colon whose preceding words do not form a complete sentence, and any single dash that is neither one of a pair nor at the end.

Ask it to offer, for each mark, the quietest alternative that would do the same job, which is usually a comma and sometimes a full stop.

What it cannot do is decide how loud your writing should be. One dash in a paragraph is a voice; nine is a tic; and where your writing sits between those is a choice about how you sound.

**Grammar: what a pronoun points at: this, it, they, which**

- Every pronoun needs an antecedent a reader can find without effort, and the antecedent is a noun, not an idea spread over three sentences.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A two-minute spoken answer planned in four lines**

**Figure 10.5** — Four lines on paper, and nothing else, because sentences get read aloud.

A spoken answer plan is four lines long and must stay four lines long, because anything longer stops being a plan and becomes a script you will read.

Give the system the question and tell it you have two minutes and want three points of roughly forty seconds each.

Ask it for four lines only: one naming the shape, and three each holding one idea, one example and one consequence in note form.

Ask it explicitly for notes rather than sentences, because a full sentence on the page will be read out and a listener can hear reading.

Ask it to end the fourth line with the words you will use to stop, so the ending is planned rather than improvised.

What it cannot do is choose your three points. It can offer six; which three you can actually say something about, with an example from your own experience, is the whole of the preparation.
