### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find the last source you wrote about.
- Say its argument in one sentence.
- Would the writer sign that sentence?

Almost every essay you write from now on will begin by reporting what somebody else argued, and most of the marks for that reporting are lost in one of two ways.

The first is reporting a weaker argument than the writer made, because a weaker one is easier to answer. A reader who has the source in front of them sees it at once.

The second is the reporting verb. Argues and claims and suggests and shows are not interchangeable. Claims hints that you do not believe it. Shows concedes that it is established.

There is one test, and it is harsh. Write the writer’s position in a single sentence, and ask whether the writer would sign it. If they would object, you have not reported them; you have answered somebody else.

Report first, in their terms. Disagree afterwards, in yours. An essay that does those two things in that order is already in the top band for the use of sources.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: reporting a writer: argues, claims, suggests, shows**

- Argues is the neutral reporting verb and it is the one to use unless you intend something more. The writer argues that the figures are incomplete.
- Claims carries doubt. A marker reads it as you signalling disagreement before you have given a reason, which costs you the reason.
- Suggests reports something the writer put forward tentatively, and it is wrong where the writer was certain. Shows reports something established, and it concedes the point.
- All four take that and a clause. The writer argues that, she claims that, the study suggests that, the data show that. The clause is the writer’s position, not yours.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A writer’s argument restated in one sentence they would accept**

**Figure 1.5** — Four checks, and you answer the argument that was actually made.

A one-sentence restatement is the cheapest insurance in academic writing, because the whole of an essay built on a misreading is worth very little however well it is built.

Give the system the source in full, not an extract, because the main claim is often not in the paragraph you were looking at.

Ask it to produce one sentence stating the writer’s position in terms the writer would accept, using the verb argues and no other.

Ask it to list separately any view that appears in the source but is one the writer is reporting in order to reject, because that is the view students answer by mistake.

Ask it to name the sentence in the source that the rest of the source supports, and to give its location.

What it cannot do is tell you whether the writer is right. It can give you the position they would sign; whether you then agree with it is the entire remaining essay, and it is yours.

> **DATA POINT · Reporting sources** — Assessments of academic writing commonly credit an accurate account of a source’s position before any response to it, and treat a misreported position as a failure of comprehension rather than of argument. What is credited is set by each assessment.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Misattribution** — Attributing to a source a view it reports in order to reject is commonly treated as a comprehension error in assessed writing. What is marked is set by each assessment.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

**Figure 2.0** — CLOSE THE BOOK. WRITE THE IDEA. THEN OPEN IT AND COMPARE.: Sunita Pillai replaced eleven words of a sentence with synonyms, kept its shape exactly, and produced something that was still a quotation.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Take one sentence from a source.
- Read it twice and close the book.
- Write what it said.

A paraphrase is the same idea in your own structure. Most school paraphrases are the same structure with some of the words swapped, and that is a different thing with a different name.

The reason is method. A student paraphrasing with the source open in front of them edits the sentence. A student paraphrasing from memory rebuilds the idea.

So close the book. Read the sentence twice, look away, and write what it said. Then open the book and compare the two side by side.

What you are looking for in that comparison is any run of three words that appears in both. Three words is where a marker starts to see the original through your version.

Two exceptions. A technical term has no synonym and must be kept: photosynthesis stays photosynthesis. And a paraphrase still names its source, because changing the words does not make the idea yours.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: paraphrasing: in other words, that is to say**

- In other words introduces your restatement of something just said, and it takes a comma after it. In other words, the expense was never the obstacle.
- That is to say does the same work in a more formal register and is commoner in written than spoken English.
- Both can follow a quotation or a report. The writer argues that the cost is recoverable. In other words, she accepts the figure and disputes the conclusion.
- Neither is a licence to repeat. If the sentence after in other words says the same thing in the same structure, delete one of them.

**Dialogue 2 · The synonym that moved the claim**

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A paraphrase checked word by word against its original**

**Figure 2.5** — Four checks on two versions, and the patchwork shows up at once.

A paraphrase check is a comparison, not a rewrite, and it must be run on a paraphrase you wrote from memory or it has nothing useful to compare.

Give the system both texts: the original sentence or passage, and your version, clearly labelled as which is which.

Ask it to list every run of three or more consecutive words that appears in both, and to say for each one whether it is a technical term with no ordinary synonym.

Ask it to compare the clause order of the two versions and say whether your structure is genuinely different or the original one with substitutions.

Ask it to name any claim that is stronger, weaker or reversed in your version, because a synonym from a thesaurus moves meaning more often than students expect.

What it cannot do is write the paraphrase. A paraphrase produced by a system is not evidence that you understood the sentence, and understanding the sentence is the only reason the exercise exists.

> **DATA POINT · Paraphrase** — Guidance on academic writing commonly distinguishes a paraphrase, which restructures an idea, from a substitution of synonyms, and requires both to be attributed. What is required is set by each institution.  
> Source: general practice overviews, 2025–2026.

> **DATA POINT · Patchwork writing** — Substituting synonyms while retaining a source’s sentence structure is commonly treated as inadequate paraphrase in academic writing guidance. What is required is set by each institution.

## Unit 3 · Where the Quotation Goes

*CEFR B1.2 · Grammar: the quotation: punctuation, capital, attribution · AI: a quotation placed, punctuated and attributed*

**Figure 3.0** — A QUOTATION PROVES NOTHING UNTIL YOU SAY WHAT IT PROVES.: Sunita Pillai quoted forty-one words, explained none of them, and was asked in the margin what she thought they showed.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a quotation in your last essay.
- Read the sentence after it.
- Does that sentence say what the quotation shows?

A quotation is somebody else’s words doing a job in your sentence, and the job has to be yours.

There are two ways in. Run it into your own grammar, with no comma and no capital: she calls the expense a recoverable cost. Or announce it with a colon, after a complete sentence of your own.

A comma before a quotation belongs with reporting verbs: she wrote, The cost is recoverable. The capital inside the marks stays as the original had it.

If you change a word to make it fit, put your change in square brackets. If you cut words out of the middle, mark the gap with three dots. Both are honest; silence is not.

And then explain it. A dropped quotation, left to argue by itself, argues nothing, and the sentence after a quotation is the sentence a marker reads most carefully.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Take any three notes from your last reading.
- Put all three in one sentence.
- What does the sentence say that none of them said?

Notes come to you one at a time and arguments do not. Turning several notes into one sentence is the operation that makes a page of notes into a paragraph.

Both holds two things that agree. Both routes are longer than the direct path. While and whereas hold two things that differ, and whereas is the more formal.

The first decision is which note goes in the main clause, because that is the one a reader will carry away. The others become phrases or subordinate clauses.

Three notes is the useful target. Two is often too easy to be worth combining, and four usually collapses: a sentence carrying four facts makes a reader reread it, and rereading is the thing good writing prevents.

The test is at the end. Read the built sentence aloud and ask whether it says something that none of the three notes said on its own. If it does not, you have made a list with joining words in it.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: notes into one sentence: both, while, whereas**

- Both introduces two items that share a property, and it takes a plural verb. Both routes are longer than the direct path.
- While introduces a contrast and can stand at the front of the sentence with a comma after its clause. While the direct path is quicker, it is unlit after four o’clock.
- Whereas does the same work in a more formal register and usually sits in the middle. The lit route is longer, whereas the direct path is quicker.
- The clause you put in the main position is the one your reader will carry away, and demoting a note to a subordinate clause is a decision about what matters rather than about length.

**A set of notes turned into one sentence that uses three of them**

**Figure 4.5** — Four checks, and three notes become one sentence worth reading.

A sentence built from notes is the smallest unit of synthesis there is, and a student who can do it reliably can write a literature paragraph.

Give the system your notes as a numbered list, in whatever order they were taken, and say which number is the one that must land.

## Unit 5 · What the Describing Word Is Attached To

*CEFR B1.2 · Grammar: modifiers and what they attach to · AI: a modifier sweep: every description attached to the right thing*

**Figure 5.0** — AN OPENING PHRASE DESCRIBES WHATEVER COMES AFTER THE COMMA.: Sunita Pillai wrote that, having been left outside overnight, she found the equipment damaged, which is not what happened to her.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a sentence of yours starting with a phrase and a comma.
- What is the first noun after the comma?
- Is that what the phrase describes?

A modifier is a word or phrase that describes something else, and the whole of its meaning depends on which something it is attached to.

An English sentence attaches an opening phrase to whatever noun follows the comma. Having been left outside overnight, the equipment was damaged works. Having been left outside overnight, I found the equipment damaged says you spent the night outside.

There are two repairs and they are not equivalent. Move the real subject to the front, or turn the phrase into a clause with its own subject: because the equipment had been left outside overnight, I found it damaged.

Only is the smallest and most expensive modifier in the language. Only I checked the first sample. I only checked the first sample. I checked only the first sample. Three positions, three different claims.

And some phrases sit between two nouns and could describe either. A reader takes the nearer one, and the sentence is ambiguous whatever you intended, so the repair is position and not explanation.

### Part 3 · Grammar and Dialogues

**Grammar: modifiers and what they attach to**

- An opening phrase attaches to the subject that follows the comma, and English offers no way to override that. Having been left outside overnight, the equipment was damaged.
- Where the subject that follows is the wrong one, you have a dangling modifier, and the first repair is to change the subject. The second is to give the phrase its own subject: because the equipment had been left outside, I found it damaged.
- Only limits the word immediately after it. Only I checked the sample says nobody else did. I checked only the first sample says I did not check the others.
- A phrase sitting between two nouns describes the nearer one for any reader, whatever you intended, so the repair is to move it rather than to add an explanation.

## Unit 6 · A List That Runs Straight

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a list of three or more things in your writing.
- Read the words before the list with each item in turn.
- Does each one make a sentence?

A list runs straight when every item behaves the same way grammatically. Three nouns, or three verbs in the same form, or three phrases of the same shape.

The test takes ten seconds. Read the words before the list, then each item on its own. The scheme would reduce the queue, improve the lighting, and the gate would be widened. The third one does not follow from the stem.

There are two repairs and students almost always choose the wrong one. You can change the odd item to match the others, or you can change the stem so that all of them fit. The second is often easier and always less damaging to the meaning.

Paired words have the same requirement. Both and and, either and or, not only and but also: whatever follows the first must match whatever follows the second in shape.

The commonest cause is growth. Lists are rarely written; they are added to. The item you put in last week was written against a stem you had forgotten, and that is the one that breaks.

**A list made parallel**

A parallelism pass is the cheapest check in the book because the rule is mechanical, and it catches an error that is almost invisible to the writer who made it.

Give the system your passage and ask it to find every list of two or more items, including the lists inside sentences and not only the bulleted ones.

Ask it to print each list with its stem, then each item on its own line joined to that stem, so you can see which combination does not make a sentence.

## Unit 7 · The Apostrophe Audit

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Count the apostrophes in your last essay.
- Pick one and say which of the two jobs it is doing.
- If it is neither, delete it.

An apostrophe does one of two jobs. It shows ownership, or it marks a letter that is missing. A plural does neither, and so takes no apostrophe at all.

For ownership, the rule is about the owner and not the thing owned. One student: the student’s essay. Several students: the students’ essays. The apostrophe goes after the owner, wherever the owner ends.

The of test settles almost every doubtful case. Turn the phrase round with of. The essay of the student gives the student’s essay. The essay of the students gives the students’ essays.

Its means belonging to it and has no apostrophe. It is always means it is. Whose means belonging to whom; who is always means who is. These four are not rules so much as four separate words.

And the audit is the whole method. List every apostrophe in the essay in order, say which of the two jobs each one is doing, and delete the ones doing neither.

## Unit 8 · A Transition Chosen From the Relation

*CEFR B1.2 · Grammar: transitions: however, therefore, moreover, instead · AI: a transition chosen from the relation and not from the sound*

**Figure 8.0** — A TRANSITION IS A PROMISE ABOUT WHAT COMES NEXT.: Sunita Pillai began four paragraphs with however, and only one of them contrasted with anything.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find a transition at the start of one of your paragraphs.
- Cover it and read the paragraph.
- Did the word tell the reader anything true?

A transition tells a reader what relation the next paragraph has to the last one, and a reader believes it before they have read a word of what follows.

There are four relations that cover most of what an essay needs. Contrast, which takes however. Consequence, which takes therefore. Addition, which takes moreover. Replacement, which takes instead.

The method is to name the relation before choosing the word. If you cannot name it, the paragraphs may not be in the right order, and no transition will repair that.

The commonest fault is choosing by sound. However sounds academic, so it appears at the top of paragraphs that are not contrasting with anything, and a reader who was promised a contrast and given an addition stops trusting the signposts.

And the test is to cover the word and read on. If the paragraph makes the same sense without it, the transition was doing nothing and should go, because a signpost pointing nowhere is worse than no signpost.

- However marks a contrast and sits at the start of its sentence with a comma after it. The route is longer. However, it is lit throughout.
- Therefore marks a consequence and makes a claim: that the second thing follows from the first. The lamps are out. Therefore, the route is unsafe after dark.
- Moreover marks an addition, and it is the most over-used of the four, because and does the same work in one syllable.
- Instead marks a replacement: the second thing happened and the first did not. The gate was not widened. Instead, the lighting was improved.

A paragraph of argument has three parts. A claim, which somebody could disagree with. Evidence, which is what you have. And an explanation, which says how the evidence supports the claim.

### Part 5 · Extended Reading

**A paragraph with its claim, evidence and explanation labelled**

**Figure 9.5** — Four checks, and you find out which of the three parts you left out.

A paragraph labelling is diagnostic rather than corrective: it tells you what your paragraph is made of, and what it is made of is usually not what you thought.

Give the system one paragraph at a time, because the labelling only means anything if the paragraph is making a single claim.

Ask it to label every sentence as claim, evidence or explanation, and to mark any sentence that is none of the three.

Ask it to test each sentence it labelled a claim by asking whether a reasonable person could contradict it, and to say so where the answer is no.

Ask it to compare each explanation sentence with the evidence it follows, and to flag it as a restatement where it uses no words that were absent from the claim and the evidence.

What it cannot do is supply your claim. The claim is the thing you are prepared to be wrong about, and nobody can decide on your behalf what you are willing to argue.

### Part 1 · Warm Up and Read

- Find your last essay plan.
- Is each line a topic or a sentence?
- Read the sentences in order and listen.

Most school essay plans are lists of topics. Lighting. Incidents. Cost. Objections. A list of topics tells you what the paragraphs are about and nothing about what they do.

A plan of sentences says, for each paragraph, the one sentence that paragraph exists to prove. Paragraph three proves that the incidents are concentrated rather than spread.

The test is to read the sentences in order and ignore everything else. If that sequence is an argument, the essay will be one. If it is a set of observations, no amount of writing will turn it into an argument.

Two faults show up immediately in such a plan. A paragraph with no sentence attached is a paragraph you are writing because you have the material. Two paragraphs with the same sentence are one paragraph.

And the last line of the plan is the sentence the whole essay proves, which should answer the question that was asked. If it answers a different question, that is cheaper to discover now than on the fourth page.
