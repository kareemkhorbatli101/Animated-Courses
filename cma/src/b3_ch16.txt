16.1  What a transfer price is for
A transfer price is the price one part of a company charges another for goods or services supplied internally. It is revenue to the supplying unit and cost to the buying unit, so the two cancel in the company accounts.
Because it cancels, the transfer price has no direct effect on company profit. Its purpose is to influence decisions and to measure performance fairly. Figure F316-02 lists the four objectives it must serve at once.
Figure F316-02. The four objectives of a transfer price
Objective (C.2.h)
What it means
At Orontes
Goal congruence
Each manager's best choice is also best for the company
A floor of variable cost keeps internal supply attractive
Fair performance evaluation
Each division's profit reflects its own work
A price below cost makes the supplier look bad
Divisional autonomy
Managers keep the right to decide
A negotiated price respects both managers
Company profit after tax
Cross-border prices affect tariffs and tax
Set within the rules, not to shift profit
The objectives can conflict. A price that gives Levant a fair margin may be high enough to push Beverages towards an outside carrier. The rest of the chapter works out where the price should sit.

16.2  Five ways of setting the price
Companies set transfer prices in five ways: at the supplier's variable cost, at its full cost, at the market price, at a price the two managers negotiate, or at two different rates for the two sides.

EXAM TRAP   The company column never moves
Changing a transfer price moves profit between divisions. If an answer shows the company better or worse off because of the price alone, something has been double-counted.
No method is right everywhere. A market price suits a product with an outside market, a negotiated price suits one without, and dual rates suit a case where the right decision and fair reporting pull apart.

16.3  The range: minimum and maximum
The minimum transfer price is what the supplier must receive to be no worse off: variable cost plus the opportunity cost of anything it gives up. The maximum is what the buyer could pay outside. Figure F316-06 draws the range for Levant and Beverages.
Figure F316-06. The bargaining range for the transfer price.
Figure F316-07 states the rule. When the minimum is below the maximum, a range exists and any price inside it leaves both divisions better off. When the minimum is above the maximum, the company should not transfer internally.

An outside carrier offers Beverages $1.00 a case. With spare capacity, the company's real cost of delivering inside is only $0.50, so going outside would cost $450,000 a year. At full capacity the real cost is $1.10, so going outside would save $90,000. Figure F316-09 sets out both.

The minimum transfer price cannot be found without knowing whether the supplier has spare capacity. With spare capacity there is no opportunity cost; at capacity the opportunity cost is the lost contribution margin.

16.5  Transfers across borders
Orontes sells to the Gulf through a distributor it part-owns, so some transfers cross a border. Figure F316-10 lists the issues that then arise.
Figure F316-10. Issues in a cross-border transfer
Issue (C.2.l)
Effect on the transfer price
At Orontes
Tariffs
A higher transfer price raises the duty paid at the border
5% on the Gulf shipment
Income taxes
Profit is taxed where it is reported
Jordan 20%, the Gulf buyer 10%
Exchange rates
A price fixed in one currency moves the profit when rates change
The dinar is pegged to the dollar; Gulf currencies are not
Currency restrictions
Cash may not be remittable, so a high price traps profit abroad
Watch controls on moving funds out
Expropriation risk
Assets or balances in a country may be seized
Keep balances low where the risk is high
Availability of materials and skills
A division may have to buy inside because nothing else is available
Specialist bottling in Jordan
Tax and tariffs pull in opposite directions. A higher transfer price moves profit to the buying country and cuts tax if the rate there is lower. It also raises the duty paid at the border, because duty is charged on the invoice value.
Tax authorities also set rules. A transfer price must be defensible as one that unrelated parties would have agreed, and documentation is usually required. A price chosen only to move profit invites adjustment and penalties.

Chapter summary
Figure F316-12 brings the chapter together.
Figure F316-12. Chapter 16 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A transfer price is an internal charge that moves profit between divisions without changing company profit, and it must serve goal congruence, fair evaluation, autonomy and after-tax profit. It may be set at variable cost, full cost, market price, a negotiated figure or dual rates, each with its own advantages. The minimum is variable cost plus any opportunity cost, and the maximum is the outside price; with spare capacity the floor is variable cost, and at capacity it is the market price. Across borders, tariffs, taxes, exchange rates, currency restrictions and expropriation risk all bear on the decision.
