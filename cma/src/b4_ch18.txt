18.2  How a true chart misleads
Distortion rarely involves a false number. Figure F418-03 shows the same four quarters of 2028 drawn two ways.
Figure F418-03. The same four figures drawn misleadingly and honestly.
On the left the axis begins at 205,000, and the third-quarter bar towers over the first. On the right it begins at zero, and the four quarters are visibly similar. The third quarter exceeds the first by 14 per cent, which the right-hand chart conveys and the left-hand chart does not.
Every figure in the left-hand chart is correct. Nothing has been invented, nothing omitted, and the axis is honestly labelled. It misleads because a bar's length is read as its value, and truncating the axis breaks that relationship while leaving the chart technically defensible.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'The axis was labelled, so the chart is honest'
A label does not undo the distortion, because readers take the message from the shape before reading the axis. For a bar chart the rule is simple and worth keeping: start at zero. A line chart may begin elsewhere, because a line's message is its slope rather than its length, but the choice should still be stated.

18.4  What a picture can and cannot do
Figure F418-05 pairs each benefit of visualization with the limitation that accompanies it, and the pairing is deliberate: each limitation is the same property seen from the other side.

The first pair carries the argument. The eye reads a pattern in a second that a table would take minutes to yield. That same speed is why a distorted chart persuades before anybody has checked the axis. Speed is the benefit and the danger together.
The last limitation is the one Part IV has repeated. A chart showing two series moving together is a picture of an association, and Chapter 14's staff birthdays would make a perfectly convincing one.

18.5  Communicating the result
An exhibit has to carry its meaning to someone who did not do the analysis and will not ask questions. Figure F418-06 shows the difference that makes.
Figure F418-06. Leading with the message rather than the method.
The strongest single change is in the title. A title naming the axes leaves the reader to work out what matters, and some will not. A title stating the finding tells them what to look for, and the chart then has one job: to show that the claim is true.
The other three rules follow from it. One message per exhibit, because a chart saying three things usually says none. The chart supports the title, by highlighting the part that carries the claim. And the caveat is shown rather than hidden, because an audience that discovers an omission later distrusts everything else.

18.6  Extended Example 18A: presenting the Gulf decision to the board
F.4.cc is a Level C outcome, so the exam asks you to choose between visualization options and defend the choice. This example does that for the decision of Chapter 17.
1. What the board must see
Not the method. The board must see three things: that the expansion is expected to contribute 1,047,500, that the realistic range runs from 539,260 to 1,503,517, and that even the low end covers the fixed cost. Three facts, and the decision follows from them.
2. Rejecting the obvious options
A table of 1,000 trial results shows everything and communicates nothing. A single number is what the finance director already tried, and it provoked the question about the worst case. A pie chart has no role here, because nothing is a share of a whole.
3. The choice
A histogram of the simulated outcomes, as in Figure F417-06, with the fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles marked and the fixed-cost line drawn across it. The shape answers the worst-case question visually, and the marked percentiles answer it numerically.
4. The title
Not 'Distribution of simulated contribution'. Something closer to: even the pessimistic tenth of outcomes covers the fixed cost. That is the finding, and the chart exists to show that it is true.
5. The caveat, shown not hidden
A line under the chart recording that the probabilities were estimated by management rather than measured. Chapter 17 made that point and it belongs on the exhibit, because the board is entitled to weigh it.
6. What would be dishonest
Truncating the horizontal axis to begin at the fifth percentile, which would make every outcome look comfortable. Or drawing only the mean, which is what started the argument. Both would be defensible in the sense that no number would be false.

Chapter summary
Figure F418-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F418-08. Chapter 18 summary map: the key rule for each section.
The form of a chart follows from what the reader must see. A bar chart serves comparison, a line a trend, a scatter plot a relationship, a histogram or box plot a distribution, and a table the case where exact values will be read off. Distortion rarely requires a false number: truncating a bar chart's axis breaks the relationship between length and value while leaving every figure correct. Bars start at zero, the aspect ratio is chosen before the result is seen, labels go directly on the data, and whatever does not inform is removed. Visualization is fast, reaches non-specialists and reveals the unexpected, and each of those strengths is also how a misleading chart succeeds. An exhibit carries one message, states its finding in the title, supports that finding with the chart, and shows its caveat rather than hiding it.
