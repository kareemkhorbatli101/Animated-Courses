WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 3 gave Orontes three products: a drum of sesame paste contributing $21.00, and ten jars of tahini from the same quantity of paste contributing $4.80 each. Chapter 5 set out a case in which the halva line earns $900,000 of contribution against $500,000 of avoidable fixed costs. Both reappear here as decisions.
9.1  Sell now or process further
A joint process produces something that can be sold as it is, or taken further and sold for more. The point at which the choice arises is the split-off point, and everything spent before it is irrelevant to the choice.
That last sentence is the whole topic. The cost of producing the paste has been incurred by the time the question arises, and it is the same whether the paste is sold or filled into jars. It is therefore a joint cost, common to both options, and it belongs in neither column — whatever basis the costing system has used to apportion it between them.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: paste or jars
Step 1. The two options. A 10 kg drum of sesame paste sells for $60.00. The same paste fills ten tahini jars selling at $12.00 each, which is 10 × 12.00 = 120.00.
Step 2. The incremental revenue. 120.00 − 60.00 = 60.00 a drum.
Step 3. The incremental cost. Filling, jars, labels and the extra handling cost $33.00 a drum. Nothing else changes.
Step 4. The decision. 60.00 − 33.00 = 27.00 a drum in favour of processing further.
Check. The same answer appears in Chapter 3's figures without any new arithmetic: ten jars contribute 10 × 4.80 = 48.00 against a drum's $21.00, and 48.00 − 21.00 = 27.00. The two routes agree because the paste's own variable cost of $39.00 a drum is in both and cancels — which is the joint cost doing exactly what the rule says it does.
Three qualifications belong in any answer on this outcome.
The further processing must have its own capacity. If filling the jars uses roaster or line time that something else needs, the opportunity cost of Chapter 6 enters and can reverse the answer.
The two markets must both exist. Processing further is worth $27.00 a drum only if the jars can be sold; if the jar market is already supplied and the paste market is not, the extra jars are inventory rather than revenue.
A joint product may be unavoidable. Where a process yields two outputs in fixed proportions, the question is never whether to make one of them but only what to do with each at split-off.

EXAM TRAP   Apportioned joint costs
A question will often give you a joint cost apportioned between the products by weight, by value or by volume. The apportionment is for inventory valuation and it is irrelevant to the decision. Say so.
Processing further can be worth doing for one joint product and not for another from the same process. Each is decided on its own incremental figures.
If the further processing cost exceeds the incremental revenue, sell at split-off — even where the final product sells for far more than the intermediate one.

9.2  Drop or add a segment
A segment is any part of a business whose results can be separated: a product line, a department, a branch, a customer group, a region. The question is whether the company is better off with it or without it, and the arithmetic is Chapter 5's avoidable-cost rule.
Keep a segment if its contribution exceeds the fixed costs that would actually be avoided by dropping it. Drop it if not.
For Orontes's halva line, with a contribution of $900,000 and avoidable fixed costs of $500,000, the line is worth 900,000 − 500,000 = 400,000 a year and should be kept. The $300,000 of apportioned factory and head office cost that makes it look unprofitable in the management accounts does not disappear on closure; it moves to the other products.
Adding a segment is the same test run forwards. A proposed new line of sesame bars would contribute $820,000 and need $600,000 of new fixed costs, so it is worth 820,000 − 600,000 = 220,000 and should be added.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the new line that was not worth adding
Step 1. The proposal as presented. Contribution of $820,000 against $600,000 of new fixed costs, a gain of $220,000.
Step 2. The question nobody asked. Where will the customers come from? The marketing plan expects a tenth of the new line's volume to be bought by customers who would otherwise have bought tahini jars — 100,000 jars a year.
Step 3. The cannibalisation. Those jars contribute $4.80 each, so 100,000 × 4.80 = 480,000 of existing contribution is lost.
Step 4. The revised decision. 220,000 − 480,000 = (260,000). The new line would make the company $260,000 a year worse off.
Check. Nothing in the new line's own figures changed. The decision reversed because the analysis was widened to include an effect on a different product — and that is the characteristic error in add-a-segment decisions, exactly as a missing opportunity cost is the characteristic error in special orders. The rule is to draw the boundary of the analysis around the company, not around the segment.
Four effects belong inside that boundary whenever a question supplies them.
Cannibalisation, as above: sales taken from the company's own products.
Complementarity, the opposite: a segment that is unprofitable alone but brings customers who buy other things. A loss-making café in a food hall is the standard example.
Shared costs that genuinely change. Dropping a segment may allow a warehouse to be given up or a delivery round to be dropped; adding one may push a shared resource past a step.
Released capacity, valued as Chapter 6 values it.

9.3  When capacity changes
Chapter 6 treated capacity as fixed and priced what it displaced. This section treats capacity as the decision.
Capacity can be added in three ways and each behaves differently.
Overtime adds hours at a premium, with no change in fixed costs. It is the most flexible and the most expensive per unit, and it is the right answer for a short peak.
A second shift adds a block of capacity and a block of fixed cost: supervision, lighting, canteen, shift premium. It is a step, and the step has to be justified by the volume that will use it.
New plant adds capacity and converts the question into the capital investment decision of Chapters 14 to 17.
The arithmetic of a step is always the same. A second shift at Orontes would add $1,200,000 of fixed costs and 800,000 jars of capacity. At a contribution of $4.80 a jar it pays for itself at 1,200,000 ÷ 4.80 = 250,000 jars — less than a third of the capacity it creates. So the shift is worth adding if the company can sell 250,000 more jars, and the whole question becomes a demand question rather than a cost one.
Three consequences of a capacity change belong in an answer.
The relevant costs of every other decision change with it. A special order refused at full capacity becomes acceptable once capacity is added, because the displaced contribution disappears. Chapter 7's two answers were the same order on two sides of this line.
The breakeven rises. A step of $1,200,000 of fixed cost raises the breakeven by 1,200,000 ÷ 4.80 = 250,000 jars, which is Chapter 4's margin-of-safety question in another form.
A step once taken is hard to untake. Shift premiums can be withdrawn and supervisors released, with difficulty; a new building cannot. The more irreversible the step, the stronger the demand evidence should be.

9.4  Income taxes in marginal analysis
Everything so far has been computed before tax. The outcome asks what tax does, and the answer has three parts.
Tax scales the answer. An incremental contribution of $300,000 at a 25 per cent rate is worth 300,000 × 0.75 = 225,000 after tax. Every option in a comparison is scaled by the same factor, so no ranking changes and the decision is the same before and after tax.
Except where the options are taxed differently, and then tax decides. Three cases recur. A capital allowance available on a purchased machine and not on a leased one, which Book 2 priced as the net advantage to leasing. Operations in two jurisdictions with different rates, so that the same contribution is worth more in one place. And a company with losses available to shelter income, for which the marginal tax rate is nil until the losses are used.
Tax follows cash, not accounting profit. A decision's tax effect is computed on the taxable amounts it changes, which is why Chapter 14 builds capital budgeting on after-tax cash flows and why depreciation enters there as a tax shield rather than as a cost.
The practical rule for Section C is short: compute before tax unless the question gives a tax rate or the alternatives are taxed differently, and when a rate is given, state the answer both ways — "an incremental gain of $300,000, which is $225,000 after tax at 25 per cent" — because a marker can see both and the board only wants one.

9.5  The recommendation
The last outcome of this topic asks for a course of action, and the five-part structure of Chapter 4 applies with two additions for marginal analysis.
State the decision, with the incremental figure that supports it: "process further: it is worth $27.00 a drum".
Name what was excluded and why: the joint cost, the apportioned overhead, the sunk development, the book value.
Name the opportunity cost or the displaced contribution, if there is one, and the capacity assumption behind it: "this assumes the filling line has spare capacity; if it does not, the answer reverses".
Widen the boundary once, deliberately. Ask what the decision does to the company's other products, its customers, its suppliers and its next year. The cannibalisation of section 9.2 and the market disturbance of Chapter 7 both live here.
Give the qualitative factors their place, chosen rather than recited.
Say what would change the answer, with a number: "the new line is worth adding if cannibalisation is below 45,833 jars a year".
That last figure is worth deriving, because it shows the habit. The new line gains $220,000 before cannibalisation, and each cannibalised jar costs $4.80, so the break-even cannibalisation is 220,000 ÷ 4.80 = 45,833 jars. Below that the line is worth adding and above it is not — and the whole decision now rests on a single marketing estimate that somebody can be asked to defend.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Segment"
English segment in management accounting means any part of a business whose results can be reported separately — a product line, a region, a customer group — and it is deliberately broader than division or department, which name parts of an organisation chart. The exam also uses it in the reporting sense of Part 1, where a segment is defined by how management reviews the business. Arabic قطاع covers both. French uses segment for the analytical sense and secteur for the reporting one — secteur opérationnel is the standard-setter's term — so the French reader meets two words where English has one, and the distinction is worth importing: the thing being analysed need not be a reportable segment at all.

At a split-off point the joint cost is the same under both options and belongs in neither: paste at $60.00 against ten jars at 10 × 12.00 = 120.00 gives an incremental revenue of 60.00 against a further cost of 33.00, so processing is worth 60.00 − 33.00 = 27.00 a drum — the same answer Chapter 3's contributions give as 48.00 − 21.00 = 27.00. Case-style practice: the drum decision, under constraint
Orontes can sell a 10 kg drum of paste at $60.00 or fill it into ten jars selling at $12.00 each, at a further processing cost of $33.00 a drum. Filling uses 0.04 hours of line time a drum, and the filling line is fully occupied; line time currently earns $900 an hour of contribution on other work. The company produces 120,000 drums of paste a year. The jar market would absorb an extra 400,000 jars; beyond that the price would have to fall.
Required. Say how many drums should be processed further and how many sold as paste, with figures. Name the two assumptions your answer depends on. Under 300 words.

What it tells you
Three of the four decisions are close: 4.00 a unit, 40,000 a year and 30,000 a year are all small enough that an error in one estimate would reverse them. Only the shift question has a clear answer, and it is a question about demand rather than about cost.

Case-style set: the drum decision, under constraint
The incremental benefit, ignoring the constraint. Processing a drum gains 60.00 − 33.00 = 27.00, as section 9.1 computed.
The constraint. Filling uses 0.04 line hours a drum, and line time earns $900 an hour elsewhere, so the opportunity cost of processing a drum is 0.04 × 900 = 36.00. Against an incremental benefit of $27.00, processing a drum makes the company 36.00 − 27.00 = 9.00 worse off. On the figures as given, none of the 120,000 drums should be processed further: all should be sold as paste.
What would change that. The answer turns entirely on the $900 an hour. Processing is worth doing if line time earns less than 27.00 ÷ 0.04 = 675.00 an hour, so the decision reverses the moment the other work on the filling line falls below that rate. Since the line's alternative work is presumably jars of other kinds, that figure should be checked rather than accepted: it is the single number the whole recommendation rests on.
The jar market limit matters only if the first test is passed. If line time were worth less than $675 an hour, the company could process up to 40,000 drums, because the market absorbs 400,000 extra jars and each drum yields ten.
The two assumptions. First, that the $900 an hour is a genuine contribution forgone — that the alternative work exists and will be lost, rather than merely delayed. Second, that the paste market will absorb all 120,000 drums at $60.00; if it will not, the drums that cannot be sold have no alternative use and the comparison for those changes entirely, because the opportunity cost of the line time is then weighed against nothing at all.
