Chapter 13
Margins, from gross profit to EBITDA
الهوامش: من الربح الإجمالي إلى الأرباح قبل الفوائد والضرائب والإهلاك
Orontes earned $2,969,100 in 2025 on sales of $42,000,000. Whether that is a good year cannot be answered from the two amounts, because a dollar of profit means one thing on forty-two million of sales and another on four hundred and twenty million. Dividing each level of profit by sales turns the amounts into rates, and a rate can be compared with last year, with a competitor and with the company's own plan.
A margin is a level of profit expressed as a percentage of sales. There are four that the exam names, and they sit one below the other on the income statement: gross, operating, net, and the margin on earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. Each one adds a layer of cost, so the four together read as a staircase, and the interesting work is never the top step on its own but the distance between the steps.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 2 built the common-size income statement, in which every line is a percentage of sales. The four margins of this chapter are four lines of that statement, given names because they are the four the exam asks for. Chapter 5 noticed that Orontes's gross margin had slipped two points over three years and promised that a later chapter would say what the slip is worth. This is that chapter.
13.1  The four margins and where they come from
A margin is a profit figure over net sales. The numerator changes; the denominator never does.
Gross profit margin is gross profit over net sales. Gross profit is what is left of the selling price after the cost of the goods themselves, so the gross margin is the margin of the trade. For Orontes, gross profit is 42,000,000 − 27,300,000 = 14,700,000, and the margin is 14,700,000 ÷ 42,000,000 = 35.0 per cent.
Operating profit margin is operating income over net sales. Operating income is gross profit less the operating expenses — selling, administration, research, credit losses and any impairment — so this margin is the margin of the business as a business, before anything to do with how it is financed or taxed. For Orontes: 4,176,000 ÷ 42,000,000 = 9.94 per cent, which rounds to 9.9.
Net profit margin is net income over net sales. It is the last line over the first, so everything that happened in the year is inside it: interest, investment income, one-off gains and losses, and tax. For Orontes: 2,969,100 ÷ 42,000,000 = 7.07 per cent, or 7.1 to one decimal.
EBITDA margin is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation over net sales. It adds back to operating profit the charges that do not move cash in the year, and section 13.5 builds it properly, because it is the only one of the four whose numerator is not printed on the face of the statement.

EXAM TRAP   One denominator, four numerators
Every margin divides by net sales, not by gross sales, not by total revenue including other income, and not by assets. A profit over assets is a return, which is Chapter 14, not a margin.
Operating income excludes interest and tax by construction. A candidate who deducts interest before computing the operating margin has computed something else.
A margin is a percentage. A markup is a percentage of cost. A 35 per cent gross margin is a markup of about 54 per cent, and the two are not interchangeable.

13.2  Gross margin and the cost of sales
The gross margin and the cost-of-sales ratio are the same fact written twice. Orontes's cost of goods sold is 27,300,000 ÷ 42,000,000 = 65.0 per cent of sales, and what is left is the 35.0 per cent gross margin. Whenever the exam asks what happened to a gross margin, the answer is always something that happened to one of those two percentages, and there are only four candidates.
Selling prices. A price rise lifts the margin; a discount campaign lowers it. The volume sold may be unchanged.
Input costs. A rise in the cost of sesame, freight or packaging lowers the margin unless the price rises with it.
Product mix. Selling more of a thin-margin line and less of a fat one lowers the average margin although no single product's margin has moved. This is the cause candidates forget.
Costing and inventory method. Under last-in, first-out, rising prices push the newest and highest costs into cost of sales and depress the margin, while first-in, first-out leaves them in inventory. A write-down of stock passes through cost of sales as well.

WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: what two points of gross margin are worth
Step 1. The size of the slip. The gross margin was 37.0 per cent in 2023 and is 35.0 per cent in 2025: 37.0 − 35.0 = 2.0 points.
Step 2. The money. Two points of the 2025 sales figure is 42,000,000 × 0.02 = 840,000. Had Orontes held its 2023 margin, gross profit would have been $840,000 higher, and because no operating expense would have changed, so would operating income.
Step 3. Against what Orontes earns. Net income for the year is $2,969,100, so the lost margin is 840,000 ÷ 2,969,100 = 28.29 per cent of the profit the company actually made.
Check. The slip is one point a year and sounds small. Expressed in money it is the largest single finding in the whole of Book 1 — larger than the $272,640 a year the trade working capital costs to fund, and larger than anything the liquidity chapters turned up. A two-point move in the biggest percentage on the statement beats a large move in a small one, every time.
EXAM TRAP   A margin can fall while profit rises
Orontes's gross profit rose from $11,840,000 to $14,700,000 over the three years while the gross margin fell. Volume did the work; the rate got worse. An answer that says profit fell is simply wrong.
"Cost of sales rose" is not an explanation of a margin fall. It is the arithmetic of it. The explanation is a price, a cost, a mix or a method.
Where the question gives no mix information, say that mix is a possible cause rather than asserting a price war.

13.3  From gross to operating: the expense ratios
Twenty-five points of Orontes's sales dollar disappear between gross profit and operating income. They disappear into one line, total operating expenses, and the ratio of that line to sales is the second number to track.
Orontes's operating expenses are 10,524,000 ÷ 42,000,000 = 25.06 per cent of sales in 2025. In 2024 they were 9,100,000 ÷ 36,400,000 = 25.0 per cent and in 2023 8,320,000 ÷ 32,000,000 = 26.0 per cent. On the face of it the company has held its overheads steady while growing, which is the opposite of the gross-margin story and would be good news.
It is not what happened. The 2025 figure contains an impairment loss of $2,724,000, which is 2,724,000 ÷ 42,000,000 = 6.49 per cent of sales on its own and did not occur in either earlier year. Strip it out and the comparison reverses.

WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the operating margin without the one-off
Step 1. Remove the charge from the expenses. 10,524,000 − 2,724,000 = 7,800,000, which is 7,800,000 ÷ 42,000,000 = 18.57 per cent of sales against 25.0 per cent in 2024.
Step 2. Add it back to profit. 4,176,000 + 2,724,000 = 6,900,000, and the operating margin becomes 6,900,000 ÷ 42,000,000 = 16.43 per cent.
Step 3. Read the two together. Orontes lost two points of gross margin and gained six and a half points of operating margin by spending proportionately less on running the business. Both are real; the reported operating margin of 9.9 per cent shows neither, because a single charge of $2,724,000 sits on top of them.
Check. The adjusted figure is the better description of the year's trading and the reported figure is the one that happened. The exam wants both, in that order, with the adjustment named. Presenting only the adjusted margin is what the term non-GAAP measure was invented to describe.
The arithmetic that links the two margins is worth stating once, because it is the shape of every question on this outcome. The operating margin is the gross margin less the operating expense ratio: 35.0 − 25.06 = 9.94 per cent. So there are exactly two ways for an operating margin to move, and a complete answer says which of the two did it, and by how much. In 2025 both moved and in opposite directions: the gross margin took one point off and the expense ratio, loaded with the impairment, took a further point.

EXAM TRAP   Which expenses are operating expenses
Interest is not an operating expense; it is a financing cost, and it belongs below operating income. Tax is not an operating expense either.
An impairment loss is an operating expense under both GAAP and IFRS, however unusual it is. It cannot be excluded from the reported operating margin, only disclosed separately.
A research expense is an operating expense of the period it is incurred in, not an asset, and cutting it raises this year's operating margin at the cost of later years.

13.4  From operating to net: what sits below the line
Three points of sales separate Orontes's operating margin from its net margin, and three things cause them: the financing cost, the non-operating items, and tax.
Start with the total of the other income and expense section, which is a net cost of $217,200: 4,176,000 − 217,200 = 3,958,800 of income before income taxes. Inside that net figure, interest expense of $390,000 is the only large item, and it is offset by $177,000 of equity-method income from Jordan Glass Co. and a handful of small gains and losses.
Then tax. Orontes's income tax expense is $989,700, so its effective tax rate is 989,700 ÷ 3,958,800 = 25.0 per cent. What is left is net income of $2,969,100.

EXAM TRAP   The net margin moves for reasons that are not trading
A company can report a better net margin on worse trading, through a tax refund, a gain on a disposal, or a fall in interest rates.
The effective tax rate is tax expense over pre-tax income, not the statutory rate. A difference between them has a cause, and the tax note gives it.
Where a question gives you a one-off gain inside net income, say so before comparing net margins across years.

13.5  EBITDA, and what it leaves out
EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. It is not a line of any income statement, which is why it has to be built, and the exam expects the build.
Start from earnings before interest and taxes, which Chapter 10 computed as income before income taxes plus interest expense: 3,958,800 + 390,000 = 4,348,800. Then add back the depreciation charged for the year, which the cash flow statement gives as $2,100,000: 4,348,800 + 2,100,000 = 6,448,800. Orontes has no amortisation of intangibles to add. The EBITDA margin is therefore 6,448,800 ÷ 42,000,000 = 15.35 per cent, or 15.4.
What EBITDA is for is comparison between companies whose financing and asset bases differ. Two food manufacturers of the same size, one owning its plant and one leasing it, one financed with debt and one with equity, will report very different net margins and similar EBITDA margins, because EBITDA removes exactly the differences that are not about making and selling food.
What EBITDA is not is cash flow, and it is not profit.

The last line is the point of the figure: 6,448,800 − 3,000,000 − 390,000 − 839,700 = 2,219,100. Adding back depreciation does not make the plant last longer, and Orontes spent $3,000,000 on property, plant and equipment in the year against a depreciation charge of $2,100,000. A company that quotes its EBITDA margin and not its capital expenditure is quoting the half of the sentence that suits it.
There is one more trap, and it is the common one in practice. Many companies report adjusted EBITDA, adding back anything they consider unusual. On Orontes's figures that would mean adding the impairment: 6,448,800 + 2,724,000 = 9,172,800, which is 9,172,800 ÷ 42,000,000 = 21.84 per cent of sales. The same year, the same statements, and a margin of 21.8 per cent instead of 7.1. Both numbers are arithmetically correct. Only one of them is net income.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Margin"
English margin is used for three different things in finance: a percentage of sales, as here; the cash a broker requires against a position; and the edge of anything. Arabic هامش carries the same breadth, and French marge likewise. In the phrase gross profit margin it is always the percentage, and a candidate who reads a margin call into a margin analysis question has lost the thread entirely. Note also that the French marge brute is a percentage while the English gross margin is used loosely for both the percentage and the amount — say gross profit for the amount and gross margin for the rate, and no reader will be in doubt.

Chapter summary
Figure F513-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F513-09. Chapter 13 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Four margins divide four profit figures by the one denominator, net sales: gross 35.0 per cent, operating 9.9, EBITDA 15.4 and net 7.1 for Orontes in 2025. The gross margin is the mirror of the cost-of-sales ratio of 65.0 per cent, and it moves for four reasons only — price, input cost, mix and costing method — so Orontes's fall from 37.0 to 35.0 per cent over three years is worth $840,000 of 2025 gross profit, or 28.29 per cent of the year's net income, and it is the largest finding in the book. The operating margin is the gross margin less the operating expense ratio, which means a complete answer says which of the two moved; in 2025 both did, and an impairment loss of $2,724,000 inside the expense ratio hides an underlying operating margin of 16.4 per cent. Below the operating line sit financing, non-operating items and tax, which is why Orontes's net margin held at about 7.1 per cent while its trading got worse. And EBITDA, built as 3,958,800 plus 390,000 plus 2,100,000, flatters every company with a plant to replace: Orontes's $6,448,800 becomes $2,219,100 once capital expenditure, interest and taxes paid are met, and becomes $9,172,800 if the impairment is adjusted away.

Case-style set: the press release and the statements
The headline is arithmetically true and economically incomplete.
What it leaves out. Adjusted EBITDA of $9,172,800 is built by adding back $2,100,000 of depreciation and then a $2,724,000 impairment loss. The impairment is an operating expense under the standards and the depreciation corresponds to assets that Orontes replaced during the year at a cost of $3,000,000. The release reports a margin of 21.8 per cent on a company whose capital expenditure exceeded its depreciation charge, and says nothing about either figure. "Up strongly on the prior year" is also unverifiable, because the prior year had no impairment to add back, so the two adjusted figures are not built on the same basis.
The reported margins. Gross 35.0 per cent, down from 36.0. Operating 9.9 per cent, down from 11.0. Net 7.1 per cent, broadly flat, and held there by equity-method income and a lower effective tax rate rather than by trading.
The figure I would insist on. Capital expenditure of $3,000,000, stated next to the EBITDA figure. One line turns a flattering ratio into a usable one: it tells the reader that the $6,448,800 of EBITDA has a prior claim on it of three million dollars before any lender or shareholder is paid, and $2,219,100 is what remained once interest and taxes paid were met as well.
