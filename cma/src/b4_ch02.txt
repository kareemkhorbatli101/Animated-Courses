WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 1 described the control environment and said the tone at the top decides whether other controls work. This chapter names the people who set that tone and says what each of them answers for.
2.1  What corporate governance is
Corporate governance is the system by which a company is directed and held to account. It exists because the people who own a company are usually not the people who run it. The owners need some way to be sure their money is used in their interest.
Figure F402-02 shows who answers to whom. Read it from the top: shareholders elect the board, the board appoints and assesses the officers, and the board, through its audit committee, appoints the external auditor.
Figure F402-02. Who answers to whom in a governed company.
One line in that diagram does the most work. The external auditor is appointed by, and reports to, the audit committee rather than the management whose statements are being audited. An auditor hired and paid by the chief financial officer could not credibly give an opinion on that officer's accounts.

2.2  What the board is responsible for

The distinction the exam tests most often is between oversight and management. A board challenges and approves a strategy; it does not write it. It satisfies itself that controls exist and work; it does not operate them. A board that starts managing has stopped overseeing, because it can no longer review its own work independently.
EXAM TRAP   Oversight is not management
If a question describes a board approving a capital project, that is oversight. If it describes a board negotiating the contract, something has gone wrong. Look for who decides and who reviews: they should not be the same people.

2.3  The hierarchy of governance documents
A company is governed by a stack of documents, each one operating within the limits of the one above it. Figure F402-04 sets out the hierarchy the exam names.
Figure F402-04. The hierarchy of governance documents.
The articles of incorporation are filed with the state and create the company: its name, purpose and authorized shares. The bylaws are the company's internal rulebook on how the board meets and how officers are appointed. Policies state what the company intends, and procedures say step by step how to do it.
Two things follow. First, a lower document can never contradict a higher one: a procedure breaching a policy is void, and so is a bylaw breaching the articles. Second, the difficulty of changing them rises as you go up. Management rewrites a procedure, the board approves a policy, and the shareholders must vote to change the articles.

The audit committee deserves particular attention. Every member must be independent. That means taking no consulting or advisory fee from the company, and not being an affiliated person. The committee appoints, pays and oversees the external auditor, and must provide a route for complaints, including anonymous ones.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'Every audit committee must have a financial expert'
Many guides say this. The requirement is to disclose whether at least one audit committee financial expert serves, and if none does, to say why not. A company may lawfully have none, provided it explains itself. Exchange rules separately require members to be financially literate, which is a lower bar.

2.4  Why personnel policy is a control
Controls are carried out by people. That single fact makes hiring, training, supervision and dismissal part of internal control rather than a separate human resources matter. Figure F402-07 takes each practice and says what it is actually controlling.

Mandatory leave and job rotation deserve a word, because their purpose is not obvious. A fraud that must be tended daily, such as moving money between accounts to hide a shortfall, is exposed when someone else sits at the desk. Two consecutive weeks is the usual requirement, because one week can be survived.
The chapter 1 failure shows the cost of getting this wrong. The purchasing manager delegated supplier approval verbally and never took it back. The duty appeared in nobody's job description, so nobody could be held to account for it.

2.5  Extended Example 2A: four decisions and who makes them
This example takes four decisions facing Orontes and asks, for each one, which document governs it and who must act. It is exam-core: questions in this area are nearly always of exactly this shape.
1. Raising the number of authorized shares
Orontes wants to issue new shares to fund the Gulf expansion, but has already issued all the shares its articles authorize. The articles of incorporation must be amended, which requires a shareholder vote. The board cannot do this alone, however clear the commercial case.
2. Changing the quorum for board meetings
The board wants to reduce the quorum from five directors to four. This is a bylaw matter, and the board can amend its own bylaws, provided the amendment does not conflict with the articles or with state law.
3. Requiring two signatures on large payments
After the supplier fraud, the board wants every payment above a threshold to carry two signatures. This is a policy: the board approves it, and management writes the procedure that puts it into effect, naming the systems, the limits and the authorised signatories.
4. Dismissing the external auditor
The chief financial officer is unhappy with the auditor's questions and wants a different firm. He cannot make that decision. The external auditor is appointed, paid and overseen by the audit committee, precisely so that the officers whose statements are audited cannot remove an auditor who challenges them.

WORKED EXAMPLE   The pattern behind all four
Ask first which document governs the decision, because that decides who may act.
The higher the document, the harder it is to change: management, then board, then shareholders.
Where a decision would let someone review their own work, the structure moves it elsewhere.
The audit committee exists to hold one such line: the auditor answers to it, not to management.

Chapter summary
Figure F402-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F402-09. Chapter 2 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Corporate governance is the system by which a company is directed and held to account, and it exists because owners are not managers. The board oversees strategy, risk, control and reporting, appoints and assesses the CEO, and owes duties of care and loyalty; it does not manage. Governance documents form a hierarchy of law, articles of incorporation, bylaws, policies and procedures, and no lower document may contradict a higher one. Every audit committee member must be independent, and the committee, not management, appoints and oversees the external auditor. Personnel policies are themselves controls, because every control is carried out by a person.

Case question: the chairman who wants both jobs
The chairman of Orontes Foods has proposed that he also take the role of chief executive, arguing that the company is moving quickly into the Gulf and needs one person able to decide. He has asked the board to approve the change at its next meeting.
The bylaws say the chairman and the chief executive must be different people. The articles of incorporation are silent on the question. Two of the seven directors are executives of the company, and the other five are independent. One of the independent directors is the chairman's brother-in-law. The chairman has held the role for nine years and was previously the company's finance director.
The chairman has also suggested that, to save cost, the audit committee could meet twice a year rather than four times, and that the external auditor could report to him directly so that the board hears a single consistent account.
The chief financial officer supports the proposal. Rania, the controller, has been asked to brief the board on what it may and may not do, and on which parts of the proposal would weaken governance even if they were lawful.
