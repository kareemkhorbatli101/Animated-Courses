WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 3 separated authorization, recording, custody and reconciliation. This chapter applies the same idea to the people who build and run the system, where one person with the right access can do all four at once.
6.1  Segregation inside the systems function
A computer concentrates power. A clerk who alters one invoice affects one transaction; a programmer who alters one routine affects every transaction it touches, silently, until someone notices. That is why the systems function has its own segregation rules.

Input manipulation is the commonest by a wide margin, and it is worth noticing why. It needs no technical skill at all: the person simply enters something false into a system working exactly as designed. The Chapter 1 supplier fraud was input manipulation, not hacking.
Direct file alteration deserves attention for the opposite reason. It bypasses the application, so the application's own audit trail shows nothing. Detecting it needs controls at the database level: restricted access, and logging that the database administrator cannot quietly switch off.
Malware and ransomware attract the most attention, but the route in is usually mundane. Most arrive through a person opening an attachment or entering a password on a false page, which makes them a training and access problem as much as a technical one.

6.4  Physical and logical access
Access control has two halves, shown in Figure F406-05. Physical access controls decide who can reach the machine. Logical access controls decide who can reach the programs and the data.
Figure F406-05. Physical access against logical access.
Physical controls are the older idea and still necessary. A server room needs a lock, a record of who entered, and a procedure for destroying old drives. A disk carried out of the building defeats every password on it.
The physical controls also cover equipment that leaves the room. Laptops and portable drives carry the same data outside every lock the company owns. Disposal, encryption of portable media and a register of what has been issued therefore belong here rather than with the logical controls.
Figure F406-06 takes the logical controls in turn.

Least privilege is the one that decays fastest. Access accumulates as people change roles, because granting is easy and revoking is forgotten. The access review exists to find the clerk who still holds the rights of a job she left two years ago.
EXAM TRAP   A log nobody reads is not a control
Logging appears in almost every list of access controls, and it earns its place only if someone reviews it. A question describing extensive logging with no review is describing a detective control that detects nothing.

6.5  Extended Example 6A: one programmer, four duties
This example takes a single arrangement at Orontes and shows how one person's access collapsed the four duties of Chapter 3 into one pair of hands. It is exam-core.
1. The arrangement
When Orontes installed its payables system, one developer built it and stayed on to support it. He writes the code, moves his own changes into production, holds the database administrator password, and is the person who investigates when a payment query arises.
2. Mapping it to the four duties
He can create a supplier record directly in the database, which is authorization. He can alter the posting routine, which is recording. Releasing a payment run gives him effective custody. And he investigates the queries that would reveal any of it, which is reconciliation. One person, four duties.
3. Which threats become available
Program alteration, because he changes code with no independent review. Direct file alteration, because he holds the database password and can edit records outside the application. Data theft, because he can extract the supplier and payment history. In the second case the application's audit trail would show nothing.
4. What the controls should be
Separate development from production: he may write code, but someone else moves it into live use. Take the database password away and give it to a separate administrator, logged and reviewed. Move payment release to the cashier. Move query investigation to accounts payable.
5. What it costs
Almost nothing in money, and something real in convenience: a change that took him an afternoon now needs another person. That is the trade the chapter is about, and the answer at Orontes is clear, because the alternative is one person able to move cash without trace.

Chapter summary
Figure F406-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F406-08. Chapter 6 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A computer concentrates power, so the systems function has its own segregation: the analyst designs, the programmer writes, the operator runs, the librarian controls versions and data control checks input and output, and no one of them may both change the system and use it unobserved. The threats are input manipulation, which is the commonest and needs no skill, program alteration, direct file alteration, data theft, sabotage, and malware. Physical access controls decide who can reach the machine; logical access controls decide who can reach the data, through unique identification, authentication, least privilege, access reviews, logging that someone actually reads, and removal on exit.

Case question: the developer who never left
When Orontes installed its payables system six years ago, an external developer named Fadi built it. He was then hired permanently to support it, and no one has reviewed the arrangement since. Fadi writes the code, moves his own changes into production, and holds the database administrator password.
He also holds a user account in the payables application with rights to release a payment run, which was granted during a staff shortage two years ago and never removed. When a supplier queries a payment, accounts payable forwards the query to Fadi because he can read the database directly and answer fastest. Orontes has no separate database administrator and no written procedure for moving code into production.
The group controller, Rania, has been asked to review systems security before the Gulf expansion. She has found no evidence of wrongdoing. Fadi is well regarded and has twice worked unpaid weekends to recover from a failed upgrade.
The chief financial officer is reluctant to change anything, arguing that Fadi is the only person who understands the system and that imposing controls on a trusted employee would be insulting and would slow down support.
