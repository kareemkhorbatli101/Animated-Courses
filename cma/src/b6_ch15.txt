Chapter 15
Marketable securities
الأوراق المالية القابلة للتداول
Chapter 13 ended with $4,402,100 of Orontes's cash that the business does not need this month and will need in instalments over the year. Leaving it in a current account costs the company the return it could have earned; locking it into a five-year bond costs the company the ability to pay for a roasting line in March. Marketable securities are the middle: instruments that earn a return and can be turned back into cash quickly and at a predictable price.
This chapter names the instruments, sets out the four variables a treasurer trades off between them, and builds a portfolio for a company whose future needs are known approximately and whose tolerance for loss is nil.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Part 1 classified Orontes's $42,000 of trading securities, its $88,500 of debt securities and its $27,000 of equity securities, and taught the accounting for each. Chapter 13 of this book found a cash balance of 75.9 days of operating payments and recommended placing the surplus against the dates it will be needed. This chapter chooses the instruments.
15.1  Why a company holds securities rather than cash
Four reasons, and the exam asks for them in these terms.
As a substitute for cash. Money needed within the year earns something while it waits, and the only requirement is that it can be realised when the day comes. This is the largest use and the one Orontes has.
As a store against a known future payment. A tax instalment in March, a bond maturing in September, a capital project's third payment in July: each is a known amount on a known date, and a security maturing on that date funds it exactly, with no risk that a sale is needed at a bad moment.
As a buffer for the unexpected, which is the precautionary motive of Chapter 13 held in an earning form rather than as a balance.
As a home for seasonal surpluses. A company that collects in three months and spends through twelve has a predictable rise and fall, and the peak sits in securities until it drains away.
Note what marketable securities are not for. They are not an investment portfolio: a treasury department buying equities with working capital is speculating with money the business needs, however good the equities. The test in every case is the one the exam applies — could this be turned into its full value in cash on the day the business needs it?

15.2  The instruments
The money market offers a short list, and the exam names it.
Treasury bills are short-term obligations of the government, issued at a discount and redeemed at face value, with maturities of a few weeks to a year. They carry no credit risk in their own currency and the deepest secondary market of any instrument, which makes them the benchmark against which everything else is priced.
Treasury notes and treasury bonds are the same issuer at longer maturities — notes to about ten years, bonds beyond. They are not money market instruments, but a note with three months left to run behaves like one, and a treasurer buys them in the secondary market for exactly that reason.
Commercial paper is unsecured short-term borrowing by a large company, issued at a discount, usually for up to 270 days. It pays more than a treasury bill because it carries the issuer's credit risk, and it is rated; Chapter 18 looks at it from the issuer's side.
Certificates of deposit are time deposits with a bank, evidenced by a negotiable certificate so that they can be sold before maturity. They pay interest rather than being issued at a discount.
Repurchase agreements are sales of securities with an agreement to buy them back at a stated price on a stated date, usually overnight. Economically they are secured loans: the lender holds the securities as collateral and earns the difference between the two prices. They are the standard way to employ cash for one night.
Bankers' acceptances are time drafts drawn on and accepted by a bank, originally to finance trade. The bank's acceptance makes the instrument the bank's obligation, so it is high-quality and trades at a small spread over treasury bills.
Money market funds pool many such instruments and offer daily access. They give a small company the diversification and the professional selection it could not arrange itself, for a management fee.
Municipal securities, in the United States, are issued by states and local authorities and their interest is exempt from federal income tax, which changes the comparison entirely — section 15.3 does the arithmetic.
Eurodollar deposits are dollar deposits held outside the United States, which pay slightly more because they fall outside domestic banking regulation.

15.3  The trade-offs
Five variables decide a selection and they pull against each other. The outcome names them and the exam's questions are always about which one is being sacrificed.
Safety, meaning the risk that the principal is not returned. For working capital this is close to absolute: the money is needed, so a security that might not pay is unusable whatever its yield. Treasury bills are safest, bank instruments next, corporate paper after that.
Marketability or liquidity, meaning how quickly the instrument can be sold at a price close to its value. A treasury bill can be sold in any size in minutes; a certificate of deposit from a small bank may take a day and a concession in price. Note that marketability is not the same as maturity: a thirty-year treasury bond is highly marketable and has a long maturity, while a three-month deposit at a small bank may be unmarketable and short.
Yield, which rises as the other four worsen. A yield materially above the benchmark is always compensation for something, and the treasurer's job is to identify what.
Maturity, which brings two separate risks. Interest rate risk, which Chapter 4 measured with duration: a longer instrument falls further if rates rise and the money has to be raised early by selling. And reinvestment risk, the mirror: a short instrument has to be rolled over at whatever rate exists then.
Taxability. Where interest on an instrument is tax-exempt, its stated yield is not comparable with a taxable one. The tax-equivalent yield puts them on the same basis: a municipal security yielding 3.0 per cent, for a company paying 25 per cent, is worth 3.0 ÷ 0.75 = 4.00 per cent of taxable yield. Anything taxable yielding less than 4.00 per cent is inferior to that municipal, and anything above it is better.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: comparing three instruments
Step 1. A treasury bill. A $1,000,000 bill with 90 days to run is offered at $985,000. The gain is 1,000,000 − 985,000 = 15,000, which over the period is 15,000 ÷ 985,000 = 1.52 per cent.
Step 2. Annualise it. There are 365 ÷ 90 = 4.0556 such periods in a year, so the bond-equivalent yield is about 1.52 × 4.0556 = 6.17 per cent.
Step 3. A municipal at 4.8 per cent, tax-exempt. Its taxable equivalent at a 25 per cent rate is 4.8 ÷ 0.75 = 6.40 per cent.
Step 4. Commercial paper at 6.5 per cent, taxable, from an A-rated issuer.
Check. On yield alone the commercial paper wins, by 0.10 points over the municipal and 0.33 over the bill. On safety the bill wins outright. On marketability the bill wins again, and the municipal is the weakest of the three. For a company's working capital the correct answer is usually the bill, and the question the exam is really asking is whether a third of a point a year — on $1,000,000 that is $3,300 — is worth the credit risk of an A-rated corporate and the loss of same-day marketability. For money that must be there, it is not.

15.4  Risk and return in a short-term portfolio
Chapter 1 established that return rises with risk. A short-term portfolio is where that principle meets a constraint which overrides it: the money is spoken for.
The constraint works like this. A working-capital portfolio exists to be spent. If it falls in value, the company does not have the option of waiting for recovery, because the payment it was held against falls due on a date. So the first filter is not return at all; it is whether the instrument will be worth its full value on the day the money is needed. Everything that survives that filter is then ranked on yield.
Two strategies follow, and both appear in questions.
Maturity matching buys instruments that mature on the dates the forecast says the money is needed. It removes interest rate risk entirely for that money, because nothing has to be sold: the instrument simply matures. It is the strategy for known commitments.
Laddering spreads the portfolio across a series of maturities — three, six, nine and twelve months — so that something matures regularly and the average yield is higher than a succession of very short instruments would give. It is the strategy for the part of the balance whose timing is uncertain, and it reduces reinvestment risk by never rolling the whole portfolio at one rate.
Both are forms of the same discipline: let the cash forecast of Chapter 13 drive the maturity structure, rather than choosing maturities by the yield curve and hoping.
Three risks remain and should be named in any answer.
Credit risk, which is managed by quality limits — a policy that nothing below A-1 or P-1 may be bought, and that no single issuer may exceed a stated share of the portfolio.
Interest rate risk, which maturity matching removes for matched money and laddering reduces for the rest.
Liquidity risk, which is the risk that an instrument cannot be sold when an unforeseen need arises, and which is managed by keeping a tranche of the portfolio in treasury bills or an overnight fund regardless of yield.
A written investment policy that states those limits is the control the exam expects a management accountant to recommend, because a treasury department left to maximise yield will eventually find an instrument that does.

15.5  Building Orontes's portfolio
Put the chapter to work. Chapter 13 concluded that Orontes should keep a working balance of about thirty days of operating payments, roughly $2,900,000, and place the rest against the dates the forecast gives. The surplus is 7,302,100 − 2,900,000 = 4,402,100.
The known commitments come first. Two projects need $6,000,000 over four quarters, of which the first instalments fall within this surplus. Each instalment is matched: a treasury bill or a government note maturing in the month the payment is due, bought today at a price that fixes the yield and requires no sale.
What is left is laddered. Divide the unmatched balance across three, six, nine and twelve-month maturities, so that a tranche matures every quarter and the yield exceeds what an overnight account would pay.
A liquidity tranche sits inside the ladder, not beside it: a portion held in treasury bills or an overnight repurchase agreement, available the same day, sized against the precautionary motive of Chapter 13.
What it is worth. At the 4 per cent available on short-dated government paper, the surplus earns 4,402,100 × 0.04 = 176,084 a year that the company earned nothing on in 2025. Against Orontes's net income of $2,969,100 that is about six per cent of a year's profit, obtained with no risk to the principal, no change to the business and no capital investment — which is why treasury management is the part of finance with the highest return on effort, and the part most often left undone.
What it does not change. The $176,084 is real and it is not a substitute for the $840,000 of gross margin Book 1 found the company giving away each year. Ranking findings by size, as Book 1's last chapter insisted, puts the margin first and this second. Both are worth doing and only one of them is easy.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Security"
English security was the false friend of Book 2, Chapter 3, where it meant both an instrument and a collateral. Here a third sense appears that is easier to miss: in the phrase safety of principal, the quality a treasurer wants, English avoids the word security altogether and uses safety, because security would be ambiguous. Arabic keeps the three apart — ورقة مالية for the instrument, ضمان for collateral, أمان for safety — and French does the same with titre, garantie and sécurité. The practical consequence is that the French sécurité, which looks like the English security, means only the third of the three, so a French speaker reading an English prospectus about securities must suppress the obvious reading.

Chapter summary
Figure F615-05 brings the chapter together.
Figure F615-05. Chapter 15 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Marketable securities hold money that will be needed within the year in an earning form: as a cash substitute, against known commitments, as a precautionary buffer and as a home for seasonal surpluses — and never as an investment portfolio, because the money belongs to the business. The instruments are treasury bills, which are the benchmark; commercial paper, certificates of deposit, repurchase agreements, bankers' acceptances, money market funds, municipal securities and Eurodollar deposits, each priced against that benchmark by what it adds in credit risk or takes away in marketability. The trade-offs are safety, marketability, yield, maturity and taxability, and every extra point of yield is compensation for one of the others: a bill bought at 985,000 and redeemed at 1,000,000 in 90 days returns 15,000 ÷ 985,000 = 1.52 per cent, which is 1.52 × 4.0556 = 6.17 per cent a year, against a 4.8 per cent municipal worth 4.8 ÷ 0.75 = 6.40 per cent taxable. In a short-term portfolio the first filter is not return but whether the money will be there on the day, after which maturity matching handles known commitments and a ladder handles the rest, under a written policy with quality and concentration limits. And for Orontes the whole exercise is worth 4,402,100 × 0.04 = 176,084 a year — six per cent of a year's profit, for no risk to principal, and still second in rank to the $840,000 of margin.

Case-style set: an investment policy for Orontes
What is wrong with the proposal. Three things, in order of seriousness. It invests money that is needed: $1,500,000 falls due each quarter for the projects and $500,000 in March for tax, and a one-year certificate cannot be used for either without selling it — and a certificate from a regional bank is the least marketable instrument on the list. It concentrates the entire balance in one issuer, so a single bank's difficulty would put the whole of Orontes's liquidity in question. And it reaches for 4.6 per cent when the premium over government paper is precisely the compensation for those two defects; the treasurer has identified the yield and not what is being given up for it.
The structure I would adopt. Keep about $2,900,000 as the working balance, roughly thirty days of operating payments. Match the known commitments: treasury bills or government notes maturing in March for the tax instalment and in each quarter for the project payments. Ladder the remainder across three, six, nine and twelve months so that a tranche matures each quarter. Keep a liquidity tranche in bills or overnight repurchase agreements for the unexpected.
Four provisions for a written policy. First, permitted instruments, by name, with government paper, bank certificates from rated institutions, A-1 or P-1 commercial paper and money market funds, and nothing else. Second, a maximum maturity, which for working capital should not exceed twelve months and should not exceed the date the money is forecast to be needed. Third, concentration limits: no more than a stated proportion with any one issuer and none at all with an unrated one. Fourth, that the cash forecast, not the yield curve, determines the maturity structure, and that the treasurer reports monthly against it.
