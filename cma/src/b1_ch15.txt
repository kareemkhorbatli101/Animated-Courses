WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
IFRS 5 has a similar idea of discontinued operations, and IFRS also has OCI. The tests and some presentation rules differ, and some IFRS OCI items never return to profit. Section 15.6 compares them.
15.1  Gains and losses
A gain or loss is an increase or decrease in equity from a peripheral or incidental transaction: something other than the company's main activities and other than a transaction with owners. Selling an old machine, settling a lawsuit and a change in the value of an investment are examples.
Gains and losses are shown net. When Orontes sold its old Dubai oven, it did not report the proceeds as revenue; it reported a gain of $3,000: the proceeds minus the carrying amount. In a multi-step statement, most gains and losses appear in other income and expense, below operating income.
Losses that arise from operating assets, such as impairments, are usually shown within operating expenses. Items that are unusual or infrequent get a separate line within continuing operations, before tax. Figure F15-02 shows where Orontes's 2025 gains and losses appear.

15.2  Expense recognition
Expenses are recognized in one of three ways. When there is a clear cause and effect, the expense follows the related revenue: cost of goods sold is recognized when the goods are sold. This is the matching principle from Chapter 11.
When a cost benefits several periods but cannot be linked to specific revenue, it is spread by systematic and rational allocation. Depreciation of the bottling line and the expiry of prepaid rent are examples.
When a cost has no clear link to future revenue, it is a period cost and is expensed at once. Advertising and research and development are expensed when incurred, even if they may bring future sales. Figure F15-03 summarizes the three approaches.

15.3  Comprehensive income and reclassification
Comprehensive income is net income plus other comprehensive income. U.S. OCI includes holding gains and losses on available-for-sale debt securities, foreign currency translation adjustments, the effective part of cash-flow hedges, and some pension gains and losses. Unrealized gains on equity securities are not OCI; they go to net income.
When an OCI item is realized, for example when an AFS bond is sold, the gain moves into net income. At the same time a reclassification adjustment removes it from OCI. Without this adjustment, the same gain would be counted twice in comprehensive income: once in OCI when it arose, and again in net income on the sale.
Figure F15-04 shows an AFS bond that Orontes buys for $100,000. Its value rises to $108,000 at the end of 2026, and Orontes sells it for $110,000 in 2027. Over the two years, comprehensive income before tax is $10,000: the total gain, counted once.
Figure F15-04. OCI and the reclassification adjustment for an AFS bond over two years.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the reclassification adjustment
Step 1. 2026: the holding gain of $8,000 goes to OCI. After tax at 25%, OCI is $6,000, and AOCI builds up by the same amount.
Step 2. 2027: the bond is sold; net income shows the realized gain of $10,000.
Step 3. OCI for 2027 shows the holding gain of $2,000 and a reclassification of −$10,000: a net OCI loss of $8,000 before tax, $6,000 after tax.
Check. AOCI for this bond returns to zero. Figure F15-05 shows both years.

Forgetting the reclassification counts a realized gain twice.
Comprehensive income adds this year's OCI, not the AOCI balance.

15.4  Is it a discontinued operation?
A disposal is reported as a discontinued operation only if three things are true. First, it is a component of an entity: its operations and cash flows can be clearly separated from the rest of the company. Second, the disposal is a strategic shift that has, or will have, a major effect on operations and results.
Third, the component has been sold, abandoned or spun off, or it is held for sale. Examples of a strategic shift are leaving a major line of business, a major geographic area or a major equity-method investment. There is no official percentage for 'major'; it is a judgment.
Selling one machine, one store or one small product is not a strategic shift. The gain or loss stays in continuing operations, as for the Dubai oven. A business that meets the held-for-sale criteria when it is acquired is also a discontinued operation.

15.5  Held for sale: criteria and measurement
A component can be reported as discontinued before it is sold, once it is held for sale. All six criteria must be met: management with authority commits to a plan; the business is available for immediate sale; an active search for a buyer has begun; a sale is probable within one year; the price is reasonable; and the plan is unlikely to change.
A held-for-sale disposal group is measured at the lower of its carrying amount and its fair value less cost to sell. Any write-down is a loss. Depreciation stops, and later increases in value are recognized only up to the losses already recorded.
Chapter 3 showed that Orontes's frozen-foods business produced a loss from discontinued operations of $450,000 in 2026. Figure F15-07 shows the timeline. The board approved the plan in March, but the business became held for sale only on June 30, when all six criteria were met.

15.6  Presentation and IFRS differences
The results of a discontinued operation, including its operating results and any remeasurement or disposal gain or loss, are shown net of their own tax below income from continuing operations. Public companies also show per-share amounts for discontinued operations. The comparative years are reclassified so that all periods are shown on the same basis.
On the balance sheet, the assets and liabilities of a discontinued operation are shown separately for all periods presented. Held-for-sale assets that are not a discontinued operation are also shown separately, but their results stay in continuing operations.
Figure F15-09 compares these rules with IFRS. Under IFRS 5, a discontinued operation must be a separate major line of business or geographical area, and earlier balance sheets are not re-presented. Some IFRS OCI items, such as a revaluation surplus, never return to profit or loss.

Chapter summary
Figure F15-10 brings the chapter together.
Figure F15-10. Chapter 15 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Gains and losses come from peripheral transactions and are shown net; unusual or infrequent items stay in continuing operations, before tax. Expenses are matched with revenue, allocated systematically, or expensed at once as period costs. Comprehensive income is net income plus OCI, and a reclassification adjustment moves realized amounts from AOCI to net income so they are not counted twice. A discontinued operation is a component whose disposal is a strategic shift with a major effect. A held-for-sale group is measured at the lower of carrying amount and fair value less cost to sell, and depreciation stops. Results are shown net of tax below continuing operations, and prior periods are reclassified.
