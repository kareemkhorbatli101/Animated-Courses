12.1  The system development life cycle
The system development life cycle (SDLC) is the ordered sequence by which a system is planned, built and run. Figure F412-02 shows the five stages the exam names.
Figure F412-02. The five stages of the system development life cycle.
The two design stages are the pair candidates confuse. Conceptual design answers what the system should do, in the language of the business. Physical design answers how it will do it: the files, the programs, the screens and the controls. One is a decision about purpose, the other about construction.
Conversion deserves a word of its own. Moving from the old system to the new can be done directly, in parallel, as a pilot in one site, or in phases. Running both systems in parallel is the safest and the most expensive, and the choice is a judgement about what a failure would cost.

12.2  Business process analysis
Business process analysis examines how work is actually done, in order to improve it. Figure F412-03 sets out the steps.
Figure F412-03. The steps of business process analysis

What it involves
At Orontes
1 Map the current process
Draw what actually happens, not what the manual says

Its role in system performance rests on a single principle, and it is the most useful sentence in this chapter. Automating a bad process does not fix it: it makes the same errors faster and at greater volume, and makes them harder to see because nobody is handling them any more.
The first step is where the work is. Mapping what actually happens, rather than what the manual says, routinely reveals steps that exist only because someone once needed them. Chapter 7's flowchart is the tool, and the question is the same: who does this, and why.

12.3  Robotic process automation
robotic process automation (RPA) uses software robots to carry out rule-based steps on structured data, working through existing screens as a person would. Figure F412-04 sets out what it does and where it stops.
Figure F412-04. What robotic process automation does, and where it stops
Robotic process automation (F.3.c)
What it means
At Orontes
What it does
Software robots copy rule-based keyboard and mouse steps on structured data
Matching invoices to orders and flagging exceptions
Benefits
Speed, accuracy, operation outside working hours, a complete log of every action
A three-hour reconciliation finished overnight
No change to systems
It works through existing screens, so no integration project is needed
Useful where an ERP cannot yet reach
Limitation: brittleness
A changed screen or report layout stops the robot
A vendor update breaks the invoice matching
Limitation: scope
Only stable, rule-based, structured work; judgement defeats it
Deciding whether an unusual order is genuine
Control requirement
A robot needs its own identity, access rights and segregation
A robot that can both post and approve is one person with two duties
Its appeal is that it needs no integration project. Where an ERP cannot yet reach, or where two systems must be joined and neither can be changed, a robot can do the keying that a person was doing, overnight and without error.
The control point is the one most often missed. A robot is a user: it needs its own identity, its own access rights and its own place in the segregation of duties. A robot that can both post a transaction and approve it is one person holding two duties, and Chapter 3 applies to it exactly as to anybody else.
EXAM TRAP   A robot is not judgement
Robotic process automation handles stable, rule-based work on structured data. If the task requires deciding whether something is reasonable, it is outside the technique, however it is described. Adding artificial intelligence to the description does not change what the robot itself does.

12.4  Cloud computing and software as a service
Cloud computing is on-demand access to a shared pool of computing resources over a network. The recognised definition gives it five characteristics: self-service on demand, broad network access, resources pooled across customers, rapid elasticity, and metered use. Figure F412-05 shows the three service models.
Figure F412-05. The three cloud service models and who manages what.
Efficiency comes from three of those characteristics. Metered use turns a capital purchase into an operating cost paid only for what is consumed. Elasticity means capacity can rise for a peak and fall again. And self-service means a server is available in minutes rather than after a procurement cycle.
Four deployment models cut across the three service models. A private cloud serves one organization, a community cloud a group with shared concerns, a public cloud anyone who pays, and a hybrid combines them. The distinction the exam tests is simply that service models and deployment models are different questions.
software as a service (SaaS) is the model Orontes is most likely to meet: the provider runs the application and the company simply uses it. Figure F412-06 sets out both sides.
Figure F412-06. The advantages and disadvantages of software as a service
Advantage of SaaS
Disadvantage

Recurring cost forever, which may exceed a purchase
Updates

Two disadvantages deserve weight. The company relies on the provider's controls rather than its own, which is why a report on those controls is normally obtained and read. And data residency can matter legally: Chapter 11 showed that Jordanian and Saudi law both constrain where personal data may be held.

12.5  Blockchain, distributed ledgers and smart contracts
A blockchain is a distributed, append-only ledger of cryptographically linked blocks, agreed between participants by consensus rather than by a central authority. A distributed ledger is the wider class, and a smart contract is code held on such a ledger that executes itself when its conditions are met.
Figure F412-07 states what each term means and where the technology actually stands.
Figure F412-07. Blockchain terms and their status

What it means

Blockchain

WATCH: NOT YET TESTABLE   An honest assessment, as at October 2026
Between 2018 and 2022 it was widely predicted that blockchain would replace accounting systems. That did not happen. The Australian securities exchange cancelled its blockchain settlement replacement in 2022 after large write-offs, and no standard-setter recognises triple-entry accounting. What did materialise is narrower: accounting and audit of crypto assets, tokenised funds, stablecoin payments, and some trade finance and provenance work. A candidate who can say what the technology is and where it genuinely applies is better prepared than one who repeats the predictions.
The exam asks you to recognise potential applications, so know the realistic ones. Where several parties must share one record and do not fully trust each other, a distributed ledger has something to offer. Where one company keeps its own books, a database is simpler, faster and cheaper.

12.6  Extended Example 12A: judging four proposals
F.3.d asks you to evaluate where a technology would improve the processing of accounting data. This example judges four proposals put to the Orontes board, accepting two and refusing two.
Figure F412-04 and Figure F412-06 supply the evidence this evaluation needs, because each sets out not only what a technology does but what it costs and where it stops.
1. The test to apply
Three questions settle most proposals. Which process does this change, and is that process worth keeping at all? What specifically improves: cost, speed, accuracy or capability? And what new risk or control requirement does it create?
2. Proposal one: a robot to match invoices
Accounts payable spends three hours a day matching invoices to orders and goods received notes. The work is rule-based and the data is structured, so a robot fits. It improves speed and accuracy, and it creates one new requirement: the robot needs its own identity and must not also approve payment. Accept.
3. Proposal two: moving the expenses system to a hosted service
The expenses system is small, standard and not a competitive advantage. A hosted service removes the maintenance and the upgrades. The risks are dependence on the provider and the location of the data, both manageable for expenses. Accept, after reading the provider's controls report and confirming where the data sits.
4. Proposal three: a blockchain for the supplier ledger
A consultant proposes recording supplier balances on a blockchain, to make them tamper-evident. But Orontes keeps its own books, there is no group of mutually distrustful parties needing one shared record, and the ERP database with proper access controls already prevents undetected alteration. Refuse: it answers a problem the company does not have.
5. Proposal four: automating the current month-end close
The close takes eleven days, and the proposal is to automate it as it stands. The first question defeats it: the process includes two days reconciling systems that the ERP has now merged, and a manual upload that no longer exists. Refuse for now, analyse the process, and automate what survives.

Chapter summary
Figure F412-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F412-09. Chapter 12 summary map: the key rule for each section.
The system development life cycle runs systems analysis, conceptual design, physical design, implementation and conversion, and operations and maintenance, with users involved throughout and each stage approved before the next. Business process analysis maps what actually happens and removes waste before anything is automated, because automating a bad process makes the same errors faster. Robotic process automation carries out rule-based steps on structured data without changing the underlying systems, and a robot needs its own identity and its own segregation of duties. Cloud computing gives on-demand, metered, elastic access to shared resources, and software as a service trades maintenance for dependence on the provider's controls and the location of the data. Blockchain's realistic applications are narrow, and the claim that it would replace accounting systems did not materialise.

Case question: four proposals and a consultant
The Orontes ERP has been live for six months. Four proposals are now before the board. The first is a software robot to match supplier invoices against orders and goods received notes, work that accounts payable currently performs for three hours each day and which follows fixed rules on structured data.
The second is to move the small expenses system to a hosted service run by the supplier, removing the maintenance and the upgrades. The expenses system is standard, is not a competitive advantage, and holds the home addresses of staff who claim mileage.
The third comes from an external consultant, who proposes recording supplier balances on a blockchain so that they become tamper-evident. Orontes keeps its own books and no other party shares the supplier ledger.
The fourth is to automate the month-end close exactly as it is performed today. The close takes eleven days and still includes two days reconciling the plant and ledger figures, which the ERP now holds as one value, and a manual upload that no longer exists. No one has examined the close since the ERP went live, and the finance team has asked only for it to run faster.
