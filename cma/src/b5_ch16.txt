Chapter 16
The market's ratios: EPS, P/E, yield and payout
مؤشرات السوق: ربحية السهم ومكرر الربحية والعائد والتوزيعات
Every ratio so far has been built from the statements alone. The ratios in this chapter need one more number, and it comes from outside the accounts entirely: the price at which the shares change hands. Orontes's shares closed 2025 at $9.25, against $7.80 a year earlier.
That one price turns the accounts into market measures. Earnings per share scales the profit to the unit a shareholder owns; the price/earnings ratio says how many years of that profit the market is paying for; the earnings and dividend yields say what the shareholder earns on the price paid; and the payout ratio says how much of the profit is handed over rather than kept. Together they are how an owner, rather than a lender or a manager, reads a company.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Part 1 printed Orontes's earnings per share as $0.37, basic and diluted, on a weighted average of 8,099,726 shares. Chapter 15 computed book value per share of $2.5177 and a market-to-book ratio of 3.67 at a price of $9.25. This chapter derives the earnings figure, says what makes a diluted figure differ from it, and uses the price four more times.
16.1  Earnings per share
Basic earnings per share is the profit attributable to common shareholders divided by the weighted-average number of common shares outstanding during the period.
Two words in that sentence do the work. Attributable means after any preferred dividends, because a preferred dividend is not the common shareholders' profit; Orontes has no preferred stock, so its whole net income of $2,969,100 is attributable. Weighted-average means each share counts for the fraction of the year it was outstanding, because a share issued in December did not help earn the year's profit.
Orontes began 2025 with 8,000,000 shares and ended with 8,100,000, the 100,000 new shares having been issued on January 2. Each was outstanding for 364 of the 365 days: 100,000 × 364 ÷ 365 = 99,726, and the weighted average is 8,000,000 + 99,726 = 8,099,726 — the figure on the face of the income statement.
So basic earnings per share is 2,969,100 ÷ 8,099,726 = 0.3666, which the statements present as $0.37.

EXAM TRAP   The share count is where the marks are
A share split or a stock dividend is applied retrospectively to every period presented, because no cash came in and nothing was earned differently. A share issue for cash is weighted from its date.
Treasury shares are excluded from the moment they are repurchased, weighted in the same way.
Earnings per share is the one ratio the standards define and require on the face of the income statement. Every other ratio in Book 1 is an analyst's construction.

16.2  Diluted earnings per share
Diluted earnings per share asks what the figure would have been if everything that could become a common share had become one. Convertible bonds, convertible preferred stock, options and warrants are all potential common shares, and a company that has issued them has promised away part of each future dollar of profit.
Orontes reports the same figure for basic and diluted, which tells you it has no potential common shares outstanding. Most listed companies do, so the exam tests the mechanics on figures the question supplies.
Two methods cover almost everything.
The treasury stock method, for options and warrants. Assume they are exercised at their exercise price and that the company uses the proceeds to buy its own shares back at the average market price. The net new shares are the difference.
The if-converted method, for convertible debt and convertible preferred. Assume conversion at the start of the period. Add the shares that would be issued, and add back to the numerator the interest after tax, or the preferred dividend, that would no longer be paid.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: two kinds of dilution
Step 1. Options, by the treasury stock method. Suppose Orontes had 500,000 options outstanding at an exercise price of $5.00, with an average market price of $9.25. The proceeds would be 500,000 × 5.00 = 2,500,000, which buys back 2,500,000 ÷ 9.25 = 270,270 shares, so the net new shares are 500,000 − 270,270 = 229,730. Diluted shares are 8,099,726 + 229,730 = 8,329,456, and diluted earnings per share is 2,969,100 ÷ 8,329,456 = 0.3565.
Step 2. A convertible bond, by the if-converted method. Suppose instead Orontes had $2,000,000 of 5 per cent convertible bonds exchangeable for 400,000 shares. The interest saved is 2,000,000 × 0.05 = 100,000, and after tax at the company's 25 per cent effective rate that is 100,000 × 0.75 = 75,000. The numerator becomes 2,969,100 + 75,000 = 3,044,100 and the share count 8,099,726 + 400,000 = 8,499,726, so diluted earnings per share is 3,044,100 ÷ 8,499,726 = 0.3581.
Step 3. Test each for dilution. A security is only included if it reduces earnings per share. The bond adds 75,000 of income for 400,000 shares, which is 75,000 ÷ 400,000 = 0.1875 a share, below the basic figure of 0.3666, so it dilutes and must be included. Had the bond carried a high enough coupon that its per-share effect exceeded 0.3666, including it would raise earnings per share, and an antidilutive security is excluded.
Check. Both figures are below the basic 0.3666, which is the whole point: diluted earnings per share can never be higher than basic. Where a question gives several instruments, rank them by their per-share effect, include them cheapest first, and stop at the one that stops reducing the figure.

EXAM TRAP   Dilution has a direction
Diluted earnings per share is always less than or equal to basic. A computed diluted figure above the basic one means an antidilutive security has been included by mistake.
A loss changes the direction of the test: when the company reports a loss, potential shares reduce the loss per share and are therefore antidilutive, so diluted equals basic.
The treasury stock method uses the average market price for the period, not the closing price.

16.3  The price/earnings ratio and the earnings yield
The price/earnings ratio is the market price of a share divided by its earnings per share. For Orontes: 9.25 ÷ 0.3666 = 25.23.
Read it two ways, and the exam uses both. Arithmetically, the market is paying 25.23 dollars for each dollar of last year's earnings, or about twenty-five years of earnings at this level. Economically, a high multiple is the market saying it expects earnings to grow, or that it regards them as low-risk, or both; a low multiple is the market expecting decline, or demanding a discount for risk.
The earnings yield is the same relationship inverted: earnings per share over price, 0.3666 ÷ 9.25 = 3.96 per cent. It is the more useful form when comparing a share with a bond, because a bond is quoted as a yield and a 3.96 per cent earnings yield can be set directly against the 6 per cent Orontes pays its own bank.

A multiple of twenty-five on a food manufacturer growing sales at 15 per cent a year is a price that assumes the growth continues. EXAM TRAP   Which earnings in the price/earnings ratio
A trailing multiple uses the last reported year. A forward multiple uses a forecast. They are not comparable, and a question that gives you a forecast expects the forward figure.
Diluted earnings per share is the conventional denominator for a company with potential common shares, because it is the more prudent figure.
A loss-making company has no meaningful price/earnings ratio. The ratio is left blank rather than reported as a negative number.

16.4  Dividend yield and payout
The dividend per share is dividends declared over the shares that will receive them: 450,000 ÷ 8,100,000 = 0.0556.
The dividend yield is that over the price: 0.0556 ÷ 9.25 = 0.60 per cent. An Orontes shareholder receives six-tenths of one per cent a year in cash, which is next to nothing, and expects the rest of the return in the share price.
The dividend payout ratio is dividends over earnings, which Chapter 15 computed as 15.16 per cent from the totals and which comes to the same thing per share: 0.0556 ÷ 0.3666 = 15.17 per cent, the small difference being the share count — the dividend is declared on the 8,100,000 shares in issue and the earnings are divided by the 8,099,726 weighted average.

EXAM TRAP   Yield is on price, payout is on earnings
The dividend yield divides by the share price; the payout ratio divides by earnings. Dividing the dividend by book value or by the par value is a common slip and gives a meaningless figure.
A company can pay a dividend it has not earned: a payout ratio above 100 per cent means the dividend came out of reserves or out of borrowings, which is a finding worth stating.
The declared dividend, not the paid dividend, drives both ratios. Orontes paid $50,000 of cash and declared $450,000.

16.5  Shareholder return, and what a multiple cannot say
A shareholder's return for a year is the dividend received plus the change in the price, over the price at the start. For an Orontes shareholder in 2025 the price gain is 9.25 − 7.80 = 1.45 and the dividend is 0.0556, so the total is 1.45 + 0.0556 = 1.5056, and the return is 1.5056 ÷ 7.80 = 19.30 per cent.
That is the figure an owner actually earned, and it is worth setting against the three returns Book 1 has computed from the accounts: a return on assets of 10.22 per cent, a return on equity of 16.15 per cent, and now a shareholder return of 19.30 per cent. They answer different questions. The first two ask what the company earned on the money invested in it; the third asks what the market paid the shareholder, which depends on the company's results and on a change in sentiment the company does not control.

Three limits of the market ratios close the chapter, and all three are examinable.
The denominator is an accounting number. Every multiple in this chapter divides a market price by a figure the standards produced, so everything Chapter 13 said about the impairment and everything Chapter 17 will say about earnings quality lands inside the ratio. A price/earnings ratio of 25.23 computed on earnings that include a one-off gain is not a multiple of anything repeatable.
The numerator is a price on one day. Orontes's multiple of 25.23 uses the December 31 close. A week earlier or later it would differ, and nothing about the company would have changed.
A multiple is a comparison and not a value. Twenty-five times earnings is high against a food manufacturer and low against a software company. The ratio is the beginning of a valuation question, which Book 2 answers properly with the dividend discount model and the relative-valuation multiples.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Yield"
English yield in finance always means an income expressed as a percentage of a price or a principal — a dividend yield, an earnings yield, a bond's yield to maturity. It never means the amount itself. Arabic العائد covers the same ground and is also used for a return, so the qualifier carries the distinction: عائد التوزيعات for the dividend yield, العائد على الأصول for the return on assets. French is the most careful of the three: rendement for a yield on a price, rentabilité for a return on capital employed, and taux for a rate. Note the trap in both English and French: a yield rises when the price falls, so a share whose yield has improved may simply have had a bad year.

Case-style set: an offer at twenty times earnings
The offer price. Twenty times basic earnings per share of $0.3666 is $7.33 a share. On 8,100,000 shares the total consideration is about $59,400,000.
How it compares. The offer is below the market price of $9.25 — a discount of about 21 per cent — and well above the book value of $2.5177, at nearly three times book. Both comparisons matter and they point in opposite directions. Against the market, the offer is poor: a shareholder could sell at $9.25 today. Against the balance sheet, it looks generous, and that tells you only that the balance sheet omits the brand, the recipes and the future, as Chapter 15 explained.
What I would tell the shareholders. Reject an offer struck at a multiple below the prevailing market multiple of 25.23 unless the buyer can show why the market price is unsustainable. A control premium normally pushes an offer above the market price, not 21 per cent below it.
The figure a careful shareholder would raise is the $2,724,000 impairment loss. It depresses 2025 earnings, so a multiple of twenty applied to those earnings prices the company on a year containing a charge that will not recur. On the operating margin excluding that charge, Orontes's underlying earnings were materially higher, and the offer should be tested against those as well — which is an argument for a higher price, not a lower one.
