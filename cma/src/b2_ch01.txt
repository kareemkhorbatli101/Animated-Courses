WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
In Book 1 you met inventory, cost of goods sold and the idea that expenses are matched with revenue. You also saw that Orontes carries inventory at cost under ASC 330. This book explains how that cost is built.
1.1  Cost objects
A cost object is anything for which a separate cost is measured. At Orontes it can be a division, a product line, one private-label job, a customer such as Barada Wholesale, or an activity such as machine setups.
Managers choose cost objects to answer questions. What does job T-0303 cost? Is the halva line profitable? How much does it cost to serve Barada? Each question needs a different cost object.
The same cost can be treated differently for different cost objects. That is why the next section always asks: direct or indirect for which cost object?

1.2  Classifying costs
A direct cost can be traced to a cost object in an economical way. An indirect cost cannot, so it must be allocated. The supervisor of the tahini line is indirect for one job, but direct for the Tahini & Spreads division.
A second classification matters for the statements. Product costs are direct materials, direct labor and manufacturing overhead; they go to inventory first. Period costs are selling and administrative costs; they are expensed when incurred. Figure F201-02 shows both paths.
Figure F201-02. Product costs go to inventory and then COGS; period costs are expensed at once.
Two totals appear often in exam questions. Prime cost is direct materials plus direct labor. Conversion cost is direct labor plus manufacturing overhead: the cost of turning materials into products.

1.3  Cost pools and assigning costs to activities
A cost pool is a group of individual costs that are assigned together. Instead of assigning every electricity bill and every supervisor's salary one by one, Orontes groups indirect costs into pools, such as machine operations, batch setups, quality testing and facility costs.
Assignment then happens in two stages. First, resources such as salaries and power are assigned to the activities that use them. Second, each pool is assigned to jobs and products with its own base. Figure F201-04 shows the two stages.
Figure F201-04. Two-stage assignment: resources to cost pools, pools to cost objects through drivers.
Tahini & Spreads uses one plantwide pool today: all its overhead is applied with machine hours. Chapter 12 shows how its Pulses & Spices line uses several activity pools, and why that can change product costs.

1.4  Cost drivers
A cost driver is a factor that causes a cost to change. Grinding sesame takes machine time, so machine hours drive grinding power and maintenance. A new recipe needs a setup, so the number of setups drives setup costs.
The link must be causal. Sales revenue may move with factory power costs, but selling more at higher prices does not cause more power use. A driver chosen only because it moves with the cost can give wrong product costs when conditions change.
Drivers work at different levels. A volume-based cost driver rises with each unit or hour. Other drivers rise with batches, with the number of products, or not at all, as with facility costs. Figure F201-05 gives Orontes examples.

A question may offer a base that moves closely with the cost but does not cause it, such as sales revenue for factory power. Choose the base that causes the cost.

1.6  Extended Example 1A: Tahini & Spreads, full year 2027
This example follows one full year of costs through the accounts and into the division's income statement. The schedules are exam-core: questions often ask for cost of goods manufactured or cost of goods sold. The entries are for depth.
1. Scenario
On January 1, 2027 Tahini & Spreads held raw materials of $130,000, work in process of $180,000 and finished goods of $420,000. During the year:
Materials: purchases $3,260,000; direct materials used $3,000,000; indirect materials $240,000.
Labor: direct labor $1,800,000; indirect labor $480,000.
Other factory overhead: service department charges $690,000; depreciation $840,000; utilities and other costs $740,000.
Overhead applied: 92,000 machine hours × $30 = $2,760,000.
Year-end balances: work in process $690,000; finished goods $1,380,000.
Sales and period costs: sales $8,700,000; selling expense $610,000; administrative expense $390,000.
2. Plan
Separate product costs from period costs. Record the entries, post them, prepare the schedule of cost of goods manufactured and the income statement, and check that the balances tie.
3. Calculations
Total manufacturing costs = $3,000,000 + $1,800,000 + $2,760,000 = $7,560,000.
Cost of goods manufactured = $180,000 + $7,560,000 − $690,000 = $7,050,000.
Cost of goods sold = $420,000 + $7,050,000 − $1,380,000 = $6,090,000.
Actual overhead = $240,000 + $480,000 + $2,270,000 = $2,990,000, so overhead is underapplied by $230,000.

Chapter summary
Figure F201-12 brings the chapter together.
Figure F201-12. Chapter 1 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A cost object is anything whose cost is measured. Direct and indirect depend on the cost object; product and period costs decide whether a cost waits in inventory or is expensed at once. Indirect costs are grouped in pools and assigned with bases that should cause the cost. Product costs flow from raw materials to work in process, finished goods and cost of goods sold, and the schedule of cost of goods manufactured records that flow.

Case question: the halva line
In the second quarter of 2027, Tahini & Spreads ran its halva line for Barada and other buyers. The line mixes sesame paste and sugar, cooks the mix and pours it into trays. A small team of workers runs the line, and a supervisor manages the team. The division also pays for factory utilities, machine depreciation, a sales team and head-office staff.
Lina, the division accountant, is preparing the quarterly cost report for the halva line. She has the costs of the quarter and the inventory balances at the start and the end of the quarter. Some costs belong to the product and stay in inventory until the halva is sold. Others are period costs and go straight to the income statement.
The division manager wants three things from the report. First, what it cost to make the halva finished in the quarter. Second, what the halva sold in the quarter cost. Third, the best base for assigning factory utilities to halva batches, because the line now runs longer machine cycles for Barada's large trays.
Barada has asked for a price for next quarter's trays, so the manager needs the cost figures quickly. Lina notes that the sales team's salaries and the head-office costs must not reach the product cost. She also checks that the materials and labor on the report are the ones actually used on the halva line, not on the tahini lines next to it.
