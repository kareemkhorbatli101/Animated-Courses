Chapter 2
Cost Behavior and the Relevant Range
سلوك التكلفة والمدى الملائم
The Oils division expects to run its crushers for 38,400 machine hours in 2027. What if a good harvest pushes that higher, or a drought pulls it lower? Some costs will move with the hours and some will not. Managers must know which, before they can budget, price or control.
This chapter explains cost behavior: how a cost responds when activity changes. Figure F202-01 shows the four patterns.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 1 sorted costs into direct and indirect, and product and period. Here we sort them a third way, by behavior. The Oils figures come from the same ledger: the olive-press lease from Book 1, the service department charges and the 24 months of maintenance data.
2.1  Four cost behaviors
A variable cost changes in total in proportion to activity. Power for the crushers costs $5 for every machine hour, so twice the hours means twice the power bill.
A fixed cost stays the same in total when activity changes. Factory supervision costs $420,000 a year whether the crushers run a little or a lot. A mixed cost has both parts: maintenance and utilities cost $216,000 a year plus $12 per machine hour.
A step cost is fixed over a band of activity and then jumps. Above the relevant range, Oils needs a second shift, which adds a supervisor costing $180,000. Figure F202-02 shows all four patterns with these Oils costs.

2.3  The relevant range
The relevant range is the band of activity in which the cost behavior assumptions hold. Oils has data from 2,000 to 4,300 machine hours a month. Inside that range, maintenance and utilities follow $18,000 plus $12 per hour.
Outside the range the formula may fail. Above 4,300 hours a month Oils must run a second shift, so the fixed part jumps. Below the range, the $18,000 intercept is only an extrapolation: nobody has seen the plant at zero hours. Figure F202-04 shows the data, the range and the step.
Figure F202-04. The relevant range on the 24-month maintenance and utilities data.
A change in assumptions changes the cost. If a new contract turns the maintenance charge into a fixed fee, the cost becomes fixed. If volume moves outside the range, a new cost function is needed.

2.4  The time frame: short run and long run
Whether a cost is fixed depends on the time frame. In the short run, such as one budget year, the press lease and supervision salaries cannot easily change. In the long run, Orontes can end the lease, change the staff or buy new machines, so every cost can change.
Some fixed costs come from long-term decisions, such as leases and depreciation: these are committed fixed costs. Others, such as training or advertising, are set each year by managers: these are discretionary fixed costs. Figure F202-05 classifies four Oils costs by time frame.

FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Charges fixes, التكاليف الثابتة
French 'charges de structure' and Arabic التكاليف الثابتة both mean fixed costs, but in daily speech 'fixed' can suggest 'permanent'. In cost accounting, fixed means only 'does not change with activity in this range and period'.

2.5  Separating costs into fixed and variable parts
Budgets need a cost function: total cost = fixed cost + variable rate × activity. To build it, each cost must be separated into its fixed and variable parts. The simplest method is account analysis: an accountant reviews each account and classifies it by judgment.
Mixed accounts need a second step. For maintenance and utilities, Oils uses the fixed monthly contract and the rate per machine hour estimated from its records. Chapter 3 shows two ways to estimate them: the high-low method and regression.

2.6  Extended Example 2A: the Oils overhead budget, 2027
This example builds a flexible overhead budget from the cost function, tests it at several volumes and records one month's invoices by behavior. The budget calculations are exam-core; the entries are for depth.
1. Scenario
Oils plans 38,400 machine hours for 2027, but the harvest could give anything from 30,000 to 48,000. Above 51,600 hours a year, a second shift adds $72,000 of maintenance and $180,000 of supervision.
2. Plan
Use the cost function from Section 2.5. Compute total overhead at each volume, check which volumes are inside the relevant range, and see what happens to cost per machine hour.
3. Calculations
Budget volume: $2,100,569 + $20 × 38,400 = $2,868,569, or $74.70 per machine hour.
Low volume: $2,100,569 + $20 × 30,000 = $2,700,569, or $90.02 per hour.
High volume: $2,100,569 + $20 × 48,000 = $3,060,569, or $63.76 per hour.
Above the range (55,000 hours): $2,100,569 + $252,000 + $20 × 55,000 = $3,452,569.

Chapter summary
Figure F202-12 brings the chapter together.
Figure F202-12. Chapter 2 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Variable costs change in total with activity and are constant per unit. Fixed costs are constant in total and fall per unit as activity rises. Mixed costs have both parts, and step costs jump at set levels. These rules hold only inside the relevant range and for a stated time frame; in the long run all costs can change. Account analysis separates each cost into its fixed and variable parts to give a cost function for budgets.
