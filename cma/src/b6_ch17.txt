Chapter 17
Inventory: lead time, safety stock, EOQ and JIT
المخزون: فترة التوريد والمخزون الاحتياطي وكمية الطلب الاقتصادية والإنتاج في الوقت المحدد
Orontes holds $2,450,000 of inventory and turns it 11.49 times a year, which is 31.8 days of cover. Barada holds a third of its assets as stock and turns it 9.00 times. Neither figure is right or wrong on its own: a company that holds too little loses the sale it cannot supply, and one that holds too much funds, stores, insures and eventually writes down goods nobody wanted.
This chapter sets out why inventory exists, what it costs in both directions, and the two pieces of arithmetic the exam asks for: the reorder point, which says when to order, and the economic order quantity, which says how much. It closes on just-in-time, which is the attempt to make both questions smaller.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Book 1 computed Orontes's inventory turnover of 11.49 times and its days sales in inventory of 31.8, and found inventory growing 6.52 per cent against sales growth of 15.38 — the healthy direction. It also found Barada's stock up 41 per cent on sales up 11, which it called the warning. This chapter explains what decides either number.
17.1  Why inventory is held
Four reasons, and the exam asks for them by name.
To meet demand that is not perfectly predictable. A customer who wants something today will buy it from somebody who has it. The stock held for this reason is the buffer, and its size depends on how variable the demand is and on how expensive a lost sale is.
To decouple the stages of a process. A mill that stops when the roaster stops is a mill that runs at the roaster's worst day. Work in progress between the stages lets each run at its own rhythm. This is the reason just-in-time attacks most directly, by removing the need rather than the stock.
To obtain quantity discounts and reduce ordering effort. Buying in larger lots costs less per unit in both price and administration, and the saving is paid for in storage and funding — which is exactly the trade-off the economic order quantity resolves.
To guard against price rises or supply interruptions. A company whose input is seasonal, imported, or subject to a volatile price may hold more than it needs now because it expects to need it later at a worse price. Orontes importing sesame is this case, and Chapter 6 offered it a cheaper alternative in the forward market.
What sets the level, beyond the reasons, is a short list: the predictability of demand, the length and reliability of the lead time, the cost of a stockout, the cost of money, the shelf life of the goods, and the terms available from suppliers.

17.2  The three costs
Everything in this chapter is a trade between three costs, and the outcome requires each to be identified and computed.
Carrying costs rise with the quantity held. They are the cost of funding the money in the stock, which is the largest item; storage, handling and insurance; shrinkage from theft and damage; and obsolescence. They are usually expressed as an amount per unit per year, or as a percentage of the unit cost — twenty to twenty-five per cent a year is a common figure once funding, storage and obsolescence are added.
Ordering costs fall with the quantity held, because a larger order means fewer orders. They are the administrative cost of raising and receiving an order, the inspection, the transport where it does not vary with quantity, and for a manufacturer the cost of setting up a production run.
Stockout costs arise when the stock runs out: the lost contribution on the sale, the expedited freight to get goods urgently, the idle production line, and the customer who goes elsewhere permanently. They are the hardest to measure and usually the largest, which is why they are estimated rather than computed and why companies hold more stock than any formula suggests.

EXAM TRAP   Which cost is which
Funding the stock is a carrying cost, not an ordering cost, however the invoice is paid.
A set-up cost in a factory behaves exactly as an ordering cost does, and the economic order quantity formula is used for production batches with the set-up cost in place of the ordering cost.
Quantity discounts are not in the basic formula. Where a question offers one, compute the total cost at the economic order quantity and at each discount break and compare them.

17.3  Lead time, safety stock and the reorder point
Lead time is the interval between placing an order and receiving the goods. It is why a company cannot wait until the shelf is empty: an order placed on the day of the stockout arrives a week after it.
The reorder point is the stock level at which the next order is placed. In the simplest case it is the quantity that will be used during the lead time: daily usage times lead time in days.
Safety stock is the buffer held against the two things that go wrong — demand higher than expected during the lead time, and a lead time longer than expected. Adding it to the reorder point gives the full rule.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: when to order
Step 1. The usage. A company uses 10,000 units a year over 250 working days, which is 10,000 ÷ 250 = 40 units a day.
Step 2. The lead time. Five working days, so the usage during the lead time is 40 × 5 = 200 units.
Step 3. The safety stock. Demand is variable and the supplier is occasionally late, so the company holds 100 units of safety stock — about two and a half days of usage.
Step 4. The reorder point. 200 + 100 = 300 units: when the stock falls to 300, the next order goes out.
Check. The safety stock is held all the time and used occasionally, so it is a permanent addition to the average stock and its whole cost is a carrying cost: at $4 a unit a year, 100 × 4 = 400 a year. That is the price of the protection, and it should be compared with the cost of the stockouts it prevents. Where a question gives the probability of a stockout at each level of safety stock and the cost of one, the answer is the level at which the expected stockout cost plus the carrying cost is lowest.

17.4  How much to order
The economic order quantity is the order size at which the total of carrying and ordering costs is lowest. It comes from a formula the exam does not require to be derived: twice the annual demand times the cost of placing one order, divided by the carrying cost of one unit for a year, all under a square root.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the economic order quantity
Step 1. The inputs. Annual demand is 10,000 units, each order costs $50 to place, and carrying one unit for a year costs $4.
Step 2. The numerator. Twice the demand times the ordering cost: 2 × 10,000 × 50 = 1,000,000.
Step 3. Divide by the carrying cost. 1,000,000 ÷ 4 = 250,000, and the square root of 250,000 is 500 units.
Step 4. What follows. At 500 units an order, the company places 10,000 ÷ 500 = 20 orders a year, costing 20 × 50 = 1,000. Its average stock is half the order quantity, 500 ÷ 2 = 250 units, costing 250 × 4 = 1,000 to carry. Total cost is 1,000 + 1,000 = 2,000 a year.
Check. The two costs are equal at the optimum, and that is not a coincidence: it is a property of the formula and a quick way to verify an answer. It is also why the total cost curve is flat near the bottom — ordering 450 or 550 units instead of 500 costs only a few dollars more a year — which matters in practice, because it means the formula's precision is not worth agonising over while its direction is worth following.
The outcome asks how a change in one variable affects the answer, and the square root supplies the rule: every input moves the order quantity by its own square root.
Double the demand and the order quantity rises by about 41 per cent, not 100, because the square root of two is about 1.41. This is why a company twice the size does not hold twice the stock, and it is the single most examinable consequence of the formula.
Double the ordering cost and the quantity rises by the same 41 per cent: with an ordering cost of $100, the numerator becomes 2 × 10,000 × 100 = 2,000,000, and dividing by 4 and taking the root gives about 707 units.
Double the carrying cost and the quantity falls by about 29 per cent, to about 354 units, because dividing by two inside a square root multiplies by about 0.71.

17.5  Just-in-time, and the turnover a margin allows
Just-in-time is the proposition that stock held to protect a process is a symptom, and that the process should be fixed instead. Materials arrive as they are needed, in small quantities, from suppliers close by and contractually committed; production is pulled by demand rather than pushed by a schedule; and the buffer between stages is removed so that any problem stops the line and has to be solved rather than absorbed.
What it achieves is examinable. Inventory falls, often by most of it, releasing the funding and the space. Quality improves, because a defect cannot hide in a pile of stock. Obsolescence and write-downs fall. And the discipline exposes problems — an unreliable machine, a supplier who is sometimes late — that stock was concealing.
What it requires is equally examinable, and it is the reason just-in-time is not universal. Reliable suppliers, geographically close, and few of them. Reliable transport. Reliable machines. Short set-up times, so that small batches are economic, which is the same as saying that the ordering cost in section 17.4 has been engineered down. Accurate demand information. And a willingness to stop the line, which is a cultural matter more than a technical one.
The risks are the mirror: a single supplier's failure, a transport disruption, a natural disaster or a pandemic stops a line that has no buffer, and the saving of years can be lost in a month. The exam expects both halves.
The last outcome of this chapter is a relationship rather than a calculation, and it is the one Book 1 prepared. Inventory turnover and gross margin tend to move in opposite directions across businesses. A supermarket turns its stock thirty times a year on a gross margin near 25 per cent; a jeweller turns it twice on a margin near 50. Each is viable because the product of the two is enough: turnover times margin is a crude measure of the gross profit earned on each dollar invested in stock, and businesses survive at many combinations of the pair.
The practical consequences are two. A company comparing its turnover with another's must compare margins at the same time, or it is comparing two different business models — which is exactly what Book 1 warned about when it set Barada's 14 per cent gross margin against Orontes's 35. And a company that wants to raise its turnover by cutting stock must ask what that does to its margin: if the stock cut causes stockouts, discounting to recover the customers, or expedited freight, the turnover will rise and the margin will fall, and the company will be no better off.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Stock"
English stock means two entirely different things in these two books, and the two meanings sit in adjacent chapters. Inventory is called stock in British usage, so stockout, stock turn and safety stock all concern goods. Shares are called stock in American usage, so common stock, preferred stock and stock dividend all concern equity. The exam, written in American English, uses stock for shares and inventory for goods, and a candidate who learned the British sense must read carefully: a stock dividend has nothing to do with the warehouse. Arabic avoids the clash completely with مخزون for goods and أسهم for shares, and French does the same with stocks and actions. Note the one survival of the goods sense in American usage: a company is said to be out of stock, never out of inventory.

Chapter summary
Figure F617-07 brings the chapter together.
Figure F617-07. Chapter 17 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Stock is held to meet uncertain demand, to decouple the stages of a process, to buy in economic lots and to guard against price or supply shocks, and its level is set by the predictability of demand, the lead time, the cost of a stockout, the cost of money and the shelf life. Three costs trade against each other: carrying costs rise with quantity, ordering costs fall with it, and stockout costs are the largest and least measurable. Lead time fixes when to order — 10,000 ÷ 250 = 40 units a day, 40 × 5 = 200 during a five-day lead time, plus 100 of safety stock, so 200 + 100 = 300 — and the safety stock's whole cost, 100 × 4 = 400 a year, is the price of the protection. The economic order quantity fixes how much: 2 × 10,000 × 50 = 1,000,000, divided by 4 gives 250,000, whose square root is 500 units, at which 20 orders cost 1,000 and 250 units of average stock cost 1,000, the two always equal at the optimum. Every input moves the answer by its square root, so doubling demand raises the order by 41 per cent and not 100. And just-in-time removes the need for the buffer rather than the buffer itself, at the price of requiring everything else to be reliable — while turnover and margin move in opposite directions across businesses, which is why a supermarket at thirty times and a jeweller at two are both viable and neither can be judged by the other's figure.

Case-style set: a bulk offer to Orontes
What the discount is worth. Three per cent of $9,000,000 is 9,000,000 × 0.03 = 270,000 a year, so the purchasing manager's figure is right as far as it goes.
What the extra stock would cost. Buying six months at a time means an average stock of about three months' usage, which is 9,000,000 ÷ 4 = 2,250,000, against roughly 9,000,000 ÷ 8 = 1,125,000 under quarterly ordering. The extra stock is therefore about $1,125,000, and at a carrying cost of 22 per cent a year that is 1,125,000 × 0.22 = 247,500. Against the discount of $270,000, the net gain is about $22,500 a year — a tenth of what the manager claimed, and inside the margin of error of the 22 per cent estimate.
What else I would want to know. First, the shelf life and the storage: sesame held for six months must stay in condition, and if additional or refrigerated space is needed, the carrying rate is higher than 22 per cent. Second, the price risk: committing to six months at today's price is a position in the sesame market, which Chapter 6 showed can be taken more cheaply and more precisely with a forward contract than by buying the physical. Third, the cash: a shipload is a single large payment, and Chapter 13's forecast must show the company can make it without drawing a facility that costs more than the discount. Fourth, whether the supplier would offer the same discount for a commitment to volume rather than a single delivery, which would capture most of the $270,000 with none of the stock.
On these figures I would not take the offer as proposed, and would ask for the fourth option.
