WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
A mixed cost has a fixed part and a variable rate (Chapter 2). Account analysis splits it by judgment. The methods here use past data instead, and regression adds measures of how reliable the result is.
3.1  Why estimate cost functions
Budgets, prices and flexible budgets all need a cost function: total cost = fixed cost + variable rate × activity. Managers have four main ways to estimate it. Figure F203-02 compares them.

3.2  The high-low method
The high-low method draws a line through two points: the months with the highest and the lowest activity. The variable rate is the change in cost divided by the change in activity. The fixed cost is what is left at either point.
Choose the points by activity, not by cost. In the Oils data the highest cost, $69,400, came in a month with 4,200 machine hours, not in the busiest month. Figure F203-03 marks both.
Figure F203-03. The high-low line through the highest and lowest activity months.

3.3  Simple regression
Regression analysis fits the line that best explains cost using all the data points. In simple regression there is one independent variable x, the activity, and one dependent variable y, the cost. The equation is y = a + b x.
The intercept a is the estimated fixed cost for the period. The slope b is the variable cost for each extra unit of activity. Figure F203-05 shows both on a graph.
Figure F203-05. Anatomy of a simple regression line: intercept a and slope b.
Software prints more than a and b. The coefficient of determination R² shows how much of the cost variation the activity explains. Each coefficient has a t-statistic: above about 2 means it is reliably different from zero. The standard error of the estimate shows how far actual costs typically lie from the line. 3.4  Using the regression equation
To forecast, put the planned activity into x. At 3,200 machine hours, Oils maintenance and utilities should cost $18,000 + $12 × 3,200 = $56,400.
A forecast is only as good as the range of the data. The Oils data run from 2,000 to 4,300 machine hours a month. A forecast inside that range is reasonable; a forecast far outside it is a guess.

3.5  Using regression to estimate fixed costs
The intercept is regression's estimate of fixed cost: $18,000 a month for Oils maintenance and utilities, or $216,000 a year. Chapter 2 used exactly this figure in the Oils overhead budget.
Use the intercept with care. It is the cost the line predicts at zero activity, and zero lies far outside the data. It is a sound estimate of fixed cost inside the relevant range, not a promise of what the plant would cost if it stopped.
The t-statistic of the intercept helps. For Oils it is 18.93, well above 2, so the fixed part is clearly greater than zero. A small t-statistic would mean the fixed estimate is weak.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Constante, الحد الثابت
French texts call the intercept 'la constante' or 'ordonnée à l'origine', and Arabic texts الحد الثابت. The word 'constant' suggests a fact, but the intercept is only an estimate from the data.

3.6  Multiple regression
Multiple regression uses two or more independent variables: y = a + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂. Each coefficient is the cost per unit of its own variable, with the other variables held constant.
Suppose Oils also records the number of setups each month. A multiple regression might give $15,000 a month, plus $11.5 per machine hour, plus $150 per setup. Figure F203-08 reads each term. Book 4 shows how such equations are estimated and tested.

3.7  Extended Example 3A: the Oils maintenance and utilities cost function
This example runs both methods on the full 24 months of Oils data and turns the result into the 2027 budget. There are no journal entries here: the output is a forecast that feeds the budget.
1. Scenario
Oils has monthly maintenance and utilities costs for January 2025 to December 2026, with machine hours from 2,000 to 4,300. For 2027 it plans 3,200 machine hours a month, or 38,400 for the year.
2. Plan
Estimate the cost function with high-low and with regression. Read the regression output. Forecast the month and the year, compare the two methods, and pass the result to the budget.
3. Calculations: regression by hand
Software does this work, but seeing it once shows what b and a mean. Figure F203-09 builds the slope from the sums: b = Sxy ÷ Sxx = $12, and a = ȳ − b × x̄ = $18,000. R² = 1 − SSE ÷ SST = 0.987.

Chapter summary
Figure F203-12 brings the chapter together.
Figure F203-12. Chapter 3 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A cost function has a fixed part and a variable rate. The high-low method draws a line through the highest and lowest activity months; it is quick but uses only two points. Simple regression fits the best line through all points and reports R², t-statistics and the standard error. To forecast, put the activity in x and stay inside the relevant range. The intercept estimates fixed cost within that range. Multiple regression adds more drivers, each with its own rate.
