WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapters 12 and 13 traced overhead to activities, products and customers. This chapter widens the view: across the years of a product's life, and across all the activities of a business.
14.1  Life-cycle costing
Life-cycle costing plans and tracks every cost of a product from research to withdrawal. It groups costs into three stages: upstream costs before production, manufacturing costs, and downstream costs after production.
Upstream costs include research, design and testing. Downstream costs include marketing, distribution, customer service and the costs of leaving the market. Figure F214-02 shows the three stages for the sugar-free halva.
Figure F214-02. The three stages of the sugar-free halva's life-cycle cost.

14.2  Locked-in costs and the strategic value of the design stage
A cost is incurred when the money is spent. It is committed, or locked in, when a decision makes it unavoidable later. The recipe, the tray and the choice of channels fix most future costs long before they are paid.
At the end of design, the halva team estimates that 80% of the life-cycle cost is already committed, while only 7.8% has been spent. Figure F214-05 shows the two lines over the product's life.
Figure F214-05. Costs committed and costs incurred over the halva's life.
This is the strategic value of life-cycle costing: the cheapest time to reduce cost is the design stage. Once production starts, managers can only control the small share of cost that is still open.
EXAM TRAP   Committed is not incurred
An exam statement that most costs are incurred at the design stage is false. Most costs are committed at design and incurred later, during production and sales.

14.3  The value chain
The value chain is the chain of activities a company performs to design, make, sell, deliver and support its products. Value chain analysis studies each activity to find where the company can lower cost or add value for customers.
Michael Porter divided the chain into two groups. Primary activities make and deliver the product: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. Support activities help all of them: firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development and procurement. Figure F214-06 shows the chain for the Oils division.
Figure F214-06. Porter's value chain for the Oils division.
The chain does not stop at the company's walls. The olive growers before Orontes and the distributors after it are part of a wider value system, and many savings come from the links between them.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Chaîne de valeur, سلسلة القيمة
French 'chaîne de valeur' and Arabic سلسلة القيمة mean Porter's chain of activities, not a supply chain of companies. The supply chain links several companies; the value chain is inside one.

14.4  The steps in value chain analysis
Value chain analysis has five steps: identify the activities, assign costs and assets to them, find what drives each cost, study the links inside and outside the company, and look for opportunities. Figure F214-07 applies the steps to the Oils division.
Figure F214-07. The five steps of value chain analysis in the Oils division
Step
Oils division example
1. Identify the activities
Receive olives, press, bottle, ship, sell, handle complaints
2. Assign costs and assets
Cost per liter of each activity (Figure F214-08)
3. Find the cost drivers
Olive quality, batch size, bottle type, delivery distance
4. Study the links
With olive growers, glass supplier, distributors and customers
5. Find opportunities
Lower cost where customers do not see value; add value where they will pay

14.5  Value chains and competitive advantage
A company gains an advantage in one of two ways. Cost leadership means doing the activities more cheaply than rivals. Differentiation means doing some activities in a way customers value and will pay more for.
The bulk competitor follows cost leadership: its cost per liter is $11.00. Orontes follows differentiation: cold-pressed, traced oil costs more, but customers pay $4.50 more per liter. Figure F214-09 compares the two strategies.

14.6  Value-added and non-value-added activities
A value-added activity is one customers would pay for, such as filling and labeling bottles. A non-value-added activity adds cost but no value, such as moving pallets or reworking errors. Some activities, such as final inspection, add no value but are necessary for now.

Chapter summary
Figure F214-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F214-14. Chapter 14 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Life-cycle costing adds upstream, manufacturing and downstream costs so that a product is judged on everything it costs. Most costs are committed at the design stage, long before they are incurred. Value chain analysis studies Porter's primary and support activities in five steps, and shows whether a company competes on low cost or on valued differences. Removing non-value-added activities lowers cost without reducing value to customers.
