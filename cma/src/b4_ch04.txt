Chapter 2 said the audit committee appoints the external auditor. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act is where that requirement comes from, along with the certifications the CEO and CFO sign.
4.1  The Sarbanes-Oxley sections that matter
The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 followed a run of accounting failures. Five of its sections concern internal control directly, and Figure F404-02 sets them out.
Figure F404-02. The Sarbanes-Oxley sections that concern internal control
Section
What it requires

404(a)
Annual management report assessing the effectiveness of internal control over financial reporting

Section 302 and Section 906 both require the CEO and CFO to certify, and candidates often ask why there are two. Section 302 is the civil certification about the content of the report and the controls behind it. Section 906 is the criminal one, carrying up to twenty years for a willful violation.
Section 404 has two halves that are often confused. Part (a) requires management to assess internal control over financial reporting each year. Part (b) requires the external auditor to attest to it, and that half does not apply to every company.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'Auditors must keep workpapers for seven years'
The statute, Section 802 says five years. The SEC's own rule and the PCAOB's standard then require seven. Both numbers are correct in their place, and a question may test either. Say five if asked about the Act, and seven if asked about the SEC or PCAOB requirement.

4.2  Who must obtain an auditor attestation
Section 404(b) binds accelerated filers and large accelerated filers. Non-accelerated filers were permanently exempted by the Dodd-Frank Act in 2010. Figure F404-03 shows the division, and records a proposal that has not been adopted.
Figure F404-03. Who must obtain an auditor attestation on internal control.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'Smaller reporting companies are exempt from 404(b)'
Only if they are also non-accelerated. Since the SEC's 2020 amendments the two labels overlap but are not the same. A smaller reporting company with a float of $75 million or more, and revenue of $100 million or more, is still an accelerated filer and still needs the attestation.
WATCH: NOT YET TESTABLE   As of October 2026
In May 2026 the SEC proposed raising the large accelerated threshold to $2 billion. It would also remove the accelerated filer and smaller reporting company categories, leaving about 81% of companies exempt from the Section 404 attestation. It had not been adopted when this book went to review. Learn the current rules, and expect the exam to test the principle rather than the threshold.

4.3  What the PCAOB does
The Public Company Accounting Oversight Board was created by the Act itself. Before 2002 the audit profession set its own standards and inspected itself; the PCAOB replaced that arrangement. Figure F404-04 sets out its four roles.
Figure F404-04. What the PCAOB does
PCAOB role (E.1.q)
What it does
Why it matters
Registration
Audit firms of issuers must register with the PCAOB
No registration, no audit of a public company
Standard setting
Issues auditing standards, subject to SEC approval
AS 2201 governs the audit of internal control
Inspection
Inspects registered firms' audit work
Findings drive firm-wide remediation
Enforcement
Investigates and disciplines firms and individuals
Sanctions up to barring an auditor
For this chapter the second role is the one that matters. The PCAOB issues the auditing standards, subject to approval by the SEC, and the standard governing an audit of internal control over financial reporting is AS 2201.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'Auditing Standard No. 5'
Many study guides still call it AS No. 5. The PCAOB renumbered its standards in 2016 and it is now AS 2201. The content did not change, but the old name signals a book that has not been updated.
A second point is worth knowing because candidates mix it up. The SEC gives guidance to management on its own 404(a) assessment; the PCAOB gives guidance to auditors on the 404(b) attestation. Different bodies, different audiences, same subject.

4.4  Top-down against bottom-up
AS 2201 requires the auditor to use a top-down approach to select the controls to test. This is the PCAOB's required and preferred approach, and the exam asks for it by name.
Figure F404-05. The five steps of the top-down, risk-based approach.
Figure F404-05 shows the five steps. The auditor starts at the financial statements and the risk of material misstatement. From there the path runs to entity-level controls, then to significant accounts and their assertions, then to the likely sources of misstatement. Only then are the controls selected.
A bottom-up approach does the opposite. It starts with a list of controls and tests them, hoping that the coverage turns out to be adequate. Figure F404-06 compares the two directly.
Figure F404-06. Top-down against bottom-up
Top-down, risk-based
Bottom-up
Starting point
The financial statements and the risk of material misstatement
Individual process-level controls
Direction
From risk down to the controls that address it
From the controls up to whatever they happen to cover
Scope
Only controls that address an identified risk
Potentially every control in the company
Entity-level controls
Tested first, and may reduce testing below
Often tested last or not at all
Cost and evidence
Less testing, better targeted
More testing, much of it irrelevant
PCAOB position
Required by AS 2201.21
Not the standard
The difference is not merely efficiency, though a top-down audit does cost less. It is about evidence. A bottom-up audit can test a hundred controls and still miss the one that matters, because nothing in the method connects the testing to the risk.

4.5  The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act
The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977 has two sets of provisions, and only one of them is about bribery. Figure F404-07 sets them out.
Figure F404-07. The two sets of FCPA provisions
FCPA provision (E.1.t)
What it says
Who it binds
Anti-bribery
Prohibits corruptly offering anything of value to a foreign official to obtain or keep business
Issuers, domestic concerns, and others acting in U.S. territory
Books and records
Keep books in reasonable detail that accurately and fairly reflect transactions and dispositions of assets
Issuers only
Internal accounting controls
Maintain a system giving reasonable assurance over authorization, recording, access and periodic comparison
Issuers only
Exception
Facilitating payments for routine governmental action, such as processing a visa
Narrowly read
Defenses
The payment was lawful under written local law; or a reasonable bona fide expenditure
Two affirmative defenses
The accounting provisions are the ones this exam cares about. They require an issuer to keep books in reasonable detail that accurately and fairly reflect its transactions. They also require a system of internal accounting controls giving reasonable assurance over four things: that transactions are authorized; that they are recorded so that statements can be prepared; that access to assets is authorized; and that recorded assets are compared with actual assets at reasonable intervals.
Two points follow that are easy to miss. The accounting provisions bind issuers only, while the anti-bribery provisions reach much wider. And a company can breach the accounting provisions with no bribe anywhere in the picture, because poor records and weak controls are themselves the offence.
EXAM TRAP   The FCPA does not require COSO
The Act requires a system of internal accounting controls giving reasonable assurance over the four matters listed above. It names no framework. Most companies use COSO because it is the recognised one, not because the FCPA says so.
WATCH: NOT YET TESTABLE   As of October 2026
The statute has not changed. Enforcement has. It was paused in February 2025 and restarted in June 2025 under Department of Justice guidelines setting four priorities, with a further policy in March 2026. Separately, the Foreign Extortion Prevention Act of December 2023 made it a crime for a foreign official to demand a bribe. Expect the exam to test the statute rather than the enforcement posture.

4.6  Extended Example 4A: planning an audit of the Orontes controls
This example walks the top-down approach through one Orontes audit and shows what a bottom-up auditor would have done instead. It is exam-core, and E.1.r is one of only six Level C outcomes in this book, so expect to be asked to judge rather than recall.
1. Step one: the financial statements
The auditor starts by asking where a material misstatement could appear in the Orontes statements. Inventory is large and includes spices that are valuable and portable. Revenue includes Gulf export sales with unusual terms. Those two attract attention; the small items do not.
2. Step two: entity-level controls
Next the auditor tests the controls that run across everything: the control environment described in Chapter 1, the period-end reporting process, and the controls over management override. Strong entity-level controls can reduce the testing needed below, which is why they come second and not last.
3. Step three: significant accounts and assertions
Inventory is a significant account, but not every assertion about it matters equally. Existence is the assertion at risk for portable spices; valuation matters for slow-moving stock. The auditor names the assertion, not just the account.
4. Step four: likely sources of misstatement
How could inventory existence actually go wrong at Orontes? Stock could be removed from the spice store and the record adjusted, which is the pairing Chapter 3 examined. That is a specific, testable route, not a general worry.
5. Step five: the controls to test
Only now does the auditor choose: the separation of the storekeeper from the stock records, the independent count, and the reconciliation of depot records to plant dispatches. Three controls, each tied to an identified risk.
6. What a bottom-up auditor would have done
A bottom-up auditor would have asked for the list of controls and started testing. Many hours would go on purchase order approvals and payroll authorisations, which are real controls over immaterial risks. The spice store might be reached eventually, and might not. Nothing in the method would reveal the gap.

9. Tie line
WORKED EXAMPLE   The pattern behind the approach
Start at the statements, because that is where materiality is decided.
Test entity-level controls early, because they can reduce work below.
Name the assertion at risk, not just the account.
Select controls last, and only those addressing an identified risk.
10. How the exam asks it
EXAM TRAP   Exam view
A question may describe an audit plan and ask whether it is top-down. Look at where it starts. If the first step is a list of controls, it is bottom-up, whatever it is called. If the first step is the risk of material misstatement, it is top-down.

Chapter summary
Figure F404-10 brings the chapter together.
Figure F404-10. Chapter 4 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Sarbanes-Oxley requires the CEO and CFO to certify reports under section 302 and, criminally, under section 906; management to assess internal control annually under 404(a); and the auditor to attest under 404(b) for accelerated and large accelerated filers only. Section 802 governs record retention. The PCAOB registers, inspects, disciplines and sets standards for auditors of issuers, and its AS 2201 requires a top-down, risk-based approach that starts at the financial statements and ends at the controls to test. The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act prohibits bribery of foreign officials and, for issuers, requires accurate books and a system of internal accounting controls.
