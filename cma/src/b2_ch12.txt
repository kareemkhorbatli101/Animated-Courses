Activity-Based Costing

The Pulses & Spices line of Tahini & Spreads packs two products: lentils in large, simple runs, and za'atar in many small, carefully tested batches. For years both carried overhead at one rate per labor hour, and the report said lentils lost money. This chapter shows why that report was wrong.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 1 defined cost pools and cost drivers. Chapter 8 compared plantwide and departmental overhead rates. Chapter 10 allocated service costs in two stages. ABC uses the same ideas with more pools and better drivers.
12.1  Why one overhead rate can mislead
A traditional system collects overhead in one pool and spreads it with one volume measure, such as direct labor hours. That works when every product uses overhead in the same way as it uses labor.
Pulses & Spices has $900,000 of overhead. At $36 a labor hour, lentils receive $720,000 because they use most of the labor. Za'atar receives $180,000, although its many small batches need most of the setups, tests and receipts. Figure F212-02 compares the two views.
Figure F212-02. Traditional one-rate allocation compared with five ABC activity rates.
The result is cost distortion. High-volume, simple products are overcosted, and low-volume, complex products are undercosted. Prices and product decisions based on these costs go wrong.
EXAM TRAP   Volume drivers for batch costs
A setup costs the same whether the batch is large or small. If an exam question spreads setup costs by units or labor hours, the answer will overcost the large batches.

12.2  The elements of an ABC system
An activity is a task that uses resources, such as a setup or a quality test. Each activity has an activity cost pool: the total cost of doing it. Activity-based costing assigns overhead to these pools first and then to products.
The first stage uses resource drivers, such as the share of staff time spent on each activity. The second stage uses activity drivers, such as the number of setups each product needs. Both are cost drivers: they measure what causes the cost. Figure F212-03 shows the two stages.

12.3  The cost hierarchy
ABC groups activities by what causes them. Unit-level costs rise with every unit, like packing hours. Batch-level costs rise with each batch, like setups, whatever the batch size.
Product-sustaining costs support one product line, such as its recipe design. Facility-sustaining costs keep the whole site running, such as rent. Figure F212-05 places the five Pulses & Spices pools in the hierarchy.

12.4  Computing product cost with ABC
For each pool, the activity rate is the pool cost divided by the total driver quantity. A product's overhead is the rate times the driver quantity it uses, added over all pools.

12.5  ABC as a cost accumulation system
ABC does not need new ledger accounts. Materials and labor go to work in process as before; overhead is applied at the activity rates instead of one rate. Completed units move to finished goods and then to cost of goods sold, as in job costing.
Because unit costs change, inventory values change too. At the end of 2027 the unsold packs are worth $142,550 with ABC and $133,500 with the old rate. Figure F212-10 shows the difference.

Spoilage follows the Chapter 7 rules. Normal spoilage is built into the rates of the activities that cause it, such as quality testing. Abnormal spoilage is removed from product cost and expensed as a loss.
For example, if a za'atar batch fails its test for a normal reason and is thrown away, its cost stays in the blending and testing pools and reaches the good packs through the rates. A batch ruined by a power cut is abnormal and is written off as a loss.
ABC suits companies with many products that use activities in different ways. With one simple product, one rate gives almost the same answer at a lower cost.

12.6  Benefits, limitations and strategic value
Activity-based allocation gives more accurate product costs where products differ in complexity. It shows batch-level and product-sustaining costs that a volume rate hides. It also links cost to activities that managers can change.
ABC has costs too. It needs data on many drivers, judgment to design the pools, and regular updates. Facility costs are still allocated without a true driver. Figure F212-11 lists the benefits and limitations.
Figure F212-11. Benefits and limitations of ABC

The strategic value is in the decisions. With ABC costs, the division may raise the za'atar price, make larger za'atar batches to cut setups, or stop pushing lentils away. ABC informs these choices; it does not make them.
WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   Beyond the exam
Time-driven ABC estimates the time each activity takes instead of surveying staff. Activity-based management uses ABC to improve activities. Both are useful in practice, but they are not in the 2024 Part 1 outcomes.

Chapter summary
Figure F212-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F212-14. Chapter 12 summary map: the key rule for each section.
One volume rate overcosts simple, high-volume products and undercosts complex ones. ABC assigns resources to activity pools with resource drivers and pools to products with activity drivers. Costs sit at unit, batch, product-sustaining and facility levels. Each rate is the pool cost divided by the driver quantity. ABC uses the normal accounts, changes inventory values and gives better information for pricing and product decisions, at the cost of more data and judgment.

The Beverages division makes two juices on the same bottling line. Standard juice runs in long batches for supermarkets. Premium juice is a new small-batch product for hotels, with its own recipe and extra quality tests on every batch.
The division applies overhead to both juices at one rate per machine hour. The premium juice uses few machine hours, so it receives little overhead, and the sales team has priced it on that cost.
The controller, Sami, suspects that the premium juice is undercosted. He has collected four overhead pools: machine operations, setups, quality tests and recipe design. The recipe design cost can be traced to each product line. He has also recorded how much of each driver each juice used in 2027.
Sami must prepare an ABC analysis for the division manager before the new hotel price list is printed. He needs the activity rates, the overhead each juice receives under ABC and the overhead per case under both methods.
