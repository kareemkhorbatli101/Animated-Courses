15.2  The challenge of fitting a model
A model is a simplification of reality chosen to be useful. The central difficulty is that the data contains both the pattern you want and noise you do not, and no model can separate them perfectly. Figure F415-03 shows what goes wrong at each extreme.
Figure F415-03. Underfitting, a good fit, and overfitting.
underfitting means the model is too simple to capture the pattern: a flat line through a rising relationship explains almost nothing. overfitting is the subtler and more dangerous failure. A complicated model can be made to pass through every observed point. It then fits the past perfectly and predicts the future worse than a straight line would.
The reason is that it has learned the noise as well as the signal, and the noise will not repeat. The defence is to test a model against data it has never seen, which Chapter 16 returns to, and to prefer the simplest model that does the job.

15.3  Clustering and classification
Both techniques put cases into groups, and the exam asks which is appropriate when. Figure F415-04 answers it.
Figure F415-04. Clustering against classification
Technique (F.4.n)
What it assumes
What it does
At Orontes
Clustering
Unsupervised: no labels exist
Finds natural groups by similarity
Which distributors behave alike, when nobody has said
Classification
Supervised: labels exist
Assigns a case to a known category
Is this order likely to be returned, given past returns
How to choose
Ask whether the categories are already known
If they are, classify; if not, cluster
Return or not is known; distributor types are not
The test is a single question: are the categories already known? classification needs labelled history. It learns from cases whose answer is known, and then assigns new ones. clustering needs no labels at all, because it looks for natural groupings and leaves the analyst to decide what, if anything, they mean.
At Orontes, whether an order was returned is recorded, so predicting returns is classification. Which distributors behave alike has never been recorded by anyone, so finding that out is clustering.

15.4  Multiple and logistic regression
multiple regression estimates how a quantity depends on several variables at once. logistic regression does the same job where the thing being predicted is a yes or a no. Figure F415-05 distinguishes them.
Figure F415-05. Multiple against logistic regression
Technique (F.4.o)
Predicts
The output is
The question it answers
Multiple regression
A quantity
Cases, revenue, cost, hours
How many cases will we sell?
Logistic regression
A yes or no
Probability between 0 and 1
Will this customer pay late?
Choosing
Look at what is being predicted
A number or a category
If the answer is a quantity, use multiple regression
The choice follows entirely from what is being predicted. A quantity, such as cases or cost or hours, calls for multiple regression. A category, such as whether a customer will pay late, calls for logistic regression, whose output is a probability between zero and one rather than a number of cases.
A multiple regression equation takes the form of an intercept plus a coefficient for each variable multiplied by that variable's value. Each coefficient says how much the result changes when that variable rises by one, holding the others constant. That last clause is what makes it more useful than looking at one variable alone.

15.5  Using the equation
Fitting the eight quarters of Beverages data to price, mean temperature and promotion weeks gives the equation below. Only this calculation is examinable in this part of the syllabus, so it is worth being able to do it without hesitation.
WORKED EXAMPLE   The fitted equation
Cases = 339,267 − 8,085 × price + 1,457 × temperature + 2,706 × promotion weeks
Price carries a negative coefficient: raise the price and sell fewer cases.
Temperature and promotion carry positive coefficients: both raise demand.
Every sign is what a reader would expect, and that is worth checking in any regression before trusting it. A coefficient with the wrong sign usually means the variables are entangled with each other rather than that the world has changed.

15.6  How well does the model fit?
Figure F415-07 sets out the measures the exam names, with what each tells you and what none of them tells you.
Figure F415-07. Measures of fit
Measure (F.4.q)
Range
What it tells you
Note
Correlation coefficient, R
Between -1 and +1
Direction and strength of a straight-line relationship
Negative means they move oppositely
Coefficient of determination, R squared
Between 0 and 1
The share of variation in the result that the model explains
0.90 means 90 per cent explained
The link
R squared is R multiplied by itself, in simple regression
So R of -0.8 gives R squared of 0.64
The sign disappears when squared
Adjusted R squared
Always at or below R squared
Penalises adding variables that do not help
Use it to compare models of different sizes
Standard error of the estimate
In the units of the result
The typical distance of a point from the line
Smaller is better
What none of them prove
Not cause
A high R squared on unrelated series is still a coincidence
Chapter 14's warning applies
The correlation coefficient runs from minus one to plus one and describes a straight-line relationship: its sign gives the direction, its size the strength. The coefficient of determination is that number squared, so it runs from zero to one and gives the share of the variation in the result that the model explains.
Squaring destroys the sign, which is the point candidates most often miss. A correlation of -0.8 and one of plus 0.8 both give an R squared of 0.64. Both describe an equally strong relationship, running in opposite directions.
EXAM TRAP   A high R squared is not proof
R squared measures how much variation the model accounts for, not whether the relationship is real. Chapter 13's cases and staff birthdays could produce a respectable R squared and would still be a coincidence. Judgement about plausible cause comes first, and the statistic second.

15.7  Extended Example 15A: predicting next quarter's demand
This example performs the one calculation this part of the syllabus examines, and then asks how far the answer can be trusted. It is exam-core.
1. The question
Beverages expects to hold the price at $20.00 next quarter, the forecast mean temperature is 26 degrees, and 4 promotion weeks are planned. How many cases should the plant expect?
2. The calculation
WORKED EXAMPLE   Applying the equation
Intercept: 339,267
Price: −8,085 × 20.00 = (161,700)
Temperature: 1,457 × 26 = 37,882
Promotion: 2,706 × 4 = 10,824
Expected demand: 226,273 cases
3. The commonest arithmetic error
Treating the price coefficient as positive gives 549,673 cases, which is far too high. The sign is part of the coefficient, and dropping it is the mistake the exam most often tests in this calculation.
4. How good is the fit?
The model's R squared is 0.9989, so it accounts for almost all the variation in quarterly cases. The adjusted figure is 0.9982, slightly lower because it penalises the three variables used. The standard error is 555 cases, which is the typical distance of an observation from the line.
5. What that does not mean
An R squared this high on 8 observations should make a reader cautious rather than confident. With few observations and three variables, a model can fit the past closely without predicting well, which is the overfitting of section 15.2. The honest statement is that the relationship looks strong and needs more quarters to confirm.
6. Answering Chapter 14's question
The temperature coefficient of 1,457 cases a degree, applied to a seasonal swing of some twenty degrees, moves demand far more than the promotion coefficient of 2,706 applied to a few weeks. Temperature dominates. But promotion is positive and material, so the Chapter 14 analyst who proposed cutting it was wrong.

Chapter summary
Figure F415-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F415-09. Chapter 15 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Analytics divides into four types by the question asked: descriptive for what happened, diagnostic for why, predictive for what will happen and prescriptive for what to do. Fitting a model means separating pattern from noise, and a model too simple underfits while one too complex overfits and predicts worse than a straight line. Clustering groups unlabelled data and classification assigns labelled data to known categories. Multiple regression predicts a quantity and logistic regression a yes or no. The equation is applied by multiplying each coefficient by its value and adding the intercept, keeping the signs. The correlation coefficient runs from minus one to plus one, and its square, the coefficient of determination, gives the share of variation the model explains, though neither establishes cause.

Case question: the forecast for the hot quarter
The Beverages division has fitted a regression to eight quarters of data. Cases sold are explained by price, mean temperature and the number of promotion weeks. The price coefficient is negative, and the temperature and promotion coefficients are both positive.
The model reports an R squared of 0.9989, an adjusted R squared of 0.9982 and a standard error of 555 cases. The divisional accountant has described the model to the board as 'virtually certain' and has proposed using it to set the production plan for the next two years. The plant manager has separately asked what the model says demand will be if price is held at 20.00, forecast temperature is 26 degrees and four promotion weeks are planned.
