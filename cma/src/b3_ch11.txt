11.1  Measuring performance against the plan
Performance is measured against the operational goals set in the budget: revenue, manufacturing costs, non-manufacturing costs and profit. Figure F311-02 shows what each measure says about June.
Figure F311-02. Measures of performance against operational goals

A difference between an actual result and a budgeted figure is a variance. It is a favorable variance when it raises operating income above the budget and an unfavorable variance when it lowers it. The words describe the effect on income, not whether the news is good.
EXAM TRAP   Favorable is not always good
A favorable material price variance may come from buying poor-quality concentrate that later causes spillage. Always ask what caused the variance before judging performance.

11.3  The flexible budget
The flexible budget answers the question: what should the results have been at the volume actually sold? It uses the actual volume with budgeted prices and budgeted costs. Fixed costs do not change, because they do not depend on volume.

11.4  Sales-volume, sales-price and flexible-budget variances
The sales-volume variance is the difference between the flexible budget and the master budget. It equals the change in units multiplied by the budgeted contribution margin per unit: (72,000 − 75,000) × $8.20 = $24,600, unfavorable.
The sales-price variance compares the actual price with the budgeted price on the units actually sold: ($20.40 − $20.00) × 72,000 = $28,800, favorable. Note the different bases: the volume variance uses the budgeted margin, the price variance uses actual units.
The flexible-budget variance is the difference between the actual result and the flexible budget: $7,232, favorable. Figure F311-08 summarizes all of them and shows how they add up.

EXAM TRAP   Volume at margin, price at actual units
Valuing the volume variance at the selling price, or the price variance at budgeted units, are the two most common errors. The volume variance uses the budgeted contribution margin; the price variance uses the units actually sold.

The concentrate price variance of $8,740 came from a world price rise, which the plant cannot control; the usage variance of $4,500 and the labor efficiency variance of $960 came from one long changeover. Chapter 12 calculates these variances in full.
Common reasons for variances include changes in input prices, the quality of materials, the skill or mix of workers, machine problems, the volume produced, and errors in the budget itself. A variance is a question, not an answer.

Three items pass the rule. The favorable price variance is investigated as well as the unfavorable ones, because the division wants to know whether the higher price can be kept. Labor and variable overhead are below the rule and are left alone.

Chapter summary
Figure F311-13 brings the chapter together.
Figure F311-13. Chapter 11 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Performance is measured against revenue, cost and profit goals, and a variance is favorable or unfavorable by its effect on operating income. The master-budget variance mixes volume with everything else, so it is split: the sales-volume variance values the change in units at the budgeted contribution margin, and the flexible-budget variance compares actual results with budgeted prices and costs at the actual volume. The flexible-budget variance is then opened into price and quantity differences, and the significant ones are investigated under management by exception.
