Chapter 1 defined the control environment as deciding who answers for what. This chapter applies that idea to a particular asset, and Chapter 6's access controls reappear here as part of the defence against attack.
11.1  Governance and management
Data governance is the exercise of authority and decision rights over data: who owns it, what may be done with it, and to what standard it is held. Data management is the work of carrying those decisions out. Figure F411-02 separates them.
Figure F411-02. Data governance against data management
Data governance
Data management
Question it answers
Who decides, and by what authority?
How is it actually done?
Typical outputs
Policies, ownership, decision rights, standards
Architecture, loading, quality work, storage
Who does it
A council or committee, with named data owners
Technical and operational teams
Example at Orontes
Deciding that the plant owns production data
Loading it nightly and fixing bad records
In one line
Governance decides
Management does
The distinction is worth holding firmly, because the exam tests it directly and because companies confuse it in practice. Governance decides; management does. A company with a technically excellent data team and no decision rights has management without governance, and nobody can say who is allowed to change a definition.
The practical expression of governance is the data owner: a named person, usually in the business rather than in the technology function, who answers for the quality and the use of one domain of data.

11.2  Applying a control framework to data
The exam asks specifically how COSO's Internal Control framework applies to data governance. The answer is that it is not changed at all: the same five components of Chapter 5 are asked about a different asset. Figure F411-03 works through them.
Figure F411-03. The five COSO components applied to data.
Two components do most of the work here. Information and communication becomes the question of whether anyone knows what data exists and what it means, which is why a data dictionary matters. Monitoring becomes the question of whether data quality is measured at all.
Other frameworks exist and are worth recognising by name. COBIT addresses the governance of information and technology more broadly, and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework addresses security specifically. None of them replaces COSO; they apply it to narrower subjects.

11.3  The data life cycle
Data has a life, and the exam names eight stages of it. Figure F411-04 sets them out in order.
Figure F411-04. The eight stages of the data life cycle.
The order matters less than the two distinctions inside it. Synthesis creates new values from existing ones, such as a cost per case derived from hours and output, and those derived values need governing exactly as captured ones do. Analytics examines data for patterns, which is the subject of Part IV.
The pair most often confused is the last two. Archival removes data from the live system but does not destroy it: the data still exists, still has to be protected, and is still discoverable in a dispute. Purging destroys it. A company that believes archiving has discharged its obligations has misunderstood both.

11.4  Preprocessing: getting data ready
Raw data is rarely fit for analysis. Preprocessing is the work of making it so, and the exam names four steps. Figure F411-05 sets them out.
Figure F411-05. The four steps of preprocessing
Preprocessing step (F.2.d)
What it does
At Orontes
Consolidation
Bring the sources together into one set
Plant output, payroll hours and ledger cost in one table
Cleaning (cleansing)
Remove duplicates, fix errors, handle missing values and outliers
Two spellings of one supplier merged; a blank rate filled or flagged
Transformation
Put the data into the form the analysis needs
Dates to quarters, amounts to a single currency, categories coded
Reduction
Cut the data to what the analysis actually needs
Twelve monthly columns replaced by four quarterly ones
data cleaning absorbs most of the effort in practice. It covers duplicates, plain errors, missing values and outliers, and each of those raises a judgement rather than a rule. A missing rate can be deleted, estimated or flagged, and the three choices give three different answers downstream.
data reduction is the step candidates misread. It does not mean throwing data away carelessly; it means cutting the data to what the analysis needs, so that the result is readable and the computation is manageable. Twelve monthly columns become four quarterly ones because the question is about seasons, not months.
EXAM TRAP   Preprocessing is not analysis
Every one of these four steps happens before the analysis begins. A question describing the fitting of a model or the search for a pattern has left preprocessing behind. The test is whether the data is being prepared or interrogated.

11.5  Why a retention policy must be written
A record retention policy states how long each kind of record is kept and when it is destroyed. Figure F411-06 sets out why having it written down matters, which is what this Level C outcome asks you to discuss.
Figure F411-06. Why a written retention policy matters
Why a written policy matters (F.2.e)
The reason
At Orontes
Legal and tax requirements
Records must exist for as long as a law or authority may ask
Jordanian tax and company law periods
Audit and contract needs
Evidence must survive long enough to support a claim or an audit
Supplier contracts and the records behind them
Privacy obligations
Personal data must be deleted once its purpose has ended
Jordan's Personal Data Protection Law, in force since 2024
Cost and risk of keeping
Data held beyond its purpose costs money and can still be stolen
Twelve years of customer records nobody uses
Consistency
Everyone destroys to the same rule, or destruction looks selective
One schedule, applied by everyone
Litigation hold
Routine destruction stops once a dispute is foreseeable
Deleting on schedule during a dispute looks deliberate
Two of those reasons deserve emphasis. Consistency protects the company: when everyone destroys to the same published schedule, destruction is routine. When destruction is decided case by case, every deletion invites the question of why that record and not another.
A litigation hold is the other. Once a dispute or an investigation is foreseeable, routine destruction must stop for the records concerned. Continuing to delete on schedule during a dispute is how an innocent company acquires the appearance of a guilty one, and Chapter 4 showed that obstructing an investigation carries up to twenty years.
Keeping everything forever is not the safe alternative, which is the point candidates miss. Data held past its purpose costs money, can still be stolen, and under Jordan's data protection law, in force since 2024, personal data must be deleted once the purpose has ended. The policy exists to answer both directions.

11.6  Controls and tools against cyberattack
Figure F411-07 lists the controls and tools the exam names, and says whether each prevents or detects.
Figure F411-07. Controls and tools against cyberattack
Control or tool (F.2.f)
What it is
Prevent or detect
At Orontes
Vulnerability scanning
Automated search for known weaknesses
Detect
Weekly, across all servers
Penetration testing
An authorized simulated attack by skilled testers
Detect
Annually, and after major change
Biometrics
Authentication by a physical characteristic
Prevent
Entry to the server room
Advanced firewalls
Application-aware filtering with intrusion prevention
Prevent
At the internet boundary
Access controls
Unique identity, least privilege, periodic review
Prevent
Every system, every user
Intrusion detection and SIEM
Logs collected centrally, correlated and alerted on
Detect
Continuous, with someone watching
The pair most often confused is the first two. Vulnerability scanning is automated and looks for known weaknesses across everything, which makes it broad, cheap and shallow. Penetration testing is a skilled person attempting an authorized attack, which makes it narrow, expensive and deep. A scan finds the unlocked door; a test finds out what someone could do once through it.
Biometrics authenticates by a physical characteristic rather than something known or carried, which removes the sharing and theft that defeat passwords. It is a stronger authentication, not a different kind of control: the access rules of Chapter 6 still decide what the authenticated person may do.
WATCH: NOT YET TESTABLE   As of October 2026
The NIST Cybersecurity Framework was revised to version 2.0 in February 2024, adding a sixth function, Govern, to the existing Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover. The addition makes the same point this chapter opens with: security is a governance question before it is a technical one.

11.7  Extended Example 11A: preparing the Gulf customer data
This example prepares one real dataset for analysis and then asks how long any of it should be kept. F.2.e is a Level C outcome, so the second half asks for a judgement.
1. The dataset
Orontes wants to analyse three years of Gulf orders. The data sits in the new ERP, but the first two years were loaded from the old system, and the loading was imperfect.
2. Consolidation
Orders, deliveries and customer records come from three tables and must be joined. The join is possible because each carries the customer key of Chapter 10, which is the first practical dividend of a relational design.
3. Cleaning
The old system allowed free text, so one distributor appears as three spellings and must be merged to one. Fourteen orders have no delivery date. Two orders show a quantity a hundred times the usual, which is either a decimal error or a genuine bulk order, and the analyst must find out which rather than guess.
4. Transformation
Amounts are held in three currencies and must be converted to one. Dates become quarters, because the question is about seasonality. Customer size becomes a category rather than a number, so that small and large distributors can be compared.
5. Reduction
Thirty-six monthly columns become twelve quarterly ones, and forty product codes become six families. Nothing true is lost, because the question concerns seasons and families, not months and codes.
6. Then the retention question
The dataset contains the names, telephone numbers and addresses of individual buyers at each distributor. That is personal data, and it falls under Jordan's law for Orontes and under Saudi law for Saudi residents. The analysis needs the orders; it does not need the buyers' names.
7. The judgement
The right answer is to remove the personal identifiers during preprocessing, which serves the analysis and the obligation at once. Where identifiers are genuinely needed, the retention policy must state how long and the data must be purged, not merely archived, when that period ends.

Chapter summary
Figure F411-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F411-09. Chapter 11 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Data governance exercises authority and decision rights over data; data management carries those decisions out. Governance decides and management does. COSO's framework applies to data unchanged: the same five components are asked about a different asset. The life cycle runs capture, maintenance, synthesis, usage, analytics, publication, archival and purging, and archival is not purging. Preprocessing consolidates, cleans, transforms and reduces, all before analysis begins. A written retention policy keeps records as long as law and evidence require, destroys them consistently, and stops destruction once a dispute is foreseeable. Controls against attack include vulnerability scanning and penetration testing, biometrics, advanced firewalls, access controls and continuous monitoring.

Case question: the records nobody would delete
Orontes has never had a written retention policy. Each department decides for itself what to keep, and the practice has been to keep everything. The new ERP holds twelve years of customer records, including the names, telephone numbers and home addresses of individual buyers at each Gulf distributor. No department has ever been told how long its records should be kept, and nobody has been asked to decide.
The sales director wants three years of order data analysed for seasonality. The data sits in three tables, uses three currencies, contains one distributor recorded under three spellings, and includes fourteen orders with no delivery date and two with quantities a hundred times the usual.
Separately, a dispute has arisen with a former distributor in the United Arab Emirates, and lawyers have been instructed. A clerk in sales, tidying old files, has begun deleting correspondence with that distributor because it is more than two years old.
Rania has been asked to advise on the analysis, on the personal data, and on the clerk's tidying. Jordan's Personal Data Protection Law has applied in full to Orontes since March 2025.
