12.1  Standard costing and why companies use it
In a standard costing system, materials, labor and overhead are recorded at standard cost, and the differences between standard and actual are recorded as variances. Inventory and cost of goods sold therefore carry the same cost for every unit, as in Chapter 8.
Figure F312-02 gives the main reasons for adopting such a system. Together they explain why Orontes keeps standards even though its actual costs are known.
Figure F312-02. Reasons for adopting a standard costing system
Reason for a standard costing system (C.1.j)
What it gives Orontes
Simpler record keeping
Inventory and cost of goods sold are recorded at standard, so every unit carries the same cost
Control by exception
Variances point to what differed from the plan (Chapter 11)
Responsibility
Each variance has an owner: purchasing, the plant, the sales team
Budgeting and pricing
The standard card builds the budget (Chapters 6 to 8) and supports price decisions
Motivation
Clear, attainable targets for each input (Book 2, Chapter 18)
The system has costs of its own. Standards must be reviewed as prices and methods change, as Book 2, Chapter 18 showed, and a standard that is out of date produces variances that mean nothing.

Measuring at usage matches the variance to the period in which the material was consumed. The exam usually measures at purchase when it gives the quantity bought; otherwise use the quantity used.
EXAM TRAP   Which quantity?
The price variance always uses an actual quantity, bought or used; the usage variance always uses the standard price. Using the standard quantity for the price variance, or the actual price for the usage variance, double-counts part of the difference.

Chapter summary
Figure F312-15 brings the chapter together.
Figure F312-15. Chapter 12 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A standard costing system records costs at standard and reports the differences as variances, which simplifies record keeping and supports control by exception. Every input variance uses three columns: actual quantity at actual price, actual quantity at standard price, and standard quantity allowed at standard price. The price or rate variance is valued on the actual quantity, measured at purchase or at usage; the efficiency or usage variance is valued at the standard price. Price variances usually belong to purchasing and usage variances to the plant, but the two are often caused by the same decision.

Case question: bottling variances in the Oils division
The Oils division bottles extra-virgin olive oil on its own line, as Book 2 described when the division decided not to send the work outside. Each liter should use one bottle at a standard price, and a standard number of minutes on the line at a standard wage rate.
Last month the division bottled its planned volume of oil. The buyer found a new glass supplier and paid less than the standard price for every bottle, and he bought more bottles than the month needed, to secure the lower price. The line, however, broke more bottles than usual while the operators learned the new supplier's thinner glass, and it also worked more hours than the standard allows.
The division manager has seen only the total cost of bottles, which is below standard, and has congratulated the buyer. The controller, Rania, wants to show the full picture: the price and usage variances for bottles, and the rate and efficiency variances for labor, and whether the buyer's decision really saved money.
She must also decide which quantity of bottles to use for the price variance, because the number bought differs from the number used.
