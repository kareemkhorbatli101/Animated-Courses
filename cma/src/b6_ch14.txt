Chapter 14
Speeding collections and slowing payments
تسريع التحصيل وإبطاء المدفوعات
A customer puts a cheque in the post on Monday. It arrives on Thursday, is opened and banked on Friday, and the funds become available the following Tuesday. The customer's account was debited a week after the money left their intention, and for that week the amount belonged to nobody in particular. It is called float, and managing it is the most mechanical and the most immediately profitable part of treasury work.
This chapter is about shortening the delay on money coming in and lengthening it on money going out, within the terms agreed and the law. The tools are unglamorous — a post office box, a bank account that empties itself every night, a payment released on the day it is due rather than the day the invoice is approved — and on the scale of a company like Orontes, collecting $115,068 a day, each day of float is worth exactly that much.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Book 1 computed Orontes's daily sales as 42,000,000 ÷ 365 = 115,068 and its days sales outstanding as 34.3. Chapter 13 of this book showed a month failing by $246,000 against its minimum balance. Everything in this chapter either shortens the 34.3 days or moves the shortfall.
14.1  Float, in three parts
Float is the delay between one party's records and the other's. Collection float works against the company; disbursement float works for it; net float is the difference, and a company with more of the second than the first has a bank balance larger than its own ledger shows.
Collection float has three components and each is attacked differently.
Mail float is the time from the customer sending the payment to the company receiving it. It is attacked by moving the receiving point closer to the customer, which is what a lockbox does, or by removing the post from the process entirely with an electronic transfer.
Processing float is the time from receipt to deposit: opening, recording, preparing the deposit, taking it to the bank. It is attacked by having the bank do the work, which again is a lockbox, or by direct credit to the account.
Availability float, also called clearing float, is the time from deposit to the funds becoming usable. It is attacked by depositing in a bank where the item will clear fastest, and it has largely shrunk in markets where electronic clearing has replaced paper.
Disbursement float is the same three delays on the paying side, seen from the other end. A cheque posted on the last permitted day, drawn on a bank where it will take time to clear, leaves the company's money in the company's account longer.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: what a day of float is worth to Orontes
Step 1. The daily flow. Orontes collects 42,000,000 ÷ 365 = 115,068 a day on average.
Step 2. One day. Removing one day of collection float releases $115,068 of cash permanently — not once, but permanently, because every subsequent day's collections arrive a day earlier too.
Step 3. Three days. A lockbox arrangement cutting mail and processing float by three days releases 115,068 × 3 = 345,204.
Step 4. The annual value. At the 4 per cent Orontes could earn on short-dated securities, that released cash is worth 345,204 × 0.04 = 13,808 a year, for ever, against whatever the arrangement costs.
Check. The released amount is a stock and the benefit is a flow, and confusing the two is the classic error on this outcome. Three days of float is a one-off release of $345,204 into the bank account; the recurring benefit is only the return earned on it. A candidate who compares $345,204 with an annual bank fee has compared a balance with a rent.

14.2  Speeding collections
Six methods, and the exam expects them named and distinguished.
A lockbox is a post office box in a city near a group of customers, emptied several times a day by a bank that opens the mail, deposits the cheques and reports the details to the company. It removes most of the mail float, because the box is closer, and all of the processing float, because the bank does the work.
Electronic funds transfer removes mail and processing float entirely, and most of the availability float. Where a customer will pay electronically, every other method is inferior.
A preauthorised debit lets the company draw the agreed amount from the customer's account on the due date. It is standard for regular payments — rent, subscriptions, instalments — and it removes the decision to pay from the payer, which is its advantage and the reason some customers resist it.
Concentration banking moves the money from many local accounts into one. A company with branches collects locally, each branch banking where its customers are, and the balances are swept daily into a single concentration account. Three benefits follow: the total idle balance is far smaller than the sum of many local minimums; the one large balance can be invested on better terms than many small ones; and the company borrows once rather than in several places. The transfers themselves are made by depository transfer cheque or, more usually now, by electronic transfer.
Prompt and accurate invoicing is the cheapest method of all and the most often neglected. An invoice issued three days after delivery is paid three days later, and an invoice with an error restarts the whole clock.
A discount for early settlement buys speed with margin. Chapter 18 prices what that costs the seller and Chapter 16 decides whether to offer it.

14.3  Is the lockbox worth it?
The calculation is a small net present value and the exam sets it often. Compare the recurring benefit of the cash released with the recurring cost of the service.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the lockbox decision
Step 1. The flows. A company collects $200,000 a day through its central office. A bank offers a lockbox network that would cut total float by 2.5 days. The bank charges $0.35 an item and the company receives 60,000 items a year. The company can earn 5 per cent on released funds.
Step 2. The cash released. 200,000 × 2.5 = 500,000, released once and held permanently.
Step 3. The annual benefit. 500,000 × 0.05 = 25,000 a year.
Step 4. The annual cost. 0.35 × 60,000 = 21,000 a year.
Step 5. The decision. 25,000 − 21,000 = 4,000 of net annual benefit, so the lockbox is worth having — but only just, and the margin is thin enough that a rise in volume or a fall in interest rates would reverse it.
Check. Two refinements change the answer and both appear in questions. If the bank asks for a compensating balance instead of a fee, the cost is the return forgone on that balance, computed as in section 14.4. And if the company has borrowings outstanding, the benefit should be computed at its borrowing rate rather than at its investment rate, because the released cash repays debt rather than buying securities — and at Orontes's 6 per cent rather than 4, the same release is worth half as much again.

14.4  Compensating balances
A bank may be paid in fees or in balances. A compensating balance is a sum the borrower agrees to keep in a non-interest-bearing account, from which the bank earns by lending it on, and its effect is to raise the effective cost of whatever it is attached to.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: what a compensating balance costs
Step 1. The facility. A company borrows $2,000,000 at a stated 8 per cent and must keep a compensating balance of 10 per cent of the loan.
Step 2. What it actually has. 2,000,000 × 0.90 = 1,800,000 of usable money, because $200,000 must stay in the account.
Step 3. What it pays. Interest is charged on the whole loan: 2,000,000 × 0.08 = 160,000.
Step 4. The effective rate. 160,000 ÷ 1,800,000 = 8.89 per cent, against a stated 8 per cent.
Check. The stated rate was never the rate. Two adjustments can change the answer and the exam uses both. If the company would in any case have held $200,000 in that account for transaction purposes, the requirement costs nothing extra and the effective rate stays at 8 per cent. And if the balance earns some interest, only the shortfall against the market rate is a cost. Read the question for which case it describes.
Compensating balances also attach to services rather than loans: a bank may provide a lockbox or a concentration network free in exchange for balances left with it. The comparison is then between the fee it would otherwise charge and the return forgone on the balance, and the arithmetic is identical.

14.5  Slowing disbursements
The mirror image, and the one where the line between good management and sharp practice has to be drawn explicitly.
Legitimate methods, which the exam expects.
Pay on the due date, not before. An invoice on 30-day terms should be paid on day 30. Paying on day 12 because the approval happened to finish then is a free loan to the supplier.
Centralise payments. One payment run from one account is easier to control, to time and to fund than payments made by several locations.
Use a zero-balance account. Each disbursement account is kept at nil, and the master account funds it each day with exactly the amount of cheques presenting. No idle balances sit waiting in the payment accounts.
Use controlled disbursement. The bank notifies the company early each morning of the exact total that will clear that day, so the treasurer funds that amount and invests everything else — the practice that makes a zero-balance structure usable.
Use a payable-through draft, which is presented to the company for approval before it is paid, adding a day and giving a control point against fraud.
Consider a single payment run a week rather than daily, which slows payment on average by a few days and costs nothing.
Methods that cross the line, and the exam has a view. Deliberately drawing cheques on a distant bank to extend clearing time — remote disbursement — is legal in most places and is regarded as an abuse by banks and by the professional codes. Delaying payment past agreed terms without agreement is not float management; it is unilaterally taking credit, and Chapter 18 prices what it does to a supplier relationship. Mailing on a Friday afternoon so that the item cannot be processed until Tuesday is a decision the company should be willing to describe to the supplier.
The overdraft system belongs here because it changes the question. In markets where overdrafts are normal, a company holds no cash at all: the account runs negative during the month, interest is charged daily on the balance, and the whole apparatus of minimum balances and daily funding is replaced by a limit and a rate. That is cheaper and simpler than holding precautionary cash, and it is why a treasurer in such a market manages the limit rather than the balance. Barada's $240,000 overdraft is this arrangement arriving unplanned rather than chosen.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Float"
English float in treasury means the money in transit between two sets of records, and the metaphor is of something suspended between two states. The word does three other jobs in finance that a candidate meets in the same syllabus: a company floats when it lists its shares, so a flotation is an issue and flotation costs are its fees; a floating rate is one that resets; and the free float is the proportion of a company's shares available to trade. Four meanings, one word, no overlap in sense. Arabic uses العائم for the treasury sense and الطرح for a flotation, keeping them apart. French uses float as a borrowing for the treasury sense, introduction en bourse for the flotation and taux variable for the floating rate — again, three different words. Where English is ambiguous, read the noun next to it.

Chapter summary
Figure F614-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F614-08. Chapter 14 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Float is the delay between two sets of records, in three parts on the way in — mail, processing and availability — and one on the way out, and for a company collecting 42,000,000 ÷ 365 = 115,068 a day, three days of it is 115,068 × 3 = 345,204 released once and held for ever. Collections are accelerated by a lockbox, by electronic transfer, by preauthorised debit, by concentration banking that sweeps many local accounts into one, by invoicing promptly, and by a discount that buys speed with margin. The lockbox decision is a template: 200,000 × 2.5 = 500,000 released, 500,000 × 0.05 = 25,000 of annual benefit against 0.35 × 60,000 = 21,000 of fees, a net 4,000 — and the released cash is a stock while the benefit is a flow. A compensating balance raises the effective rate, because 2,000,000 × 0.08 = 160,000 of interest on 2,000,000 × 0.90 = 1,800,000 of usable money is 160,000 ÷ 1,800,000 = 8.89 per cent, unless the balance would have been held anyway. And payments are slowed legitimately by paying on the due date, centralising, zero-balance accounts, controlled disbursement and payable-through drafts — while remote disbursement is an abuse and paying late without agreement is simply a breach of terms.

Case-style set: three proposals for Orontes
The lockbox. Two days of float at $115,068 a day releases 115,068 × 2 = 230,136 of cash. Orontes has no short-term borrowings, so the benefit is the 4 per cent available on securities: 230,136 × 0.04 = 9,205 a year. The cost is 0.30 × 48,000 = 14,400 a year. The proposal loses about $5,000 a year and should be declined as it stands. It would be worth having if the fee were nearer $0.19 an item, or if the company were borrowing at 6 per cent, in which case the benefit would be 230,136 × 0.06 = 13,808 — still short, which is the honest answer.
Concentration banking. Releasing $400,000 of local minimum balances is worth 400,000 × 0.04 = 16,000 a year against transfer costs that are typically a few thousand. This is the better of the first two and should be done. It also improves control, because one balance is easier to invest and to supervise than four.
Extending supplier payments to 45 days. This is not a float proposal and should not be presented as one. The terms agreed are 30 days; paying on day 45 takes credit the suppliers have not granted. The $300,000 it would release is a loan taken without asking, at a price that will be charged back in worse terms, slower deliveries, or a supplier who prefers a competitor — none of which appears in the calculation. Decline it, and if the company genuinely wants 45-day terms, negotiate them openly and pay whatever the supplier asks in price for the extra fifteen days.
Recommendation: proceed with concentration banking, decline the lockbox at this fee and re-open it at a lower one, and reject the supplier proposal outright.
