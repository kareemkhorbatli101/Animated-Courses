WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
The purchasing clerk in Chapter 1 could both approve a supplier and release payment to it. That single sentence describes a failure of segregation of duties, which is where this chapter begins.
3.1  What segregation of duties means
Segregation of duties means splitting a transaction so that no one person can both carry it out and conceal it. It is the most powerful control activity available, and usually the cheapest, because it costs an organizing decision rather than money.
Figure F403-02 gives examples across the areas the exam uses.
Figure F403-02. Segregation of duties across five areas
Area
What separation looks like
Which duties are split
Cash receipts
The cashier who receives cash does not post the ledger or do the bank reconciliation
Custody separated from recording and reconciliation
Purchasing
The buyer who approves a supplier does not release the payment
Authorization separated from custody of cash
Payroll
The person who adds a new employee does not approve the payroll run
Authorization separated from recording
Inventory
The storekeeper does not maintain the stock records or perform the count
Custody separated from recording and verification
Systems
The programmer does not have access to live data or production programs
Development separated from operations
Notice what the examples have in common. In each case the person who can take or misdirect something is not the person who records it, and neither of them is the person who later checks that records and reality agree.

3.2  The four functional responsibilities
The exam names four responsibilities that should be held by different people, or at least by different departments. Figure F403-03 shows them, and the pairings that create danger.
Figure F403-03. The four duties and the dangerous pairs.
Figure F403-04 defines each one precisely, because the distinction between authorization and custody is where candidates most often slip.
Figure F403-04. The four functional responsibilities
Functional responsibility (E.1.j)
What it means
At Orontes
Authority to execute transactions
Deciding that the transaction should happen at all
The purchasing manager approves a new supplier
Recording transactions
Entering it in the accounting records
The accounts clerk posts the invoice
Custody of assets
Holding or controlling the asset itself
The cashier holds the cash; the storekeeper holds the stock
Periodic reconciliation
Comparing the records with the assets actually there
A third person reconciles the bank statement
The logic is always the same. A person who holds only one of the four can make a mistake or commit a fraud, but someone else will see it. A person who holds two can make the entry and remove the evidence, which is why the pairs matter more than the individual duties.
EXAM TRAP   Two duties, not one, create the risk
A question that describes someone performing one of the four duties describes a normal job. Look for the second duty. The answer is almost always the pair, not the person.

3.3  Independent checks and verification
An independent check is a comparison performed by someone who did not do the work being checked. The word independent is the whole of the control: a reconciliation performed by the person who made the entries proves nothing, because the same misunderstanding or the same intent produced both.
Figure F403-05 lists the checks the exam expects, who must perform each, and what it would catch.
Figure F403-05. Independent checks and who must perform them
Independent check
Who must perform it
What it would catch
Bank reconciliation
Someone who neither handles cash nor posts the ledger
Cash recorded but never banked
Stock count
A team that does not include the storekeeper
Stock recorded but not present
Supplier statement reconciliation
Accounts payable, not the buyer
Invoices paid twice or paid to the wrong party
Review of exception reports
A supervisor, not the clerk who caused the exception
Overrides and unusual amounts
Internal audit
A function reporting to the audit committee, not to the manager audited
Weaknesses the line would not report on itself
Independent checks are detective rather than preventive. They do not stop the error; they find it afterwards. That is why they sit alongside segregation rather than replacing it, and why their value depends entirely on someone acting when a difference appears.

3.4  Safeguarding assets
A safeguarding control limits who can physically reach an asset and establishes who is answerable for it. These matter most where assets are portable and valuable, which at Orontes means cash, saffron and the spice store rather than the bottling line. Figure F403-06 lists the kinds the exam expects.
Figure F403-06. Safeguarding controls
Safeguarding control (E.1.l)
Examples
What it achieves
Physical barriers
Locked stores, fenced yards, safes, restricted areas
Stops the simple taking of an asset
Access control
Badges, keys, biometrics, visitor logs
Records who was able to reach the asset
Environmental protection
Fire suppression, cold-chain alarms, backup power
Protects assets from loss as well as theft
Custodial accountability
One named custodian per store or cash float
Makes a shortage someone's responsibility
Periodic counts
Independent counts compared with records
Detects loss that prevention missed
Insurance and bonding
Cover for losses, and fidelity bonds on staff handling cash
Limits the damage once a loss occurs
Custodial accountability deserves attention because it is often forgotten. A store with three keyholders and no named custodian has no one to ask when stock is short. Assigning one custodian turns a general loss into a specific question.

3.5  Prenumbered forms and authority limits
A prenumbered form carries a sequential number printed before use. Figure F403-07 shows why that simple feature is a control.
Figure F403-07. How a prenumbered sequence exposes a missing document.
The sequence makes completeness testable. Anyone can account for the numbers used, and a gap is a question that somebody must answer. Without it, a document that was destroyed, or never raised at all, leaves no trace.
Authority limits work the same way from the other direction. If the policy names who may receive goods and who may approve a payment, and up to what amount, then an unapproved transaction has nobody who could legitimately have made it. The limit converts a vague expectation into a testable rule.

3.6  Extended Example 3A: redesigning the Orontes purchasing cycle
This example takes the failure of Chapter 1 and rebuilds the cycle properly. It is exam-core: questions give a flawed arrangement and ask which duty to move.
1. The cycle as it was
One purchasing clerk could add a supplier to the approved list, raise the order, confirm that goods had arrived and release the payment. Of the four responsibilities, he held authorization, recording and effective custody of the cash leaving the company. Only reconciliation sat elsewhere, and nobody performed it.
2. Which duty to move first
Supplier approval moves to the purchasing manager, named in the job description rather than delegated verbally. That single change breaks the authorization and custody pair, which is the pairing that let money leave the company.
3. The redesigned cycle
Authorization sits with the purchasing manager, who approves suppliers and orders above a stated limit. Recording sits with the accounts clerk, who posts invoices. Custody sits with the storekeeper, who receives goods, and with the cashier, who releases payment. Reconciliation sits with a fourth person, who compares the supplier statement with the ledger each month.
4. What else changes
Goods received notes are prenumbered, so a delivery that was never recorded leaves a gap. A payment above the limit needs two signatures. The supplier master file is printed and reviewed quarterly by someone outside purchasing, which is an independent check on the authorization itself.
5. What it still cannot do
If the purchasing manager and the cashier agree together, the design fails. That is collusion, and Chapter 1 explained that no separation defeats it. The redesign buys reasonable assurance against one person acting alone, which is what segregation is for.

Chapter summary
Figure F403-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F403-09. Chapter 3 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Segregation of duties splits a transaction so that no one person can both carry it out and conceal it. Four responsibilities must be held separately: authority to execute, recording, custody of the asset, and periodic reconciliation. Independent checks are performed by someone who did not do the work being checked, and they detect rather than prevent. Safeguarding controls limit physical access and name a custodian who answers for what is held. Prenumbered forms make completeness testable, and authority limits turn an expectation into a rule that can be tested.
