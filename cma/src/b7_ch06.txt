Chapter 6
Opportunity cost, and relevant cost in a numerical scenario
تكلفة الفرصة البديلة والتكلفة الملائمة في سيناريو رقمي
The most important cost in many decisions is one that nobody paid, nobody recorded and nobody can look up. If Orontes uses its roaster for an hour to make drums, that hour cannot be used to make jars, and the contribution the jars would have earned is a real cost of the drums. It is called an opportunity cost, and it is the subject of the first half of this chapter.
The second half puts everything from Chapter 5 and this one into a single numerical scenario, because the exam's version of this outcome is always a table of figures with several traps in it and one right answer.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 5 defined an implicit cost as one that involves no payment, and named the opportunity cost as the most important of them. Chapter 3 ranked Orontes's three products by contribution per unit and by contribution per sales dollar, and said that where some other resource is scarce, neither ranking is right. This chapter supplies the ranking that is.
6.1  What an opportunity cost is
An opportunity cost is the benefit given up by using a resource in one way rather than in its best available alternative. Three features define it and the exam tests each.
It arises only where there is an alternative. A resource with nothing else to do has no opportunity cost: an idle machine used for an extra order costs nothing in forgone contribution, because nothing was forgone. That is why the phrase idle capacity appears in almost every special-order question, and Chapter 7 turns on it.
It is measured as the best alternative forgone, not as the sum of all alternatives. A kilogram of sesame that could be used for a jar earning $4.80 of contribution or sold for $2.00 has an opportunity cost of $4.80, not $6.80.
It is a cost even though no payment is made. It never appears in the accounts, it never appears in a cost ledger, and omitting it from an analysis will approve work that destroys value.
Two common forms recur.
The forgone contribution of displaced work. Accepting an order that fills capacity used by existing sales costs the contribution of the sales displaced.
The forgone receipt from an asset. Using a building the company owns costs the rent it could have earned; using material already in stock costs whatever the material could otherwise have realised.

6.2  Opportunity cost when a resource is scarce
The clearest case of an opportunity cost is a bottleneck: a machine, a skilled grade of labour, a material in short supply, a certification, a licence. Where one resource limits output, every unit of it used for one product is a unit not available for another, and the opportunity cost is whatever that other product would have contributed.
The rule that follows is the one Chapter 3 promised. Rank products by contribution per unit of the constrained resource, not per unit of product and not per sales dollar.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the roaster is the bottleneck
Step 1. The constraint. Orontes's roaster is fully used. A jar takes 0.004 roaster hours, a bar 0.001 and a drum 0.020.
Step 2. Contribution per roaster hour. For the jar: 4.80 ÷ 0.004 = 1,200 an hour. For the bar: 1.20 ÷ 0.001 = 1,200 an hour. For the drum: 21.00 ÷ 0.020 = 1,050 an hour.
Step 3. The ranking. The jar and the bar are equally good at $1,200 an hour, and the drum is the worst of the three — although it has by far the highest contribution per unit, at $21.00 against $4.80 and $1.20.
Step 4. The opportunity cost. An hour given to drums earns $1,050 and forgoes $1,200, so each drum hour costs the company 1,200 − 1,050 = 150 of contribution. On 2,000 drum hours that is 2,000 × 150 = 300,000 a year.
Check. Chapter 3 ranked the drum first on contribution per unit and third on contribution per sales dollar. Here it is third again, for a third reason, and the three rankings would produce three different product strategies. Which one is right depends entirely on what limits the business, and establishing that is the first question to ask of any product-mix problem — before computing anything.
Two refinements complete the outcome.
The ranking holds while the constraint holds. If the roaster could be run an extra shift, the constraint moves elsewhere and the ranking changes with it. A constraint is a fact about the present configuration, not about the products.
Relieving the constraint has a value, and the opportunity cost measures it. If an hour of roaster time is worth $1,200 of contribution, then an extra hour bought in — overtime, a subcontractor, a second machine — is worth up to $1,200, and anything cheaper than that is worth doing. That figure is sometimes called the shadow price of the constraint, and it is the most useful number a bottleneck analysis produces.

6.3  A full scenario
The exam's version of this outcome is a table with traps. Here is one, worked in full.
Orontes is asked to quote for a one-off contract to supply a hotel group with 40,000 jars of a special blend. The following facts are supplied.
Sesame: the contract needs 20,000 kg. Orontes holds 12,000 kg bought last year at $3.00 a kilogram; sesame now costs $3.40 and is used continuously.
Labour: 2,000 hours of filling-line labour, paid $18 an hour. The line is fully employed on standard jars; the staff cannot be increased in the time available.
Packaging: special jars and labels costing $1.10 a unit, bought in.
Machine time: 160 roaster hours. The roaster is fully used, and the contribution forgone is $1,200 an hour.
Supervision: the existing supervisor would oversee the contract within the present salary of $6,000 a month.
Development: $25,000 was spent last month designing the blend at the hotel group's request.
Overhead: the company absorbs factory overhead at $4.00 a jar; none of it would change.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the minimum price
Step 1. Sesame. All 20,000 kg must be valued at the replacement cost of $3.40, because the material is in continuous use: using the 12,000 kg in stock means buying 12,000 kg more. The relevant cost is 20,000 × 3.40 = 68,000. The $3.00 original price is sunk.
Step 2. Labour. The workers are already employed and already paid, so their wages are not incremental — but their time is. Using 2,000 hours on this contract displaces standard production, so the relevant cost is the contribution forgone on the jars not made. Where a question gives that contribution directly, use it; here the roaster hours in step 4 are the binding constraint and the labour follows them, so the labour cost is the wages only if extra hours must be paid for. On these facts no extra wages arise, so the relevant labour cost is nil and the displacement is captured in step 4.
Step 3. Packaging. Bought in specially, so wholly incremental: 40,000 × 1.10 = 44,000.
Step 4. Machine time. 160 hours at a forgone contribution of $1,200 an hour: 160 × 1,200 = 192,000. This is the opportunity cost of section 6.2, and it is the largest number in the analysis.
Step 5. Supervision, development and overhead. All three are excluded. The supervisor's salary does not change; the $25,000 is sunk; the $4.00 a jar is an absorption rate, not a cost that moves.
Step 6. The minimum price. 68,000 + 44,000 + 192,000 = 304,000, which is 304,000 ÷ 40,000 = 7.60 a jar.
Check. Note the three figures a careless answer would include: the $25,000 of development, the $36,000 of wages at $18 an hour, and the $160,000 of absorbed overhead at $4.00 a jar. Adding all three would give a minimum price of $12.63 a jar, and the contract — which is worth taking at anything above $7.60 — would be refused. Note also the one figure a careless answer would omit: the $192,000 of forgone contribution, without which the minimum price falls to $2.80 and the company would accept work that makes it poorer.

EXAM TRAP   The minimum price is a floor, not a quote
The relevant cost is the price below which the contract makes the company worse off. What to actually charge is a commercial question, and Chapters 10 to 13 answer it.
A minimum price computed on relevant costs is valid for a one-off contract with idle or displaceable capacity. It is not a basis for regular pricing, because fixed costs have to be covered by somebody.
Where labour is paid whether or not the contract is taken, its wages are not relevant; where the contract requires overtime or new hires, they are.

6.4  Where the traps are
Four traps account for most of the marks lost on this outcome, and all four appeared in section 6.3.
The recorded cost of something already owned. Material at the price paid, a machine at book value, a building at cost. Each is sunk; what matters is what the thing could otherwise do.
Labour that is paid anyway. A salaried workforce costs the same whether a contract is taken or not, so its wages are not incremental — but its time may be, if the work displaces something else. The relevant cost of labour is nil where there is spare capacity, the wages where extra hours must be bought, and the forgone contribution where existing work is displaced.
Absorbed or apportioned overhead. An absorption rate exists to put a full cost on a product for inventory valuation and reporting. It is not a statement that the overhead moves with the product, and in a relevant-cost analysis it is excluded unless the question says the overhead itself changes.
The missing opportunity cost. The three traps above cause costs to be included that should not be; this one causes a cost to be omitted that should be included, and it is the more dangerous, because the error approves work rather than refusing it.

A fifth trap is subtler and worth a sentence of its own. An opportunity cost exists only where the alternative is genuinely available. If the displaced jars could not have been sold anyway — because demand is short, or the warehouse is full — then nothing is forgone and the opportunity cost is nil. A question that says "the company can sell all it produces" is telling you the alternative exists; a question that says demand is 3,500,000 jars and capacity is 4,000,000 is telling you it does not.

6.5  What to write
An analysis of this kind is marked on its reasoning as much as its arithmetic, so three habits are worth building.
Give every item a line, including the ones excluded. A table with a reason against each excluded item shows that the exclusion was decided rather than forgotten, and it is the difference between a sound answer and one that looks lucky.
Name the opportunity cost explicitly. Write "contribution forgone on 160 displaced roaster hours at $1,200" rather than "machine cost $192,000". The marker is looking for the concept, and the words carry it.
State what the answer is a floor for. A minimum price is not a recommendation to charge it; it is the point below which the company is worse off. The sentence "the contract is worth taking at any price above $7.60 a jar, and what to charge depends on what the hotel group would pay" is the professional ending, and it is the hinge into the pricing chapters that follow.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Cost"
English cost in this chapter means four different things within a paragraph: the amount paid, as in the original cost; the amount that would be paid, as in replacement cost; the amount given up, as in opportunity cost; and the amount charged by an accounting system, as in absorbed cost. Only the second and third are relevant to a decision, and only the first and fourth appear in the records — which is the whole difficulty of the topic in one sentence. Arabic تكلفة covers all four and relies on the qualifier. French coût does the same, with coût de remplacement, coût d'opportunité and coût complet as settled phrases. The safeguard in every language is to never write the word alone.

Chapter summary
Figure F706-07 brings the chapter together.
Figure F706-07. Chapter 6 summary map: the key rule for each section.
An opportunity cost is the benefit forgone from the best alternative use of a resource: it exists only where an alternative is genuinely available, it is measured as the best one and not the sum of them, and it appears in no ledger. Material already held is valued at replacement cost where it is in regular use and at the scrap or alternative value forgone where it is not, never at what was paid. Where one resource is scarce, products rank by contribution per unit of that resource, so Orontes's jar and bar at 4.80 ÷ 0.004 = 1,200 an hour beat the drum at 21.00 ÷ 0.020 = 1,050 — a third ranking, different from both of Chapter 3's — and the $1,200 is also the shadow price, the most a company should pay to relieve the bottleneck. In a full scenario the relevant cost of a 40,000-jar contract is 68,000 + 44,000 + 192,000 = 304,000, or 304,000 ÷ 40,000 = 7.60 a jar, once the sunk development, the salaried wages and the absorbed overhead are excluded and the 160 × 1,200 = 192,000 of forgone contribution is included. Four traps account for the lost marks, of which the omitted opportunity cost is the worst because it approves work rather than refusing it. And the answer is a floor rather than a quotation, which is where the pricing chapters begin.

What it tells you
Two of the six items are excluded entirely and one is included at more than its wage rate. A full-cost quotation on these figures would have charged 30.00 an hour of overhead on 1,000 hours and 18,000 of design, and would have refused work worth taking at anything above 29,600.

Case-style set: the hotel contract, revisited
The one-off contract. At $9.00 a jar against a relevant cost of $7.60, the contract adds 9.00 − 7.60 = 1.40 a jar, which on 40,000 jars is 40,000 × 1.40 = 56,000 of contribution. Taken on its own and for one year, it is worth having.
The three-year commitment. This changes the question entirely, for three reasons. First, the $7.60 floor contains $4.80 a jar of forgone contribution at today's constraint; if demand for jars grows, the roaster time becomes more valuable and the floor rises, while the price is fixed at $9.00. Second, the floor also contains sesame at today's replacement cost of $3.40, and a three-year price commitment with no escalation clause takes the whole of the input price risk — which Book 2's Chapter 6 showed can be hedged, at a cost that should be priced into the quotation. Third, a fixed price for three years in a company whose gross margin has fallen a point a year compounds the problem Book 1 identified.
I would accept the one-off contract and decline the three-year commitment at $9.00, offering instead a three-year arrangement with an annual price review linked to the sesame price, or a higher fixed price that funds the hedge.
The second roaster. It costs $1,800,000 and adds 3,000 hours a year. At the current shadow price of $1,200 an hour it would release 3,000 × 1,200 = 3,600,000 of contribution a year, which pays for the machine twice over in a year — if the output could be sold. That condition is the whole of the question: the shadow price assumes every displaced jar had a buyer, and Orontes sells 3,500,000 jars into a market where it has been buying volume with price. The roaster is worth buying only if the sales exist, and the figure to establish before spending $1,800,000 is demand, not capacity.
