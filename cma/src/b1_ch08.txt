WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
If you studied IAS 2, you know one test: the lower of cost and net realizable value. U.S. GAAP uses the same test for most inventory. The new part is the older test, lower of cost or market, which U.S. GAAP still requires for LIFO and the retail method. Also note: IFRS reverses write-downs when prices recover; U.S. GAAP never does.
8.1  Which test applies?
At each balance sheet date, the company checks whether the cost of its inventory is still recoverable. The test depends on the cost flow method, as Figure F08-02 shows.
Inventory measured by FIFO, average cost or specific identification uses the lower of cost and net realizable value (LCNRV).
Inventory measured by LIFO or the retail inventory method uses the lower of cost or market (LCM) (Section 8.2).
Figure F08-02. Decision: the cost flow method decides whether LCNRV or LCM applies.
Net realizable value (NRV) is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, minus the costs to complete, sell and transport the goods. If NRV is below cost, the company records a write-down to NRV. The loss goes to income, usually inside cost of goods sold, or as a separate line if it is large.
WORKED EXAMPLE   LCNRV for Orontes olive oil
In Chapter 7, Orontes used FIFO for olive oil. Its ending inventory was 2,000 cases at $50. In late December, a large competitor cut prices. Orontes now expects to sell each case for $54, with $6 of selling and delivery costs. Figure F08-03 shows the test.

The write-down of $4,000 is a loss of 2025. The new amount, $48 a case, is now the cost basis of these cases. If prices recover in 2026, Orontes does not reverse the write-down under U.S. GAAP. A write-down recognized in an interim quarter is the only exception: it may be recovered in a later quarter of the same fiscal year.
● IFRS CONTRAST   IAS 2 reverses write-downs
IAS 2 uses LCNRV for all inventories, including retail, and it has no LCM test. When prices recover, IAS 2 requires a reversal, limited to the original write-down. If NRV rises to $53 in 2026, an IFRS company in Orontes's position would reverse $4,000. Under U.S. GAAP the reversal is zero.
EXAM TRAP   NRV has no profit deduction
NRV = selling price − costs to complete, sell and transport. Do NOT deduct a normal profit; that only defines the LCM floor.
Check the method first: FIFO or average → LCNRV; LIFO or retail → LCM.
U.S. GAAP never reverses an annual write-down. IFRS does.

8.2  Lower of cost or market
Under LCM, market starts with replacement cost: the current cost to buy or make the item again. But market has two limits. It cannot be higher than a ceiling equal to NRV, and it cannot be lower than a floor equal to NRV minus a normal profit margin.
The procedure has two steps. First, find market: take the middle value of replacement cost, the ceiling and the floor. Second, compare market with cost and take the lower. Figure F08-04 shows three Orontes products that the company measures under LIFO.
Figure F08-04. LCM number lines: the floor, replacement cost or the ceiling sets market.
Why the limits? The ceiling stops the company from carrying inventory above what it can recover. The floor stops an excessive write-down that would create an abnormally high profit when the goods are sold later.

The item-by-item basis gives a total write-down of $4,500. A company may also apply LCM by category or to the total inventory, if it does so consistently. On the total basis, total market ($121,500) is compared with total cost ($122,000), and the write-down is only $500, because gains on some items offset losses on others. The item-by-item basis is the most conservative.
One more rule: if a company has a firm, non-cancellable purchase commitment at a price above the current market, it records the expected loss now, in the current period.
EXAM TRAP   Find market first
Do not compare replacement cost directly with cost. First limit it by the ceiling and the floor.
When replacement cost is below the floor, market is the floor. When it is above the ceiling, market is the ceiling.
Read the basis in the question: item by item, by category or total.

In French, dépréciation des stocks is an inventory write-down, not depreciation (amortissement). In Arabic, تخفيض قيمة المخزون is the write-down; do not use استهلاك, which is depreciation.

8.3  The retail inventory method and the gross profit method
Retailers sell thousands of items. They know the selling price of every item on the shelf, but tracking the cost of each one is expensive. The retail inventory method estimates ending inventory at cost from retail prices. It uses a cost-to-retail ratio: goods available at cost divided by goods available at retail.
Retail prices change during the year. A markup raises the selling price above the original retail price. A markdown lowers it. The conventional retail method includes net markups in the ratio but leaves out net markdowns. This gives a lower ratio and a lower ending inventory, which approximates LCM. The average-cost retail method includes both.

A LIFO retail method also exists. It leaves beginning inventory out of the ratio, includes markdowns, and adds a new layer each year at that year's ratio. The exam tests it less often.
The gross profit method
The gross profit method estimates ending inventory from the usual gross profit rate. It is used for interim reports, to check a physical count, and to estimate inventory lost in a fire or a theft. It is not acceptable for annual financial statements.
A fire destroyed part of the Orontes warehouse in Jebel Ali. Beginning inventory was $80,000, purchases were $300,000 and net sales were $400,000. The usual gross profit rate is 30% of sales. Estimated cost of goods sold is $280,000, so estimated inventory at the date of the fire is $380,000 − $280,000 = $100,000. Goods costing $12,000 were saved, so the estimated fire loss is $88,000.
EXAM TRAP   Markup on cost is not a margin on sales
If the question gives a markup on cost, convert it: gross profit rate on sales = markup ÷ (1 + markup). For example, a markup of one half on cost is a gross profit rate of one third on sales.
Conventional retail: markups IN the ratio, markdowns OUT.
Freight-in goes in the cost column only; normal shrinkage is deducted from retail after the ratio.

The weighted average and specific identification have their own strengths. The weighted average is simple, smooths price changes and is hard to manipulate, but its results are neither fully current on the balance sheet nor on the income statement. Specific identification gives exact matching for unique items, but for identical items it is costly and lets management choose which costs to report.
The main trade-off is between the two statements. LIFO gives a more realistic income statement when prices change, but an out-of-date balance sheet. FIFO gives a realistic balance sheet, but its income includes holding gains. The weighted average sits between them.

8.5  Recommending a method
A Level C question may give you a set of facts and ask you to recommend a method. Use Figure F08-09 as a decision path, then support your answer with the facts in the question.
Figure F08-09. Decision path for recommending an inventory cost flow method.
Always check the facts that can rule a method out. If the company, or its parent, reports under IFRS, LIFO is not possible. If items are identical and sold in large volumes, specific identification is costly and open to manipulation. If the company wants the U.S. tax benefit of LIFO, it must also report LIFO profits to investors, and it must avoid liquidating old layers.
WORKED EXAMPLE   A recommendation for Orontes
Orontes reports under U.S. GAAP, and olive oil prices are rising. The finance director wants lower taxes but also a balance sheet that lenders trust. Olive oil is a perishable, identical product that Orontes ships oldest-first.
LIFO would lower taxes while prices rise, but conformity would lower reported profits too, and the balance sheet would show old costs.
FIFO matches the physical flow and gives lenders a current inventory amount, but taxes are higher.
Recommendation: if the tax saving is the priority, LIFO, with clear disclosure of the LIFO reserve; if lender relations and the balance sheet matter more, FIFO. The answer depends on the stated priority, and a good exam answer names it.
EXAM TRAP   Recommendations need reasons
In a case-based question, a recommendation earns credit only if it follows from the facts given. Check IFRS, taxes, price trends, the physical flow and the cost of records before you choose.

Chapter summary
Figure F08-10 brings the chapter together.
Figure F08-10. Chapter 8 summary map: the key rule for each section.
FIFO, average cost and specific identification use the lower of cost and NRV. LIFO and the retail method use the lower of cost or market, where market is replacement cost between a ceiling and a floor. Write-downs go to income and are never reversed under U.S. GAAP, although IFRS reverses them. The retail method estimates cost from a cost-to-retail ratio, and the gross profit method gives estimates only. Each cost flow method has strengths and weaknesses, so a recommendation must follow the facts: IFRS reporting, tax goals, price trends and the physical flow.

Written task: model answer
Since 2017, U.S. GAAP has used the simpler lower of cost and net realizable value test for most inventory, including FIFO and average cost. The FASB kept the older lower of cost or market test for LIFO and the retail method. Under LCM, market starts with replacement cost. The ceiling (net realizable value) stops the company from carrying inventory above the amount it can recover. The floor (net realizable value minus a normal profit) stops an excessive write-down that would create an abnormal profit when the goods are sold later.
A good answer: states which methods use LCM and which use LCNRV; defines market as replacement cost within limits; explains the ceiling; explains the floor; mentions that the write-down goes to income and is not reversed (bonus).
