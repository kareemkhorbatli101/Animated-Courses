Everything so far in this book has been done risk by risk. This chapter raises the view to the whole company, which is what enterprise risk management means and why it exists: the sum of well-managed parts is not a well-managed company, because risks interact, resources are finite, and the only level at which a portfolio of risks can be compared with the capital behind it is the top.
Five outcomes meet here. What enterprise risk management is and what it is for; how it connects to governance, analytics, portfolio thinking, performance management and internal control; how to evaluate a scenario and recommend a response; how to prepare the cost-benefit analysis that justifies any of it; and the COSO framework the exam uses as its reference.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 3 showed that expected losses add but unexpected losses do not, which is the arithmetic case for looking at risk across a whole company. Chapter 6 gave the process and the appetite. Chapter 8 gave the measures. Book 3's Chapter 17 ranked projects under a capital constraint, which is the same problem this chapter applies to risk spending.
9.1  What enterprise risk management is
Enterprise risk management is the process, directed by the board and applied across the whole organisation, of identifying and managing the risks to its strategy and objectives within an appetite the board has set, so as to provide reasonable assurance that the objectives will be achieved.
Four elements of that definition carry the examinable weight.
Across the whole organisation. Risk is considered in every unit and function and then aggregated, rather than managed separately in each. The failure it corrects has a name — the silo — in which each department manages its own exposures competently and nobody sees the combination.
Directed by the board. The appetite is set at the top, the significant risks are reported there, and the process is owned by management rather than delegated to a technical function.
Applied to strategy and objectives. Enterprise risk management is about what could stop the company achieving what it has set out to do, including the risk that the strategy itself is wrong, which no departmental process can raise.
Reasonable assurance, not certainty. No system prevents all loss; the standard is a sensible balance of cost and benefit, which section 9.5 computes.
The objectives of an enterprise programme, which the exam asks for, are to align risk-taking with appetite and strategy; to improve the quality of risk responses by choosing among them consistently; to reduce surprises and losses; to identify and manage risks that cross units; to seize opportunities, which is Chapter 5's exploitation at enterprise level; and to deploy capital more effectively, because a company that knows its aggregate exposure knows how much capital it needs.
The benefits follow from those objectives and were set out in Chapter 4; three are specific to the enterprise view.
A portfolio perspective. Because unexpected losses do not add, the enterprise exposure is smaller than the sum of the parts. Where Orontes's four areas report unexpected losses totalling 1,900,000 and the enterprise figure is 1,400,000, the 1,900,000 − 1,400,000 = 500,000 of difference is a diversification benefit that no division can see from where it stands — and a company that manages risk only in silos will buy 500,000 more protection than it needs.
Consistent prioritisation. One appetite, one set of scales, one method of comparing responses means that the next dollar of risk spending goes where it does most good, instead of to whichever manager argued hardest.
And visible risk interactions. The combinations that cause serious losses — a currency movement, a customer failure and a supply interruption with one economic cause — are invisible to each department and obvious from the centre.

9.2  The COSO framework
The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission publishes two documents the exam refers to: the Internal Control — Integrated Framework, which underlies Chapter 15's treatment of control, and Enterprise Risk Management — Integrating with Strategy and Performance, the 2017 revision of the 2004 enterprise framework. Candidates meet both versions, and it is worth knowing each.
The 2004 framework presented a cube: eight components against four categories of objective — strategic, operations, reporting and compliance — across the units of the entity. The eight components were the internal environment, objective setting, event identification, risk assessment, risk response, control activities, information and communication, and monitoring. The structure is recognisably the process of Chapter 6 with a control environment wrapped around it, and questions still use the eight component names.
The 2017 framework is the current one and is organised quite differently, as five interrelated components containing twenty principles, with risk presented as part of strategy and performance rather than as a separate exercise.
Governance and culture. The board's oversight of risk, the operating structure, the desired culture, the commitment to core values, and the attraction and retention of capable people. Chapters 16 and 17 of this book are this component.
Strategy and objective-setting. Analysing the business context, defining risk appetite, evaluating alternative strategies, and formulating business objectives. The central insight of the 2017 revision lives here: the greatest risk is not a risk to the strategy but the possibility that the strategy itself is misaligned with the mission, or that the company has chosen a strategy whose implications it has not understood.
Performance. Identifying risk, assessing severity, prioritising risks, implementing responses, and developing a portfolio view — Chapters 5 to 8 of this book.
Review and revision. Assessing substantial change, reviewing risk and performance, and pursuing improvement in enterprise risk management — Chapter 6's monitoring step.
Information, communication and reporting. Leveraging information systems, communicating risk information, and reporting on risk, culture and performance.
Three themes of the revision are examinable.
Risk is integrated with performance, not parallel to it. The same meeting that reviews results reviews the risks to them.
Culture is treated as a component in its own right, because frameworks do not fail technically; they fail where people do not use them.
And risk appetite connects strategy to execution, which is why Chapter 6 insisted that an appetite must be expressed in limits somebody applies.

9.3  What it integrates with
The outcome asks how enterprise risk practices integrate with five other things, and each connection has a specific content.
Corporate governance. The board sets the appetite, oversees the process, and satisfies itself that it works; the audit committee or a separate risk committee does the detailed review; management owns the risks; internal audit provides independent assurance that the process operates. That division is often summarised as the three lines: the business owns and manages risk, risk and compliance functions set the framework and challenge, and internal audit provides independent assurance.
Risk analytics. The measurement of Chapter 8 — the distributions, the at-risk figures, the loss databases, and increasingly the models that predict failures from operating data. Analytics serve the process; they are not the process, and a company with excellent analytics and no appetite statement has measured things nobody can act on.
Portfolio management. The aggregation of section 9.1: risks combined across units with correlations taken into account, compared with capital and appetite, and resources allocated across them as Book 3's Chapter 17 allocated capital — to the risks where the next dollar does most good.
Performance management. Risk belongs in the same conversation as results, which means three practical things: targets set with the risk of missing them understood; variances investigated for what they reveal about exposure as well as about performance; and incentives designed so that they do not pay for risk-taking the company has not agreed to. Book 4's Chapter 17 and Book 1's analysis of earnings quality both bear on that last point.
Internal control. Controls are the execution layer for the reduction response of Chapter 5. Enterprise risk management decides which risks matter and how far they should be reduced; internal control delivers the reduction; and the control framework reports whether the controls work. Chapter 15 connects internal control to ethics, which is the dimension that no framework can supply by itself.

9.4  Evaluating a scenario and recommending
The outcome asks for scenarios to be evaluated and mitigation recommended, which is the practical skill the essay section of the exam tests. A method in five steps produces an answer that earns the marks.
Describe the scenario precisely, including what else is true. A scenario is not one variable moving; it is a coherent situation. "The sesame harvest fails" implies a higher input price, a supply interruption, a competitor under the same pressure, and possibly a currency effect if the shortfall is imported.
Trace the consequences through the figures. Volume, price, contribution, operating income, cash, covenant headroom, in that order, because the question that matters is not what profit falls to but whether anything breaks.
Identify the breaking points. Which covenant, which facility, which customer commitment, which regulatory threshold fails first, and when. Chapter 6's 4,348,800 − 1,560,000 = 2,788,800 of covenant headroom is this kind of figure.
Choose responses from Chapter 5's five, with the cost-benefit test of section 9.5, and say what each does to the breaking points rather than to the average.
Recommend, with an owner, a cost, a date and a trigger — the observable condition that would cause the response to be activated, which converts a plan into a decision somebody can take.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: a scenario and its mitigation
Step 1. The scenario. A poor harvest raises hulled sesame prices by 25 per cent for a year and Orontes's single supplier cannot deliver for six weeks.
Step 2. Through the figures. The supply interruption costs 46,027 a day of contribution, so six weeks is about 1,933,134. The price increase, on variable costs that are mostly sesame, would remove a further large part of the 16,800,000 annual contribution unless prices can be raised, which Book 3's Chapter 11 says is difficult in a market-priced product.
Step 3. The breaking point. Operating income of $4,348,800 against a covenant floor of 1,560,000 gives 2,788,800 of headroom, and the supply interruption alone consumes two thirds of it. Combined with the price effect the covenant is the first thing to break, not the cash balance.
Step 4. Responses and their cost. Qualify a second supplier (cost 60,000 a year); hold four weeks of buffer stock (cost 180,000 of carrying); contract forward for part of the crop as Book 2's Chapter 6 did (cost: the forward premium and the loss of any price fall); and open a conversation with the lender about the covenant before it is needed rather than after.
Step 5. The recommendation, with triggers. Qualify the second supplier now. Build buffer stock when the harvest forecast first falls below the five-year average, not when the delivery fails. Hedge half the expected volume at the point the forward price is below budget. And review the covenant at the half-year.
Check. Note what the last step adds. Without triggers, a scenario analysis is an interesting document; with them, it is a set of decisions that have already been taken and only need to be activated, which is the difference between a company that responds in a week and one that responds in a quarter.

9.5  The cost-benefit analysis
Every risk response competes for money with every other use of money, and the instrument that decides is a cost-benefit analysis. The outcome asks you to prepare one, which means knowing what goes on each side.
The costs, and candidates usually stop too early.
The direct cost of the response: the premium, the contract, the equipment, the salaries of the people performing a control.
The implementation cost: systems, training, project time.
The operating friction: the delay an approval adds, the sales lost to a tighter credit policy, the capital tied up in buffer stock.
And the new risks the response creates, which Chapter 5 insisted on.
The benefits.
The reduction in expected loss, which is the measurable core: inherent expected loss less residual.
The reduction in unexpected loss, which is worth more than it looks because it reduces the capital or facilities the company must hold.
Second-order effects: lower premiums, better terms from lenders, fewer disruptions to customers, and the cost-of-capital effect of Chapter 4.
And any operational benefit that comes with the control, since many risk responses also improve the process — a second supplier is also a negotiating position.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the second supplier
Step 1. The present position. Chapter 6 assessed the single-source supply failure at a likelihood of 0.25 against an impact of 1,933,134, giving an expected loss of 0.25 × 1,933,134 = 483,284.
Step 2. After the response. Qualifying a second supplier and holding buffer stock reduces the likelihood to 0.05, so the expected loss becomes 0.05 × 1,933,134 = 96,657.
Step 3. The benefit and the cost. The reduction is 483,284 − 96,657 = 386,627 a year, against costs of 240,000 — 60,000 of qualification and oversight and 180,000 of carrying the stock — so the net benefit is 386,627 − 240,000 = 146,627 a year and the benefit-to-cost ratio is 386,627 ÷ 240,000 = 1.61.
Step 4. Over the life of the decision. Treating it as a five-year commitment and discounting at the 9 per cent of Book 3, the annual net benefit is worth 146,627 × 3.8897 = 570,335, against a one-off qualification and trial cost of 150,000, giving 570,335 − 150,000 = 420,335 of value created.
Check. Three things make this a complete analysis rather than an arithmetic exercise. The benefit on unexpected loss is not in the figures: reducing the likelihood from 0.25 to 0.05 removes most of the chance of the event that would consume the covenant headroom, and that is worth more to the board than the 146,627. The buffer stock has an operational cost beyond its carrying charge, since stock ages and this is a food product. And the whole calculation rests on two judgments — the 0.25 and the 0.05 — so the honest presentation states them as such and shows what the answer becomes if the reduction is half as effective.
Four principles for any cost-benefit analysis in risk work.
Use cash flows and incremental amounts, exactly as Book 3's Chapter 14 required, and ignore allocated costs.
Include the benefits that cannot be quantified, as a stated list beside the arithmetic, rather than omitting them and allowing the quantified ones to decide alone.
Test the judgments that drive the answer.
And state the limit of the analysis. A control whose benefit is the avoidance of a catastrophic loss cannot be justified by expected-value arithmetic, because the expected value of a rare event is small; the justification is the survival test of Chapter 3, and the cost-benefit analysis has nothing to say about it.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Enterprise", "framework" and "integrated"
Enterprise in enterprise risk management means the whole entity, every unit and function, and it is doing real work: the word was chosen to contrast with the departmental risk management it replaced. English also uses enterprise for a venture and for initiative, and neither is meant here. Arabic المؤسسة and French entreprise both mean the organisation as a whole, so the sense carries. Framework is a word to use carefully: a framework is a structure for organising work, not the work, and a company that says it has adopted a framework has said nothing about whether it manages risk. Integrated, in the COSO titles, is the strongest claim in either document — it means the practices are part of how the business is run rather than an exercise performed beside it — and it is the claim most companies fail. When an exam question asks whether a company's risk management is effective, the answer turns on integration and culture rather than on whether a framework has been adopted.

Chapter summary

Figure F809-03. Chapter 9 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Enterprise risk management is the board-directed, organisation-wide process of managing risks to strategy and objectives within an appetite, giving reasonable assurance rather than certainty, and its purpose is to correct the silo: its objectives are to align risk-taking with strategy, improve responses, reduce surprises, manage risks that cross units, seize opportunities and deploy capital better, and its distinctive benefits are the portfolio view — divisional unexpected losses of 1,900,000 against an enterprise 1,400,000 showing 1,900,000 − 1,400,000 = 500,000 of diversification — consistent prioritisation and visible interactions. COSO's 2004 cube set eight components against four objective categories; the 2017 revision uses five components and twenty principles, treats culture as a component, integrates risk with performance, and makes the misaligned strategy the greatest risk of all. Case-style set: the board paper
The proposal. Qualify a second sesame supplier and hold four weeks of buffer stock, at a cost of $240,000 a year.
The arithmetic. A six-week interruption costs 46,027 a day of contribution, about 1,933,134 in total. On the present single-source arrangement the likelihood is assessed at 0.25 in a year, so the expected loss is 0.25 × 1,933,134 = 483,284. With a second supplier and buffer stock the likelihood falls to 0.05 and the expected loss to 0.05 × 1,933,134 = 96,657. The benefit is therefore 483,284 − 96,657 = 386,627 a year against 240,000 of cost, a net 386,627 − 240,000 = 146,627 and a ratio of 386,627 ÷ 240,000 = 1.61. Over five years at 9 per cent the annual net benefit is worth 146,627 × 3.8897 = 570,335, against 150,000 of one-off qualification cost, leaving 570,335 − 150,000 = 420,335 of value.
The eleven clean years. They are evidence and they are not proof. An event assessed at a quarter in a year would be expected to occur about three times in eleven years, so eleven clean years is either good fortune or evidence that the 0.25 is too high — and that question is answerable, from the supplier's own delivery record, from the harvest history, and from what happened to competitors in the poor crop years. If the honest figure is 0.10 rather than 0.25, the benefit falls to about 155,000 and the proposal is marginal. The board should ask for that work rather than decide on the anecdote.
Against the roasting line. The two are not competing uses of the same money, and presenting them as alternatives misstates the choice. The roasting line creates 1,041,630 of value by adding capacity; the supply arrangement protects the contribution the existing capacity earns. A new line that cannot be fed is worth nothing, which makes the supply arrangement a condition of the investment rather than a rival to it.
What the board should decide. Approve the supplier qualification now, at 60,000, since it is cheap, reversible and improves the negotiating position with the incumbent. Defer the buffer stock, which is the expensive 180,000, until the likelihood has been tested against the delivery and harvest records, and set a trigger: build the stock when the harvest forecast falls below the five-year average. That gives the board most of the protection immediately, at a quarter of the cost, with the rest conditional on evidence.
