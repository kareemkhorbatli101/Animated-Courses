Chapter 17
Revenue, receivables and the quality of earnings
الإيرادات والذمم المدينة وجودة الأرباح
Net income is an opinion. Cash is a fact. Everything between the two is measurement, and measurement requires estimates: how much of this year's sales will be returned, how many of these receivables will never be collected, how long this machine will last, whether this goodwill is still worth what was paid for it. Every one of those estimates was made by the management whose results they determine.
That is not a scandal. It is the price of accrual accounting, which is the only system that can tell you anything useful about a period shorter than the life of the business. But it means the analyst's last question is never what the ratios say. It is how much weight the ratios will bear — whether this year's $2,969,100 is a number that will repeat, and whether the revenue it came from is the kind of revenue that comes back next year.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Part 1 taught the five-step revenue model and the allowance for credit losses. Chapter 13 found an impairment loss of $2,724,000 sitting inside Orontes's operating expenses, and Chapter 4 found sales growing about 15 per cent a year. This chapter asks what those three facts, taken together, say about how much the 2025 profit can be relied on.
17.1  Measuring income: four things that shape the number
Four forces sit between the economics of a year and the profit reported for it, and the exam names all four.
Estimates. Orontes's statements contain an allowance for credit losses of $156,000, a depreciation charge of $2,100,000 that depends on assumed useful lives, a deferred tax liability of $999,625 built on expected future rates, and an impairment of $2,724,000 that rests on a forecast of what a brand and a plant will earn. None of these is a fact and all of them are in net income.
Accounting methods. Within the standards there are choices: first-in, first-out or weighted average for inventory; straight line or an accelerated basis for depreciation; whether a cost is capitalised or expensed. Two identical companies choosing differently report different profits, and the exam tests the direction of the difference rather than the bookkeeping.
Incentives to disclose, or not to. Management is rewarded on reported results, borrows against covenants written on reported ratios, and is judged by analysts' forecasts of reported earnings. Where an estimate has a range, the incentives are not neutral. A company that must keep a debt-to-equity covenant at 1.50 — Barada's situation in Chapter 9 — has a reason to prefer every estimate that lifts equity.
The needs of different users. A lender wants to know whether the interest will be paid and prefers conservatism. A shareholder wants to know what the business will earn and prefers neutrality. A tax authority wants a figure computed under its own rules. The same transaction serves all three badly if it is measured for only one, which is why the standards aim at neutrality rather than at any user's preference.

EXAM TRAP   An estimate is not an error
A change in estimate is accounted for prospectively and never restated. A change in accounting principle is applied retrospectively. An error is corrected by restating the prior periods. Chapter 18 computes the ratio effect of each.
Conservatism is not neutrality. A persistently conservative estimate understates income now and overstates it later, and the exam treats a reversal of an over-provision as a quality problem too.
A disclosed estimate is better than an undisclosed one whatever its value. The note matters more than the number.

17.2  The source, the stability and the trend of revenue
Revenue is the top line and the most examined number in the statements, because every other line depends on it and because it is the easiest to flatter. Three questions settle its quality.
Where does it come from? Revenue from many customers in several markets is worth more than the same amount from one customer, because the loss of one customer is survivable. Revenue from a long-term contract is worth more than revenue from a spot sale. Revenue earned as principal is a different thing from a commission earned as agent, even where the amount is identical.
How stable is it? Recurring revenue — subscriptions, maintenance, consumables — repeats without being won again. One-off revenue does not. A company reporting a record year on a single large order has a problem next year that its income statement does not show.
Which way is it moving, and how? A rising trend built on volume is worth more than the same trend built on price, and both are worth more than one built on terms — longer credit, generous return rights, discounts to pull next quarter's orders into this one.
For Orontes the trend is clear and the quality of it is not flattering. Sales rose from $32,000,000 to $36,400,000 to $42,000,000, and the gross margin fell from 37.0 to 36.0 to 35.0 per cent. Chapter 13 valued the margin given away at $840,000 a year on current sales. Growth bought with price is real growth and it is the least durable kind, because the price concession stays when the volume goes.

EXAM TRAP   Gross or net, principal or agent
A company acting as agent reports only its commission. Consider a business that bills customers $10,000,000 and keeps $1,000,000, passing the rest to a supplier. As principal it reports revenue of $10,000,000 at a 10 per cent margin; as agent it reports revenue of $1,000,000 at a 100 per cent margin. Net income is identical and every revenue-based ratio differs by a factor of ten.
Revenue growth from an acquisition is not organic growth. The exam expects the distinction to be stated.
A record revenue figure says nothing about quality on its own. The segment note, the margin and the receivables say the rest.

17.3  Revenue against receivables, revenue against inventory
This is the most practical idea in the chapter and the one most often tested with numbers. Revenue, receivables and inventory are mechanically linked: a genuine sale creates a receivable that turns into cash, and it removes inventory. So the three should grow at broadly the same rate, and when they do not, the divergence is the finding.
Receivables growing faster than revenue means one of three things: the company has extended its credit terms, its customers have slowed down, or some of the revenue was never going to be collected. All three reduce the quality of the reported revenue, and all three show up in days sales outstanding, which Chapter 12 computed.
Inventory growing faster than revenue means the company has built stock that it has not yet sold — because it expects demand that has not arrived, or because demand has already fallen. Either way, next year carries a risk of a write-down, which passes through cost of sales and hits the gross margin.
Orontes passes this test. Sales rose 15.38 per cent; accounts receivable rose $384,000 on an opening $3,760,000, which is 384,000 ÷ 3,760,000 = 10.21 per cent; and inventory rose $150,000 on an opening $2,300,000, or 150,000 ÷ 2,300,000 = 6.52 per cent. Both balances grew more slowly than sales, which is the healthy direction and means the company collected and sold faster in 2025 than in 2024.

WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: reading the allowance against the receivable
Step 1. The gross receivable. Orontes's receivables are stated net of an allowance of $156,000 in 2025 and $140,000 in 2024, so the gross figures are 4,144,000 + 156,000 = 4,300,000 and 3,760,000 + 140,000 = 3,900,000.
Step 2. The allowance as a proportion. 156,000 ÷ 4,300,000 = 3.63 per cent in 2025, against 140,000 ÷ 3,900,000 = 3.59 per cent in 2024.
Step 3. Read it. The allowance has been held at about the same proportion of the book while the book grew, which is consistent and is what a reader hopes to see.
Check. The alternative would have been the finding. An allowance falling as a proportion of a rapidly growing receivable book is one of the classic ways to lift reported profit: the credit loss expense is lower, net income is higher, and nothing has to be invented. Orontes's credit loss expense of $63,000 against a growing book is modest but the allowance ratio held, so the two facts are consistent and there is no finding here.

17.4  When the recognition or the measurement changes
A change in how revenue is recognised or measured changes the top line without anything happening in the business, and the exam asks for the effect on revenue, on profit and on the ratios.
Three kinds of change come up.
A change of timing. Recognising at delivery rather than at shipment, or over the life of a contract rather than at its start, moves revenue between periods. The year of the change gains or loses; the total over the life of the contract is the same.
A change in the measurement of variable consideration. Revenue is recognised net of expected returns, rebates, discounts and penalties. Raising the estimate of returns reduces revenue now.
A change in the principal-or-agent conclusion. This changes the gross amount with no effect on profit at all, as section 17.2 showed, and it is the change with the largest effect on every ratio.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: a bigger returns provision
Step 1. The change. Suppose Orontes concludes that expected returns on its 2025 sales are $200,000 higher than it first estimated. Revenue is recognised net of expected returns, so revenue falls to 42,000,000 − 200,000 = 41,800,000.
Step 2. The profit effect. The gross profit falls by the same $200,000 less the cost of the goods coming back, and at the company's 25 per cent effective tax rate the after-tax effect is, approximately, 200,000 × 0.75 = 150,000, so net income falls to 2,969,100 − 150,000 = 2,819,100.
Step 3. The ratios. The net margin becomes 2,819,100 ÷ 41,800,000 = 6.74 per cent against 7.07 per cent; the asset turnover falls because sales fell; the return on assets falls because profit fell more in proportion. Earnings per share falls to about 0.3480 and the price/earnings ratio rises at an unchanged price.
Check. One estimate, revised by less than half of one per cent of sales, moves the net margin by a third of a point and every return with it. That is the sensitivity the phrase earnings quality is about, and it is why a reader goes to the note on estimates before computing a single ratio.

EXAM TRAP   The direction of a contract liability
Cash received before performance is a contract liability, not revenue. Orontes's contract liabilities rose from $120,000 to $180,000, which means it holds more customer money against goods not yet delivered — revenue deferred into 2026, and a small sign of quality rather than a problem.
A change in revenue recognition policy is a change in accounting principle and is applied retrospectively. A change in an estimate of returns is prospective.
Where the question gives a tax rate, state the profit effect after tax. Where it does not, state it before tax and say so.

17.5  Earnings quality: the determinants and the indicators
Earnings quality is how well this year's reported profit predicts next year's cash. High-quality earnings are persistent, backed by cash, and produced by the business the company says it is in. Low-quality earnings are none of those things, and the accounts are not wrong in either case.
The determinants — what makes earnings high or low quality — are three.
Persistence. Does the item repeat? A sale repeats; a gain on the sale of equipment does not. Orontes's net income contains $3,000 of gain on disposal and $177,000 of equity-method income, neither of which comes from selling food.
Cash backing. Does the profit turn into cash? Orontes's cash from operations is $7,531,100 against net income of $2,969,100, which is 7,531,100 ÷ 2,969,100 = 2.54 times. A ratio above one is the healthy direction, and 2.54 is strong — although Chapter 13 noted that $4,824,000 of the gap is simply depreciation and the impairment, so the figure flatters the company a little.
Freedom from manipulation. How much of the profit rests on estimates that management chose? The impairment of $2,724,000 is the largest discretionary item in Orontes's 2025 statements, and it works in the unusual direction: it reduced this year's profit, which lowers the base for every future comparison.
The indicators — what a reader looks at — are the practical half of the outcome.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Quality"
English quality in earnings quality does not mean how large the earnings are or how good the company is. It means how far the reported figure can be relied on to repeat and to turn into cash. A company with low earnings of high quality is a better investment than one with high earnings of low quality, and candidates who read the word as a synonym for size answer the question backwards. Arabic جودة carries the same sense of fitness for purpose, and French qualité des résultats is the settled term. Note the near-neighbour: in English, earnings management means the deliberate shaping of reported profit within the rules, and is not a compliment; gestion des résultats in French is exactly as neutral in form and as pointed in use.

Chapter summary
Figure F517-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F517-08. Chapter 17 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Four forces shape a reported profit — estimates, method choices, the incentives of the people who disclose, and the different needs of lenders, owners and tax authorities — and in Orontes's case the estimates include a $2,724,000 impairment built on a forecast. Revenue is judged by its source, its stability and its trend, and Orontes's trend is growth bought with two points of gross margin, which is the least durable kind. Revenue, receivables and inventory should move together: Orontes's receivables grew 10.21 per cent and its inventory 6.52 per cent against sales growth of 15.38, which is the healthy direction, while Barada's grew 38 and 41 per cent against 11 and produced the overdraft. A change in recognition timing moves revenue between periods, a change in the returns estimate moves it now — $200,000 of extra returns takes the net margin from 7.07 to 6.74 per cent — and a reclassification from principal to agent cuts revenue without touching profit. And earnings quality is persistence, cash backing and freedom from manipulation: Orontes scores 2.54 times on cash backing and holds its allowance ratio at about 3.6 per cent, carries only $180,000 of one-off income in $2,969,100 of profit, and fails on the one indicator that matters most.

Case-style set: the analyst's note on quality
Verdict. Orontes's 2025 earnings are of good quality in everything except their source, and the exception is the one that matters for a forecast.
Three indicators support the first half. Cash from operations of $7,531,100 covers net income of $2,969,100 2.54 times, so the profit is arriving in cash. Receivables grew 10.21 per cent and inventory 6.52 per cent against sales growth of 15.38 per cent, so the growth is not sitting in working capital. And the allowance for credit losses was held at 3.63 per cent of the gross book against 3.59 per cent, so the receivable has not been flattered by a thinner provision.
The exception is the trend. A gross margin of 35.0 per cent against 37.0 two years ago means the growth was bought with price, and a price concession is permanent in a way a volume gain is not. Earnings that grow while the margin falls are earnings with a limit on them.
The adjustment I would make is to remove the impairment loss of $2,724,000 from the earnings used in a valuation, because it is a non-recurring charge and a multiple applied to a year containing it understates the business. I would also strip the $177,000 of equity-method income and the $3,000 disposal gain, which together are small but do not come from making food. The adjusted figure is the one to capitalise; the reported one is the one that happened.
