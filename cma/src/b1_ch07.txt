Inventory I: Goods, Costs and Cost Flows
المخزون (1): البضاعة والتكاليف وتدفق التكلفة
Inventory is often the largest current asset of a manufacturer or a retailer. It is also the link between the balance sheet and the income statement: every unit is either still in inventory at the end of the year or part of cost of goods sold. In this chapter you learn which goods and which costs go into inventory, how costs flow out to cost of goods sold, and what happens when the count is wrong.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
You already know periodic and perpetual systems, FIFO and the weighted average from your studies in Arabic or French. Two things may be new.
LIFO. IFRS does not allow LIFO, so many readers in the Middle East and North Africa have never used it. U.S. GAAP allows it, and the CMA exam tests it often.
The U.S. words. The exam says inventory, never stock, and it uses the U.S. shipping terms FOB shipping point and FOB destination.
7.1  Which goods belong in inventory?
Inventory means goods held for sale in the ordinary course of business, goods in production, and materials that will be used in production. A retailer such as Barada Wholesale, Inc. has one inventory account. A manufacturer such as Orontes has three: raw materials, work in process and finished goods.
The basic rule is simple: include the goods that the company controls on the balance sheet date, wherever they are. Control usually follows legal title. Three situations need care.
Goods in transit. The shipping term decides who controls goods that are on a truck or a ship at year-end. Under FOB shipping point, control passes to the buyer when the goods leave the seller, so the buyer includes them. Under FOB destination, the seller keeps control until the goods arrive, so the seller includes them.
Consignment. In a consignment, the consignor (the owner) gives goods to a consignee, who sells them for a commission. The goods stay in the consignor's inventory. The consignee never includes them, even though they are in its shop.
Goods held for others. Goods in the warehouse that belong to another company must be taken out of the physical count. Goods the company owns that are held somewhere else must be added.
Figure F07-02 turns these rules into three questions. Use it for every year-end item in an exam question.
Figure F07-02. Decision tree: should goods be included in inventory at year-end?.
Two special cases appear in later chapters. When customers have a right of return, the seller records revenue net of expected returns and a separate asset for the goods it expects to get back (Chapter 11). In a bill-and-hold sale, the seller keeps the goods in its inventory unless strict conditions show that the buyer already controls them.
EXAM TRAP   The FOB flip
FOB shipping point: the BUYER includes goods in transit. FOB destination: the SELLER includes them. Many candidates reverse these.
The consignee never includes consigned goods, even when they are on its own shelves.
Read the question: is the company the buyer or the seller, the consignor or the consignee?
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   FOB is not the Incoterm you know
In international trade, FOB (free on board) is an Incoterm about loading goods onto a ship. In U.S. exam questions, FOB shipping point and FOB destination are simple shipping terms that decide who controls goods in transit, by truck, rail or sea. Do not bring Incoterm rules into these questions.

7.2  Which costs belong in inventory?
Inventory is recorded at cost. Cost means all the costs needed to bring the goods to their present condition and location. These are inventoriable costs: they stay in inventory as an asset, and they become cost of goods sold when the goods are sold. Other costs are period costs: they are expenses in the period in which they occur.
For goods that are bought, cost includes the purchase price, freight-in, import duties, non-refundable taxes and handling, less trade discounts and any purchase discount taken for early payment. For goods that are made, cost also includes direct labor and production overhead.
Fixed production overhead needs care. It is allocated to units on the basis of normal capacity: the output the plant expects on average over several periods. If output is unusually low, the fixed overhead that is not allocated is an expense of the period. It is not added to inventory. In the same way, abnormal waste (unusual spoilage, idle time or extra freight) is an expense, not inventory cost.
Period costs include freight-out to customers, sales commissions, advertising, general and administrative costs, and the storage of finished goods. Interest on inventory that is produced routinely is also a period cost. Figure F07-03 lists both groups.

7.3  Cost flow assumptions
Unit costs change during the year. When a company sells some units and keeps others, it must decide which costs go to cost of goods sold and which stay in ending inventory. This decision is a cost flow assumption. It does not need to match the physical flow of the goods.
Every method starts from the same total, the cost of goods available for sale:
Beginning inventory + Purchases = Goods available for sale = Cost of goods sold + Ending inventory
Specific identification tracks the actual cost of each item. It suits unique, expensive items, such as cars or jewelry.
FIFO (first-in, first-out) sends the oldest costs to cost of goods sold. Ending inventory holds the newest costs.
LIFO (last-in, first-out) sends the newest costs to cost of goods sold. Ending inventory holds the oldest costs.
Weighted-average cost divides the cost of goods available by the units available and uses this one unit cost for both cost of goods sold and ending inventory.

Periodic and perpetual systems
In a periodic inventory system, the company counts the goods at year-end and calculates cost of goods sold for the whole period. In a perpetual inventory system, it updates inventory and cost of goods sold after every purchase and every sale. The average method in a perpetual system is the moving average: the company calculates a new average after each purchase.
FIFO gives the same result in both systems, because the oldest units are always sold first. LIFO and the average method do not: under a perpetual system, each sale can only use the costs that exist on the date of the sale. Figure F07-05 shows the Orontes results. The sales took place in April, August and November.

7.4  Effects on income, taxes and assets
When prices are rising, the methods give very different results. FIFO puts the old, cheaper costs into cost of goods sold. So FIFO gives the lowest cost of goods sold, the highest gross profit, the highest income tax and the highest ending inventory. LIFO gives the opposite. The weighted average falls in between. When prices are falling, all these relationships reverse. Figure F07-07 shows the Orontes numbers.
Figure F07-07. Effects of FIFO, weighted average and LIFO when prices are rising.
Each method has a strength. LIFO matches current costs with current revenues, so its income is closer to current economic reality, but its balance sheet shows old costs. FIFO shows ending inventory close to current cost, but its income includes gains from holding inventory while prices rise. Chapter 8 compares the methods in more detail and asks you to recommend one.
The LIFO reserve
Companies that use LIFO must help readers compare them with FIFO companies. The LIFO reserve is the difference between FIFO (or current cost) inventory and LIFO inventory. For Orontes, the LIFO reserve at the end of 2025 is $100,000 − $84,000 = $16,000.
An analyst can convert LIFO results to FIFO with two relationships. FIFO inventory equals LIFO inventory plus the LIFO reserve. FIFO cost of goods sold equals LIFO cost of goods sold minus the increase in the LIFO reserve during the year. Orontes started the year with no reserve, so the increase is $16,000, and FIFO cost of goods sold is $288,000 − $16,000 = $272,000. At an illustrative tax rate of 25%, LIFO saved $4,000 of tax this year.
Two more LIFO rules
LIFO liquidation. If a LIFO company sells more units than it buys, old low-cost layers flow into cost of goods sold. Income rises for one year. This is not a lasting improvement, and the company should disclose it if it is material.
LIFO conformity rule. This is a U.S. tax rule. A company that uses LIFO for its tax return must also use LIFO in its financial statements. This is why U.S. companies that want the tax benefit of LIFO also report lower LIFO profits to investors.
EXAM TRAP   Signs and directions
To get FIFO cost of goods sold, SUBTRACT the increase in the LIFO reserve from LIFO cost of goods sold.
In rising prices, LIFO gives LOWER income and LOWER taxes, not higher.
A LIFO liquidation raises income once. Do not treat it as a better business.
Periodic and perpetual LIFO give different answers. FIFO does not.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Reserve does not mean reserve
The LIFO reserve is not an equity reserve (احتياطي in the equity section, or réserve in French accounts). It is only the difference between two inventory amounts. It is sometimes called the LIFO allowance or revaluation to LIFO.

7.5  Inventory errors
Cost of goods sold depends on the count: beginning inventory plus purchases minus ending inventory. If ending inventory is wrong, cost of goods sold is wrong in the same year. And because this year's ending inventory is next year's beginning inventory, the error moves into the next year too.
Look at Figure F07-08. At the end of 2025, Orontes counted some goods twice, so ending inventory was overstated by 40 (USD 000). Cost of goods sold was understated by 40, so pretax income was overstated by 40. In 2026, beginning inventory was too high, so cost of goods sold was overstated and pretax income was understated by 40.
Figure F07-08. An overstated ending inventory: income is overstated in year 1 and understated in year 2.
Over the two years, the two errors cancel. Retained earnings are overstated at the end of 2025 but correct at the end of 2026. This is a counterbalancing error. Remember the tax effect: at a 25% tax rate, net income was overstated by only 30 in the first year.

Chapter summary
Figure F07-10 brings the chapter together.
Figure F07-10. Chapter 7 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Inventory includes the goods a company controls at year-end, wherever they are. Its cost includes everything needed to bring the goods to their present condition and location, but not abnormal, selling or administrative costs. A cost flow assumption splits the cost of goods available between cost of goods sold and ending inventory. In rising prices, FIFO gives higher income, taxes and inventory, and LIFO gives lower ones; the LIFO reserve connects the two. An error in ending inventory reverses in the next year, so retained earnings are correct after two years.

Written task: model answer
Prices rose during 2025, so LIFO gives lower income taxes. LIFO puts the newest, most expensive costs into cost of goods sold, which lowers gross profit and taxable income; for olive oil, LIFO saves $4,000 compared with FIFO. However, the LIFO conformity rule means that if Orontes uses LIFO on its U.S. tax return, it must also use LIFO in its financial statements, so it will report lower profits to investors too. Ending inventory will also show older, lower costs. Orontes should disclose the LIFO reserve ($16,000) so that readers can compare it with FIFO companies.
A good answer: says LIFO gives lower taxes when prices rise; explains the link through cost of goods sold and taxable income; explains the conformity rule correctly; mentions the LIFO reserve for comparison; uses the Orontes figures (bonus).
