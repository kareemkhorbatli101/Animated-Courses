Resolving a dilemma, and the fraud triangle

Chapter 13 said what the obligations are. This chapter is about the harder question: what to do when meeting them puts you in conflict with the person who decides your career.
It covers four things. What an ethical dilemma actually is, and the process the Statement sets out for resolving one. The particular family of dilemmas a management accountant meets most — pressure to manipulate a forecast, an analysis, a result or a budget — and how to propose a resolution. The fraud triangle, which explains why ordinary people commit fraud. And how to use that model to identify and manage the risk of fraud, which is where the ethics of this chapter rejoins the risk management of the first half of the book.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 13 gave the four standards and showed three engaged at once by a single request. Chapter 12 catalogued the frauds and noted that nobody is asked to commit one on their first day. Book 1's Chapter 17 showed what manipulation looks like from outside, in the quality of reported earnings. This chapter is the view from inside the room.
14.1  What a dilemma is
An ethical dilemma is a situation in which the professional faces a choice between courses of action where at least one obligation cannot be met, or where meeting an obligation carries a personal or organisational cost.
Three things it is not, and distinguishing them saves a great deal of confused writing in an exam.
It is not a disagreement about judgment. A difference of view about the right estimate for a provision, honestly held on both sides, is a technical disagreement, and the resolution is evidence and discussion. It becomes ethical when one party insists on an answer they know the evidence does not support, or when the disagreement is resolved by authority rather than by argument.
It is not a hard decision with an unpleasant outcome. Closing a plant is painful and may be entirely proper; the ethical questions are how the decision is made, who is told what and when, and whether the people affected are treated fairly.
And it is not resolved by the fact that something is legal, customary in the industry, or approved by somebody senior. Chapter 15 develops the first, Chapter 17 the third, and the second was never an argument.
Two features define the real thing.
An obligation is in play. Honesty about a number, completeness in a report, the duty to disclose a conflict, the duty to report a control failure: one of the specific obligations of Chapter 13 applies and somebody is asking for it not to be met.
And there is a cost to meeting it. This is what makes it a dilemma rather than an administrative matter, and it is why the resolution process exists: an obligation that costs nothing needs no process.

14.2  The resolution process
The Statement sets out what a member should do when faced with an ethical issue, and the exam expects the sequence. It is a ladder, climbed one rung at a time.
Start with the organisation's own policy. Many employers have an established policy for resolving ethical conflicts, and where one exists it should be followed first.
Discuss the issue with the immediate supervisor, unless the supervisor is involved in it. Many situations are resolved here, because they arose from pressure, misunderstanding or haste rather than from intent.
Where the supervisor is involved, or where the matter is not resolved, take it to the next level of management, and continue upward. The Statement contemplates escalation to the audit committee, the board or another appropriate governing body, and contact with levels above the immediate supervisor should normally be initiated only with the supervisor's knowledge, assuming the supervisor is not implicated.
Clarify the issues with an objective adviser. The IMA offers an ethics helpline for confidential discussion of how the Statement applies, and the point of the step is to test your own reading of the situation against somebody who has no interest in the outcome.
Consult your own attorney about your legal obligations and rights. This is a separate step and a real one, because the legal position — on disclosure, on employment, on protection for reporting — differs by jurisdiction and is not something a professional should guess at.
Five practical disciplines belong with the ladder, and an answer that includes them is better than one that recites the steps.
Document contemporaneously. Dates, requests, what was said, what was provided, what was decided. A note written at the time is evidence; a recollection written later is not.
Raise the obligation rather than the accusation. "This treatment is not supported by the standard, and the board pack would need to disclose the basis" is a conversation that can be had; "you are asking me to commit fraud" is not, and it usually ends the possibility of resolution.
Propose a workable alternative. Most requests have a legitimate underlying problem — a covenant, a target, a customer — and a professional who offers a proper way to address it is far more persuasive than one who only refuses.
Separate what you must not do from what you would prefer not to do. You may not sign something false; you may dislike a presentation that is defensible. Spending credibility on the second leaves none for the first.
And do not go outside the organisation except where legally prescribed. The Statement's position is explicit, and Chapter 18's whistleblowing frameworks exist to provide an internal route that works.
Resignation is the last resort, not the first, and it is not in itself a resolution: it removes the professional from the situation and leaves the situation. Where it becomes necessary — because the conduct continues and every internal route is exhausted — the obligations of confidentiality survive it, as Chapter 13 said.

14.3  Manipulation of forecasts, analyses, results and budgets
The second outcome names the family of issues a management accountant meets most often and asks for resolutions to be proposed. The forms are worth naming, because recognising one is half the answer.
Manipulating results. Recognising revenue early — shipping goods before they are wanted, which is channel stuffing; holding a period open past its end; deferring or capitalising costs that should be expensed; releasing a provision because a result is short; delaying a write-down, as Chapter 13's controller was asked to do. The arithmetic is usually small relative to the company and decisive relative to the target: releasing a provision from 240,000 to 90,000 adds 240,000 − 90,000 = 150,000 to profit, which is 150,000 ÷ 4,176,000 = 3.59 per cent of Orontes's operating income — immaterial to an auditor and quite enough to turn a miss into a hit.
Manipulating forecasts. Presenting a forecast the preparer believes to be unachievable because it is what the board wants to hear; or the reverse, building in slack so that the target can be beaten. A manager who adds 8 per cent to a 3,000,000 cost budget has created 3,000,000 × 0.08 = 240,000 of invisible slack, and the company will plan on it, price on it and allocate capital on it.
Manipulating analyses. Choosing the discount rate, the base period, the comparator or the scenario that produces the wanted answer; omitting the sensitivity that would undermine the recommendation; presenting one option with its risks and the preferred one without. Book 3's capital budgeting is particularly exposed, since every appraisal rests on assumptions somebody chose.
Manipulating presentation. The truncated axis, the comparison against a revised target, the favourable variance explained and the unfavourable one attributed to timing, the one-off gain in operating income and the one-off cost below it.
Proposing a resolution, which the outcome requires, follows a pattern.
Identify the legitimate problem behind the request. Channel stuffing is usually a revenue shortfall; budget slack is usually a fear of being held to a forecast made under uncertainty; a released provision is usually a covenant. Addressing the real problem is what makes the refusal constructive.
State the obligation and its consequence precisely, without accusation: what the standard requires, what the disclosure would have to say, and what happens if the treatment is later reversed.
Offer the legitimate alternatives. For a covenant: talk to the lender early, which Chapter 9 recommended as a trigger. For a revenue shortfall: report it with the reasons and the recovery plan. For an unachievable forecast: present it as a range with the assumptions stated, which satisfies Credibility and is also better management information.
Escalate if the request stands, by the ladder of section 14.2.
And document throughout.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the quarter that was 150,000 short
Step 1. The request. With two weeks of the quarter left, the sales director asks for 250,000 of orders due for despatch in the first week of the new quarter to be shipped early, "so the quarter lands."
Step 2. What it does. At a 40 per cent contribution margin the early shipment brings 250,000 × 0.40 = 100,000 of contribution into this quarter and takes it out of the next.
Step 3. Is it a breach? It depends on facts, not on the request. If the customers have ordered the goods and accept delivery, early despatch is a commercial decision and the revenue is properly recognised. If the goods are shipped without agreement, on sale-or-return, or with a side understanding that they may be sent back, it is not revenue at all and the accounts would be misstated.
Step 4. The resolution. Establish which it is, in writing, from the customers' own orders. Where the orders are genuine, do it and disclose the pull-forward in the commentary, because Credibility requires the board to know that the quarter was helped by 100,000 of next quarter's contribution. Where they are not, refuse the accounting treatment, say why, and escalate if it is pressed.
Check. Notice that the ethical answer is not "refuse". It is "find out, then treat it correctly and say what was done". A professional who refuses everything that looks awkward loses the standing needed to refuse the thing that matters.

14.4  The fraud triangle
The third outcome asks for the three components, and the model is the most useful single idea in this half of the book. It holds that fraud requires three conditions together, and that removing any one of them prevents it.
Pressure, also called incentive or motivation. A reason to commit the act. It may be personal — debt, medical costs, gambling, addiction, a lifestyle that cannot be sustained on the salary, a family expectation — or organisational: a target, a bonus threshold, a covenant, a share price, a plant whose closure depends on the numbers, a fear of losing a job. Note that financial statement fraud is almost always organisational pressure and asset misappropriation almost always personal, which is why the two have different warning signs.
Opportunity. A way to do it and a belief that it will not be found. Opportunity is created by weak controls, by the absence of segregation, by access that nobody reviews, by authority that nobody checks, and above all by a person who can both perform and conceal. Of the three sides, this is the one the organisation controls directly, which is why Chapter 7's controls are the practical centre of fraud prevention.
Rationalisation. A way of reconciling the act with one's own self-image, because the great majority of occupational fraud is committed by people who do not think of themselves as criminals. The standard forms are worth knowing, because they appear in interviews and in exam cases: "I am only borrowing it"; "they owe me, after what I have done for them"; "everybody does it"; "the company can afford it"; "I had no choice"; "it is only until the quarter turns"; "it is not really stealing, nobody is hurt".
Two extensions appear in the literature and are worth a line. Some writers add capability — the person must have the position, the knowledge and the nerve to carry it out — making a fraud diamond; and in financial statement fraud, arrogance or entitlement is often added, describing a senior person who believes the rules do not apply to them. The three-sided model is what the exam asks for; the extensions are useful in an answer that has already given it.

14.5  Using the triangle
The fourth outcome asks how a professional uses the model to identify and manage the risk of fraud, and the answer is one action for each side.
Reduce opportunity, which is the practical half. Everything in Chapter 7: segregation of duties, authorisation limits, reconciliations performed by people who do not hold the asset, restricted master file access, mandatory leave and rotation, audit trails that cannot be altered, and review of the exceptions rather than only of the totals. Add to those the specific detective tests that find the frauds of Chapter 12 — duplicate payments, payments just below approval thresholds, new suppliers, credit notes, write-offs, manual journals posted near a period end and reversed after it.
Reduce pressure where it is created by the organisation. This is the part management controls and rarely examines. Targets that cannot be met honestly, bonus structures with cliff edges, a culture in which missing a number is unsurvivable, and a sales commission paid on orders rather than on cash collected: each of these manufactures pressure, and a company that creates the motive and then relies on controls to stop the act has made its own problem. Chapter 17 treats this as a leadership question.
Attack rationalisation, which sounds the softest and is not. A code of conduct that is specific about the acts in question, training that uses real situations rather than slogans, visible and consistent enforcement at every level, a route to raise a concern, and leaders who talk about the awkward cases: all of these make the stories harder to tell oneself. The particular enemy of rationalisation is consistency — the senior person who is treated exactly as a junior one would be, which Chapter 12 noted and Chapter 17 develops.

Four warning signs are worth knowing for the exam, because situations are written around them.
Behavioural: an employee who never takes leave, who resists control changes, who insists on handling one relationship personally, whose lifestyle exceeds their income, or who has an unusually close relationship with a supplier or customer.
Process: reconciliations that are late or performed by the wrong person, a rising volume of manual journals, repeated overrides, missing documentation, and customer or supplier complaints that stop reaching the people who should hear them.
Numerical: margins that move without explanation, round-number entries, payments just below thresholds, credit notes concentrated after period ends, and write-offs concentrated in one person's accounts.
And structural: a dominant individual, a finance function too small for segregation, a long-tenured person in a sensitive role with no cover, and an audit committee that meets rarely.
The professional's own position is the last point and the most personal. The triangle applies to you. Pressure will come from a target or a superior; opportunity exists because you prepare the numbers; and rationalisation is available in the words "it is only a presentation" or "the quarter will correct it". Knowing the model is some protection against being inside it.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Aggressive", "conservative" and "manage"
Three English words do most of the work of disguising manipulation, and a management accountant should recognise all of them. Aggressive accounting sounds energetic and commercially minded; it means treatments at or beyond the edge of what the standards allow. Conservative sounds prudent and careful; applied to a provision taken in a good year to be released in a bad one, it means building a reserve to manage earnings, which is not prudence. And manage, in earnings management, is the most effective of the three, because managing is what managers are supposed to do: the phrase makes the deliberate shaping of a reported result sound like competence. Arabic إدارة الأرباح and French gestion du résultat carry the same flattering implication. The habit worth forming is to replace the adjective with the act: not "we are being aggressive on revenue" but "we are recognising revenue before the customer has agreed to take the goods" — and then see whether the sentence still sounds acceptable.

Chapter summary
Figure F814-04 brings the chapter together.
Figure F814-04. Chapter 14 summary map: the key rule for each section.
An ethical dilemma is a situation where an obligation is in play and meeting it carries a cost; it is not a technical disagreement honestly held, not a hard decision with an unpleasant outcome, and not resolved by legality, custom or seniority. The resolution process is a ladder: the organisation's own policy first, then the immediate supervisor unless the supervisor is involved, then upward to the next level and if necessary the audit committee or board, with an objective adviser such as the IMA helpline to test your reading and your own attorney on your legal position — supported by contemporaneous documentation, by raising the obligation rather than the accusation, by proposing a workable alternative, by separating what you must not do from what you would prefer not to do, and by not going outside the organisation except where legally prescribed; resignation is a last resort that resolves nothing by itself. Manipulation comes as results, forecasts, analyses and presentation — a provision cut from 240,000 to 90,000 adding 240,000 − 90,000 = 150,000, which is 150,000 ÷ 4,176,000 = 3.59 per cent of operating income, and 8 per cent of slack on a 3,000,000 budget hiding 3,000,000 × 0.08 = 240,000 — and the resolution is to find the legitimate problem behind the request, state the obligation precisely, offer a proper alternative, escalate if it stands, and document. The fraud triangle names pressure, opportunity and rationalisation, with capability sometimes added, and it is used by reducing opportunity through the controls of Chapter 7, by removing pressure the organisation itself manufactures, and by attacking rationalisation through a specific code, real training and consistent enforcement — with behavioural, process, numerical and structural warning signs to identify where all three coincide, including in one's own position.

Case-style practice: the last week of the year
Orontes's financial controller is told by the chief executive, in the last week of the financial year, that the interest cover covenant will be missed unless operating income reaches 1,560,000, and that three things should therefore happen: an inventory write-down of 180,000 should be deferred to the new year; the distributor provision should be reduced from 240,000 to 90,000; and 250,000 of January despatches should be brought forward into December. The chief executive adds that the bank "will not look at this closely" and that the adjustments will reverse naturally next year.
Required. Evaluate the three requests, identify the standards and the fraud-triangle elements present, and set out exactly what the controller should do. Under 400 words.

Case-style set: the last week of the year
The three requests, separately. The inventory write-down is required by the technical standards if the stock's net realisable value has fallen, and the timing of the recognition is not discretionary; deferring it misstates the accounts. The provision reduction from 240,000 to 90,000 adds 240,000 − 90,000 = 150,000 to profit, which is 150,000 ÷ 4,176,000 = 3.59 per cent of operating income, and it is improper unless the evidence about the distributor has changed — the request is driven by the covenant, not by the evidence. The December despatches are different in kind: if customers have ordered the goods and will accept them, early shipment is a commercial decision and the 250,000 × 0.40 = 100,000 of contribution is properly recognised; if not, it is not revenue. So two requests are improper as stated and one depends on facts the controller can establish.
The standards engaged. Competence, because duties must be performed in accordance with technical standards and the write-down and provision are governed by them. Credibility, on three obligations: fair and objective communication, disclosure of everything influencing a user's understanding — here the bank and the board — and the reporting of deficiencies. And the principles of honesty and objectivity directly, since the purpose of the adjustments is to produce a covenant outcome rather than a true result.
The fraud triangle. Pressure is explicit and organisational: a covenant floor of 1,560,000 in a week. Opportunity exists because the adjustments are within the controller's own preparation and nobody between them and the accounts would question them. Rationalisation is supplied by the chief executive in two phrases — "the bank will not look closely" and "they will reverse naturally next year". All three sides are present, which is the condition this chapter says to treat as high risk.
What the controller should do. Establish the facts on the despatches, in writing from customer orders, and act on them. Prepare the accounts correctly on the other two items. Put the position to the chief executive in a written note that states the obligations rather than making an accusation, sets out what the disclosure would have to say, and offers the legitimate alternative: approach the lender now, before the test date, with the forecast and a plan, which Chapter 9 identified as the proper trigger. Keep a contemporaneous record of the request and the response. If the instruction stands, escalate to the audit committee chair, with the supervisor's knowledge where that is possible. Consider the IMA helpline to test the reading, and take personal legal advice before any external step. Resignation is not the answer yet; it is what remains if every internal route fails, and it would leave the accounts in the state the controller is trying to prevent.
