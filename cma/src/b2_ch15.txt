WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 14 separated value-added from non-value-added activities. This chapter applies that idea to a whole process, step by step, and puts a price on the change.
15.1  Process analysis
A business process is a chain of steps that turns an input into a result for a customer, such as an order into cash. Process analysis studies each step: what it does, who does it, how long it takes, what it costs and whether it adds value.
The first tool is a process map, a diagram of the steps in order. Figure F215-02 maps Tahini's order-to-cash process. Only two of its eight steps, picking and shipping, are steps the customer values.
Figure F215-02. Process map: Tahini's order-to-cash process today.
The second tool measures each step. The cycle time from order to cash is 9 days, and the staff time is 2.10 hours per order.

15.2  Business process reengineering and its savings
Business process reengineering is a radical redesign of a process, not a small improvement. Instead of typing orders faster, Tahini lets customers order online; instead of preparing invoices by hand, the invoice is created from the shipment record.
Figure F215-04 shows the new process. Two steps disappear, four are shortened, and staff time falls to 0.87 hours per order. Cycle time falls to 4 days, so cash arrives sooner.
Figure F215-04. Process map: the redesigned order-to-cash process.
The savings must be net. Figure F215-05 compares the yearly cost of the two processes, including the cost of correcting faulty orders, and deducts the yearly running cost of the new ordering portal. The net yearly saving is $200,000.

15.5  Benchmarking and competitive advantage
Benchmarking helps create an advantage in four ways, listed in Figure F215-12. It sets targets that others already achieve, brings in ideas from outside, focuses on measures customers feel, and builds a habit of improving.
Figure F215-12. Benefits of benchmarking for competitive advantage
Benefit
What it gives the company
Realistic targets
Targets based on what others already achieve
New ideas
Practices the company would not have thought of
Customer focus
Measures such as cycle time that customers feel
Lasting advantage
A habit of improving faster than rivals
For Tahini, a four-day cycle time is now a selling point: Barada receives its orders faster than from other suppliers, and fewer invoices need correcting.
The advantage lasts only if benchmarking continues. Competitors improve too, so today's best practice becomes tomorrow's normal practice.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Étalonnage, المقارنة المرجعية
French 'étalonnage' also means calibrating an instrument. In management it means benchmarking. Arabic المقارنة المرجعية (or المقارنة بالأفضل) is the management term; avoid المعايرة, which means calibration.

15.6  Extended Example 15A: the business case for the new order-to-cash process
This example builds the three-year business case the division manager signed. It is exam-core.
1. Scenario
Volume: 8,000 orders a year; staff cost $20 an hour; correcting a faulty order costs $50.
Change: one-off cost $150,000; running cost $12,800 a year.
2. Calculations
Old process: staff $336,000 + corrections $20,000 = $356,000 a year.
New process: staff $139,200 + corrections $4,000 = $143,200 a year.
Net yearly saving: $212,800 − $12,800 = $200,000.
3. The three-year view
Figure F215-13 sets out the years. The one-off cost is recovered in 0.75 years, and the net saving over three years is $450,000.

Chapter summary
Figure F215-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F215-14. Chapter 15 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Process analysis maps and measures each step of a process. Reengineering redesigns the process radically, and its net saving is the old cost minus the new cost minus the cost of the change. Best practice analysis adapts what leaders do. Benchmarking compares processes with internal units, competitors, functional leaders or the best organizations anywhere, using the same definitions on both sides, and it creates a lasting advantage only if it continues.

Case question: Levant's receiving dock
Levant Distribution receives about two thousand trucks a year at its main warehouse. Each truck is unloaded, every carton is counted, the goods are inspected, the receipt is typed into the records and the pallets are put away.
Drivers often wait for hours, and suppliers complain. The operations manager, Karim, has studied the process with a stopwatch and found that receiving takes four hours per truck, far more than at the best distributors in the region.
Karim proposes a redesign. Suppliers will send an advance notice of every delivery with barcodes on each pallet. Staff will scan the pallets instead of counting cartons, inspect only a sample, and the records will be created from the notice without typing. The change needs scanners and a small software link. Karim hopes to start the new process in January.
The finance manager, Nadia, must check Karim's figures before the board meeting. She needs the yearly cost of the current process, the net yearly saving and the net saving over three years. She also wants to benchmark the new process against the best-practice distributor, using the same definition of receiving time.
