Chapter 7 covered the types of backup: full, incremental and differential. This chapter asks a different question about the same copies, namely where they are kept and what they are for.
8.1  Why the internet is riskier
A leased line is a connection the company pays for and nobody else uses. The internet is a shared road. The traffic crosses equipment owned by strangers, by a route chosen at the time, and anything on that route can in principle read or alter what passes. Figure F408-02 lists the risks that follow.

The difference is one of exposure rather than of kind. A private line can be tapped, and an insider can read either one. But the internet places the traffic within reach of far more people, none of whom the company has any relationship with.
That is also why the internet is used anyway. A leased line to every Gulf customer would cost more than the business they bring. The practical answer is not to avoid the shared road but to make the traffic unreadable while it is on it.

8.2  Data encryption
Data encryption converts readable data into a form that cannot be understood without a key. Figure F408-03 shows the two families and how they fit together.
Figure F408-03. Symmetric and asymmetric encryption compared.
Symmetric encryption uses one key for both directions. It is fast and suits bulk data, but it leaves a problem. The key itself must reach the other party somehow, and sending it over the same untrusted network defeats the purpose.
Asymmetric encryption solves that. Each party has a pair of keys, and what one encrypts only the other can decrypt. Anyone may hold the public key, so nothing secret has to be exchanged first. Reversing the pair gives a digital signature: encrypting with the private key proves who sent the message, since only that person holds it.
In practice the two are combined. A secure web connection uses asymmetric methods to agree a symmetric key, then uses that faster key for the conversation itself.
WATCH: NOT YET TESTABLE   As of October 2026
The current standard for traffic in transit is TLS, with version 1.2 the minimum accepted. Separately, the United States standards body published its first post-quantum encryption standards in August 2024, anticipating machines that could break today's asymmetric methods. Neither detail is likely to be examined; the principles above are.

8.3  Firewalls
A firewall sits between two networks and allows or blocks traffic according to rules. It is a border post: everything crossing is examined against a list of what is permitted, and whatever is not permitted is refused. Figure F408-04 sets out the types.
Figure F408-04. Types of firewall
Type of firewall (E.2.j)
What it does
Note

Allows or blocks by address and port
Cheap and fast; blind to content

Tracks whole conversations, not single packets

Firewalls have three common uses. They guard the boundary between the company and the internet. They divide the internal network so a compromise in one part does not reach another. And they create a protected zone for servers that must be reachable from outside.
EXAM TRAP   What a firewall does not do
A firewall does not stop phishing, because the user invites the traffic in. It does not stop an insider, who is already inside. And it does not stop malware carried on a device brought into the building. A question offering a firewall as the answer to any of these is offering the wrong answer.
A firewall is a rule set, and a rule set ages. Ports opened for a project that ended, addresses permitted for a supplier no longer used, and exceptions granted in an emergency all accumulate exactly as system access does. The rules need the same periodic review that Chapter 6 required for user rights.

8.4  Why the backup must leave the building
Chapter 7 described the types of backup. The question here is simpler and more often got wrong: where the copies are kept.
Every copy in one building shares one fate. A fire, a flood or a burst pipe destroys the live system and the backup together. The company then discovers it had been taking backups faithfully for years to no purpose. That is the whole argument for a remote site.
The common rule of thumb is three copies, on two kinds of media, with one of them off site. Regularity matters as much as location: a copy taken monthly means losing up to a month of work, which for most businesses is the same as losing everything. And programs must be copied as well as data, because restoring records into a system nobody can rebuild is only half a recovery.

The last component carries the most weight. A plan that has never been tested is a set of assumptions. The assumption that fails is usually a small one: a contact list held only on the system that is down, or a backup nobody has ever tried to restore.
Continuity planning covers more than systems. It asks where staff would work, how customers and suppliers would be told, which premises could be used, and who may authorise spending in the first hours. A company whose systems recover into an unusable building has recovered nothing.

8.6  Hot, warm and cold sites
An alternate site is where processing resumes when the main site cannot be used. Figure F408-07 shows the three kinds.
Figure F408-07. Hot, warm and cold recovery sites.
The choice is an economic one, and two measures decide it. The recovery time objective is the longest the business can be without the system. The recovery point objective is the most data it can afford to lose, measured in time, which decides how often backups must run.
A four-hour recovery time objective needs a hot site, whatever it costs. A tolerance of two weeks makes a cold site perfectly rational. Paying for a hot site where a cold one would do is as much a failure of judgement as the reverse, because the money has to come from somewhere.

8.7  Extended Example 8A: choosing a recovery arrangement for Orontes
This example works through the choice for three Orontes systems and shows why one answer does not fit them all. It is exam-core: questions give a tolerance and ask for the site.
1. The business impact analysis
Three systems matter. The plant control system runs the bottling lines and stops production within minutes of failing. The payables and receivables system can wait a working day without serious harm. The management reporting system can wait two weeks, because the month-end pack can be rebuilt.
2. Setting the objectives
Plant control is given a recovery time objective of four hours and a recovery point objective of fifteen minutes. Payables and receivables get one day and four hours. Management reporting gets two weeks and one day.
3. Choosing the sites
Plant control needs a hot site with near-continuous replication, because four hours allows no time to configure anything. Payables and receivables can use a warm site: the hardware waits, and a day is enough to restore and configure. Management reporting can use a cold site, since two weeks is time to bring equipment in.
4. What the backup schedule must then be
The recovery point objective sets the backup frequency, and this is the connection candidates miss. Fifteen minutes of tolerable loss means replication rather than nightly backups. Four hours means copies several times a day. One day means the nightly backup is enough.
5. The cheapest improvement
Orontes currently holds every copy in the server room beside the live machines. Moving one copy off site costs very little and removes the single risk that would otherwise defeat all three plans at once, which is a fire in that room.

Chapter summary
Figure F408-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F408-09. Chapter 8 summary map: the key rule for each section.
The internet is a shared road, exposing traffic to interception, spoofing, tampering and denial of service, which is why encryption matters far more there than on a private line. Symmetric encryption is fast but must distribute a key; asymmetric encryption avoids that and also gives digital signatures; the two are used together. A firewall filters traffic by rule, but stops neither phishing nor insiders. Backups must be regular, must cover programs as well as data, and must include a copy held away from the site. Business continuity planning keeps the business running and disaster recovery restores the systems, both starting from the business impact analysis. The recovery time objective chooses between a hot, warm and cold site; the recovery point objective chooses the backup frequency.

Case question: the backup in the server room
Orontes keeps every backup copy on a dedicated server standing beside the live machines in the head office server room. Copies are taken nightly. The Gulf expansion means that export orders will arrive over the internet from distributors in Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates, rather than by telephone as now.
