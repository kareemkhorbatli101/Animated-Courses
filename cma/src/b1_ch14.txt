WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
If you learned IFRS 16, you know one lessee model: every lease is like a purchase financed by a loan. U.S. GAAP keeps two models. The balance sheet looks almost the same, but the income statement and the cash flow statement can be very different.
14.1  What is a lease?
A lease is a contract that gives the right to control the use of an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for payment. The lessee uses the asset; the lessor provides it. The asset must be identified, and the supplier must not have a real right to swap it for another.
The lessee controls the asset if it gets almost all of its economic benefits and decides how it is used. The lease term is the period the lessee cannot cancel, plus any renewal periods it is reasonably certain to use.

14.2  Finance or operating? The five criteria
A lessee classifies each lease at the start. It is a finance lease if it meets any one of five criteria; otherwise it is an operating lease. Figure F14-02 lists them. Many companies use about 75% or more of economic life as a major part, and about 90% or more of fair value as substantially all.

14.3  Initial measurement
For both types, the lessee records a lease liability and a right-of-use asset at the start. The liability is the present value of the lease payments not yet paid. The discount rate is the rate implicit in the lease if the lessee can find it; otherwise it is the lessee's incremental borrowing rate.
Lease payments include fixed payments, payments that depend on an index such as CPI, a purchase price the lessee is reasonably certain to pay, and amounts it expects to pay under a residual value guarantee. The right-of-use asset starts at the liability, plus any prepaid payments and initial direct costs, minus incentives received.
Orontes uses 6%, the rate on its bank note from Chapter 1. Figure F14-04 shows the present values: $421,236 for the warehouse and $866,276 for the olive press. The undiscounted payments are higher, because part of each payment is interest.

14.4  Subsequent measurement: two expense patterns
For a finance lease, the lessee records interest on the liability and, separately, amortization of the right-of-use asset, usually straight-line. Interest is highest at the start, when the liability is largest, so total expense falls each year. Figure F14-05 shows this front-loaded pattern for the olive press.

14.5  Presentation
On the balance sheet, the right-of-use assets and lease liabilities of finance leases are shown separately from those of operating leases, or disclosed with the line items that contain them. Each liability is split into a current part, the principal due within a year, and a noncurrent part.
On the income statement, a finance lease shows interest expense and amortization separately; an operating lease shows one lease cost in operating expenses. In the statement of cash flows, finance-lease principal is financing; its interest and all operating-lease payments are operating. New leases are a noncash investing and financing activity, disclosed separately.
Figure F14-08 summarizes the presentation for Orontes at the end of 2026. The olive-press liability of $668,253 includes a current part of $209,905, the principal that will be paid in 2027.

14.6  Short-term leases, sale and leaseback, and IFRS 16
A short-term lease lasts 12 months or less and has no purchase option that the lessee is reasonably certain to use. A lessee may choose, by class of asset, to keep such leases off the balance sheet and expense the payments straight-line. U.S. GAAP has no exemption for low-value assets.
In a sale and leaseback, a company sells an asset and leases it back. It is a sale only if control passes to the buyer under the revenue rules. If the leaseback is a finance lease, or the seller can buy the asset back, there is no sale: the cash received is a loan.
IFRS 16 has one lessee model: every lease is accounted for like a finance lease, with depreciation and interest. It allows an exemption for low-value assets and remeasures the liability when an index such as CPI changes. Figure F14-09 lists the main differences.

Chapter summary
Figure F14-10 brings the chapter together.
Figure F14-10. Chapter 14 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A lease is the right to control an identified asset for a period. A lessee classifies a lease as finance if it meets any of five criteria, and as operating otherwise. Both types start with a lease liability at the present value of the payments and a right-of-use asset based on it. Finance leases show interest and amortization, so expense is higher early; operating leases show one straight-line cost. Finance-lease principal is a financing cash flow; everything else is operating. Leases of 12 months or less may stay off the balance sheet, and U.S. GAAP has no low-value exemption.

It is therefore an operating lease. Barada still records a lease liability and a right-of-use asset at the present value of the payments, 77,313.
