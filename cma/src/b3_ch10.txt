Book 1 built these three statements from accounting records. Here they are built from budgets, before the year begins. Chapter 1 set the strategic objectives the plan must meet.
10.1  Purpose and relationships
Pro forma financial statements show what the financial statements would look like if the plan happens. The income statement shows planned profit. The pro forma balance sheet shows what the division will own and owe, and the pro forma statement of cash flows shows where cash will come from and go.
All three come from the same budgets, so they must agree with one another. Figure F310-02 shows the links. The operating budgets give profit, the cash budget gives cash, and the capital budget gives the new asset.
Figure F310-02. How the budgets feed the three pro forma statements.
The statements are also a last check on the plan. If the balance sheet does not balance, or the cash flow statement does not end at the cash in the cash budget, a schedule somewhere is wrong.

EXAM TRAP   Fixed costs stay fixed
In a scenario, fixed costs do not change with volume and the variable cost per unit does not change unless the question says so. Changing both at once is the commonest error in sensitivity questions.

10.6  Extended Example 10A: looking beyond next year
The board also asks what cash the division will need up to 2032, the end of the strategic plan. Medium- and long-term forecasts are built differently from the quarterly cash budget. This example is exam-core.
1. What changes with the horizon
A short-term cash budget works from schedules of known orders and invoices. A medium-term forecast works from the strategic plan: planned growth, the investment it needs, and the working capital that grows with sales. Figure F310-12 lists the factors it must consider.
Figure F310-12. Extended Example: factors in a medium- or long-term cash forecast
Factor in a medium- or long-term cash forecast (B.6.e)
At Orontes

When the plant reaches its limit and what the next line would cost

Receivables, inventory and payables grow with sales
Financing and repayment
Loan repayments, interest rates and the dividend policy
Economy and market
Growth, inflation, exchange rates and competitors (Chapters 2–3)
Uncertainty
Scenarios rather than one forecast; the longer the horizon, the wider the range
2. The Beverages picture
Growth: the juice market is growing (Chapter 2), so sales and working capital will keep rising.
Capacity: the plant will reach its limit within a few years, as Chapter 6 showed for the third quarter.
Cash: operations generate $3,702,450 a year at present volumes, which funds the current plan but not a second line.
3. The answer
The division tells the board that it can fund 2028 from its own cash, but that a second filling line would need outside financing or a lower dividend. The longer the horizon, the wider the range, so the forecast is given as scenarios rather than one figure.

Chapter summary
Figure F310-13 brings the chapter together, and closes Part II.
Figure F310-13. Chapter 10 summary map: the key rule for each section.
The three pro forma statements translate the budgets into the financial statements the plan would produce, and must agree with each other. The income statement is prepared under several assumptions, because one plan hides the risks. The plan is then tested against the strategic objectives. Every balance-sheet line comes from a schedule, the cash flow statement ends at the cash budget's closing balance, and the statements together show the outside financing required and what the dividend policy leaves in the business.

Case question: the tahini pro forma statements
The Tahini & Spreads division has finished its operating and cash budgets for 2028 and must now present pro forma statements to the Orontes board. Its budgeted operating income for the year is known, and the division pays interest on a loan taken out to build its Gulf warehouse. The warehouse was finished last year, so no further capital spending is planned for 2028.
Group policy sets the income tax rate and a dividend payout rate that every division applies to its net income. Dividends for a year are declared in December and paid to head office early in the following year, so they appear as a liability at the year end.
The division's controller, Rana, must calculate net income, the dividend, the retained earnings that will appear on the pro forma balance sheet, and the division's return on investment measured on average total assets. The board will compare that return with the group objective of 11.8% for 2028.
Samir, the division manager, also asks what would happen to net income if sales volume fell by five per cent, and whether the division would then need to borrow more from head office.
