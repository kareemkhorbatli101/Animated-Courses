Chapter 12
Target pricing, target costing and value engineering
التسعير المستهدف والتكلفة المستهدفة وهندسة القيمة
Cost-plus pricing asks what the company needs and hopes the customer agrees. Target costing asks what the customer will pay and then requires the company to fit inside it. The first is a calculation; the second is a discipline, and it is the one that works in a market where the price is not the company's to choose.
Orontes is about to design a squeeze bottle of tahini sauce for restaurants and canteens. The food-service buyers it has spoken to will pay $6.40 a bottle and not a cent more, because that is what the alternatives cost them. The design engineers' first estimate is $6.70 a bottle. This chapter is about what happens next.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 10 set out three pricing approaches and said that the sequence a well-run company follows is to establish what the market will pay, subtract the profit required, and manage the cost down to what is left. Chapter 11 explained why the market's price is not usually negotiable for a product with close substitutes. This chapter is that sequence, in full.
12.1  Finding out what the customer values
Before a target price can be set, somebody has to know what the customer is buying. The outcome names the techniques, and they divide into three families.
Asking customers directly. Surveys, interviews and focus groups establish what features matter and what the product is compared with. Conjoint analysis is the formal version: customers choose between bundles of features at different prices, and the analysis infers what each feature is worth to them. It is the most reliable method and the most expensive.
Watching what customers do. Test markets, trial pricing in one region, and analysis of how sales respond to past price changes — which is the elasticity of Chapter 11, estimated from the company's own history rather than assumed.
Studying competitors and the alternatives. What rivals charge, what features they include, what a customer would do if neither product existed. Reverse engineering a competitor's product to establish what it must cost to make is standard practice in manufacturing and tells a company both what its rival's margin is and what is technically possible.
For the squeeze bottle, the answer came from the third family and part of the second. Food-service buyers compare a bottle of tahini sauce with the cost of making the sauce themselves from bulk paste, and with two competing branded bottles at $6.50 and $6.60. A new entrant with no reputation must price below both, and the buyers interviewed were clear that $6.40 is the figure at which they would switch.
That is the target price, and it is now a constraint rather than an outcome.

12.2  Target pricing and target costing
Target costing reverses the cost-plus sequence, and the outcome asks for the steps. There are five.
Determine the target price. What the market will pay, from the techniques of section 12.1, for a product with the features customers want.
Determine the target operating income a unit. What the company requires, usually derived from a return on the capital the product will employ, which section 12.3 computes.
Subtract to find the target cost. Target price less target operating income is the cost the product must not exceed.
Compare with the estimated cost of the product as designed. The difference is the cost gap.
Close the gap before the product is launched, by value engineering, and by redesigning the product, the process and the supply chain until the estimated cost falls to the target.
Two features of that sequence distinguish it from everything in Chapter 10.
The cost is an output, not an input. In cost-plus the cost determines the price; here the price determines the cost that is permitted.
It happens before production starts. By the time a product is in production, most of its cost has been committed by its design — the materials chosen, the number of parts, the tolerances, the process required. Estimates vary, and the usual figure quoted is that design locks in about eighty per cent of a product's lifetime cost while accounting for a small fraction of the spending. Target costing is therefore a design activity, and it is run by a cross-functional team of engineers, buyers, production staff, marketers and accountants, because no one of those functions can close a cost gap alone.
Where the gap cannot be closed, the discipline requires the project to be abandoned. That is the hardest part of the method and the part that makes it work: a company that launches anyway has merely used an elaborate process to arrive at a price it could not afford.

12.3  The two target calculations
The outcome asks for two figures, and the arithmetic is short.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the target cost of a squeeze bottle
Step 1. The target price. $6.40 a bottle, from section 12.1.
Step 2. The capital and the required return. The line would employ $3,000,000 of capital — the filling equipment, the inventory and the receivables — and the board requires 18 per cent on new product capital, a premium over the company's 9.04 per cent cost of capital that reflects the risk of a new line.
Step 3. The target operating income. In total: 3,000,000 × 0.18 = 540,000 a year. A unit, on a forecast of 1,200,000 bottles: 540,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 0.45 a bottle.
Step 4. The target cost. 6.40 − 0.45 = 5.95 a bottle.
Step 5. The cost gap. The engineers' first estimate is $6.70, so the gap is 6.70 − 5.95 = 0.75 a bottle, which on 1,200,000 bottles is 1,200,000 × 0.75 = 900,000 a year.
Check. The $900,000 is the number that makes the method work. A gap stated as 75 cents a bottle sounds like a detail for the purchasing department; stated as $900,000 a year against a target profit of $540,000, it is plainly a question of whether the product should exist. Scaling a unit gap to the annual total is the first thing to do with it.
Two refinements appear in harder questions.
The target operating income may be given directly as a margin — "the company requires an operating margin of 8 per cent" — in which case the target income a unit is simply the price times that margin: 6.40 × 0.08 = 0.512.
The target cost may be expressed over the product's life rather than a year, including the costs of design, launch, support and disposal. That is life-cycle costing, and it matters most where those costs are large relative to manufacturing — software, pharmaceuticals, aircraft.

12.4  Value engineering
Value engineering is the systematic examination of a product and its production process to find ways of delivering the functions the customer values at a lower cost. The emphasis on function is what distinguishes it from cost cutting: the question is not "what can we remove" but "what does this part do, and is there a cheaper way of doing it".
The method has four moves, and a question will usually need at least two of them.
Simplify the design. Fewer parts, fewer fasteners, fewer operations, common components shared with other products, a tolerance relaxed where precision does nothing for the customer.
Substitute materials or processes. A different polymer, a moulded part instead of an assembled one, a supplier's standard component instead of a bespoke one.
Redesign the process. Fewer handling steps, a shorter route through the factory, a change in batch size, a machine moved.
Work with suppliers. Open-book costing, joint design, longer contracts in exchange for lower prices, and moving a component's design to the supplier who makes it.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: closing the 75-cent gap
Step 1. The starting cost. $6.70 a bottle, made up of a nozzle at 0.80, the bottle at 1.10, the label at 0.20, filling labour at 0.60, the sauce itself at 3.00, and allocated overhead at 1.00.
Step 2. The nozzle. The first design uses a bespoke three-part nozzle. The supplier's standard two-part nozzle does the same job: 0.80 − 0.55 = 0.25 saved.
Step 3. The bottle and the label. A standard bottle in place of a custom shape saves 1.10 − 0.95 = 0.15, and printing the label directly on the bottle saves 0.20 − 0.12 = 0.08.
Step 4. The process. Filling the standard bottle runs faster and needs no change of tooling between batches: 0.60 − 0.45 = 0.15 saved.
Step 5. The overhead. Fewer parts and one less handling step reduce the activities that drive the overhead, taking the allocation from 1.00 to 0.88, a saving of 0.12.
Step 6. The total. 0.25 + 0.15 + 0.08 + 0.15 = 0.63 from the first four, and with the overhead 0.63 + 0.12 = 0.75 — exactly the gap. The product can be made for $5.95 and should go ahead.
Check. Note what was not done. The sauce itself, at $3.00, was left alone, because it is the thing the customer is buying: reducing the sesame content would close the gap faster and destroy the product. Value engineering protects the functions the customer values and attacks everything else, and an answer that proposes diluting the product has missed the whole idea.

12.5  Value-added and nonvalue-added costs
The last outcome of this chapter supplies the vocabulary that value engineering uses to decide where to look.
A value-added cost is one the customer would be willing to pay for if they understood what it was: the sesame in the sauce, the filling of the bottle, the moulding of the nozzle, the design that makes the bottle easy to pour. Remove it and the product is worth less.
A nonvalue-added cost is one that adds nothing the customer values: moving material between stores, waiting between operations, inspecting work that should have been right, reworking what was wrong, counting inventory, expediting a late order, storing finished goods nobody has ordered.
The test is the customer's willingness, not the company's necessity. Some nonvalue-added activities are unavoidable — a company must count its inventory and inspect its output — and calling them nonvalue-added is not a criticism of the people who do them. It is a statement about where the cost ought to be attacked first, because reducing a nonvalue-added cost takes nothing away from the product.
The classic framework applies five categories to manufacturing time, and the exam uses it.
Processing time adds value. Inspection, moving, waiting and storing do not. The ratio of processing time to total time is the manufacturing cycle efficiency, and in most factories it is startlingly low — a part that takes four hours to process may spend three weeks in the building.

Three uses of the distinction close the chapter.
It directs the cost gap work. Attack the nonvalue-added costs first, because they can be removed without touching the product.
It supports the just-in-time argument of Book 2's Chapter 17 with a cost language: a buffer of work in progress is storage, moving and waiting, all nonvalue-added.
And it is a reporting idea as well as a design one. A cost report that splits each department's spending into value-added and nonvalue-added activity tells a manager something no conventional variance report does, which is why it belongs in the same examination as the performance measures of Part 1.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Target"
English target in this chapter is a constraint rather than an aspiration, and that is the opposite of its everyday sense. A sales target is something to aim at and perhaps miss; a target cost is a ceiling the product must come in under or not be made at all. The same word does both jobs in the same syllabus, and the difference is the whole discipline of the method. Arabic مستهدف carries the aspirational sense most naturally, and the constraint sense has to be read from the context. French cible is used for both — prix cible, coût cible — with the same ambiguity, and French practice adds à ne pas dépasser when the constraint is meant. In an answer, say which it is: "a target cost of $5.95, which the design must not exceed".

Chapter summary
Figure F712-07 brings the chapter together.
Figure F712-07. Chapter 12 summary map: the key rule for each section.
What a customer values is established by asking them, by watching what they do, and by studying competitors and alternatives — which for the squeeze bottle gives a target price of $6.40 set by two rival bottles and the option of making the sauce in house. Target costing then reverses the cost-plus sequence in five steps: the price, the required profit, the permitted cost, the gap, and the work of closing it before the design is fixed, by a cross-functional team, with abandonment as the honest answer where the gap will not close. The two calculations are short: 3,000,000 × 0.18 = 540,000 of required profit is 540,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 0.45 a bottle, so the target cost is 6.40 − 0.45 = 5.95 against an estimate of 6.70 — a gap of 0.75 a bottle and 1,200,000 × 0.75 = 900,000 a year. Value engineering closes it by simplifying the design, substituting materials, redesigning the process and working with suppliers, and here five changes worth 0.25 + 0.15 + 0.08 + 0.15 = 0.63 plus an overhead saving of 0.12 close it exactly — while the $3.00 of sauce is protected, because it is what the customer is buying. And the search is directed by the distinction between value-added costs, which the customer would pay for, and nonvalue-added costs such as moving, waiting, inspecting, reworking and storing, which can be removed without taking anything away from the product.

Case-style set: the bottle, two years later
The new target. The market price is now $6.00. The required profit is unchanged at 3,000,000 × 0.18 = 540,000, which over 1,200,000 bottles is 0.45 a unit, so the target cost becomes 6.00 − 0.45 = 5.55. The current cost is $5.95, so the new gap is 5.95 − 5.55 = 0.40 a bottle, or 1,200,000 × 0.40 = 480,000 a year.
Two places I would look. First, the allocated overhead of $0.88. The first round of value engineering took it from 1.00 to 0.88 by removing parts and a handling step; the activities behind it should now be examined directly, because an allocation that falls when parts are removed is an allocation driven by real activity and therefore reducible again. Second, the supply contracts. The bottle and nozzle are now standard components bought in volume for two years, and a longer contract or a second supplier qualified against the first is the ordinary way to take another five or ten cents out of a component bought 1,200,000 times a year.
The place I would not look is the sauce. It is $3.00 of the $5.95, it is the largest single component, and it is the product. Reducing the sesame content to close a 40-cent gap would turn a branded sauce into a cheaper one and hand the competitor the argument it needs.
What I would also put to the board. The competitor is at $5.95 and our target cost is now $5.55, which means they are either making it for less than we can or accepting a thinner return. Before spending on another round of value engineering, I would reverse engineer their bottle — which is what section 12.1 is for — and find out which of the two it is.
