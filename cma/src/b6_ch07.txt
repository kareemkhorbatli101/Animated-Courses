Chapter 7
Swaps, leases, convertibles and warrants; tax and capital structure

Chapter 6 dealt with contracts that move a price risk. This chapter deals with contracts that change the shape of a company's funding: an agreement that turns floating-rate debt into fixed without repaying anything, a lease that obtains an asset without buying it, a bond that may become a share, a right to subscribe that is attached to one security and traded as another.
They look like four unrelated instruments and they have one thing in common. Each lets a company obtain a financing outcome it could reach in a more obvious way, more cheaply or more flexibly than the obvious way allows. The chapter closes on the tax system, which is the reason several of them exist at all.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 4 established that interest is deductible and dividends are not, so 6 per cent debt costs 4.5 per cent after tax at a 25 per cent rate. Chapter 3 described the features that raise or lower a coupon. Part 1 taught the accounting for leases. This chapter uses all three.
7.1  Interest rate swaps
An interest rate swap is an agreement between two parties to exchange interest payments on a notional principal for a stated period. One pays fixed and receives floating; the other does the opposite. The principal itself is never exchanged, which is why it is called notional: it exists only to compute the payments.
The mechanics are simple. A company with $10,000,000 of floating-rate debt agrees to pay 6 per cent fixed and receive the floating rate. If the floating rate for a period is 4.5 per cent, the company pays 10,000,000 × 0.06 = 600,000 and receives 10,000,000 × 0.045 = 450,000, so it settles 600,000 − 450,000 = 150,000 net. Meanwhile it is paying 4.5 per cent to its own lender, which the swap receipt covers exactly. Its total cost is 6 per cent fixed, whatever the floating rate does.
Why not simply borrow at a fixed rate in the first place? Three reasons the exam accepts.
The company's circumstances changed. The debt was issued years ago on a view of rates that no longer holds, and repaying a bond early costs a call premium and issue costs, as Chapter 4 computed. A swap changes the exposure without touching the debt.
The company can borrow more cheaply in one market than the other. This is the comparative advantage argument and it is the classic exam question. Suppose a strong company can borrow fixed at 5.0 per cent or floating at the reference rate plus 0.3 points, while a weaker one pays fixed 7.0 per cent or floating plus 1.0 point. The strong company is better in both markets, but it is better by 7.0 − 5.0 = 2.0 points in the fixed market and only by 1.0 − 0.3 = 0.7 points in the floating one. The difference, 2.0 − 0.7 = 1.3 points, is a gain available to be shared: each borrows where its advantage is largest and they swap, and both end up paying less than they could have on their own.
Flexibility and cost. A swap can be arranged in days, costs no issue fees and can be reversed with an offsetting swap.
The risks are real and examinable. A swap carries counterparty risk, since the other side may fail — which is why most swaps now clear through a central counterparty, as futures do. And a swap entered without a matching exposure is a speculation on rates, exactly as a surplus hedge was in Chapter 6.

7.2  Currency swaps
A currency swap exchanges principal and interest in one currency for principal and interest in another. Unlike an interest rate swap, the principal is exchanged — at the start, at the end, or both — because the two amounts are in different currencies and there is no way to net them.
The use is straightforward. A company that earns in one currency and has borrowed in another has a mismatch: its debt service rises when the borrowing currency strengthens, and the business does nothing to offset it. A currency swap converts the obligation into the currency the company earns, which is the natural hedge of Chapter 6 constructed artificially.
Two motives recur in questions.
Access. A company may be able to borrow cheaply in its home market and not at all in a market where it has just started operating. It borrows at home, swaps into the foreign currency, and services the foreign debt from the foreign revenue.
Matching. A company with a long-term foreign investment uses the swap so that both the asset and the liability move with the same rate, so that a currency move changes the value of both together and the net position is stable. That is the translation exposure of Book 1, Chapter 18, managed.
The risks are the interest rate swap's risks plus one. Counterparty risk is larger, because the principal is exchanged and the amounts at stake are bigger than a stream of interest differences. And the swap fixes the rate for its term, so a company whose foreign operations shrink is left with a hedge larger than its exposure, which is a speculation it did not intend.

EXAM TRAP   Notional against exchanged
An interest rate swap's principal is never exchanged. A currency swap's principal is. A question that says "the principal is exchanged at the start" has told you which swap it is.
A swap does not change the underlying borrowing. The original loan stays on the balance sheet and the swap sits beside it.
Both parties to a swap can be better off, because they start from different costs in two markets. Nobody creates money; a gap in relative pricing is shared.

7.3  Leases as a source of finance
A lease is a contract giving the use of an asset for a period in exchange for payments. As a financing device it has one defining feature: the company obtains the asset without paying for it at once, and the lessor keeps legal title.
The accounting distinction Part 1 taught matters commercially as well. A finance lease transfers substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership — a long term relative to the asset's life, a bargain purchase option, present value of payments close to fair value — and it is capitalised, so an asset and a liability both appear. An operating lease does not transfer those risks and, under current US GAAP, still produces a right-of-use asset and a lease liability on the balance sheet, with the expense presented as a single operating cost rather than as interest and depreciation.
The advantages the exam expects are these.
Conservation of cash. No large payment at the start, so the company's own capital stays available.
Access for a company that cannot borrow. The lessor retains title, so its exposure is better secured than a lender's, and it will often provide for a weaker company at a rate no bank would offer.
The tax position of the lessor. A lessor with taxable profits can use the depreciation allowances; a loss-making lessee cannot. The lessor prices the lease to keep part of that benefit and pass part to the lessee, so a lease can genuinely be cheaper than borrowing for a company with no taxable income. This is the single most examinable reason to lease.
Protection against obsolescence, where the lease is short and the asset is technology.
Fewer covenants than a comparable loan, and faster arrangement.
The disadvantages are the mirror image. The implicit interest rate in a lease is usually higher than a secured loan's; the company has no residual asset at the end unless it buys one; a long lease is a fixed obligation exactly as debt is, and the rating agencies and Book 1's fixed-charge coverage ratio both treat it as such; and breaking a lease early is normally expensive.
Chapter 11 computes the net advantage to leasing properly, as a discounted cash flow against the alternative of borrowing and buying. The point here is the list of reasons a company would want one.

7.4  Convertibles and warrants
A convertible bond is a bond that its holder may exchange for a fixed number of common shares. A warrant is a right to subscribe for new shares at a stated price, usually issued attached to a bond and then detachable and traded separately.
Both are debt with an equity option attached, and the arithmetic of both is the same.
The conversion ratio is the number of shares a bond converts into. The conversion price is the par value divided by that ratio: a $1,000 bond converting into 25 shares has a conversion price of 1,000 ÷ 25 = 40.00 a share. The conversion value is the ratio times the current share price: at $36 a share it is 25 × 36 = 900, and at $48 a share it is 25 × 48 = 1,200. A convertible's market price is never below the higher of its conversion value and its value as a straight bond, because either floor can be realised.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: what the convertible costs the issuer
Step 1. The saving. A company issues $10,000,000 of convertible bonds at a 5 per cent coupon when its straight debt would cost 7 per cent. The saving is two points: 10,000,000 × 0.02 = 200,000 a year of interest not paid.
Step 2. What was given away. The conversion right. At a conversion price of $40 against a current share price of $34, the holders may buy shares at $40 that the company could otherwise have sold at the market price of whatever day they convert.
Step 3. The outcome if the shares do well. If the shares reach $60, holders convert: the company issues 250,000 shares for $10,000,000 of debt extinguished, which is $40 a share for stock worth $60. It has sold equity at a third below the market, and the cheap coupon over the life was the price it received for doing so.
Step 4. The outcome if the shares do badly. If the shares stay at $30, nobody converts, the bonds are repaid at par, and the company has borrowed at 5 per cent instead of 7. The saving was real and the option expired worthless.
Check. A convertible is not cheap debt. It is debt sold together with a call option on the company's own shares, and the discount on the coupon is the premium received for the option. Whether it was a good bargain is known only afterwards, which is why the exam asks for the mechanism rather than for a verdict.

Warrants differ from convertibles in three ways the exam tests. Exercising a warrant brings new money into the company, because the holder pays the exercise price in cash; converting a bond brings none, since debt is simply extinguished. A warrant is detachable and trades separately, so the bond and the option can end in different hands. And warrants are usually longer-dated than the conversion rights attached to bonds.
A warrant's intrinsic value works exactly as a call's: at an exercise price of $30 with the shares at $38, it is 38 − 30 = 8.
Both instruments dilute. Book 1, Chapter 16 computed diluted earnings per share for exactly these securities, by the if-converted method for a convertible and the treasury stock method for a warrant.

7.5  Taxes, capital structure and investment
The outcome asks how income taxes affect capital structure and capital investment decisions, and the answer has four parts.
The shield favours debt. Interest is deductible and dividends are not, so each dollar of interest saves the company its tax rate in cash. On $10,000,000 of debt at a 25 per cent rate, the whole shield is worth 10,000,000 × 0.25 = 2,500,000 in present value terms if the debt is permanent — the standard textbook result, and the reason a tax system that favours one kind of money changes how companies are funded.
Distress sets the limit. The shield is worth nothing to a company that cannot pay. Past some level of borrowing the expected costs of financial distress — a higher rate demanded by lenders, suppliers tightening terms, customers doubting warranties, management's time consumed, and in the extreme the direct costs of a bankruptcy — rise faster than the shield grows. The optimum is where the two margins meet, and it differs by industry: a company with stable cash flows and tangible assets can carry far more debt than one whose value is a brand and a pipeline of research.
Personal taxes work the other way. Interest received is usually taxed at a higher personal rate than dividends and capital gains, so investors demand more for holding debt than the corporate shield alone would suggest, and part of the company-level advantage is given back. The exam mentions this as a qualification rather than asking for a calculation.
Tax reaches investment decisions too, and in three places. Depreciation is deductible, so an asset generates a tax shield of its own: Orontes's $2,100,000 depreciation charge saves 2,100,000 × 0.25 = 525,000 of tax a year, and that saving is a cash flow of the project that bought the asset. Only after-tax cash flows are discounted, so a project's revenues and costs are taken net of tax. And the discount rate is the after-tax cost of capital, which Chapter 8 builds — using the after-tax cost of debt, because the shield has already been counted there.
One warning, repeated from Chapter 4 because candidates lose marks on it. The tax shield is not a reason to prefer a bad project. A project that earns less than its cost of capital destroys value whether or not it was financed with debt, and the financing decision is separate from the investment decision. Mixing them is the most common error in Section B and Book 3 is built on keeping them apart.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Notional"
English notional in finance means a reference amount used to compute payments and never itself paid — the notional principal of a swap. In ordinary English it means imaginary or theoretical, which is close enough to mislead: the notional amount is not imaginary, and the payments computed on it are entirely real. Arabic المبلغ الإسمي, literally the nominal amount, keeps the sense of a stated reference. French notionnel is the technical term and montant notionnel the full phrase, and French usefully reserves nominal for the par value of a security. The practical consequence is in the exam: a question that gives you a notional principal of $10,000,000 is not telling you that anybody will pay ten million, only that the interest difference is computed on it.

An interest rate swap exchanges fixed payments for floating on a notional principal that is never exchanged: pay 6 per cent and receive 4.5 on ten million and 600,000 − 450,000 = 150,000 changes hands, and two companies with different relative costs in two markets can share a 1.3-point gain by each borrowing where its advantage is larger. Case-style set: Barada's financing, reconsidered
The four options, by what each costs the owner.
The 7 per cent loan costs 7.0 × 0.75 = 5.25 per cent after tax, and it breaches the covenant: total liabilities would be $5,400,000 against equity of $3,100,000, which is 5,400,000 ÷ 3,100,000 = 1.74 against a limit of 1.50. It is unusable as drawn.
The share subscription costs the owner a quarter of the company permanently and removes the covenant problem entirely, because equity rises by the amount raised. Book 1 valued what is being given away at more than the cash received on every multiple except the investor's own.
The lease is the most promising of the two new options. It funds the equipment specifically, the lessor holds title and so prices for a better-secured exposure than a bank's, and Barada's thin profitability means the allowances are worth more to the lessor than to Barada — part of which comes back in the rate. It is, however, a fixed charge: it will be capitalised as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability, so whether it breaches the covenant depends entirely on how that covenant defines debt, and that definition is the first thing to read.
The convertible at 4 per cent is cheap in cash terms, saving about 3 points against the bank loan, and it dilutes only if the shares reach $7.50 — a quarter above their current value. But it is still debt until conversion, so it fails the covenant exactly as the loan does.
Recommendation: the lease, subject to the covenant definition. It funds the asset that needs funding, costs less in dilution than the subscription, and is the only one of the four whose pricing reflects something Barada genuinely has — an asset worth holding title to.
