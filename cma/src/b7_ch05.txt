Chapter 5
What is relevant: sunk, avoidable, explicit, implicit
ما هو ملائم للقرار: التكاليف الغارقة والتكاليف القابلة للتجنب والصريحة والضمنية
A decision is a choice between futures. Any cost that is the same under both futures cannot affect the choice, and any cost already incurred cannot be changed by it. Those two sentences eliminate most of the numbers in a management accountant's records from most of the decisions a manager takes, and learning which ones survive is the subject of this chapter.
The discipline is harder than it sounds, because the costs that must be ignored are often the largest and the most carefully recorded. A machine that cost $1,200,000 and still stands at $700,000 in the books has a claim on the attention that a $40,000 scrap value does not — and in a replacement decision the $700,000 is irrelevant and the $40,000 is not.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 2 made the point in its simplest form: because fixed costs do not move with volume, the effect of selling 500,000 more jars is 500,000 × 4.80 = 2,400,000 and nothing else needs to be computed. That is relevant costing applied to one decision. This chapter states the rule that made it work.
5.1  The two tests
A cost or a revenue is relevant to a decision if it passes both of these tests.
It lies in the future. A decision taken today can change what happens from today onwards and nothing that happened before. Money already spent is spent whatever is chosen.
It differs between the alternatives. If a cost is the same under every option, including it changes every total by the same amount and changes no ranking. Including it is not wrong, exactly — it wastes effort and invites error.
Both tests must be passed. A future cost that does not differ is irrelevant; a differing cost that is already incurred is irrelevant. The exam's favourite trap is a cost that passes one test loudly and fails the other quietly.
Two further names describe the same idea from different angles, and the outcome asks for them.
An incremental cost, also called a differential cost, is the difference in total cost between two alternatives. It is the relevant amount stated as a number rather than as a test.
A marginal cost is the cost of one more unit, which Chapter 7 develops. In most exam settings it equals the variable cost per unit, and the two words are used interchangeably at this level.

EXAM TRAP   Both tests, every time
Depreciation is never relevant in itself. It is the spreading of a past payment, so it fails the first test. What is relevant about an owned asset is what else could be done with it — sold, or used elsewhere — which is section 5.4's subject.
An allocated share of a common cost is almost never relevant. The question is not how the cost is shared but whether the total changes.
A cost can be relevant for one decision and irrelevant for another, with no change in the cost itself. Relevance is a property of the decision, not of the cost.

5.2  Sunk costs
A sunk cost is a cost already incurred that cannot be recovered by any choice now available. It fails the first test absolutely, and the outcome asks specifically why.
The reason is that a decision changes the future and the past is identical under every option. Suppose Orontes spent $300,000 on a specialised packaging film for a product line it is now reconsidering. Whether the line continues or stops, the $300,000 has gone. It appears in neither column of the comparison, so it cannot favour either.
What is relevant about the film is what can still be done with it. If it can be sold for $40,000, that $40,000 is relevant because it is receivable under one option and not the other. If it can be used on another line, saving $180,000 of purchases that would otherwise be made, that saving is relevant. And the choice between those two uses is itself a decision: using the film saves $180,000 and forgoes the $40,000 scrap, so the net benefit of using it is 180,000 − 40,000 = 140,000. The original $300,000 appears nowhere in any of that arithmetic.
Why candidates and managers include it anyway is worth naming, because the exam sometimes asks for the behavioural point. It is called the sunk cost fallacy, or throwing good money after bad: having spent $300,000, a manager feels that abandoning the line wastes it, and continues in order to justify the original decision. The spending is wasted either way; continuing merely adds to it. The discipline is to ask what the choice is from here, which is also the only question a reader of the accounts can answer next year.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: replacing a machine
Step 1. The facts. Orontes owns a filling machine bought four years ago for $1,200,000, now standing in the books at $700,000. A new machine costs $900,000 and would save $350,000 a year of operating costs for four years. The old machine could be sold today for $250,000 and has no value at the end of the four years.
Step 2. What is irrelevant. The $1,200,000 original cost, the $700,000 book value, and the $450,000 loss that selling at $250,000 would produce in the accounts. All three are the past, recorded in different ways.
Step 3. What is relevant. The $900,000 to be paid, the $250,000 to be received, and the $350,000 a year to be saved.
Step 4. The decision. The net outlay is 900,000 − 250,000 = 650,000, and the savings are 350,000 × 4 = 1,400,000, so replacing is better by 1,400,000 − 650,000 = 750,000 before any discounting. Chapter 16 discounts it properly; the ranking does not change.
Check. The accounting loss of $450,000 is the figure that stops this decision being taken in practice, because it appears in next year's income statement and nobody wants to explain it. It is not a cash flow and it does not change the merits, and an answer that says so — and then says the loss should be explained to the board in advance — is the one worth full marks.

5.3  Avoidable and unavoidable
An avoidable cost is one that will not be incurred if an activity is discontinued. An unavoidable cost will be incurred anyway. The pair applies whenever a question asks whether to drop a product, close a department, shut a branch or stop serving a customer.
The distinction is not the same as variable and fixed, and confusing the two is the commonest error in this topic. Most variable costs are avoidable, because they stop when the activity stops. But some fixed costs are avoidable too: a supervisor whose post would be eliminated, an insurance policy that would be cancelled, a lease that would be surrendered. And some variable costs are unavoidable, as where a supply contract commits the company to a minimum quantity whatever it produces.
The test is the same two tests of section 5.1, applied to the stopping decision: does the cost lie in the future, and does it differ between continuing and stopping?
A separable fixed cost belongs entirely to one activity and usually goes with it. A common fixed cost is shared, and usually does not: closing the halva line does not reduce the factory rent, the chief executive's salary or the audit fee, and allocating a share of those to the line never made them avoidable.

5.4  Explicit and implicit
An explicit cost involves a payment: wages, materials, rent, interest. It appears in the accounting records because somebody was paid.
An implicit cost involves no payment and is a genuine cost all the same: the value of something the company already owns that is used up or given up by a choice. It appears in no ledger, which is why it has to be reasoned about rather than looked up.
The most important implicit cost has its own name and its own chapter. An opportunity cost is the benefit forgone by using a resource for one purpose instead of its best alternative use, and Chapter 6 computes it. But the category is wider.
The owner of a small business who works in it without a salary bears an implicit cost: the wage that could have been earned elsewhere. The company that uses a building it owns bears the rent it could have received. And the shareholders of any company bear the implicit cost of their capital, which is precisely the point Book 1 made about economic profit — accounting profit charges for the explicit cost of debt and not for the implicit cost of equity.
That gives the pair of definitions the exam wants.
Accounting profit is revenue less explicit costs.
Economic profit is revenue less explicit and implicit costs together.
A business can show an accounting profit and an economic loss, which means it is earning less than the resources it consumes could earn elsewhere — and a business in that position is being kept alive by a cost nobody is charging it for.

5.5  Putting it to work
A relevant-cost analysis has a shape, and following it is most of the skill.
Identify the alternatives, including the option of doing nothing, which is always available and often omitted.
List the future cash flows under each. Cash flows, not accounting charges: the exam's items are receipts and payments, and depreciation, allocations and book values are excluded by construction.
Remove anything that is the same under both. What remains is the difference, and the difference is the decision.
Add the implicit costs that the ledger does not contain, which in practice means the opportunity costs of Chapter 6.
State the answer as a difference, not as two totals. "Replacing is better by $750,000" is an answer; "option one costs $4,250,000 and option two costs $5,000,000" invites the reader to do the subtraction and to wonder what was in each.
Two habits are worth building now, because every chapter from here to Chapter 9 uses them.
Build the analysis as a single column of differences wherever possible. It is faster, it cannot contain an irrelevant item without the error being obvious, and it produces the answer directly.
Say what was excluded and why. One line — "the $700,000 book value is sunk and is not included" — tells a marker that the exclusion was a decision rather than an oversight, and it is worth more than the arithmetic that follows it.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Relevant"
English relevant in management accounting is a technical term with a two-part definition, and it is not a synonym for important, useful or interesting. A cost can be enormous, carefully recorded and central to the business, and still be irrelevant to the decision in hand — which sounds like a contradiction until the definition is remembered. Arabic ملائم carries the everyday sense of suitable and has to be read as the defined term. French uses coût pertinent, and pertinent in French has the same everyday pull towards apt or appropriate. The one safeguard in all three languages is to say the definition aloud when using the word: future, and different between the alternatives.

Chapter summary
Figure F705-06 brings the chapter together.
Figure F705-06. Chapter 5 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A cost is relevant if it lies in the future and differs between the alternatives, and both tests must be passed, which excludes depreciation, book values and allocated shares of common costs from almost every decision. A sunk cost fails the first test absolutely: $300,000 already spent on packaging film appears in no column, while the $40,000 it would fetch as scrap and the $180,000 it would save in use both do, so using it is better by 180,000 − 40,000 = 140,000. In a replacement, the $1,200,000 original cost, the $700,000 book value and the $450,000 accounting loss are all irrelevant, while 900,000 − 250,000 = 650,000 of net outlay against 350,000 × 4 = 1,400,000 of savings decides it by $750,000. Avoidable costs are those that stop when the activity stops, which is not the same as variable: a supervisor's post can be avoidable and a contractual minimum purchase unavoidable. Explicit costs are paid and implicit ones are not, and the difference between them is the difference between accounting profit and economic profit. And a relevant-cost answer is a single column of differences, stated as a difference, with the exclusions named.

Case-style set: the film and the line
The film first. The $300,000 already spent is sunk and belongs in neither column. What is relevant is what the film can still do: used on the tahini line it saves $180,000 of purchases, and scrapped it brings $40,000. Using it is therefore better by 180,000 − 40,000 = 140,000, and that is true whether the halva line continues or closes — which means the film does not bear on the closure decision at all. Two separate questions have been presented as one.
The apportioned costs. The $300,000 of factory and head office charges do not disappear on closure; they are carried by the other lines instead. They are unavoidable and belong in neither column, and the fact that the halva line's reported profit is worse because of them is an artefact of apportionment.
The column of differences, on closing:
Lost contribution: (900,000).
Avoided separable fixed costs: 420,000.
Avoided supervisor's post: 80,000.
Proceeds from the wrapping machine: 95,000, received once.
Net effect in the first year: 420,000 + 80,000 + 95,000 = 595,000 against 900,000 lost, a loss of $305,000; and in every year after that, a loss of 900,000 − 500,000 = 400,000.
Recommendation: do not close the halva line. It covers its own avoidable costs by $400,000 a year, and closing it would transfer $300,000 of unavoidable overhead onto the remaining products without reducing it by a cent. Use the film on the tahini line, which is worth $140,000 and is a separate decision. If the line's reported loss is the reason the question was asked, the answer is that the apportionment is misleading, not that the line is.
