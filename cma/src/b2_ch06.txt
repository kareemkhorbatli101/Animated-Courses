WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
In Book 1 you valued inventory at cost and moved cost to cost of goods sold when goods were sold. Chapter 1 of this book added raw materials, work in process and finished goods. Chapter 5 introduced normal costing: actual direct costs plus overhead applied at a predetermined rate. Job order costing puts these pieces together for one order at a time.
6.1  When job order costing fits
Job order costing collects costs for each separate job, batch or customer order. It fits when the products are different from each other and each order can be identified from start to finish.
Private-label tahini fits well. One job may be a Barada order in plastic tubs, and the next a Gulf retailer's order in glass jars with a different sesame blend. Printing firms, builders, law firms and hospitals also use job costing.
Orontes's Oils division does not use it. Olive oil flows through crushing and bottling as identical liters, so costs are collected by department instead. That is process costing, the topic of Chapter 7.

6.2  The job cost sheet
A job cost sheet is the record of one job. It collects three costs: direct materials from materials requisitions, direct labor from labor time tickets, and overhead applied at the predetermined rate. Figure F206-02 shows how the documents feed the sheet.
Figure F206-02. Source documents feed each job cost sheet; the sheets support the WIP control account.
The job cost sheets form a subsidiary ledger. The total of all open sheets must equal the balance of the Work-in-Process Inventory control account in the general ledger. When a job is finished, its sheet moves to the finished goods file.

6.3  Recording materials and labor
A materials requisition moves materials out of Raw Materials Inventory. If the materials are traced to a job, the debit goes to Work-in-Process Inventory and the job cost sheet. If they are indirect materials, such as cleaning chemicals or machine oil, the debit goes to Manufacturing Overhead Control.
Labor time tickets work the same way. Hours on a job are direct labor, debited to Work-in-Process Inventory. Supervision, idle time and maintenance labor are indirect labor, debited to Manufacturing Overhead Control. The credit is Wages Payable.
In March, Tahini & Spreads issued direct materials of $250,000 and indirect materials of $20,000. Direct labor was $150,000 and indirect labor was $40,000. Only the direct amounts reach the job cost sheets.
EXAM TRAP   Indirect costs do not go to WIP
A common wrong answer debits Work-in-Process Inventory with indirect materials or indirect labor. They are overhead. They reach the jobs only through the predetermined rate.

6.4  Applying overhead
Actual overhead is not known until the end of the year, but a job needs a cost when it is finished. So job costing uses normal costing: overhead is applied with a predetermined rate. For 2027 Tahini & Spreads uses $30 per machine hour: $24 fixed plus $6 variable. Chapter 8 shows how the rate is set.
Applied overhead equals the actual machine hours used by the job times the predetermined rate. The debit is Work-in-Process Inventory and the credit is Manufacturing Overhead Applied. Actual overhead costs, such as depreciation and utilities, are debited to Manufacturing Overhead Control.
The two overhead accounts rarely match during the year. The difference is under- or over-applied overhead. It stays open until year-end, when Chapter 8 closes it. Figure F206-04 shows the complete flow.
Figure F206-04. Job costing cost flow: actual overhead in MOH Control, applied overhead to jobs.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Imputation, تحميل
French 'charges imputées' is applied overhead, but 'imputation' in general accounting also means posting an entry. Arabic texts call applied overhead التكاليف غير المباشرة المحملة and the difference فروق التحميل. In U.S. terms the accounts are Manufacturing Overhead Control and Manufacturing Overhead Applied.

6.5  Inventory values and cost of goods sold
When a job is finished, its total cost moves from Work-in-Process Inventory to Finished Goods Inventory. The total of the jobs finished in a period is the cost of goods manufactured. When the goods are sold, the job cost moves from Finished Goods Inventory to Cost of Goods Sold.
At any date, the inventory values come straight from the job cost sheets. Open jobs make up work in process. Finished jobs not yet sold make up finished goods. The sale price never enters these accounts; it goes to Sales Revenue.

6.6  Benefits and limitations
Job costing gives an accurate cost for each order. Managers use it to price new orders, compare the estimate with the actual cost and find unprofitable customers. It also gives the inventory values that the financial statements need.
It has limits. It needs many documents and careful records for every job. Overhead is still an allocation, so a poor allocation base can distort job costs; activity-based costing (Chapter 12) is one answer. It also does not suit identical units in a continuous flow.

● IFRS CONTRAST   U.S. GAAP and IFRS
Both ASC 330 and IAS 2 accept job costs as inventory cost when they include a proper share of production overhead. Both require abnormal costs, such as abnormal spoilage or idle capacity, to be expensed rather than added to a job (Chapters 7 and 8).

6.7  Extended Example 6A: Tahini & Spreads, March 2027
This example follows every cost of one month from the source documents to the income statement. The calculations are exam-core: the exam asks for job costs, inventory values and cost of goods sold. The journal entries and T-accounts are for depth: they show why the numbers move.
1. Scenario
On March 1, 2027 Raw Materials Inventory was $120,000. Job T-0302 was in process at $64,000, and job T-0215 was in finished goods at $90,000. During March the division worked on three jobs.
T-0302 (halva): direct materials $30,000, direct labor $20,000, 1,000 machine hours. Finished and sold.
T-0303 (Barada private-label tahini): direct materials $140,000, direct labor $80,000, 4,200 machine hours. Finished, not yet sold.
T-0304: direct materials $80,000, direct labor $50,000, 2,500 machine hours. Still in process on March 31.
Other events: materials purchased $300,000; indirect materials $20,000; indirect labor $40,000; service department charges $57,500; depreciation $70,000; utilities $62,500; jobs T-0215 and T-0302 sold on account for $330,000.
2. Plan
Classify each cost as direct or indirect. Apply overhead at the predetermined rate. Record the entries, post them, prepare the schedules and check that everything ties.
3. Calculations
Overhead applied: 7,700 machine hours × $30 = $231,000.
Job costs: T-0302 = $64,000 opening + $30,000 + $20,000 + $30,000 = $144,000; T-0303 = $346,000; T-0304 = $205,000.
Cost of goods manufactured (T-0302 + T-0303) = $490,000.
Cost of goods sold (T-0215 + T-0302) = $90,000 + $144,000 = $234,000.
Actual overhead = $20,000 + $40,000 + $190,000 = $250,000; underapplied = $19,000.

Chapter summary
Figure F206-12 brings the chapter together.
Figure F206-12. Chapter 6 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Job order costing collects costs for each separate job on a job cost sheet. Direct materials and direct labor come from requisitions and time tickets; overhead is applied at a predetermined rate times the actual base. Indirect costs go to Manufacturing Overhead Control. Open job sheets equal work in process, finished unsold sheets equal finished goods, and sold jobs make up cost of goods sold. Job costing gives accurate order costs but needs detailed records, and its overhead is still an allocation.

Case question: Tahini & Spreads in April
Tahini & Spreads works job by job. At the start of April 2027, job T-0304 was still in process, and job T-0303 was finished and waiting in finished goods for delivery to Barada. During April the division bought materials, paid its workers, ran its machines and started new jobs.
Every cost is recorded on a job cost sheet. Direct materials and direct labor are charged to the jobs directly. Overhead is applied to each job with the predetermined rate per machine hour, and actual overhead is collected in a separate account. In April the division completed job T-0304 and delivered job T-0303 to Barada.
The division controller must close April's books. She needs the cost of the completed job, the balance of work in process at the end of the month, cost of goods manufactured and cost of goods sold. She must also decide what to do with the difference between actual and applied overhead at the end of April.
The auditors will visit in May, so every balance must agree with the job cost sheets. The controller also wants to show the division manager how the April entries move costs from one inventory account to the next, from raw materials to work in process, then to finished goods and finally to cost of goods sold.

Section checks
SC206-1  Answer B. Each private-label order is different and can be identified from start to finish.
A is wrong. Identical liters in a continuous flow suit process costing.
C is wrong. Identical units suit process or standard costing.
D is wrong. Bottling is a continuous process.
SC206-2  Answer C. Costs are collected for each separate job.
A is wrong. Departments are the cost object in process costing.
B is wrong. Periods are used for period costs, not product costs.
D is wrong. A product line contains many different jobs.
SC206-3  Answer D. Direct materials + direct labor + applied overhead.
A is wrong. This is direct labor only.
B is wrong. Applied overhead must be added.
C is wrong. This applies only the fixed part of the rate.
SC206-4  Answer D. Open job sheets are the subsidiary ledger for WIP.
A is wrong. Finished unsold jobs support finished goods.
B is wrong. Sold jobs make up COGS.
C is wrong. This account holds applied overhead only.
SC206-5  Answer A. Indirect materials are actual overhead.
B is wrong. Indirect materials do not go to WIP.
C is wrong. The entry is reversed.
D is wrong. Factory costs are product costs, not COGS directly.
SC206-6  Answer C. Supervision is indirect labor.
A is wrong. Supervision cannot be traced to one job.
B is wrong. Factory supervision is a product cost.
D is wrong. Costs reach finished goods only through WIP.
SC206-7  Answer C. Actual machine hours × predetermined rate.
A is wrong. This uses only the variable rate.
B is wrong. This uses only the fixed rate.
D is wrong. This is total actual overhead for all jobs.
SC206-8  Answer B. Applied overhead is added to the jobs.
A is wrong. The entry is reversed.
C is wrong. Actual overhead is not credited here.
D is wrong. Orontes keeps actual and applied overhead in separate accounts.
SC206-9  Answer C. Completed jobs move to finished goods.
A is wrong. WIP holds open jobs only.
B is wrong. COGS is recorded at the sale.
D is wrong. Raw materials hold unused materials.
SC206-10  Answer B. COGS is the job cost.
A is wrong. This is gross margin.
C is wrong. The sale price goes to revenue.
D is wrong. Cost and price are not added.
SC206-11  Answer B. Order-level cost is the main benefit.
A is wrong. Job costing needs more records.
C is wrong. Overhead is still applied with a rate.
D is wrong. That setting suits process costing.
SC206-12  Answer A. Applied overhead is an allocation.
B is wrong. Job sheets give the inventory values.
C is wrong. Direct labor is traced to each job.
D is wrong. Law firms and hospitals use job costing.
SC206-13  Answer B. Beginning WIP + manufacturing costs − ending WIP.
A is wrong. This is cost of goods sold.
C is wrong. This is total manufacturing cost before the WIP adjustment.
D is wrong. Ending WIP must be deducted.
SC206-14  Answer D. Monthly differences usually even out; they are closed at year-end (Chapter 8).
A is wrong. The difference is not closed monthly.
B is wrong. Jobs receive applied overhead only.
C is wrong. WIP is not adjusted during the year.
