6.1  The sales budget and the profit plan
The master budget is also called the annual profit plan, because it ends in budgeted profit. The sales budget is its first schedule: the planned units and revenue for each period.
Almost every other budget depends on it. Figure F306-02 shows how sales drive production, selling expenses, cash collections and the budgeted income statement.
Figure F306-02. The sales budget at the centre of the profit plan.
That is why the sales budget deserves the most care. If sales are overstated, the company buys too much material, hires too many people and plans for cash it will never collect.

6.3  Preparing the sales budget
A sales budget has three components for each period: units, price per unit and revenue. Companies with several products or channels prepare a line for each and add them up.

6.4  From sales to production
Production does not simply equal sales, because the company holds finished goods. The production budget adds the stock wanted at the end of the period and subtracts the stock already on hand at the start. Figure F306-06 shows the formula.
Figure F306-06. How the production budget is calculated.
The ending stock comes from the inventory policy. Beverages keeps 20% of next quarter's sales in stock, so that it can meet orders while the next batch is filled.
Other factors also shape the production budget: plant capacity, storage space, the shelf life of juice and the wish to keep the workforce stable. Figure F306-07 lists them for Beverages.

Chapter summary
Figure F306-13 brings the chapter together.
Figure F306-13. Chapter 6 summary map: the key rule for each section.
The sales budget is the first schedule of the annual profit plan, and almost every other budget depends on it. It starts from a forecast that considers the market, the economy, prices, competitors, past results and customers, and it sets units, price and revenue for each period. The production budget equals sales plus desired ending inventory minus beginning inventory, and it must respect the inventory policy, capacity, storage and shelf life.

Case question: the Tahini & Spreads jar budgets
The Tahini & Spreads division has agreed its sales volume for 2028: the evidence-based figure of Chapter 3, split into quarters by the sales team. Sales are highest in the third quarter, when Gulf buyers stock up before the winter season. Last year the division ran short of jars in the third quarter and lost part of a Gulf order, so the sales director wants the budgets checked carefully this time.
The division keeps finished jars equal to 10% of the next quarter's sales, so that it can meet orders while the next batch is filled. At the start of 2028 it expects to hold exactly that amount. The filling line can produce about 450,000 jars a quarter, and tahini keeps for twelve months, so neither capacity nor shelf life is a problem this year.
The controller, Rana, must now prepare the sales budget and the production budget by quarter. She also wants the new assistant accountant to understand which factors belong to the sales forecast and which belong to the production budget. Rana will present both budgets to the division manager, Samir, at the planning meeting next week, together with a short note on the stock policy.
