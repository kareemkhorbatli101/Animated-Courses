WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 7 treated spoilage and rework in product cost, and Chapter 15 redesigned a process in one big step. This chapter improves processes in many small steps and measures what poor quality really costs.
16.1  Continuous improvement techniques and quality
Continuous improvement means improving processes in many small, regular steps instead of one big change. Each step removes a little waste or a cause of defects, and quality rises over time.
The best-known approach is kaizen, in which the people who do the work suggest the improvements. Each change is tested with the PDCA cycle: plan, do, check, act. Figure F216-02 shows the cycle.
Figure F216-02. The PDCA cycle behind kaizen.
Two wider approaches support it. Total quality management makes quality everyone's job and lets the customer define it. Six Sigma uses data and statistics to cut defects to very low levels. Figure F216-03 compares the four techniques.
Figure F216-03. Continuous improvement techniques compared

16.2  Continuous improvement and ideal standards
An ideal standard assumes perfect conditions: no waste, no breakdowns, no idle time. A currently attainable standard allows for normal waste and is hard but possible. Chapter 5 used attainable standards for costing; this section shows how the two work together.
The ideal shows the direction and the size of the waste still in the process. Each year the attainable standard moves closer to it. Beverages allows 2.0% normal spoilage this year, aims for 1.2% after its kaizen program, and treats 0.0% as the ideal. Figure F216-05 shows the path.
Figure F216-05. Moving the spoilage standard toward the ideal.
Figure F216-06 compares the two kinds of standard. Judging people against the ideal standard can demotivate them, because it is never met; using it to show the remaining waste motivates improvement.

16.3  The four costs of quality
The cost of quality has four parts. Prevention costs stop defects before they happen. Appraisal costs find defects by inspection and testing. Together they are the costs of conformance.
Internal failure costs arise from defects found before the product reaches the customer, such as scrap and rework. External failure costs arise from defects the customer finds, such as returns, complaints and lost sales. Figure F216-07 shows the four categories.
Figure F216-07. The four categories of the cost of quality.

EXAM TRAP   Hidden external failure costs
Lost sales and lost customers do not appear in the ledger, but they are external failure costs. An exam answer that leaves them out understates the cost of quality.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Coûts de la qualité, تكاليف الجودة
French also says 'coût d'obtention de la qualité'. The IMA Arabic glossary uses تكلفة النوعية; Arab textbooks usually say تكاليف الجودة. Both mean the four categories, not the price of a high-quality product.

Chapter summary
Figure F216-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F216-14. Chapter 16 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Continuous improvement raises quality in many small steps, using kaizen, the PDCA cycle, total quality management and Six Sigma. The ideal standard shows the direction, while attainable standards move toward it each year. The cost of quality has four parts: prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure, including hidden lost sales. Spending more on prevention pays when failure and appraisal costs fall by more, and the financial impact is always the net change.
