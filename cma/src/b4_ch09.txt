9.1  What an accounting information system does
An accounting information system captures, processes, stores and reports the economic events of a business. Figure F409-02 sets out those functions with an Orontes example of each.
Figure F409-02. What an accounting information system does
What an AIS does
Meaning
At Orontes
Capture
Record each economic event as it happens
A goods received note raised at the gate
Process
Classify, value and summarise the events
The invoice matched and posted to payables
Store
Keep the record so it can be found again
The supplier account and the audit trail
Report
Present the result to whoever must decide
The payables ageing of Book 1, Chapter 6
Control
Ensure what is captured is complete and authorized
The input controls of Chapter 7
The fifth row is the one that connects this chapter to the rest of the book. A system that captures and reports but does not control what it captures produces numbers nobody should rely on, which is why Chapters 6 and 7 came first.
Notice what the definition does not say. It does not confine the system to financial data. The cases produced, the hours worked and the energy used are all economic events, and a modern system captures them alongside the amounts.

9.2  The system within the value chain
The value chain divides a business into the primary activities that create the product and the support activities that make them possible. Figure F409-03 shows where the accounting information system sits.
Figure F409-03. The accounting information system within the value chain.
It is a support activity, and it is unusual among them because it touches every primary activity rather than one. Inbound logistics generates receipts, operations generates production records, sales generates invoices, and service generates warranty claims. Each becomes an entry.
The practical consequence is that the system is the only place where the whole business can be seen at once. That is why its design decides what management can ask. A system organised only around the ledger cannot answer a question about a product or a customer.

9.3  The transaction cycles
Transactions are grouped into cycles, each covering a sequence of related events. Figure F409-04 shows the four operating cycles and the ledger they all feed.
Figure F409-04. The four transaction cycles feeding the general ledger.
The revenue cycle, often called revenue to cash, runs from the customer order to the collection of cash. The expenditure cycle, or expenditure to disbursement, runs from the purchase request to the payment. The production cycle converts materials into finished goods, and the payroll cycle turns hours into wages.
The general ledger and reporting cycle is the fifth, and it differs from the others. It originates few transactions of its own: its work is to receive the summarised results of the other four, close the period, produce the statements, and hold the audit trail that leads back from any total to the entries behind it.
Each cycle has its own documents, master files and controls. That is why Chapter 3 could discuss segregation cycle by cycle. A weakness in one cycle rarely stays there, because the ledger receives whatever the cycle sends it.

9.4  The cost of separate systems
Most companies arrive at several systems rather than choosing them. A plant buys software to schedule production, sales buys something to track customers, and finance keeps the ledger it has always had. Each works. Figure F409-05 sets out what it costs to leave them apart.

What it means

The first two challenges are the ones candidates name, and the fifth is the one that matters most. When no system holds the whole picture, a question as ordinary as which product earns the most becomes a project rather than a query. By the time it is answered the month has moved on.
There is a control dimension too. The transfer between two systems is where errors hide, because it belongs to neither. A monthly upload that nobody reconciles is exactly the kind of gap Chapter 3 would call a missing independent check.

9.5  Extended Example 9A: one case of oil, four cycles
This example follows a single case of olive oil from the purchase of the olives to the collection of cash, naming the cycle at each step. It is exam-core: questions give an event and ask which cycle it belongs to.
1. Buying the olives
The purchasing department raises a request, the manager approves it, the storekeeper receives the delivery and accounts payable pays the farmer. That is the expenditure cycle from end to end, and it creates a payable and then removes it.
2. Making the oil
Materials are issued to the pressing line, labour is recorded against the batch and overhead is applied at the rate Book 2 established. The production cycle moves the cost from raw materials through work in process to finished goods.
3. Paying the people who made it
The hours recorded against that batch also flow through the payroll cycle, which calculates gross pay, deducts what must be deducted and pays the net amount. The same hours therefore appear in two cycles, for two different purposes.
4. Selling it
A Gulf distributor orders, the warehouse ships, the system invoices and the cash arrives sixty days later. That is the revenue cycle, and it creates a receivable and then removes it.
5. Where the separate systems hurt
At Orontes the production system holds the batch and its hours, payroll holds the same hours, and the ledger holds the cost. Nothing joins them automatically. To ask what that case of oil actually cost, somebody must export from three systems and match them by hand.
6. What the ledger sees
The ledger receives four summarised postings and knows the margin on the case. It cannot say whether the margin came from a good price, a cheap harvest or an efficient shift, because the detail that would answer that stayed behind in the cycles.

Chapter summary
Figure F409-07 brings the chapter together.
Figure F409-07. Chapter 9 summary map: the key rule for each section.
An accounting information system captures, processes, stores, reports and controls the economic events of a business, financial and non-financial alike. In the value chain it is a support activity that touches every primary one, which makes it the only place the whole business can be seen at once. Transactions are grouped into cycles: revenue to cash, expenditure to disbursement, production, and human resources and payroll, each feeding a general ledger and reporting cycle that closes the period and holds the audit trail. Keeping financial and non-financial systems separate costs duplicate entry, figures that disagree, reconciliation effort, delay, the loss of any single view, and a transfer between systems that belongs to nobody.

Case question: what did the case of oil cost?
Orontes runs three systems that do not connect. The plant system schedules production and records the hours and the cases produced on each batch. A separate payroll system holds the same hours for the purpose of paying wages. The accounting system holds the ledger and the costs. None of the three systems was chosen; each was bought by a different department over the past nine years.
The sales director has asked a simple question: what did one case of premium olive oil cost to make last month, and how much of that was labour? The controller's team exported data from all three systems and spent two days matching batches by date and shift before producing an answer.
While matching, the team found that the plant system recorded 240,000 cases for the month and the ledger showed 238,500. Nobody could explain the difference, and the monthly upload from the plant system to the ledger is not reconciled by anyone.
The board is considering whether to replace all three with a single system. The finance director has asked Rania to set out what the present arrangement actually costs, beyond the licence fees, before any proposal is prepared.
