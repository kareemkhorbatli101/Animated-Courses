WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
You have studied accounting before, perhaps in Arabic or French. The ideas in this chapter are not new to you: assets and liabilities, debit and credit, the trial balance. Three things are new.
The English names. Some are close to your language and some are not. The False-Friend boxes warn you about words that look familiar but mean something else.
The U.S. rules. The CMA exam uses U.S. GAAP. If you learned IFRS, most ideas are the same, but some names and some rules differ. The IFRS Contrast boxes show where.

1.1  Who uses financial statements, and why?
A company collects thousands of facts every day: sales, purchases, salaries and loans. Financial statements summarize these facts in a standard form for people outside the company. The four main statements are the balance sheet, the income statement, the statement of changes in equity and the statement of cash flows.
Most companies prepare general-purpose financial statements. These are reports for many readers at the same time, not for one special reader. The U.S. conceptual framework names the most important readers. The primary users are existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors. They give resources (money, goods or credit) to the company. They usually cannot ask the company for special reports, so they depend on the published statements.
Each user makes a different decision, so each user looks for different information. Figure F01-02 summarizes the main users and their needs.

Other groups also read financial statements: employees, customers, governments, tax authorities and the public. Managers read them too. However, managers are not primary users of general-purpose statements, because they can get any internal information they need. Reporting for managers is management accounting, the subject of most of the CMA exam.
What do all these users need? In simple terms, they ask three questions.
What does the company have, and what does it owe? The balance sheet answers this.
How did the company perform in the period? The income statement answers this.
Where did cash come from, and where did it go? The statement of cash flows answers this.
Users also want to judge stewardship: how well the managers used the owners' resources.
What makes information useful?
The conceptual framework lists the qualities of useful information. There are two fundamental qualities. Relevance means the information can make a difference to a decision. It helps users predict the future (predictive value) or check their past predictions (confirmatory value). Relevance includes materiality: an item is material if leaving it out or stating it wrongly could change users' decisions.
Faithful representation means the information shows what it claims to show. It is complete, neutral and free from error. Four enhancing qualities make information even more useful: comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability. Finally, there is a cost constraint: the benefit of reporting information should be greater than its cost.
EXAM TRAP   Who is a primary user?
Primary users are investors, lenders and other creditors. Managers are users, but not primary users of general-purpose statements. Also remember: relevance and faithful representation are the two fundamental qualities. Comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability are only enhancing qualities.

1.2  The building blocks: elements and the accounting equation
Every amount in the financial statements belongs to an element. The FASB conceptual framework defines ten elements. Three of them describe the balance sheet at one date.
An asset is a present right of an entity to an economic benefit. Examples: cash, accounts receivable, inventory and equipment.
A liability is a present obligation of an entity to transfer an economic benefit. Examples: accounts payable, notes payable and wages payable.
Equity is the residual interest in the assets after the liabilities are deducted. It is the owners' claim.
The other elements describe changes during a period.
Revenues and expenses come from the company's central, ongoing operations. When Orontes sells olive oil, it earns revenue. When it pays plant workers, it has an expense.
Gains and losses come from peripheral or incidental events. If Orontes sells an old delivery truck for more than its carrying amount, it has a gain.
Investments by owners, such as buying new shares, increase equity. Distributions to owners, such as dividends, decrease equity.
Comprehensive income is the total change in equity from sources other than owners. You will meet it in Chapters 3 and 15.
The accounting equation connects the three balance sheet elements:
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
The equation always holds, because creditors (liabilities) or owners (equity) finance every asset. Equity has two main parts. Contributed capital is what owners paid in: common stock at its par value, plus additional paid-in capital (APIC). Retained earnings (RE) are the past net income that the company kept instead of paying it out as dividends. Figure F01-03 shows the full picture.
Figure F01-03. The accounting equation A = L + E, with equity split into contributed capital and retained earnings.
Look at the lower part of Figure F01-03. Revenues and gains increase retained earnings. Expenses and losses decrease it. Dividends also decrease it, but a dividend is not an expense. It is a distribution to owners, so it never appears in the income statement.
● IFRS CONTRAST   Same idea, different words
The IFRS Conceptual Framework (2018) defines an asset as a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. The idea is the same as in U.S. GAAP; only the wording is different. IFRS also uses the word income for both revenue and gains, while U.S. GAAP keeps revenues and gains apart. You may also know these IFRS names: statement of financial position (balance sheet), share capital (common stock) and share premium (additional paid-in capital).
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Words that look familiar
stock — In U.S. English, stock means shares: common stock, a stock dividend. In French and British English, stock often means inventory. In a CMA question, inventory is always inventory.
income and revenue — Arabic دخل is not إيراد. Revenue is the gross amount from sales. Net income is what remains after expenses. Do not use income when the question means revenue.
résultat and charges — In French, résultat is the profit or loss (net income), and charges are expenses. In English, charges usually means fees.
balance — In French, la balance is the trial balance. The balance sheet is le bilan.

1.3  Double entry: debits and credits
Accountants record every transaction with the double-entry system. Each transaction affects at least two accounts, and total debits always equal total credits. This is why the accounting equation always balances.
Debit simply means the left side of an account, and credit means the right side. Neither word means good or bad, and neither word always means increase. Whether a debit increases an account depends on the type of account. Each account has a normal balance: the side that increases it. Figure F01-04 shows the rules.
Figure F01-04. Debit and credit rules: which six account types increase with a debit and which with a credit.
The expanded equation at the bottom of Figure F01-04 is a useful memory aid. Accounts on the left side of the equation (assets, expenses and dividends) increase with a debit. Accounts on the right side (liabilities, equity and revenues) increase with a credit.
A contra account reduces a related account, so it has the opposite normal balance. For example, accumulated depreciation reduces equipment. It has a credit balance, but it belongs with the assets. The allowance for credit losses reduces accounts receivable in the same way (Chapter 6).

A dividend is not an expense. It reduces retained earnings, not net income.

1.4  The accrual basis and the matching principle
When should a company record revenues and expenses? U.S. GAAP financial statements use the accrual basis. Under the accrual basis, a company records revenue when it earns it, that is, when it delivers the goods or services. It records expenses when it incurs them. The timing of the cash does not decide the period. The cash basis records items only when cash is received or paid. The cash basis is simple, but it is not acceptable under U.S. GAAP.
Figure F01-07 shows why the difference matters. On December 20, 2025, Orontes ships olive oil to GreenBasket Supermarkets and sends an invoice for 90. The goods cost Orontes 54. GreenBasket pays on January 15, 2026.
Figure F01-07. Timeline of a December sale collected in January: accrual basis versus cash basis.
Under the accrual basis, Orontes records the revenue and the cost of goods sold in fiscal 2025, the year it delivered the goods. The gross profit of 36 belongs to 2025. In January, the cash receipt only changes one asset (the receivable) into another asset (cash). Under the cash basis, the revenue would fall in 2026, which is the wrong period.
The matching principle
The matching principle says: record expenses in the same period as the revenues they help to produce. There are three ways to apply it.
Cause and effect. Some costs link directly to a sale. Cost of goods sold is the classic example: Orontes records it at the same time as the sales revenue.
Systematic and rational allocation. Some costs help many periods, such as a bottling line. Depreciation spreads the cost over the years of use.
Immediate recognition. Some costs have no clear link to future revenue, such as most advertising and office salaries. They are expenses in the period in which they are incurred.

1.5  Who writes the rules? U.S. GAAP and IFRS
In the United States, the accounting rules are called U.S. GAAP (generally accepted accounting principles). The FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) writes them. The FASB is a private, independent organization. It keeps all U.S. GAAP in one place: the Accounting Standards Codification (ASC). The ASC is the single source of authoritative nongovernmental U.S. GAAP. It is organized by topic numbers. For example, ASC 606 covers revenue and ASC 842 covers leases. When the FASB changes a rule, it issues an Accounting Standards Update (ASU), which amends the Codification.
The SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) is a U.S. government agency. It has legal authority over the financial reporting of public companies, and it recognizes the FASB as the standard setter. For companies that report to the SEC, SEC rules are also part of authoritative GAAP. Do not confuse these bodies with the PCAOB, which oversees the audits of public companies. The PCAOB sets auditing standards, not accounting standards.
Outside the United States, many countries use IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards). The IASB (International Accounting Standards Board) issues them, under the IFRS Foundation. Many countries in the Middle East and North Africa, for example Jordan and the United Arab Emirates, require IFRS for listed companies. In this book, Cedar Retail S.A.L. is our IFRS company. Figure F01-08 shows both systems.
Figure F01-08. Who writes the rules: SEC, FASB and the ASC for U.S. GAAP; IFRS Foundation and IASB for IFRS.
The CMA exam tests U.S. GAAP. However, Section A also asks about the main differences between U.S. GAAP and IFRS (Chapter 17). If you learned accounting under IFRS, this is good news: most concepts are the same. Every chapter has an IFRS Contrast box, so Chapter 17 will be revision for you.
Both systems rest on a conceptual framework: the basic ideas behind the standards, such as the users, the elements and the qualities of useful information. You studied the U.S. version in Sections 1.1 and 1.2.

The four statements are linked. Figure F01-09 shows the two main links. Net income flows into retained earnings, and the net change in cash explains the cash balance on the balance sheet.
Figure F01-09. How the four statements connect through net income, retained earnings and cash.
You can see both links in the January transactions of Orontes. Net income was 80: revenue of 300, minus cost of goods sold of 180, minus wages of 40. The dividend declared was 50, so retained earnings rose by 30. Cash rose by 1,060: an operating outflow of 40, an investing outflow of 1,200 and a financing inflow of 2,300.
Notice that net income and the change in cash are very different numbers. This is the accrual basis at work.

Chapter summary
Figure F01-11 brings the chapter together. Each box now shows the key rule for its topic.
Figure F01-11. Chapter 1 summary map: the key rule for each of the seven topics.
General-purpose financial statements serve investors, lenders and other creditors. Useful information is relevant and faithfully represented. The statements are built from elements that the accounting equation links: assets equal liabilities plus equity. Every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits. The accrual basis records revenues when they are earned and expenses when they are incurred, and it matches expenses to the revenues they help to produce. The FASB writes U.S. GAAP in the ASC, and the IASB writes IFRS. The CMA exam uses U.S. GAAP and tests the main IFRS differences. The balance sheet shows one date, while the other three statements cover a period, and net income and cash link them.

Written task: model answer
A cash dividend is a distribution of the company's resources to its owners. It is not an expense, because it does not help the company earn revenue; it simply returns part of the profits to the shareholders. The income statement shows only revenues, expenses, gains and losses, so the dividend does not reduce net income. Instead, the dividend reduces retained earnings directly, and the statement of changes in equity shows this reduction.
A good answer: says a dividend is a distribution to owners; says it is not an expense; says net income is not affected; says retained earnings decrease; names the statement of changes in equity (bonus).
