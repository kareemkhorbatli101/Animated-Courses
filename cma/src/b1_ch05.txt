WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
IFRS uses the same three sections, but IAS 7 lets companies choose where to put interest and dividends. U.S. GAAP gives no choice. If you learned the IFRS habit of putting interest paid in financing, you must unlearn it for this exam.
5.1  Purpose and structure of the statement of cash flows
The statement of cash flows reports a company's cash receipts and cash payments for a period. Users need it because profit is not cash. It helps them judge whether the company can produce cash, pay its debts and dividends, and fund its growth without new financing.
The statement explains the change in cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash together. Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments with an original maturity to the holder of three months or less. Moving money between these categories is not a cash flow.
Cash flows are grouped into operating activities, investing activities and financing activities. Figure F05-02 is Orontes's statement for 2025. Operating activities provided $7,531,100, investing used $4,179,000, and financing provided $1,850,000. Cash rose by $5,202,100, to the $7,302,100 shown on the balance sheet.

5.2  Classifying cash flows
Operating activities are the main revenue activities: cash from customers and cash paid to suppliers and employees. Anything that is not investing or financing is also operating. Investing activities involve long-term assets and other companies' securities: Orontes paid $3,000,000 for equipment and $1,200,000 for its Jordan Glass shares.
Financing activities are with owners and lenders. They include issuing and buying back shares, borrowing and repaying principal, and paying dividends. Orontes received $1,500,000 from its share issue and $800,000 from the bank note, repaid $400,000 of debt and paid $50,000 of dividends.
U.S. GAAP fixes four items that students often get wrong. Interest paid, interest received, dividends received and income taxes are all operating. Only dividends paid are financing. Figure F05-04 compares these rules with IFRS.

5.3  The indirect method
Most U.S. companies use the indirect method for operating activities. It starts with net income and removes everything that is not an operating cash flow. First, add back noncash expenses and losses, such as depreciation, impairments and an increase in deferred tax liabilities.
Second, remove gains and losses on investing and financing items; the cash from those sales belongs in investing. Also subtract equity-method income, which is not cash, and add the dividends received from the investee.
Third, adjust for changes in current operating assets and liabilities from the balance sheet. When receivables rise, the company has recorded revenue it has not yet collected, so the increase is subtracted. Figure F05-06 gives the sign rules.
Figure F05-06. Sign rules for the indirect method, with Orontes examples.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: Orontes's operating cash flow
Step 1. Start with net income: $2,969,100.
Step 2. Add depreciation, impairments and deferred taxes, which used no cash: $4,924,000.
Step 3. Subtract equity-method income of $177,000, add dividends from investees of $70,000, and subtract the oven gain of $3,000 and unrealized gains of $4,000.
Step 4. Working-capital changes reduce cash by a net $248,000.
Check. The total, $7,531,100, equals net cash from operating activities in Figure F05-02.
EXAM TRAP   Indirect-method traps
Start from net income, never from operating income.
Dividends payable and notes payable are financing items. Do not include their changes in working capital.
Show the full proceeds of a sale in investing, and remove the gain from operating.

5.4  The direct method and required disclosures
The direct method shows the main classes of operating receipts and payments, such as cash from customers and cash paid to suppliers. The FASB encourages it, but few companies use it. A company that uses it must still show the reconciliation of net income to operating cash flow.

5.5  Limitations and links between the four statements
Cash flows are harder to manage than profits, but not impossible. A company can raise operating cash near year-end by delaying payments to suppliers or by selling receivables. The statement also shows only one year, and it is not a measure of profit.
Important noncash deals, such as buying a building with a mortgage, appear only in the notes. Some classifications are also arbitrary: interest paid is operating under U.S. GAAP but may be financing under IFRS, which makes companies harder to compare.
Finally, one year of cash flows can mislead. A company that stops investing will show strong cash flow for a while, even if its future is weak.
Figure F05-08 shows how Orontes's four statements fit together. Net income starts the statement of cash flows and increases retained earnings. Changes in balance sheet accounts drive the working-capital adjustments. Closing cash equals the cash on the balance sheet.

WATCH: NOT YET TESTABLE   Proposals and future changes
In August 2026 the FASB proposed new disclosures about the parts of cash equivalents and about some digital assets. It is only a proposal; the definition of cash equivalents does not change. ASU 2023-09 already requires income taxes paid by jurisdiction; know this at awareness level.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Tableau de financement
French tableau de financement is an older funds statement based on working capital, not a statement of cash flows. The IFRS term is tableau des flux de trésorerie. Arabic قائمة التدفقات النقدية is the correct term for this statement.

Chapter summary
Figure F05-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F05-09. Chapter 5 summary map: the key rule for each section.
The statement of cash flows explains the change in cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash in three activities. Under U.S. GAAP, interest, dividends received and income taxes are operating, and dividends paid are financing; noncash investing and financing deals are disclosed, not shown in the body. The indirect method starts with net income, adds noncash expenses, removes gains and equity-method income, and adjusts for working capital. The direct method shows receipts and payments but still requires the reconciliation, and indirect-method users disclose interest and taxes paid. Closing cash equals balance sheet cash, and all four statements come from one set of records. Part I is now complete.
