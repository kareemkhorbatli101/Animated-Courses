Chapter 9
Investments in Debt and Equity Securities
الاستثمار في أدوات الدين وأدوات حقوق الملكية
Companies invest spare cash in bonds and shares, and they buy shares in suppliers and partners. U.S. GAAP measures these investments in different ways, depending on what the company intends to do with them and how much influence it has. In this chapter you learn the categories, how each one is measured, and where the gains and losses go.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
If you studied IFRS 9, you classify financial assets by business model and cash flows. U.S. GAAP classifies debt by management's intent, and it has one big surprise for IFRS readers: there is no option to put gains on shares in OCI. If you studied the older IAS 39, the words available-for-sale and held-to-maturity will feel familiar.
9.1  Three categories of debt securities
A debt security gives its holder a right to interest and to repayment, like a bond. An equity security gives an ownership interest, like shares of common stock. This section is about debt securities. Section 9.4 covers equity securities.
When a company buys a debt security, it classifies it into one of three categories, and it reassesses the classification at each reporting date. The category depends on management's intent and ability, not on the type of bond. Figure F09-02 shows the questions.
Trading securities are bought and held mainly to sell in the near term.
Held-to-maturity (HTM) securities are securities the company has the positive intent and the ability to hold until they mature.
Available-for-sale (AFS) securities are all other debt securities.
Figure F09-02. Decision tree for classifying a debt security as trading, held-to-maturity or available-for-sale.
HTM is a strict category. If the company would sell the bond when interest rates change or when it needs cash, it does not have the intent to hold it. And if it sells or transfers HTM securities for other reasons, it taints the whole HTM portfolio, which raises doubt about its intent for all its other HTM securities. The standard allows a few exceptions, such as a sale after the issuer's credit quality falls sharply, or a sale very close to maturity.
A company may also elect the fair value option for a security when it first recognizes it. The election is irrevocable, and all changes in fair value then go to net income.
EXAM TRAP   Intent decides
The same bond can be trading, AFS or HTM. Read the facts about intent and ability.
A company that might sell when rates change cannot use HTM.
Equity securities are never trading, AFS or HTM under current U.S. GAAP.

Interest revenue: the effective-interest method
A bond is bought at a bond premium when its stated interest rate is higher than the market rate, and at a bond discount when it is lower. Under the effective-interest method, interest revenue for each year is the carrying amount at the start of the year multiplied by the market rate at purchase. The cash received is the face amount multiplied by the stated rate. The difference amortizes the premium or discount, so the carrying amount moves toward face value.

On January 1, 2025, Orontes buys a 3-year bond with a face amount of $100,000 and a stated rate of 6%, paid each December 31. The market rate is 5%, so Orontes pays $102,723, a premium of $2,723.

Selling an AFS security: the reclassification adjustment
While Orontes holds an AFS bond, its unrealized gains and losses build up in accumulated OCI, part of equity. When Orontes sells the bond, the realized gain or loss moves to net income through a reclassification adjustment, sometimes called recycling. Figure F09-05 follows the bond until Orontes sells it at the end of 2026.
Figure F09-05. Timeline of an available-for-sale bond: OCI while held, recycled to net income on sale.
On the sale, the realized gain is the price minus the amortized cost: $101,500 − $100,952 = $548. Accumulated OCI for the bond returns to zero, so the gain is never counted twice.
Transfers between categories
Transfers between categories are made at fair value. When a security moves into trading, any unrealized gain or loss not yet in net income goes to net income immediately. When an AFS security moves to HTM, the unrealized amount stays in accumulated OCI and is amortized over the remaining life. Transfers into or out of trading should be rare.
EXAM TRAP   Where does it go?
Trading: fair value changes in net income. AFS: in OCI. HTM: not recognized.
Interest revenue = beginning carrying amount × market rate, not face × stated rate.
On sale of AFS debt, recycle the accumulated OCI; do not count the gain twice.

9.3  Credit losses on debt securities
HTM securities are measured at amortized cost, so they follow CECL, like the receivables in Chapter 6: the company records an allowance for lifetime expected credit losses from day one. For very safe bonds, such as U.S. Treasury bonds, the expected loss can be zero.
AFS securities follow a different model. A security is impaired when its fair value is below its amortized cost. If the company intends to sell it, or will more likely than not have to sell it before it recovers, it writes the security down to fair value through net income. Otherwise it separates the decline into two parts. The credit part is recorded in an allowance through net income. The rest of the decline goes to OCI.
WORKED EXAMPLE   The fair-value floor
An AFS bond has an amortized cost of $50,000 and a fair value of $44,000. The allowance can never be larger than amortized cost minus fair value: $6,000.
Case A: the present value of expected cash flows is $47,000. The credit loss is $3,000 (allowance, net income), and the other $3,000 of the decline goes to OCI.
Case B: the present value of expected cash flows is $42,000. The credit loss would be $8,000, but the allowance is limited to $6,000. Nothing goes to OCI.
If credit quality improves later, the allowance can be reversed through net income.
EXAM TRAP   The AFS floor
AFS allowance ≤ amortized cost − fair value. HTM has no such limit.
If the company intends to sell, there is no allowance: write the bond down to fair value.

9.4  Equity securities and the equity method
For shares, the key question is how much influence the investor has over the investee, the company whose shares it holds. Figure F09-06 shows the three levels.
Figure F09-06. Influence spectrum: fair value, the equity method or consolidation, by share of voting stock.
No significant influence: fair value through net income
Shares with a readily determinable fair value are measured at fair value, and all changes go to net income. There is no AFS category and no OCI option for shares. Dividends received are income. For example, Orontes bought listed shares for $20,000; at year-end they are worth $23,500, and Orontes received dividends of $400. Net income includes an unrealized gain of $3,500 and dividend income of $400.
For shares with no readily determinable fair value, such as shares of a private company, the investor may use the measurement alternative: cost minus impairment, adjusted when an orderly transaction in the same or a similar share shows a new price.
Significant influence: the equity method
An investor with significant influence can take part in the investee's financial and operating decisions. Influence is presumed when the investor holds 20% to 50% of the voting stock, but the evidence decides: a board seat, participation in policy decisions or large transactions between the companies can show influence even below 20%. With significant influence, the investor uses the equity method.
Under the equity method, the investment starts at cost. It increases by the investor's share of the investee's net income and decreases by the dividends the investor receives. The dividends are not income, because they only return part of the investment.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Orontes and Jordan Glass Co.
On January 1, 2025, Orontes buys 30% of Jordan Glass Co., its bottle supplier, for $1,200,000. Jordan Glass's net assets have a book value of $3,000,000, so Orontes's share of book value is $900,000. The basis difference of $300,000 has two parts: $30,000 for Orontes's share of equipment worth more than its book value, which is depreciated over 10 years ($3,000 a year), and $270,000 of goodwill, which is not amortized.
In 2025 Jordan Glass earns $500,000 and pays dividends of $200,000. Figure F09-07 shows the investment account.
Figure F09-07. Equity-method T-account for Orontes's 30% investment in Jordan Glass Co., 2025.
Orontes reports equity-method income of $147,000 and an investment of $1,287,000 at the end of 2025. The dividends of $60,000 are cash in and investment out; they do not change net income.

Three more equity-method rules appear in exam questions. Losses reduce the investment only to zero, unless the investor has guaranteed the investee's debts. Profits on sales between the two companies are eliminated until the goods are sold to outsiders. And when an investor first gains significant influence, it starts the equity method from that date; it does not restate prior years. An investor may also elect the fair value option for an equity-method investment.
Crypto assets that meet the ASU 2023-08 definition are measured at fair value through net income. This rule became effective for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2024, so it is now testable.
● IFRS CONTRAST   IFRS 9 and IAS 28
IFRS 9 classifies debt by the business model and the cash flows (amortized cost, FVOCI or FVPL), not by intent. For shares, IFRS 9 allows an irrevocable election to put fair value changes in OCI, with no recycling on sale; U.S. GAAP has no such option. Under IAS 28, an equity-method investment that is impaired can later be written back up; under U.S. GAAP the impairment is not reversed.
EXAM TRAP   Dividends and percentages
Equity method: dividends reduce the investment. They are not income.
Take only the investor's share of the investee's income.
Depreciate the part of the basis difference assigned to assets; do not amortize the goodwill.

Chapter summary
Figure F09-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F09-09. Chapter 9 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Debt securities are trading, held-to-maturity or available-for-sale, depending on intent and ability. Trading and AFS securities are at fair value; trading gains and losses go to net income and AFS gains and losses go to OCI until the sale, when they are reclassified. HTM securities stay at amortized cost, and interest revenue always follows the effective-interest method. Credit losses use CECL for HTM and an allowance limited by fair value for AFS. Shares without significant influence are at fair value through net income. With significant influence, the equity method adds the investor's share of income and subtracts dividends.

Written task: model answer
The category of a bond depends on what management intends to do with it. If the bond is trading, changes in its fair value go to net income each year. If it is available-for-sale, the changes go to OCI and reach net income only when the bond is sold. If it is held-to-maturity, fair value changes are not recognized at all; only interest revenue affects income. Jordan Glass is under the equity method, so Orontes already records its share of Jordan Glass's income; the dividends simply return part of the investment, so they reduce the investment instead of increasing income.
A good answer: explains that intent decides the category; states the treatment of each of the three categories; mentions reclassification from OCI on sale; explains why dividends reduce the equity-method investment; uses the Orontes context (bonus).
