WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
If you studied IFRS 15, you already know the five steps: the FASB and the IASB wrote the two standards together. The main differences are small, and Section 11.6 lists them. The words are the same too, so learn them exactly.
11.1  Revenue and the core principle
Revenue is the inflow from a company's main activities, such as selling goods or providing services. Under ASC 606, a company recognizes revenue when it transfers control of promised goods or services to a customer. It measures revenue at the amount it expects to be entitled to in exchange.
Control means the customer can direct the use of the asset and obtain almost all of its benefits. The key question is therefore not when the company is paid, but when the customer gains control. Cash can arrive before, at or after that moment.
ASC 606 applies one model to all contracts with customers: goods, services, construction and software. The model has five steps. Each step answers one question, and each uses the answer to the step before.

11.2  Steps 1 and 2: the contract and its performance obligations
Step 1: identify the contract. A contract exists only if the parties have approved it and are committed to it, each party's rights and the payment terms can be identified, and the contract has commercial substance. Finally, it must be probable that the company will collect substantially all of the consideration.
If these criteria are not met, cash received from the customer is a liability, not revenue. In U.S. GAAP, probable means likely: a higher level than the IFRS meaning of more likely than not.
Step 2: identify the performance obligations. Each promise to transfer a distinct good or service is a separate obligation. A good or service is distinct if the customer can benefit from it on its own and it is separately identifiable in the contract, not highly dependent on other promises.
Figure F11-02 applies the five steps to a contract that Orontes signs with GreenBasket on October 1, 2026, for $1,080,000. GreenBasket buys olive oil, a beverage dispenser installed in its stores, and two years of dispenser maintenance. Each can be bought separately, so there are three performance obligations.

11.3  Steps 3 and 4: the transaction price and its allocation
Step 3: determine the transaction price, the amount the company expects to receive. It includes variable consideration, such as rebates and bonuses. The company estimates it using the expected value, for many possible outcomes, or the most likely amount, for an all-or-nothing outcome.
Variable amounts are included only to the extent that a significant reversal of revenue is not probable later. This is the constraint on variable consideration. Figure F11-03 compares the two estimation methods for a volume rebate.

Three other items change the transaction price. A significant financing component is removed when payment and delivery are far apart; if they are within one year, the company may ignore it. Consideration payable to a customer, such as a slotting fee paid to a supermarket for shelf space, reduces revenue. Sales taxes and VAT collected may be excluded from revenue by policy.
Step 4: allocate the transaction price to the performance obligations in proportion to their standalone selling prices. When an observable price is not available, the company estimates it, for example from market prices or expected cost plus a margin. The residual approach is allowed only when a price is highly variable or uncertain.
A discount is normally shared by all obligations. Figure F11-04 shows the GreenBasket contract: standalone prices total $1,200,000, but the contract price is $1,080,000. Each obligation therefore receives 90% of its standalone price.
Figure F11-04. Allocating the GreenBasket discount by relative standalone selling price.
EXAM TRAP   Price and allocation traps
Revenue for each obligation is its allocated price, not its standalone selling price.
A slotting fee paid to a customer reduces revenue; it is not a marketing expense unless Orontes receives a distinct service.
Do not use the residual approach when standalone prices can be observed.

11.4  Step 5: recognizing revenue over time or at a point in time
Step 5: recognize revenue when, or as, each obligation is satisfied. An obligation is satisfied over time if the customer receives the benefit as the company performs, if the company improves an asset the customer controls, or if the asset has no other use and the company has a right to payment for work done so far.
Progress over time is measured by an output method, such as units delivered, or an input method, such as costs incurred. If no over-time criterion is met, revenue is recognized at a point in time: when control passes. Figure F11-05 shows the test.
Figure F11-05. Decision chart: is revenue recognized over time or at a point in time?.

11.5  Special situations
When another party is involved, the company must decide whether it is a principal or an agent. A principal controls the good or service before it is transferred and reports revenue gross. An agent only arranges the sale and reports its fee or commission as revenue. Figure F11-08 shows the difference for a drop shipment by Barada.
Figure F11-08. Principal or agent: gross or net revenue on a drop shipment.
When customers may return goods, the company recognizes revenue only for goods it does not expect to be returned. It records a refund liability for expected refunds and a separate asset for its right to recover the returned goods. The asset and the liability are not netted.

Control is also the test in other cases. Goods sent on consignment are not sold until the consignee sells them. In a bill-and-hold arrangement, revenue is recognized only if the goods are identified, ready and cannot be used for other customers, and the customer asked for the arrangement. A promise to buy the goods back usually means no sale has taken place.
Customer options can create a material right, such as loyalty points, which is a separate obligation. For gift cards, the company recognizes breakage, the part customers are not expected to use, in proportion to the cards that are used. Nonrefundable upfront fees are usually deferred. Chapter 12 explains warranties.
EXAM TRAP   Special-situation traps
An agent's revenue is its commission, not the full price the customer pays.
Present the refund liability and the return asset separately.
Goods on consignment stay in the consignor's inventory.

11.6  Matching, contract costs and IFRS differences
The matching principle says that expenses are recognized in the same period as the revenues they help to earn. Cost of goods sold is recognized when the related sale is recognized. Costs with no future benefit, such as most advertising, are expensed at once, and long-lived assets are depreciated over the periods they serve.
Contract costs follow the same idea. Incremental costs of obtaining a contract, such as sales commissions, are capitalized if the company expects to recover them. Orontes pays its salesperson a 3% commission, $32,400, on the GreenBasket contract. It capitalizes this amount and expenses it as it transfers the goods and services.
If the amortization period is one year or less, the company may expense such costs immediately. Costs to fulfill a contract are capitalized only if they relate directly to it and create a resource that will be used. Under U.S. GAAP, an impairment of these assets is never reversed.

Chapter summary
Figure F11-11 brings the chapter together.
Figure F11-11. Chapter 11 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Revenue is recognized when control of goods or services passes to the customer, at the amount the company expects to receive. The five steps are: identify the contract, identify the performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it by standalone selling prices, and recognize revenue when or as each obligation is satisfied. Variable consideration is estimated and constrained, and payments to customers reduce revenue. Agents report net revenue, expected returns create a refund liability and a separate asset, and consignment is not a sale. Commissions are capitalized and expensed as revenue is earned, following the matching principle.

Written task: model answer
The transaction price is not the fixed price of 600: Orontes deducts the expected rebate of 30 and the listing fee of 10, which leaves 560. Orontes must allocate this price between the juices and the training in proportion to their standalone prices. The training was completed in September, so its 56 is revenue in 2026. Each monthly juice delivery transfers control at a point in time, so 2026 includes four deliveries of 42. Total revenue is 224, but Qasr has paid 250, so Orontes reports a contract liability of 26.
A good answer: deducts the rebate and the listing fee to get the transaction price; allocates by standalone selling prices; recognizes the training when completed; recognizes juice revenue at each delivery (point in time); explains the contract liability as cash received before performance.
