Chapter 4 compared an activity-based budget with last year plus a percentage. Book 2, Chapter 3 split costs into fixed and variable parts. Both ideas are used again here.
5.1  Zero-based budgeting
Incremental budgeting starts from last year's figures and adds or subtracts a change. Zero-based budgeting starts from zero every year: each activity must be justified again, as if it were new. It suits discretionary costs, such as head-office services, where spending can grow without a clear reason.
The budget is built from decision packages. Each package describes an activity, its cost and its benefit; managers rank the packages, and top management funds them down the ranking until the money runs out. Figure F305-02 shows the process.
Figure F305-02. The zero-based budgeting process.

5.2  Continuous (rolling) budgets
A continuous (rolling) budget always covers the same length of time ahead, usually twelve months. When a month or quarter ends, it is dropped and a new one is added at the end. It suits fast-changing markets, like juice.
Figure F305-04 shows how the Beverages budget moves forward one quarter. The quarters in between are also revised with the latest information.
Figure F305-04. A continuous (rolling) budget moves forward one quarter.

5.3  Flexible budgets
A static budget is prepared for one planned level of activity. A flexible budget can be prepared for any level, using the cost formula of each cost: fixed cost plus variable cost per unit. It is used mainly to compare actual costs with what they should have been at the actual volume.
The Beverages cost formula for a month is $350,000 of fixed costs plus $11.80 of variable cost per case. The variable cost is $10.60 of manufacturing cost from the Book 2 card and $1.20 of selling cost. Figure F305-06 draws it as a line.

5.5  The effect of incremental changes
Managers often ask how a change would affect the budget: a new price, more volume, a higher cost. The right measure is the change in contribution margin, and the answer must include every effect of the change, such as lost volume after a price rise.

Sales proposes a 5% price rise to $21.00 and expects to lose 3% of volume. Figure F305-11 compares the contribution margin: $7,380,000 now and $8,031,600 after the change.

Chapter summary
Figure F305-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F305-14. Chapter 5 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Zero-based budgeting justifies every activity from zero through ranked decision packages and funds the approved amounts. A continuous (rolling) budget drops each finished period, adds a new one and revises the rest. A flexible budget uses the cost formula to budget any level of activity, which makes fair comparison possible when volume changes. The system is chosen to fit the situation, and changes are measured by their effect on contribution margin, including any change in volume.

Case question: the Tahini & Spreads flexible budget
Last month the Tahini & Spreads division sold fewer 500 g tahini jars than planned, because a large Gulf order arrived late. The division's static budget showed a healthy operating income, and the actual result was much lower. The division manager, Samir, says his costs were well controlled; the head-office review team is not so sure. The late order came from the Gulf importer, and the jars were delivered in the first week of the following month instead.
The controller, Rana, wants to judge the month fairly. She has the selling price and variable cost per jar, the fixed costs for the month, the planned number of jars and the number actually sold.
The review team also asks two wider questions. How much would operating income rise if the late order of ten thousand jars had arrived in time? And which budget systems should the division use next year, given that its head-office charges have grown for years and its sales change quickly?
Rana will present her answers at the monthly review meeting. She will also explain to Samir why the comparison she uses is fair to him, even though his result looks poor at first sight.
