Chapter 6
Receivables: Credit Losses and Transfers
الذمم المدينة: الخسائر الائتمانية وتحويل الذمم
When a company sells on credit, it gives its customers time to pay. The promise to pay is an asset: a receivable. But not every customer will pay. In this chapter you learn when to record a receivable and how to estimate the part that will not be collected. You also learn what happens when a company sells its receivables to raise cash quickly.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
You know the allowance for doubtful accounts from your earlier studies. U.S. GAAP now calls it the allowance for credit losses. Since CECL, it must cover all the losses the company expects over the life of its receivables, not only losses that have already happened. If you know IFRS 9, the simplified approach for trade receivables is very close to CECL.
6.1  Recognizing and measuring receivables
A company records an account receivable when its right to be paid is unconditional: only the passage of time is needed before payment is due. This usually happens when it delivers the goods or services and transfers control (Chapter 11). If the company still has to do something else before it can bill, for example finish a second delivery, it has a contract asset, not a receivable.
Figure F06-02 follows one receivable from Chapter 1: Orontes delivered olive oil to GreenBasket Supermarkets on January 18 and collected part of the amount on February 8.
Figure F06-02. Timeline of a receivable from order to collection, using Orontes and GreenBasket data.
The receivable is measured at the transaction price: the amount the company expects to receive. Expected returns, rebates and cash discount amounts are variable consideration, so they reduce revenue and the receivable. Some older questions still use the gross and net methods. Under the gross method, the company records the full invoice and records a sales discount only when a customer pays early. Under the net method, it records the sale net of the discount, and records sales discounts forfeited when a customer pays late.
Trade receivables that are due within one year are not discounted to present value. Notes receivable are different. An interest-bearing note is carried at its face amount plus accrued interest. A note with no interest, or with a rate below the market rate, is recorded at its present value. The discount is then amortized to interest revenue over the life of the note.
EXAM TRAP   Receivable or contract asset?
Receivable: only time must pass before payment. Contract asset: the company must still perform.
Expected cash discounts and returns reduce the receivable at the start, not only when they happen.

6.2  The allowance for credit losses
Under current expected credit losses (CECL), a company presents its receivables net of an allowance that covers all the credit losses it expects over their life. It records these expected losses from the first day. It does not wait until a loss becomes probable. The estimate uses past experience, current conditions and reasonable and supportable forecasts.
Trade receivables are measured at amortized cost, and the allowance is a contra-asset account. The balance sheet shows receivables minus the allowance: the amount the company expects to collect.
Estimating the allowance with an aging schedule
A common method is the aging schedule. The company groups its receivables by how long they are past due and applies an expected loss rate to each group. The rates start from history and are adjusted for current conditions and forecasts. The total is the balance the allowance must have at the end of the year.

Write-offs and recoveries
When a customer's balance is clearly uncollectible, the company records a write-off: debit the allowance and credit accounts receivable. The write-off changes neither net income nor net receivables, because the expense was already recorded when the allowance was built. If the customer later pays, the company records a recovery: it first reinstates the receivable (debit accounts receivable, credit allowance) and then records the cash collection.
Figure F06-05 compares the allowance method with the direct write-off method, which records an expense only when a balance is written off. The direct method is used for U.S. tax returns. It is not GAAP when bad debts are material, because it records losses late and shows receivables at a gross amount.

WATCH: NOT YET TESTABLE   ASU 2025-05
In 2025 the FASB added a practical expedient: when forecasting losses on current receivables, any company may assume that conditions at the balance sheet date will not change. Private companies may also use cash collected after year-end. It applies to annual periods beginning after December 15, 2025, so under the one-year rule it is not yet tested on the CMA exam.
● IFRS CONTRAST   IFRS 9: expected losses, too
IFRS 9 also uses expected losses. For trade receivables it uses a simplified approach that always records lifetime losses, often with a provision matrix, which is very close to CECL. For other financial assets, IFRS 9 starts with 12-month expected losses and moves to lifetime losses only after credit risk increases significantly. CECL records lifetime losses from day one for all assets at amortized cost.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Allowance or provision?
Arabic مخصص covers two different things. An allowance (مخصص الخسائر الائتمانية) reduces an asset. A provision under IAS 37 is a liability. In a U.S. GAAP answer, always write allowance for credit losses, never provision for doubtful debts or reserve for bad debts.
EXAM TRAP   Balance first, then the expense
The aging gives the required ENDING allowance, not the expense.
Expense = required balance − unadjusted credit balance, or required balance + unadjusted debit balance.
A write-off does not change net income or net receivables.

6.3  Transferring receivables: sale or secured borrowing?
A company that needs cash before its customers pay can use its receivables in two ways. It can use them as collateral for a loan (pledging), or it can transfer them to a finance company called a factor. This is factoring. The accounting question is simple: is the transfer a sale, or is it really a loan?
U.S. GAAP treats a transfer as a sale only if three conditions are all met. First, legal isolation: the receivables are beyond the reach of the seller and its creditors, even in bankruptcy. Second, the factor can pledge or exchange the receivables. Third, the seller does not keep effective control, for example through an agreement to buy the receivables back before they mature. If any condition fails, the transfer is a secured borrowing. Figure F06-07 shows the test.
Figure F06-07. Decision tree: is a transfer of receivables a sale or a secured borrowing?.
Recourse is the factor's right to collect from the seller if customers do not pay. Recourse does not by itself make the transfer a borrowing. If the three conditions are met, a transfer with recourse is still a sale. The seller then records a recourse liability at its fair value, which increases the loss on the sale.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Orontes factors its receivables
Orontes transfers receivables with a carrying amount of $500,000 to Gulf Factor Co. The factor charges a fee of 3% ($15,000) and keeps a holdback (due from factor) of 5% ($25,000) to cover returns and discounts. Orontes receives $460,000 in cash.
Sale without recourse: debit Cash $460,000, debit Due from factor $25,000, debit Loss on sale of receivables $15,000; credit Accounts receivable $500,000.
Sale with recourse (recourse liability $6,000): the same entry, but the loss is $21,000 and Orontes also credits Recourse liability $6,000.
Secured borrowing: debit Cash $460,000, debit Interest expense $15,000; credit Liability to factor $475,000. The receivables stay on the balance sheet, marked as pledged.
Figure F06-08 compares the three treatments. Loss on a sale = carrying amount − (cash + due from factor − recourse liability). With recourse: $500,000 − ($460,000 + $25,000 − $6,000) = $21,000.
Figure F06-08. Effects of one transfer as a sale without recourse, a sale with recourse, or a secured borrowing.
The balance sheet effect is the main reason companies care. A sale removes the receivables and adds little or no debt, so ratios such as debt to equity look better. A secured borrowing keeps the receivables and adds a liability. Lenders and analysts read the notes to see which receivables are pledged or transferred.
● IFRS CONTRAST   IFRS 9 looks at risks and rewards
IFRS 9 asks first whether the seller has transferred substantially all the risks and rewards of the receivables. Factoring with substantial recourse usually keeps most of the risk with the seller, so under IFRS it often fails derecognition and is treated as borrowing. Under U.S. GAAP the same transfer may qualify as a sale if the three ASC 860 conditions are met.
EXAM TRAP   Recourse is not the test
Do not assume that every transfer with recourse is a borrowing. Apply the three conditions.
The holdback is an asset (due from factor), not part of the loss.
Pledged receivables stay on the balance sheet.

Chapter summary
Figure F06-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F06-09. Chapter 6 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A receivable is recorded when only time must pass before payment, at the amount the company expects to receive. Under CECL, receivables are shown net of an allowance for lifetime expected credit losses. The aging schedule gives the required allowance, and the credit loss expense is the amount needed to reach it. Write-offs and recoveries change neither income nor net receivables. A transfer of receivables is a sale only if they are legally isolated, the factor can pledge them, and the seller has no effective control. Otherwise it is a secured borrowing. Recourse adds a liability to a sale, and the holdback is an asset.

Written task: model answer
Under CECL, Orontes records expected losses in the allowance when it makes the sales, so the expense is already in profit. A write-off only removes a balance that the allowance already covers: receivables and the allowance fall by the same amount, and profit does not change. Factoring with recourse can still be a sale if the receivables are legally isolated, the factor can pledge them, and Orontes has no effective control, such as a duty to buy them back. In that case Orontes removes the receivables and records a recourse liability at fair value, which increases the loss on the sale.
