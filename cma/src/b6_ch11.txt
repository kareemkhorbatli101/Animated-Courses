Chapter 11
Dividends, dividend policy, debt against equity, and leasing
التوزيعات وسياستها والتمويل بالدين مقابل حقوق الملكية والتأجير
A company that has earned money has to decide what to do with it, and a company that needs money has to decide where to get it. This chapter takes both decisions: the first as dividend policy, the second as the choice between debt, equity and a lease.
They are the same decision from two sides. Every dollar paid out is a dollar that must be raised again if it is needed, at the costs Chapter 8 computed; every dollar retained is a dollar the shareholders did not receive and on which they require a return. The chapter ends on leasing, because a lease is the one financing route that can be compared with borrowing by a single arithmetic test — and the exam asks for that test.

11.1  The kinds of dividend
Four distributions appear in exam questions and only the first moves cash.
A cash dividend is a payment out of retained earnings. It reduces cash and reduces equity by the same amount, and it does not touch the income statement — a distribution is not an expense. Orontes declared $450,000 for 2025, of which $50,000 was paid in cash during the year and $400,000 stood as a dividend payable at December 31.
A stock dividend is a distribution of additional shares. Nothing leaves the company. A holder of 100 shares who receives a 10 per cent stock dividend holds 110 shares of a company worth exactly what it was worth before, so each share is worth proportionately less. In the accounts, a small stock dividend transfers an amount from retained earnings to common stock and additional paid-in capital at the market value of the shares issued: on Orontes's 8,100,000 shares a 10 per cent stock dividend would issue 810,000 shares, and at $9.25 that is 810,000 × 9.25 = 7,492,500 moved within equity. Earnings per share falls mechanically to 2,969,100 ÷ 8,910,000 = 0.3332.
A stock split divides each share into more shares. A two-for-one split on Orontes gives 16,200,000 shares, each worth about half as much, and earnings per share becomes 2,969,100 ÷ 16,200,000 = 0.1833. Accounting-wise a split changes the par value and transfers nothing; economically it is identical to a stock dividend, and the reason both exist is a convention about size — a distribution of more than about a quarter is treated as a split.
A property dividend distributes an asset rather than cash, and a liquidating dividend returns capital rather than profit, which reduces paid-in capital and is a signal in its own right.
Why issue shares that are worth nothing new? Three reasons the exam accepts. To bring a high share price into a range where small investors trade more easily. To signal confidence, since a board that splits usually expects the price to keep rising. And to conserve cash while appearing to distribute something, which is the least respectable of the three and the commonest.

11.2  How a dividend is paid
Four dates govern the process, and the exam asks which one matters.
The declaration date is when the board declares the dividend. At that moment a legal liability arises: Orontes's $400,000 dividend payable exists because the board declared before the year end and had not yet paid.
The date of record is the date on which the company looks at its share register. Whoever is on it receives the dividend.
The ex-dividend date is one business day before the record date in most markets, and it is the one that matters to an investor. A share bought on or after it does not carry the dividend. On the ex-dividend date the price falls by approximately the dividend, because the buyer no longer receives it: Orontes's $9.25 share would open at about 9.25 − 0.0556 = 9.1944, before any other news.
The payment date is when the cash goes out.
For preferred stock the process is the same with one addition. The preferred dividend must be declared and paid in full before any common dividend, and where the stock is cumulative, any arrears from earlier years must be cleared first. Preferred dividends are usually at a fixed rate on par and are declared on a regular cycle, so the discretion a board exercises over the common dividend barely exists for the preferred — in practice if not in law.

EXAM TRAP   Which date, and which effect
The liability arises on declaration, not on payment. Orontes's balance sheet carries $400,000 of dividends payable for exactly that reason.
The price adjusts on the ex-dividend date, not on the payment date. Buying the day before the ex-date to capture the dividend gains nothing, because the price falls by it.
A dividend is not an expense and never reduces net income. It reduces retained earnings.

11.3  What decides a dividend policy
A dividend policy is the rule a board follows about how much of its profit to distribute. Eight factors influence it, and the exam expects them named.
Legal and contractual constraints. Distributions are limited by law to profits available for the purpose, and covenants in loan agreements often restrict them further — a lender that has just advanced money does not want it paid out to shareholders.
Liquidity. A dividend is paid in cash, not in profit. A company can be profitable and unable to pay, which is precisely Barada's position in Book 1: an overdraft where there was none, and the profit sitting in inventory.
Investment opportunities. A company with projects earning more than its cost of capital should retain; one without should distribute. Orontes retains 84.84 per cent and has projects at 14 and 11 per cent against a cost of capital of 9.04, which is consistent.
Access to capital. A large, listed, well-rated company can pay out and raise money again cheaply. A small private company cannot, so it retains.
Stability and the signal. Boards dislike cutting dividends because a cut is read as bad news, so they raise only when the higher level can be sustained. The result is that dividends are smoothed and lag earnings, and an unexpected cut moves a share price far more than the cash involved would justify.
The clientele effect. Different investors want different things: a pension fund wants income, a taxpaying individual in a high bracket may prefer capital gains, a growth investor wants everything retained. A company's existing holders have selected it partly for its policy, so changing the policy sells the company to a different clientele and the transition is costly.
Taxes. Where dividends are taxed more heavily than capital gains, distribution is an expensive way to deliver value, which is the argument for the buyback of Chapter 10.
Control. A dividend paid out may have to be raised again by issuing shares, which dilutes the existing holders. A family company protecting control retains instead.

Three theories close the section and the exam names them. The irrelevance theory says that in a world with no taxes, no transaction costs and no information problems, dividend policy cannot affect value: a shareholder wanting cash sells a share, and one not wanting it reinvests the dividend. The bird-in-the-hand view says investors prefer a certain dividend to an uncertain capital gain and will pay more for a payer. And the signalling view says the policy matters not for the cash but for what a change in it tells the market. The irrelevance theory is the correct starting point and the real world's frictions are what give the other two their force.

11.4  Debt against equity
The choice between the two is the oldest question in corporate finance and the exam wants the arguments on both sides, not a verdict.
Debt is cheaper, for two reasons. The lender's claim is safer, so the required return is lower, and the interest is deductible, so a 6.5 per cent rate costs 6.5 × 0.75 = 4.875 per cent after tax. It does not dilute ownership or votes. Its cost is fixed, so all the upside of a good year belongs to the shareholders — the leverage of Book 1, Chapter 8. And it imposes a discipline: a company that must meet interest payments cannot easily waste cash, which is a genuine argument and not only a rhetorical one.
Debt is also dangerous. The payments are compulsory, in bad years as in good. Covenants restrict what the company may do, as Barada discovered. It must be repaid or refinanced, which brings the refinancing risk of Chapter 3. And past some level it raises the cost of everything, including equity, because the residual claim has become riskier.
Equity has the mirror properties. It never has to be repaid and its dividend is discretionary, so it absorbs a bad year instead of amplifying it. It carries no covenants. It improves the credit standing, which makes the next borrowing cheaper. But it is expensive, because the shareholders stand last and require the most; it is not deductible; it dilutes both ownership and control; and issuing it carries a signalling cost, because the market reads an issue as management's view that the shares are fully valued.

11.5  Leasing, and the net advantage to leasing
Chapter 7 listed the reasons a company leases. This section computes whether it should.
The comparison is always the same: lease the asset, or borrow the money and buy it. Both are ways of obtaining the use of an asset without paying cash today, so the investment decision — whether the asset is worth having at all — is settled first and separately. What remains is a financing comparison, and because it is a financing comparison it is discounted at the after-tax cost of debt, not at the weighted average cost of capital. That point carries marks on its own.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the net advantage to leasing
Step 1. The two offers. A roasting line costs $1,000,000 to buy, lasts five years, and would be depreciated straight-line to nil, which is $200,000 a year. Alternatively it can be leased for $240,000 a year for five years. The company's tax rate is 25 per cent and it borrows at 8 per cent.
Step 2. The discount rate. The after-tax cost of debt is 8.0 × 0.75 = 6.0 per cent, and the five-year annuity factor at 6 per cent is 4.2124.
Step 3. The cost of leasing. Each lease payment is deductible, so it costs 240,000 × 0.75 = 180,000 after tax, and the present value of five of them is 180,000 × 4.2124 = 758,232. Leasing also forfeits the depreciation allowances, which are worth 200,000 × 0.25 = 50,000 a year, with a present value of 50,000 × 4.2124 = 210,620. The total cost of leasing is 758,232 + 210,620 = 968,852.
Step 4. The cost of buying. Borrowing and buying costs the purchase price of $1,000,000 today; the interest on the borrowing is not counted separately, because discounting at the after-tax cost of debt has already accounted for the financing.
Step 5. The net advantage. 1,000,000 − 968,852 = 31,148 in favour of leasing.
Check. The answer turned on two numbers: the lease payment and the tax position. At a tax rate of nil the depreciation shield would be worth nothing, the lease payments would cost their full $240,000, and the present value of leasing would be 240,000 × 4.2124 = 1,010,976 — more than buying. That is the point Chapter 7 made in words: leasing is attractive to the company that cannot use the allowances, and it is the lessor's ability to use them that funds the rate.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Dividend"
English dividend comes from the Latin for a thing to be divided, and in finance it means the share of profit distributed. Outside finance it survives in mathematics, where the dividend is the number being divided — the same root, a different object. The warning for an exam is the phrase dividend payout: it is a ratio of a distribution to earnings, not a payment. Arabic distinguishes the two senses outright, using التوزيعات for the distribution and المقسوم for the arithmetic term. French dividende is used only for the financial sense and dividende is never the number being divided, which French calls the dividende in arithmetic as well — so French has the same overlap as English and Arabic is the clearest. Note too that dividends declared and dividends paid are different figures in the same year, as Orontes's $450,000 and $50,000 show.

A cash dividend reduces cash and equity and is never an expense; a stock dividend and a split change nothing but the count, so a 10 per cent stock dividend on Orontes's 8,100,000 shares takes earnings per share to 2,969,100 ÷ 8,910,000 = 0.3332 and a two-for-one split takes it to 0.1833. Eight factors shape a policy — law and covenants, liquidity, investment opportunities, access to capital, stability and its signal, clientele, tax, and control — and three theories frame them, of which irrelevance is the right starting point and the frictions supply the rest. Debt is cheaper because the claim is safer and the interest deductible, and dangerous because the payments are compulsory, the covenants bind and the refinancing must be found; equity is expensive, permanent, undiluted by no obligation and read as a negative signal when issued. And the lease-against-buy test is one calculation at the after-tax cost of debt: 180,000 × 4.2124 = 758,232 of payments plus 50,000 × 4.2124 = 210,620 of forfeited shield is 968,852 against a price of 1,000,000, a net advantage to leasing of 31,148 — which disappears entirely if the company has no tax to shelter.

Case-style set: Orontes's payout decision
The 40 per cent proposal. Raising the payout to 40 per cent takes the retention ratio to 60 per cent, and the sustainable growth rate to 16.15 × 0.60 = 9.69 per cent against sales growth of 15.38. The company would be growing nearly six points a year faster than its retained profit could fund, and would have to raise the difference externally every year — having just distributed it. The proposal fails on the investment-opportunity factor and on the access-to-capital factor at once.
The special dividend of 2,000,000. This is better reasoned, because a special dividend carries no expectation of repetition, which is the flexibility argument for a buyback applied to a distribution. But the cash is spoken for: the two projects need $6,000,000 of the $7,302,100 available, and distributing a further two million would leave the company with under a million of cash against $4,280,000 of current liabilities and a gross margin still falling.
The finance director's proposal. Hold the dividend, fund the projects. It is consistent with every factor that points one way for this company: projects well above the cost of capital, a growth clientele content with a 0.60 per cent yield, a tax system that favours gains, and a margin problem that argues for keeping a reserve.
Recommendation. Hold the dividend at its current level and fund both projects. Tell the market why, because a policy explained is a policy that will not be misread. Revisit the question when the projects are complete and the gross margin has stabilised: at that point a company with no remaining investment opportunities and a cash ratio of 1.72 should be returning money, and the special dividend proposed today will be the right instrument then.
