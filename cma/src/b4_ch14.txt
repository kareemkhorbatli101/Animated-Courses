14.1  What data mining is
Data mining is the search through data for patterns, relationships and anomalies that were not known in advance. The last part of that definition distinguishes it from reporting. A report answers a question somebody already had; mining looks for what nobody thought to ask.
Figure F414-02. What data mining looks for, and what it does not deliver
What data mining looks for (F.4.g)
What it means
At Orontes
Patterns
Regularities that repeat across cases
Third-quarter demand rises every year
Relationships
Variables that move together
Cases sold against mean temperature
Anomalies
Cases that do not fit the pattern

Figure F414-02 sets out what mining looks for, and its last row sets out what it does not deliver. Mining finds association, meaning that two things occur together. It cannot by itself establish that one causes the other, and Chapter 13's staff birthdays showed how readily association appears without cause.
That limitation is not a weakness of the technique but of the data. Causation is established by reasoning about mechanism, or by an experiment, and a dataset of past orders contains neither.

The first remains the largest. Preparation routinely consumes most of the effort in a mining project. Chapter 11 showed why: duplicates, missing values and outliers each demand a judgement, and the judgements change the answer.
The last is the one nobody warns about. There is always another cut of the data, another variable to add, another period to extend. 14.3  Why mining is iterative, and both art and science
Figure F414-04 shows the cycle an analyst actually follows.
Figure F414-04. The data mining cycle and its loops.
It is drawn with return arrows because it is never a straight line. A failed evaluation sends the analyst back to the model, to the preparation, or to the question itself. That happens more than once in any real project. A first model that disappoints is the normal case, not a failure of competence.
The phrase art and science describes the same cycle from a different angle. Figure F414-05 takes each part of the work and says which it is.

14.4  Structured Query Language
Structured Query Language (SQL), usually called SQL, is the standard language for asking a relational database for data and for changing what it holds. Its purpose is what the exam asks about, so Figure F414-06 sets out what a query actually says.
Figure F414-06. The parts of a SQL query.
Read it as six questions in order. Which columns do I want, from which table, linked to what, keeping which rows, summarised how, and sorted how. A join is the clause that earns the relational design of Chapter 10: without the shared key, the orders and the customers could not be read together at all.
A management accountant is not expected to write production queries. The expectation is to know what SQL is for and to read a simple query. It also includes seeing why an answer depends on which rows were kept and which were not.
EXAM TRAP   The filter decides the answer
Two analysts querying the same table can report different totals if one keeps cancelled orders and the other does not. When a figure is disputed, the first question is what the query filtered out, not whether the data is wrong.

14.5  Extended Example 14A: mining the Beverages demand data
F.4.k asks how an analyst would actually mine a dataset. This example does it, on the Beverages data that Chapter 15 will model formally.
The cycle of Figure F414-04 is the structure this example follows, step by step.
1. Understand the business
The question is whether the extra production shift should be scheduled to the promotion calendar, as now, or to something else. That is a question worth asking, because the answer would change a decision taken every quarter.
2. Understand the data
There are 8 quarters of history: cases sold, average price, mean temperature and promotion weeks. Eight observations is few, and the analyst should say so now rather than discover it later.
3. Prepare it
The preparation is Chapter 11's: consolidate the sources, clean what is wrong, transform dates into quarters, and reduce to the columns the question needs. Nothing here is new, and it is where most of the time goes.
4. Look before modelling
A first query ranks the quarters by cases. The peak quarters are the third of each year, at 240,400 cases in 2027 and 240,000 in 2028. The same quarters carry the highest temperatures, 33 and 34 degrees. Promotion weeks in those quarters were 6 and 5, which is not the highest in the dataset.
5. Form a hypothesis
The pattern suggests demand follows temperature more closely than promotion. That is a hypothesis, not a finding, and it is exactly the point at which an analyst is most likely to stop too early and report it as a result.
6. Model and evaluate
Chapter 15 fits a regression to test it, and finds that every coefficient carries the expected sign: price negative, temperature positive, promotion positive. The hypothesis survives, but the promotion effect is real as well, which the eyeball analysis had understated.
7. Where the loop bites
That last sentence is the iteration. The first look suggested promotion barely mattered; the model says it matters, just less than temperature. Had the analyst reported after step four, the recommendation would have been to cut promotion spending, and it would have been wrong.

Chapter summary
Figure F414-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F414-08. Chapter 14 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Data mining searches data for patterns, relationships and anomalies not known in advance, and it finds association rather than cause. Its challenges are data quality, size and cost, privacy, spurious findings, overfitting, and knowing when to stop. It is iterative because evaluation routinely sends the analyst back to the model, the preparation or the question, and it is both art and science because the method can be written down while the choice of question and the decision to stop cannot. Structured Query Language asks a relational database which columns, from which table, joined how, keeping which rows, grouped and sorted how. An analyst mines a dataset by starting from the decision, looking before modelling, treating a first impression as a hypothesis, and testing it.

Case question: the analyst who reported too early
An analyst at Orontes has been asked whether the extra production shift in Beverages should continue to be scheduled to the promotion calendar. She has eight quarters of data: cases sold, average price, mean temperature and the number of promotion weeks. She has not previously worked with this dataset, and nobody has told her how the shift schedule is currently decided.
She ranks the quarters by cases and finds that the peak quarters are the third of each year, which are also the hottest. The promotion weeks in those quarters are not the highest in the dataset. She writes a note recommending that promotion spending be cut, because demand appears to follow the weather instead.
Before the note goes to the board, her manager asks whether the relationship has been tested. It has not. A regression is then fitted, and it shows that temperature is indeed the stronger driver but that promotion weeks also carry a positive and material coefficient.
Separately, the analyst has noticed that cases sold correlate with the number of pallets returned damaged, and has asked whether this should be investigated as a cause of demand.
