Return on Investment, Residual Income and Measurement Issues
العائد على الاستثمار والدخل المتبقي وقضايا القياس
Chapter 15 made the Beverages division an investment center, which means its manager answers for profit and for the assets used to earn it. One number must therefore combine the two. This chapter builds that number two ways, shows where each one misleads, and ends with the accounting policies that quietly change both.

17.1  Measures, goals and feedback
A performance measure changes behavior, so it must point at what the strategy asks for. A division told to grow in the Gulf and judged only on cost will not grow in the Gulf.
Feedback must also arrive in time to act on. A variance report that reaches the plant six weeks after the month it describes cannot change the decision that caused it, as Chapter 14 showed.

17.2  Return on investment
Return on investment (ROI) is operating income divided by the operating assets used to earn it. For Beverages: $3,180,000 ÷ $12,000,000 = 26.5%.
ROI splits into two parts that managers can act on separately. Figure F317-03 shows the relationship: return on sales multiplied by asset turnover.

17.3  Residual income
Residual income (RI) is operating income less a capital charge: the required rate of return multiplied by the investment base. It answers a different question: how much did the division earn above the cost of the capital it used?

17.4  Choosing between them
The two measures can disagree, and the disagreement is the reason both exist. Beverages is offered the filling-line project of Chapter 4: $2,500,000 of assets earning $450,000 a year, a return of 18.0%.

EXAM TRAP   Compare the project with the required return
A project should be judged against the required rate of return, not against the division's current ROI. Any project above the required return raises residual income and makes the company better off.

17.5  What goes into the figures
Both measures depend on two numbers that accounting policy can move. Figure F317-11 lists the income policies that make divisions hard to compare.
Figure F317-11. Income policies that reduce comparability

A division recognising revenue earlier reports more income now
Compare only divisions using the same policy
Development spending
One division capitalises it, another expenses it

Measure on the segment margin (Chapter 15)
A division that recognises revenue earlier, or expenses development that another capitalises, will report different income for identical work. Comparison then measures the policy, not the performance.
The group therefore fixes one set of policies for internal reporting, even where local statements differ. Levant reports under IFRS in Jordan, but its figures are restated to group policy before any division is compared.
The investment base is just as open to question. Figure F317-12 lists the main issues: the inventory method, assets shared between divisions, and whether assets are measured at gross or net book value.
Figure F317-12. Policies that change the investment base
Policy (C.3.k)
Effect on the investment base
What to do
Inventory method
FIFO and weighted average give different inventory and cost of sales
Agree one method for internal measurement

The shared cold store of Chapter 15 serves two divisions
Allocate the asset on the same basis as its cost, or exclude it from both
Gross or net book value
Beverages: 26.5% on net book value, 15.9% on gross
Net book value flatters divisions with old assets
Leased assets
A leased plant may or may not appear in the base
Follow one rule across all divisions
Idle or non-operating assets
Land held for sale is not used to earn operating income
Exclude it from the investment base
The last one matters most. Beverages shows 26.5% on net book value but only 15.9% on the gross cost of $20,000,000, because its plant is partly depreciated. A division with old assets looks better than one that has just invested, which discourages exactly the investment the company wants.
Each of these choices can be defended, and none of them is about performance. What matters is that every division uses the same one, and that the basis is stated with the figures.
EXAM TRAP   Ask what the base contains
Before comparing two divisions, ask whether both use the same inventory method, the same treatment of shared assets, and the same basis for property. A difference in any of them makes the comparison meaningless.

Chapter summary
Figure F317-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F317-14. Chapter 17 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Performance measures should follow the strategy and reach managers in time to act. Return on investment is operating income divided by operating assets, and equals return on sales times asset turnover. Residual income is operating income less the required return times the investment base. ROI allows divisions of different size to be compared, but can lead a manager to reject a project that earns more than the required return; residual income avoids that fault but cannot be compared across sizes. Both depend on income and asset measurement policies, which must be the same before any comparison is made.

Case question: the tahini investment decision
The Tahini & Spreads division has been asked to grow, and its manager, Samir, has found a way to do it. A new filling and packing line would let the division supply a Gulf supermarket chain that wants its own-label tahini in large volumes.
The line would cost a known amount and would add a known amount of operating income each year. Head office expects every division to earn a required rate of return on the assets it uses, and judges divisions on return on investment. The Gulf chain has asked for a three-year supply agreement, so the extra income would be reasonably certain.
Samir has done the arithmetic and does not like the answer. The project earns more than the required return, but less than the return his division already makes, so accepting it would pull his own measure down. He has told the controller he would rather not proceed.
Rania, the controller, must calculate the division's return on investment and residual income before and after the project, show the board which measure leads to the decision the company wants, and recommend how Samir should be judged in future.
