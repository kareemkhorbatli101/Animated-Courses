Chapter 4
Growth Rates and Variation Analysis
معدلات النمو وتحليل التغير
Chapter 3 stated a movement three ways and left one question open: when a line has moved over several years, what single number describes how fast it is moving? A series of year-over-year percentages is honest but hard to hold in the head. Sales grew 13.8 per cent and then 15.4 per cent. Is that one rate or two?
This chapter answers it. A growth rate is a percentage change expressed as a rate per period, and when several periods are involved there is a right way and a wrong way to average them. Variation analysis is the same arithmetic given the name the exam uses for it.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
You already know how to calculate a percentage change: the movement divided by the earlier amount. A growth rate is that same figure, named as a rate because it attaches to a period. Everything in this chapter follows from that one definition.
4.1  The growth rate of a line
The growth rate of a line item for one period is the change divided by the opening amount.
Orontes's net sales went from $36,400,000 to $42,000,000. The growth rate is 5,600,000 ÷ 36,400,000 = 15.4 per cent. The year before, sales went from $32,000,000 to $36,400,000, a growth rate of 4,400,000 ÷ 32,000,000 = 13.8 per cent.

EXAM TRAP   Growth of a line against growth of its base
A line growing faster than sales is a line becoming a larger share of sales.
A balance-sheet line growing faster than total assets is a line becoming a larger share of the company.
A growth rate on a small line can be huge and still not matter. Always ask what the absolute amount is.

4.2  Several years: the compound rate
Two annual rates of 13.8 and 15.4 per cent do not average to one rate by adding them and halving. That is the commonest arithmetical error in this part of the syllabus, and the exam tests it directly.
The reason is that growth compounds. A line that grows 13.8 per cent and then 15.4 per cent has not grown 29.2 per cent, and it has not grown at 14.6 per cent a year in the sense that the simple average suggests. It has grown by a factor of 1.138 × 1.154, and the rate that produces the same result in two equal steps is the square root of that factor.
The measure is the compound annual growth rate, usually written CAGR. For a series running n periods from a beginning value to an ending value:
CAGR = (ending value ÷ beginning value) raised to the power of 1 ÷ n, minus 1.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the CAGR of Orontes sales
Step 1. The beginning and the end. Sales in 2023 were $32,000,000 and in 2025 were $42,000,000. There are two periods between them, not three: 2023 to 2024, and 2024 to 2025.
Step 2. The total factor. 42,000,000 ÷ 32,000,000 = 1.3125. Sales are 1.3125 times what they were.
Step 3. The rate per period. The square root of 1.3125 is 1.1456, so the CAGR is 14.56 per cent.
Check. Grow 32,000,000 by 14.56 per cent twice: 32,000,000 × 1.1456 = 36,659,200, and 36,659,200 × 1.1456 = 41,996,780, which is 42,000,000 to the nearest rounding. The simple average of 13.8 and 15.4 is 14.6 per cent, close here because the two rates are close; the two diverge sharply when the rates are far apart or when one is negative.

EXAM TRAP   Counting the periods
The exponent is 1 divided by the number of PERIODS, not the number of data points. Three annual figures give two periods.
A CAGR describes a smooth path that the company did not actually follow. It is a summary, not a history.
A CAGR cannot be calculated when the beginning value is zero or negative, for the same reason a percentage change cannot.
LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Compound" in English
English compound as a verb means to build on itself: interest compounds, growth compounds. Arabic مركب and French composé carry the same idea. Do not confuse it with English compound as a noun, meaning a mixture, or with a compound journal entry, which is one entry with several lines. The exam uses all three senses in Part 1 and Part 2.

4.3  Variation analysis
Variation analysis is the name the Learning Outcome Statements give to the calculation and interpretation of percentage change over time. It is the technique of Chapter 3 and this one, used deliberately on a chosen set of lines rather than on the whole statement.
The method has three steps, and the third is the one candidates skip.
First, choose the lines. Not every line: the ones that drive the business. For a manufacturer they are usually sales, cost of goods sold, the main operating expenses, inventory, receivables and payables.
Second, compute the variation. The percentage change, year by year, and the compound rate over the series.
Third, explain each variation that is out of line with the others. A variation that matches the rest of the statement needs no explanation; a variation that does not is the finding.

WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: explaining one variation
Step 1. Find the line that is out of step. Over 2023 to 2025 sales grew 31.2 per cent and cost of goods sold grew 35.4 per cent. Every other line on the income statement is downstream of those two.
Step 2. Size the effect. Had cost of goods sold grown at the same 31.2 per cent as sales, it would be 20,160,000 × 1.312 = 26,449,920 instead of 27,300,000. The difference is 27,300,000 − 26,449,920 = 850,080 of cost that growth alone does not account for.
Step 3. Say what it is worth. That $850,080 is gross profit Orontes did not earn, on an actual gross profit of $14,700,000. It is about 5.8 per cent of the gross profit the company made, and it is the whole of the margin decline from 37.0 per cent to 35.0 per cent.
Check. The two statements agree. The common-size column said the margin fell two points; the variation analysis says the same fall is worth $850,080 at this year's volume. A manager asked to act needs the second figure, because two percentage points is not a sum of money and $850,080 is.

4.4  Three patterns worth recognising
Most variation analyses end in one of three patterns, and naming them saves time.
The margin squeeze is Orontes's pattern: sales growing, a cost line growing faster, and every subtotal below it growing more slowly than sales. The cause is upstream — purchase prices, product mix, discounting — and the remedy is a pricing or sourcing decision, not a cost-cutting one.
The working-capital leak is Barada's pattern in Chapter 3: sales growing modestly while receivables and inventory grow sharply. The income statement looks adequate and the cash does not arrive. The cause is downstream of the sale, in collection and in stock, and Chapters 11 and 12 measure it.
The one-off distortion is the pattern of Orontes's operating income in 2025: a single line moving so far that it breaks the series on its own. The test is whether removing it restores the pattern. Take out the impairment of $2,724,000 and operating income grows 15.4 per cent in 2025 rather than 4.3, which is sales growth exactly; the series was never broken, one line was.

4.5  What a growth rate hides
A growth rate is one number standing for a year of trading, and four things disappear inside it.
Price and volume. Sales grew 15.4 per cent. That is more jars, or dearer jars, or both. Nothing in the financial statements separates them, and the two have opposite implications: more jars at the same price means the business is winning customers; the same jars at a higher price may mean only that costs were passed on. An analyst looks for unit data in the MD&A or the segment note.
Acquisition and organic growth. A company that buys another company grows. Orontes's equity-method investment in Jordan Glass rose by $1,307,000, and if that had been a controlling stake the consolidated sales would have jumped without a single extra jar being sold. Growth that comes from buying is called inorganic, and growth from the existing business is organic. The exam expects the distinction.
Currency. A company selling into the Gulf and the Levant reports in dollars. If a customer's currency weakens, the same local sales translate into fewer dollars and the growth rate falls with no change in the business. Chapter 18 takes this up.
Inflation. A general rise in prices lifts every money figure. In a year of 10 per cent inflation, growth of 15.4 per cent is about 5 per cent in real terms. Chapter 18 takes this up too.

EXAM TRAP   Real against nominal
A growth rate from the statements is nominal: it includes inflation.
A real growth rate subtracts inflation. If a question gives you an inflation rate, it usually wants the real figure.
Growth from an acquisition is not evidence that the business improved.

Chapter summary
Figure F504-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F504-08. Chapter 4 summary map: the key rule for each section.
A growth rate is the change in a line divided by its opening amount, and every line on both statements has one. Orontes's sales grew 13.8 and then 15.4 per cent, while cost of goods sold grew 15.6 and 17.2, and total assets grew 19.8 per cent against sales of 15.4. Over several periods the rates compound, so the summary measure is the compound annual growth rate: the ending value over the beginning value, raised to one over the number of periods, less one. Orontes's sales CAGR from 2023 to 2025 is 14.56 per cent. The simple average of annual rates is not the growth of the series, and the gap widens as the rates spread. Variation analysis is this arithmetic applied deliberately: choose the lines that drive the business, compute the change, and explain only the variations that are out of line — for Orontes, cost of goods sold at 16.38 per cent against sales at 14.56. A growth rate hides price against volume, organic against acquired growth, currency translation and inflation.

Case-style set: two years of Barada
Sales growth is 19,800,000 ÷ 18,000,000 = 1.100, or 10.0 per cent in 2024, and 21,978,000 ÷ 19,800,000 = 1.110, or 11.0 per cent in 2025. The CAGR is 21,978,000 ÷ 18,000,000 = 1.221, whose square root is 1.1050, so 10.5 per cent. The accountant's "about 10 per cent a year" is accurate for sales.
Gross profit is 18,000,000 − 15,120,000 = 2,880,000 in 2023; 19,800,000 − 16,830,000 = 2,970,000 in 2024; and 21,978,000 − 18,900,000 = 3,078,000 in 2025. As a percentage of sales that is 16.0, 15.0 and 14.0 per cent.
So the description is accurate about sales and wrong about stability. The gross margin has fallen a full point in each of two years, which on 2025 sales is worth about $440,000 a year of lost profit. Cost of goods sold grew at 11.8 per cent a year against sales at 10.5, and a distributor whose margin erodes steadily is not stable; it is being squeezed, by suppliers or by customers or by both.
What the three years cannot tell the bank is whether the margin fell because prices were cut to win volume or because purchase costs rose. The two call for different remedies, and the statements do not separate them.
