Chapter 18
Short-term credit, the cost of working capital, and a strategy for current assets
الائتمان قصير الأجل وتكلفة رأس المال العامل واستراتيجية الأصول المتداولة
Six chapters have measured what a company's current assets cost it. This one pays for them. Short-term credit is the other side of the working-capital balance sheet — trade credit, bank lines, commercial paper, borrowings secured on the receivables and the stock — and every one of those sources has a price that is almost never the rate it is quoted at.
The chapter ends the book where Book 1 began: with a judgment. Having priced the components, it sets out the policy question — how much current asset to hold and how much of it to fund short — and then asks a candidate to recommend a strategy for a company whose figures have run through thirty-six chapters.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 12 distinguished permanent from seasonal working capital. Chapter 14 computed an effective rate with a compensating balance. Chapter 16 priced a settlement discount from the seller's side. Book 1 found Orontes with no short-term borrowings at all and Barada with a $240,000 overdraft. This chapter completes each of those.
18.1  How much to fund short
Chapter 12 made the distinction this section builds on. Some current assets are permanent — the stock a company never goes below, the receivable book that never empties — and some are seasonal, rising and falling with the year. The financing question is which kind of money funds which.
Three policies, and the exam names all three.
The maturity matching or hedging approach funds permanent assets, including the permanent part of working capital, with long-term money, and seasonal assets with short-term money that is repaid as the season unwinds. It is the textbook benchmark: each asset is financed over its own life, so nothing has to be refinanced before it has turned into cash.
The aggressive approach funds some of the permanent requirement with short-term money. It is cheaper, because the yield curve usually slopes upward, and riskier in two ways: the rate can rise at every rollover, and the facility may not be renewed at all when it matters. Barada's overdraft funding a permanently larger stockroom is the aggressive approach arrived at by accident.
The conservative approach funds all of the permanent requirement and part of the seasonal with long-term money, so that in the trough the company holds surplus cash. It costs more and removes refinancing risk almost entirely. Orontes, with $6,400,000 of long-term debt, no short-term borrowings and $7,302,100 of cash, is at this end.
The choice is a risk decision, which is what the outcome means by the effect of risk on financing policy. A company with volatile cash flows, thin coverage or a covenant near its limit should sit conservatively; one with stable flows, wide coverage and a committed facility can afford to sit aggressively. The cost of being wrong is asymmetric: an aggressive policy saves a fraction of a point most years and fails completely in the year credit tightens.

18.2  The sources
Six sources of short-term credit, and the exam expects each described.
Trade credit is the largest and the least examined. A supplier offering 30 days is lending the company the value of the goods for a month, and Orontes's $2,050,000 of accounts payable is that loan. It arises automatically with purchases, requires no negotiation, and is free — unless a discount is being forgone, which section 18.3 prices and which makes it the most expensive money in the chapter.
A line of credit is an arrangement under which a bank will lend up to a limit, usually for a year, usually reviewed annually. An uncommitted line is a statement of intent the bank may withdraw; a committed line is a contractual obligation, for which the bank charges a commitment fee on the undrawn part. The difference matters exactly when it matters: in a downturn.
Revolving credit is a committed facility over several years that can be drawn, repaid and redrawn. It is the most reliable short-term money and the most expensive, carrying both a margin and a commitment fee.
A short-term bank loan is a single advance for a fixed period, repaid at the end or in instalments, often secured.
Commercial paper is unsecured short-term borrowing sold directly into the money market by companies of good credit standing, in large amounts, for up to 270 days. It is cheaper than a bank loan for issuers that can use it, and it is unreliable in a crisis because the buyers simply stop buying. A $5,000,000 issue for 90 days at 5.5 per cent costs 5,000,000 × 0.055 × 90 ÷ 365 = 67,808.
Secured borrowing uses the current assets themselves. Pledging receivables borrows against them while keeping them; factoring sells them outright, which section 18.4 prices. Inventory may be used through a floating charge, a warehouse receipt, or a trust receipt where the goods are identifiable.

18.3  The cost of not taking a discount
A supplier offering 2/10, net 30 is making an offer with a price. Pay on day 10 and pay 98 per cent; pay on day 30 and pay 100. The company that pays on day 30 has borrowed 98 for twenty days and repaid 100, and the exam wants that expressed as an annual rate.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: what 2/10, net 30 really costs
Step 1. The rate for the period. The discount forgone is 2 on the 98 that would have been paid: 2 ÷ 98 = 2.04 per cent for the twenty days of extra credit.
Step 2. The number of periods. The extra credit runs from day 10 to day 30, which is twenty days, so there are 365 ÷ 20 = 18.25 such periods in a year.
Step 3. The annual cost. 2.04 × 18.25 = 37.2 per cent a year.
Step 4. Compare. Orontes borrows from its bank at 6 per cent. Paying on day 30 instead of day 10 costs it 37.2 per cent a year — six times as much. A company with a facility available should always take the discount and draw on the facility to do it.
Check. The arithmetic is sensitive to both terms, and the exam varies them. On 1/10, net 30 the cost is 1 ÷ 99 = 1.01 per cent for twenty days, which is 1.01 × 18.25 = 18.4 per cent a year — still far above any borrowing rate. On 2/10, net 60 the extra credit is fifty days, so the periods are 365 ÷ 50 = 7.3 and the cost is 2.04 × 7.3 = 14.9 per cent. The longer the net period, the cheaper it is to forgo the discount, because the borrowing lasts longer for the same fee.

Two consequences follow for the two sides of the transaction.
For the buyer: take the discount unless there is genuinely no money and no facility, in which case the company has a financing problem that the discount decision merely reveals. And where a supplier is paid late anyway, the effective rate falls, which is the unspoken reason stretching is common — and Chapter 14 explained why it is not costless.
For the seller: Chapter 16 computed the cost of offering a discount as margin given up, and this section gives the buyer's view of the same number. A seller offering 2/10 net 30 is paying about 37 per cent annualised to be paid twenty days earlier, which is only sensible if the alternative is losing the sale or funding at a comparable rate.

18.4  What a bank facility and a factor really cost
Three calculations complete the pricing of short-term money, and each turns a quoted rate into an effective one.
A loan with a compensating balance, which Chapter 14 computed: interest is charged on the whole loan and only part of it is usable, so the effective rate is the interest divided by the usable funds.
A line with a commitment fee. The fee is charged on the undrawn portion, so the effective cost depends on how much of the line is used.
A discount loan, where the interest is deducted in advance. Borrowing $1,000,000 at 8 per cent on this basis gives proceeds of 1,000,000 − 80,000 = 920,000, and the effective rate is 80,000 ÷ 920,000 = 8.70 per cent rather than 8.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: a line with a commitment fee, and a factoring quote
Step 1. The line. A company arranges a $5,000,000 committed line at 7 per cent, with a commitment fee of 0.5 per cent on the undrawn part, and draws an average of $3,000,000 through the year.
Step 2. What it pays. Interest is 3,000,000 × 0.07 = 210,000 and the fee is 2,000,000 × 0.005 = 10,000, so the total is 210,000 + 10,000 = 220,000.
Step 3. The effective rate on the money used. 220,000 ÷ 3,000,000 = 7.33 per cent against a quoted 7.
Step 4. A factoring alternative. A factor offers to buy $1,000,000 of receivables with an average age of 45 days, charging a 2 per cent fee and advancing 80 per cent of the face value at 10 per cent a year. The fee is 1,000,000 × 0.02 = 20,000; the advance is 1,000,000 × 0.80 = 800,000, and the interest on it for 45 days is 800,000 × 0.10 × 45 ÷ 365 = 9,863. The total cost is 20,000 + 9,863 = 29,863 on $800,000 of money for 45 days, which is 29,863 ÷ 800,000 = 3.73 per cent for the period, or about 30.3 per cent a year.
Check. Thirty per cent looks indefensible beside a 7.33 per cent bank line, and sometimes it is not, which is the point of the comparison. Factoring without recourse transfers the default risk, which Chapter 16 priced; it removes the cost of running a credit and collection department, which can be a large part of the fee; and it is available to companies whose credit standing gives them no bank line at all. The right comparison is therefore not the rate alone but the rate less the costs and risks the factor absorbs — and Book 1 found a $15,000 loss on sale of receivables in Orontes's own income statement, which is this decision already taken for a small part of the book.

18.5  A strategy for current assets
The last outcome of Book 2 asks for a recommendation, which means the whole of the working-capital section arranged into an argument. Four steps make one.
Step one: size the requirement. Separate the permanent core from the seasonal peak, using the cash forecast of Chapter 13 and the cycle of Book 1. The permanent core is funded long; the peak is funded short, by a facility arranged before it is needed.
Step two: price every component. The cost of working capital is the sum of the funding, the carrying costs of stock, the credit losses, the discounts forgone and the interest not earned on idle balances — and the single most common error is to count only the first. Orontes's own bill reads: $272,640 to fund the trade cycle, $292,084 of interest forgone on idle cash, $63,000 of credit losses, and whatever discounts it does not take.
Step three: attack the components in order of what they are worth. Book 1's discipline, applied here, ranks the items by money rather than by ease. For Orontes the order is: put the idle cash to work, which is worth about $176,084 a year with no risk; take every supplier discount, because forgoing one costs 37.2 per cent against a 6 per cent facility; and consider lengthening customer terms, which Chapter 16 valued at over a million a year in contribution.
Step four: match the policy to the risk. A company with a falling gross margin and a covenant to respect should not also be carrying rollover risk on its permanent assets. Orontes can afford to be slightly less conservative than it is; Barada cannot afford to be as aggressive as it has accidentally become.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Credit line"
English line of credit and credit line are the same thing, and both mean a facility a borrower may draw on. The word line is a metaphor of a limit drawn, not of a queue, and nothing in the phrase says whether the bank is obliged to lend — which is why the words committed and uncommitted have to be added and why the difference is worth more than the margin. Arabic uses حد ائتماني, literally a credit limit, which states the meaning precisely and loses the metaphor. French ligne de crédit keeps the metaphor as English does, and adds confirmée or non confirmée for the distinction that matters. Note the related trap: drawdown is taking money under a facility, and drawdown in investment management is a fall in the value of a portfolio — two unconnected senses that appear in the same examination.

Chapter summary

Figure F618-08. Chapter 18 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Permanent current assets should be funded long and seasonal ones short, which is the maturity matching approach; an aggressive policy funds part of the permanent core short and is cheaper most years and exposed in the year credit tightens, while a conservative one holds surplus cash in the trough, which is where Orontes sits and where Barada did not choose to be. Six sources supply the money: trade credit, which is free until a discount is forgone; uncommitted and committed lines; revolving credit; commercial paper, cheapest for good credits and unreliable in a crisis at 5,000,000 × 0.055 × 90 ÷ 365 = 67,808 for ninety days; and borrowing secured on the receivables or the stock. Forgoing 2/10, net 30 costs 2 ÷ 98 = 2.04 per cent for twenty days over 365 ÷ 20 = 18.25 periods, which is 2.04 × 18.25 = 37.2 per cent a year against a 6 per cent facility. A quoted rate is rarely the real one: a compensating balance turns 8 into 8.89, a commitment fee turns 7 into 220,000 ÷ 3,000,000 = 7.33 per cent, interest deducted in advance turns 8 into 80,000 ÷ 920,000 = 8.70 per cent, and a factoring quote of 2 per cent plus 10 is about 30 per cent a year before the credit department it replaces. And a strategy sizes the requirement, prices every component, attacks them in order of what they are worth, and matches the policy to the risk — while remembering that all of it together is smaller than the $840,000 of gross margin that Book 1 ranked first.

Case-style set: a working-capital strategy for Barada
Ranked by what each is worth.
First, take the supplier discounts. Barada forgoes 2/10, net 30, which costs about 37 per cent a year. On purchases of roughly $18,900,000, taking the discount is worth about 18,900,000 × 0.02 = 378,000 a year — more than the company's entire net income of $283,500. It requires money on day 10 instead of day 30, which is exactly what a receivables-backed bank line at 7 per cent is for: borrowing at 7 to save 37 is the clearest arbitrage in either book.
Second, reduce the inventory. Stock rose 41 per cent on sales up 11, and at 40.6 days of cover a distributor should be able to release a substantial part of it. Every dollar released repays the overdraft and then funds the discount above. This is slow and it is where most of the money is.
Third, collect. Receivables up 38 per cent on sales up 11 means the collection period has lengthened materially; returning it to its earlier level would release cash without any loss of sales.
What I would not do is factor. At 2 per cent of face plus 11 per cent on an 85 per cent advance, the cost is above 25 per cent a year, which is dearer than the bank line and dearer than the problem. Factoring is for a company with no bank line; Barada has one available at 7 per cent against the same receivables.
What I would do first. Arrange the receivables-backed line and take the next discount. It is the fastest, the most valuable per day of effort, and it buys the time needed for the inventory and collection work — and it must be sized with the covenant in mind, since at 1.26 against a limit of 1.50 the headroom is about $360,000 of additional debt.
