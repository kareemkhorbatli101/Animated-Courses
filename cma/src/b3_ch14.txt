WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 7 budgeted $50.00 of variable overhead a machine hour and $150,000 of fixed overhead a month. Book 2, Chapter 8 set the fixed rate using a denominator volume, and Chapter 12 of this book introduced the three-column frame.
14.1  Why overhead needs its own variances
Variable overhead behaves like a material: it changes with a driver, here machine hours, so it has both a spending variance and an efficiency variance. Fixed overhead does not change with activity, so it cannot have an efficiency variance in the same sense.
Figure F314-02 compares the two. Fixed overhead still produces a second variance, but of a different kind: because the fixed rate assumes a denominator level of activity, producing more or less than that volume changes how much fixed overhead is absorbed.

14.5  Analysing variances and acting on them
A variance report is the start of a conversation, not its conclusion. Figure F314-10 gives the five questions to ask before recommending anything.
Figure F314-10. Five questions before recommending an action
Step (C.1.s)
What to ask
1. Is it significant?
Compare with the rule of Chapter 11 (5% of the budgeted figure)
2. What caused it?
Separate price from quantity, and the plan from the result
3. Who controls the cause?
A world price rise is not the plant's responsibility
4. Is it linked to another variance?
A favorable price beside an unfavorable usage usually has one cause
5. What action follows?
Remove the cause, change the standard, or accept it and explain
The order matters. Deciding on an action before knowing the cause is the commonest fault in variance reporting, and so is acting on a variance whose cause nobody in the division controls.
A favorable variance also deserves a question. A favorable spending variance that came from skipping maintenance is not good news; it has moved cost into a later month.

Chapter summary
Figure F314-12 brings the chapter together.
Figure F314-12. Chapter 14 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Variable overhead has a spending variance, measured against the standard rate applied to the hours worked, and an efficiency variance, which follows the driver. Fixed overhead has a spending variance, actual against budget, and a production-volume variance, budget against the amount applied to the output produced; the second measures absorption, not control. The four variances can be grouped in four, three or two ways, always to the same total. Before acting, ask whether the variance is significant, what caused it, who controls the cause, and whether it is linked to another variance.

Case question: overhead in the tahini plant
The tahini plant has had a busy month. A large Gulf order arrived early, so the plant produced more jars than the volume on which its fixed overhead rate was set. The plant manager, Samir, expects the overhead report to look good, because the extra output spread the fixed cost over more jars.
The controller, Rania, has the figures. She knows the standard machine hours a jar, the standard variable overhead rate, the budgeted fixed overhead for the month and the normal volume used to set the fixed rate. She also has the actual machine hours, the actual variable overhead and the actual fixed overhead for the month.
Two things complicate the picture. The plant ran more machine hours than the standard allows for the jars it made, because a mixer kept stopping, and an unplanned repair raised the fixed overhead above budget. The mixer has stopped several times this year, and the maintenance team has asked for a replacement part that would end the problem.
Rania must calculate the four overhead variances, explain which of them reflects cost control and which reflects absorption, and tell Samir which results need action inside the plant. She will present the four variances at the plant review on Thursday, with a short note on which of them Samir can act on and which belong to the sales discussion.
