Book 3, Chapter 15 asked who should answer for a result. This chapter asks what makes the result trustworthy in the first place. The Orontes company, its divisions and Levant Distribution are the same throughout.
1.1  Internal control risk and how it is managed
Internal control is a process, carried out by the board, management and other staff, designed to give reasonable assurance that the organization's objectives will be achieved. It is a process, not a document: a written procedure that nobody follows is not a control.
Control risk is the risk that internal control will not prevent or detect a material misstatement in time. It sits beside two others. Inherent risk is how exposed something is before any control is considered, and detection risk is the risk that the auditor's own work misses the problem. Figure F401-02 distinguishes them.
Figure F401-02. The three risks, and the one the auditor controls
Risk
What it means
At Orontes
Inherent risk
How open an assertion is to material misstatement BEFORE considering any control
Spices are small, valuable and easy to carry out
Control risk
The risk that internal control will not prevent or detect that misstatement in time
One storekeeper both receives and records stock
Detection risk
The risk that the auditor's own procedures miss it
The auditor samples only large issues and misses many small ones
Audit risk
inherent x control x detection
The only one the auditor can change is detection risk
The three multiply to give audit risk. Management can reduce inherent risk by changing what the business does, and control risk by designing better controls, but only the auditor can change detection risk, by testing more or differently.
Managing control risk is a cycle: identify what could go wrong, design a control, operate it, monitor whether it works, and fix it when it does not. Chapter 5 completes that cycle with testing and remediation.

1.2  The three control objectives
Internal control serves three categories of objective, set out in Figure F401-03. Everything a control does falls into one of them.
Figure F401-03. The three categories of control objective
Objective category
What it covers
At Orontes
Operations
Effectiveness and efficiency, including safeguarding assets
Spoilage below target; stock not stolen
Reporting
Internal AND external, financial AND non-financial
The monthly management pack; the audited accounts; the energy figures of Book 3
Compliance

The second category is the one most often misremembered. It is reporting, not financial reporting: it covers internal reports as well as external ones, and non-financial information as well as financial. The energy and water figures of Book 3, Chapter 18 fall under it just as the audited accounts do.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'Reliability of financial reporting'
Many study guides still say the second objective is reliability of financial reporting (their wording). That was the wording of the 1992 framework. The 2013 framework widened it to reporting, meaning internal and external, financial and non-financial. A question that offers both wordings is testing exactly this.
Safeguarding of assets is not a fourth category. It sits inside operations, because losing an asset is an operating failure, though it usually shows up in reporting too.

1.3  The control environment
The control environment is the set of standards, processes and structures that provide the basis for carrying out internal control across the organization. It is the first of the five components shown in Figure F401-04, and it is first for a reason: it decides whether the other four work.
Figure F401-04. The five components of the COSO framework.
Figure F401-05 takes the elements the exam names and shows what each one decides.
Figure F401-05. What shapes the control environment
Element of the control environment
What it decides
Why it matters
Management philosophy and style
Whether targets are pursued at any cost, or within limits
A plant manager rewarded only on output will cut inspection
Organizational structure
Who reports to whom, and where authority stops
Levant Distribution reports to the group, not to the division it serves
Policies, objectives and goals
What the company says it wants and will not do
The code of conduct; the Chapter 1 objectives of Book 3
Competence
Whether people can do the job they are given
A clerk asked to review a reconciliation he does not understand
Accountability
Whether anyone answers for a failure
A missed control that nobody is asked about
Management philosophy and style matter more than any single procedure. If a plant manager is rewarded only on output, inspection will be cut, whatever the manual says. This is what is meant by the tone at the top: not a slogan, but what people conclude from watching what management rewards and tolerates.
Structure matters in the same practical way. Levant Distribution reports to the group rather than to the division it serves, so the manager who negotiates its prices is not the manager who approves its results.

1.4  Reasonable, not absolute, assurance
No system of internal control can give absolute assurance. The standard is reasonable assurance: a high level of confidence, not a guarantee. Figure F401-06 gives the six reasons.
Figure F401-06. Why assurance is reasonable rather than absolute.
Two of them deserve attention because the exam returns to them. Collusion defeats a separation of duties designed for one person acting alone: the clerk who raises an invoice and the supervisor who approves it can agree together. Management override is the harder one, because the people who design and enforce the controls are the people best placed to step around them.
The cost-benefit limit is not an excuse but a design principle. A control that costs more than the loss it prevents makes the company worse off, so the question is always whether the control is worth its cost, not whether it is perfect.
EXAM TRAP   Reasonable means high, not weak
Reasonable assurance is a high level of assurance. A question that treats it as 'some comfort' or 'better than nothing' has the sense wrong. The word acknowledges the six limits, not a low standard.

1.5  Extended Example 1A: reading a failure back to its cause
This example takes a real failure at Orontes and traces it back through the framework. It is exam-core: questions give you the failure and ask which component or which limitation explains it.
1. What happened
A purchasing clerk at the Amman plant was able to add a supplier to the approved list and to release payments to it. Over fourteen months, payments went to a supplier that delivered nothing. The loss was found when a new controller reconciled supplier records against goods received.
2. Which risk was this?
Inherent risk was high: payments to new suppliers are easy to disguise and hard to verify. Control risk was also high, because one person held two duties that should have been separated. Detection risk was high too, since nobody reconciled suppliers to receipts until the new controller arrived.
3. Which component failed?
Control activities failed first: the duties were not separated, which Chapter 3 examines. But monitoring activities failed as well, because fourteen months passed before anyone looked. And the control environment was weak underneath both: nobody owned supplier approval, so no one was accountable when it went wrong.
4. Which objective was missed?
All three. Operations, because cash left the company for nothing. Reporting, because purchases and payables were misstated for more than a year. Compliance, because the payments breached the company's own procurement policy.
5. Could a control have stopped it?
Separating supplier approval from payment release would have stopped this particular fraud, and it costs almost nothing. But it would not stop two people agreeing together, which is collusion, and it would not stop a director instructing the clerk, which is management override. Reasonable assurance is the honest claim; absolute assurance is not available at any price.
6. Tie line
WORKED EXAMPLE   Everything connects
High inherent risk plus high control risk plus high detection risk gave a high audit risk.
Control activities failed, monitoring failed, and the control environment allowed both.
All three objectives were missed: operations, reporting and compliance.
The fix is cheap, but it buys reasonable assurance, not certainty.

Chapter summary
Figure F401-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F401-08. Chapter 1 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Internal control is a process giving reasonable assurance about objectives in three categories: operations, reporting and compliance, where reporting means internal and external, financial and non-financial. Control risk is the risk that control fails to prevent or detect a misstatement, and it sits alongside inherent and detection risk. The control environment is the foundation: structure, philosophy, policies, competence and accountability decide whether every other control works. Assurance is reasonable, never absolute, because of judgment, error, collusion, management override, external events and the cost of control.

Case question: the supplier that delivered nothing
Orontes Foods has found that a purchasing clerk at the Amman plant was able to add a supplier to the approved list and also to release payments to that supplier. Over fourteen months, payments went to a company that delivered no goods.
The clerk had been in the role for three years. The written procurement policy required supplier approval by the purchasing manager, but the manager had delegated the task verbally during a busy period and never took it back. No one reviewed the approved supplier list.
The loss came to light when a new controller, Rania, reconciled the supplier master file against goods received notes for the first time. The plant manager's bonus depends only on output and cost per case, and he told the board he had no reason to look at supplier records.
The board has asked Rania to explain which risks were high, which components of internal control failed, which objectives were missed, and whether a cheap control would have prevented the loss entirely. Orontes has three divisions and a subsidiary, and the same procurement policy applies across all of them, so the board also wants to know whether the weakness could exist elsewhere.
