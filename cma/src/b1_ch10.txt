Long-Lived Assets: Depreciation, Disposal and Impairment
الأصول طويلة الأجل: الاستهلاك والاستبعاد والهبوط في القيمة
Machines, buildings, patents and brands help a company earn revenue for many years. Their cost is therefore spread over those years, not expensed at once. In this chapter you follow a long-lived asset through its whole life: what goes into its cost, how it is depreciated, what happens when it is sold, and what happens when its value falls.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
You know depreciation from your studies. Three U.S. GAAP points may be new: U.S. GAAP does not allow revaluation to fair value; the impairment test for PP&E has two steps and uses undiscounted cash flows first; and U.S. GAAP never reverses an impairment loss on assets that are held and used.
10.1  The cost of property, plant and equipment
Property, plant and equipment (PP&E) is recorded at historical cost: everything needed to acquire the asset and get it ready for use. This includes the price, freight, installation, testing and site preparation. When a company buys land with an old building and pulls the building down, the demolition cost is part of the cost of the land. When it buys several assets for one price, it divides the price by their relative fair values.
After purchase, a capital expenditure that extends the asset's life or increases its capacity is added to its cost. A revenue expenditure, such as ordinary repairs and maintenance, is an expense of the period.
When a company builds an asset for its own use, it capitalizes interest during construction. Capitalized interest is the avoidable interest: weighted-average accumulated expenditures × the interest rate, but never more than the interest actually incurred. For example, if Orontes spends on average $2,000 (USD 000) on a new warehouse during the year and the rate on the related loan is 6%, avoidable interest is 120. Actual interest is 150, so Orontes capitalizes 120. Capitalization stops when the asset is substantially complete.
● IFRS CONTRAST   Borrowing costs under IAS 23
IAS 23 also capitalizes borrowing costs on qualifying assets. One difference: when a company invests borrowed money for a short time before spending it, IAS 23 deducts the investment income from the amount capitalized. U.S. GAAP generally does not.

10.2  Depreciation methods and their effects
Depreciation spreads the depreciable base, cost minus salvage value, over the useful life in a systematic and rational way. Four methods appear on the exam.
Straight-line method: the same amount each year, depreciable base ÷ useful life.
Double-declining-balance method: twice the straight-line rate, applied to the beginning book value. Salvage is ignored in the calculation, but the book value may not fall below salvage.
Sum-of-the-years'-digits method: depreciable base × remaining life at the start of the year ÷ the sum of the years' digits.
Units-of-production method: a rate per unit or hour of use, times actual use.
WORKED EXAMPLE   The Amman bottling line
In Chapter 1, Orontes bought a new bottling line for its Amman plant. It cost $1,200,000. Orontes expects a salvage value of $150,000 after 5 years, or after 21,000 machine hours. The depreciable base is $1,050,000.
Straight-line: $1,050,000 ÷ 5 = $210,000 a year.
Double-declining: rate 40%; year 1 = 40% × $1,200,000 = $480,000. In year 5 only $5,520 is left, so the book value ends at salvage.
Sum-of-the-years'-digits: the digits add up to 15; year 1 = $1,050,000 × 5/15 = $350,000.
Units of production: $1,050,000 ÷ 21,000 hours = $50 an hour; year 1 = 5,000 hours × $50 = $250,000.

Changes and errors
Estimates change. A new useful life or salvage value is a change in accounting estimate. It is applied prospectively: the company does not restate prior years. Suppose Orontes used straight-line for the bottling line and, after 2 years, decides it has 4 more years of life and a salvage value of $90,000. The book value then is $780,000, so each remaining year's depreciation is ($780,000 − $90,000) ÷ 4 = $172,500.
A change in depreciation method is also applied prospectively, as a change in estimate effected by a change in principle, and the new method must be preferable. An error, such as expensing a machine that should have been capitalized, is different: it is corrected by restating the prior years.
Some companies use group or composite depreciation for many similar assets. They apply one rate to the whole pool, and when an asset is retired they record no gain or loss.
EXAM TRAP   Depreciation traps
Double-declining: apply the rate to book value; never subtract salvage first, and never go below salvage.
SYD: use the remaining life at the start of the year, applied to the depreciable base.
Changes in life, salvage or method: prospective. Errors: restate.
● IFRS CONTRAST   IAS 16: components and revaluation
IAS 16 requires component depreciation: each significant part of an asset is depreciated separately. U.S. GAAP allows this but does not require it. IAS 16 also allows the revaluation model, with increases going to a revaluation surplus in OCI. U.S. GAAP does not allow upward revaluation.

10.3  Recommending a depreciation method
The best method matches the pattern in which the company uses up the asset's benefits. Figure F10-06 turns this into two questions.
Figure F10-06. Decision path for recommending a depreciation method.
A delivery truck wears out with kilometres driven, so units of production fits. A computer server loses value quickly as technology changes, so an accelerated method fits. An office building gives similar service each year, so straight-line fits. For the bottling line, machine hours drive the wear, so units of production gives the best matching; straight-line is simpler and is acceptable if use is fairly even.
Other factors can support the choice: comparability with other companies in the industry, the cost of tracking usage, and management's view of income smoothing. The tax method is a separate decision, so a company does not need to use the same method for its books.
EXAM TRAP   Give the reason
In a Level C question, a recommendation earns credit only with a reason from the facts: how the asset is used and when its benefits arrive.

10.4  Disposal of fixed assets
When a company sells or scraps an asset, it first records depreciation up to the date of disposal. Then it removes the cost and the accumulated depreciation and compares the proceeds with the carrying amount. If the proceeds are higher, it records a gain; if lower, a loss. The gain or loss is part of income from continuing operations. It is not revenue.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Selling an old oven in Dubai
An old pastry oven cost $60,000. At January 1, accumulated depreciation was $40,000, and straight-line depreciation is $8,000 a year. Orontes sells the oven after 3 months for $21,000. Figure F10-07 shows the steps.
Figure F10-07. Disposal of the Dubai oven: update depreciation, find the carrying amount, then the gain.
Two special cases appear on the exam. In an involuntary conversion, such as a fire or a government expropriation, the company records a gain or loss even if it reinvests the insurance money. In an exchange of assets with commercial substance, the new asset is recorded at fair value and the gain or loss is recognized; without commercial substance, the exchange is recorded at book value.
EXAM TRAP   Update first
Record depreciation up to the sale date before you compute the gain or loss.
Compare proceeds with the carrying amount, not with the original cost.

10.5  Impairment of long-lived assets, intangibles and goodwill
An impairment occurs when a company cannot recover an asset's carrying amount. U.S. GAAP uses different tests for different assets.
PP&E and finite-lived intangibles held and used
The company tests an asset group (the lowest level with its own cash flows) only when indicators suggest a problem, such as a sharp fall in market price, physical damage or continuing losses. The test has two steps. In the recoverability test, the company compares the carrying amount with the sum of the undiscounted future cash flows. If the carrying amount is not higher, there is no impairment, even if fair value is lower. If it is higher, the company measures the loss as carrying amount minus fair value. Figure F10-08 applies the test to Orontes's Jordan plant.
Figure F10-08. ASC 360 impairment of the Jordan plant: recoverability test, then loss against fair value.
The reduced amount becomes the new cost basis and is depreciated over the remaining life. The loss is never reversed under U.S. GAAP.
Assets held for sale
An asset is held for sale when management has committed to a plan to sell it, it is available for immediate sale, and the sale is expected within one year. It is measured at the lower of its carrying amount and fair value less cost to sell, and it is no longer depreciated. If fair value less cost to sell rises later, the company records a gain, but only up to the losses it recognized before.
Intangibles and goodwill
Finite-lived intangibles, such as patents, are subject to amortization over their useful lives and follow the PP&E test. An indefinite-lived intangible asset, such as a brand with no expected end, is not amortized. It is tested at least every year in one step: carrying amount against fair value.
Goodwill is not amortized by public companies. It is tested at least every year at the level of the reporting unit. A company may first make a qualitative assessment; if it is not more likely than not that the unit's fair value is below its carrying amount, no further test is needed. Otherwise it applies a single step: the loss is the unit's carrying amount minus its fair value, but never more than the goodwill. Figure F10-09 shows both cases.

● IFRS CONTRAST   IAS 36 and IAS 38
IAS 36 uses one step: compare the carrying amount with the recoverable amount, which is the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use (a discounted amount). IFRS allows the reversal of impairment losses on assets other than goodwill. IAS 38 requires development costs to be capitalized when six criteria are met. These are the classic IFRS habits that cost marks on the U.S. GAAP exam.
EXAM TRAP   Impairment traps
Step 1 uses UNDISCOUNTED cash flows. Step 2 measures the loss against FAIR VALUE.
Goodwill: one step, and the loss is capped at the goodwill balance.
No reversals for assets held and used, indefinite-lived intangibles or goodwill. Held for sale: gains only up to earlier losses.
Stop depreciating an asset once it is held for sale.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Impairment in three languages

Chapter summary
Figure F10-11 brings the chapter together.
Figure F10-11. Chapter 10 summary map: the key rule for each section.
PP&E is recorded at the cost of getting it ready for use, including interest on self-built assets. Straight-line, declining-balance, sum-of-the-years'-digits and units-of-production methods charge the same total at different times, and changes in estimates are prospective. The best method matches how the asset's benefits are used. On disposal, depreciation is brought up to date and the gain or loss is proceeds minus carrying amount. PP&E held and used is impaired only if undiscounted cash flows are below carrying amount, and the loss is measured against fair value. Indefinite-lived intangibles use a one-step fair value test, goodwill a single step capped at goodwill, and U.S. GAAP never reverses these losses.

Written task: model answer
Both assets had an indicator of impairment, so Orontes tested them. Under U.S. GAAP the first step is recoverability: the carrying amount is compared with the undiscounted cash flows the asset will produce. The warehouse's undiscounted cash flows are higher than its carrying amount, so it is recoverable and no loss is recorded, even though its fair value is lower. The pastry line's undiscounted cash flows are below its carrying amount, so Orontes measures a loss as carrying amount minus fair value. If the pastry line's value recovers later, U.S. GAAP does not allow a reversal of the loss.
A good answer: explains the role of the indicator; describes the recoverability test with undiscounted cash flows; explains why the warehouse passes; measures the pastry line loss against fair value; states that the loss is not reversed.
