WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 11 split the master-budget variance into a sales-volume and a flexible-budget variance. Chapter 12 split an input variance into price and efficiency. This chapter splits each of those parts once more.
13.1  Sales-mix and sales-quantity variances
When a division sells more than one product, the sales-volume variance of Chapter 11 has two causes: the total number of units, and the proportions in which they were sold. Figure F313-02 shows the split.
Figure F313-02. The sales-volume variance split into mix and quantity.
The sales-mix variance measures the proportions. It asks what the contribution margin would have been if the actual total units had been sold in the budgeted mix. The sales-quantity variance measures the total, valued at the budgeted weighted-average contribution margin.

The mix matters because the products earn different margins. Selling a larger share of the cheapest jars costs contribution margin even when the total number of units is unchanged. The effect on revenue follows the same logic, using selling prices.
EXAM TRAP   Mix uses the actual total
The sales-mix variance is always calculated on the actual total units. Using the budgeted total turns it into a different variance and the two parts will not add to the sales-volume variance.

13.2  Materials mix and yield variances
The same idea applies to inputs that can be substituted for each other. Halva is made from sesame paste and sugar in a standard blend; using more paste and less sugar changes the cost even if the total input is unchanged.
The usage variance of Chapter 12 therefore splits into a materials mix variance and a materials yield variance. Figure F313-07 shows the three columns, all valued at standard prices.
Figure F313-07. The three columns for a materials blend.

EXAM TRAP   Yield is valued at the average
The yield variance is valued at the weighted-average standard price of the blend, $1.90 a kilogram here, not at the price of one input.

13.3  Variances in service companies
A service company has no finished goods, but it still has standards: hours per delivery, hours per audit, minutes per call. Every variance in Chapters 11 to 13 applies, with labor hours in place of materials.

Levant made 2,400 deliveries, allowing 1,200 hours at $10.00; it worked 1,260 hours at $10.40. The overhead spending variance compares actual variable overhead with the standard rate applied to the hours worked. Figure F313-10 shows all four variances.

Chapter summary
Figure F313-13 brings the chapter together.
Figure F313-13. Chapter 13 summary map: the key rule for each section.
When several products are sold, the sales-volume variance splits in two. The sales-mix variance is measured on the actual total units at each product's budgeted margin, and the sales-quantity variance at the budgeted weighted-average margin. When inputs can be substituted, the usage variance splits into a materials mix variance, measured on the actual total input, and a yield variance, valued at the weighted-average standard price. All of these variances, together with price, efficiency and spending variances, apply in service companies, where hours replace materials and nothing is held in stock.
