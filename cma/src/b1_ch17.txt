WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
If you trained in IFRS, your habits will be the traps in this chapter: capitalizing development, reversing write-downs, revaluing assets and using value in use. For each area, learn the U.S. rule first and treat IFRS as the contrast.
17.1  (i) Share-based payments and employee benefits
For share awards that vest in parts, or graded vesting, U.S. GAAP lets a company recognize the cost straight-line over the whole period or tranche by tranche, when the awards have only service conditions. IFRS 2 requires tranche by tranche, which puts more expense in the early years.
U.S. GAAP also allows a policy choice to record forfeitures as they occur; IFRS 2 requires an estimate. Employer payroll taxes on share awards are recognized when the tax obligation arises under U.S. GAAP, but accrued over the vesting period under IFRS.
For a defined benefit plan, both frameworks put remeasurements in OCI. U.S. GAAP later amortizes them into net income; IAS 19 never reclassifies them. Orontes amends its plan and creates prior service cost of $50,000: U.S. GAAP amortizes $5,000 a year from OCI, while IAS 19 expenses the whole amount at once. Figure F17-02 lists the differences.

17.2  (ii) Intangible assets
The largest intangibles difference is development costs. U.S. GAAP expenses research and development as incurred, with narrow exceptions for software. IAS 38 expenses research but capitalizes development once six criteria are met, such as technical feasibility, intention and ability to complete, and a way to generate future benefits.
Suppose Orontes spends $300,000 developing a new sparkling-juice line, and all six criteria are met. Under U.S. GAAP the whole amount is an expense; under IFRS it is an intangible asset, amortized once the product is ready.
IAS 38 also allows the revaluation model for intangibles, but only where an active market exists, which is rare. U.S. GAAP never revalues intangibles upward. Both frameworks refuse to recognize internally generated brands. Figure F17-03 compares the rules.

17.3  (iii) Inventories
IFRS does not allow LIFO. U.S. GAAP allows it, and companies that use LIFO for tax must also use it in their financial statements. Chapter 7 showed how LIFO changes cost of goods sold when prices rise.
Both frameworks write inventory down when its value falls below cost. IAS 2 uses the lower of cost and NRV for all inventory. U.S. GAAP uses lower of cost and NRV for FIFO and average cost, but lower of cost or market for LIFO and the retail method.
The key difference comes when value recovers. IFRS reverses the write-down, but never above the original cost. U.S. GAAP never reverses it. Figure F17-04 shows Orontes's olive oil: both frameworks write it down by $100,000, but only IFRS adds back $50,000 when NRV recovers.
Figure F17-04. Inventory write-down and recovery: IFRS reverses, U.S. GAAP does not.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Stocks, dépréciation, provision
French stocks means inventory, not shares. French dépréciation des stocks is a write-down, not depreciation. In U.S. terms, a write-down reduces the inventory itself; do not call it a provision or a reserve.

17.4  (iv) Leases: the lessee
IFRS 16 has one lessee model: every lease is treated like a finance lease, with depreciation of the right-of-use asset plus interest. U.S. GAAP keeps two models, and an operating lease has one straight-line cost.
For Orontes's warehouse from Chapter 14, U.S. GAAP shows a lease cost of $100,000 in 2026. IFRS 16 shows depreciation of $84,247 plus interest of $25,274: $109,521, which is higher in the early years. Over the whole lease, total expense is the same.
IFRS 16 also allows an exemption for low-value assets, remeasures the liability when an index such as CPI changes, and classifies lease principal as financing. Figure F17-05 compares the rules.

17.5  (v) Long-lived assets
U.S. GAAP carries property, plant and equipment at cost less depreciation. IAS 16 also allows the revaluation model for a whole class of assets: the assets are carried at fair value, and increases go to a revaluation surplus in OCI.
Suppose Orontes's land in Amman cost $2,000,000 and is now worth $2,600,000. Under U.S. GAAP it stays at cost. Under IFRS, if Orontes chose the revaluation model, it would show the land at fair value and a surplus of $600,000 in OCI.
IFRS requires component depreciation for significant parts with different lives; U.S. GAAP allows but does not require it. Both frameworks capitalize borrowing costs on assets under construction, but IFRS deducts income earned on specific borrowings. Figure F17-06 lists the differences.

17.6  (vi) Impairment
U.S. GAAP tests a held-and-used asset group in two steps. First, it compares the carrying amount with undiscounted future cash flows. Only if the carrying amount is higher does it measure a loss, equal to the carrying amount minus fair value.
IAS 36 uses one step. It compares the carrying amount of an asset or cash-generating unit with its recoverable amount: the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use, a discounted cash-flow measure. Because there is no undiscounted screen, IFRS losses often come earlier.
The frameworks also differ after the loss. IFRS reverses an impairment when the recoverable amount rises, but not for goodwill and not above the carrying amount without the loss. U.S. GAAP never reverses an impairment of a held-and-used asset or of goodwill. Figure F17-07 applies both tests to Orontes's beverage plant.
Figure F17-07. The same plant under the U.S. two-step test and the IFRS one-step test.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: the beverage plant
Step 1 (U.S.). Undiscounted cash flows of $4,200,000 are above the carrying amount of $4,000,000: the plant is recoverable, so there is no loss.
Step 2 (IFRS). Recoverable amount is the higher of $3,500,000 and $3,700,000: $3,700,000.
Step 3 (IFRS). The carrying amount is higher, so the loss is $300,000.
Check. If the U.S. test had failed, the loss would have been measured against fair value: $500,000. Figure F17-07 shows both tests.

Chapter summary
Figure F17-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F17-09. Chapter 17 summary map: the key difference in each area.
In share-based pay, U.S. GAAP allows a straight-line choice for graded vesting; IFRS requires tranche by tranche. Pension remeasurements are amortized from OCI under U.S. GAAP but never recycled under IFRS, and prior service cost is expensed at once only under IFRS. Development costs are expensed under U.S. GAAP and capitalized under IFRS when six criteria are met. LIFO is U.S. only, and write-down reversals are IFRS only. U.S. operating leases have a straight-line cost; IFRS 16 front-loads all leases. Revaluation is allowed only under IFRS. U.S. impairment uses undiscounted cash flows and then fair value, with no reversal; IFRS uses recoverable amount and allows reversal except for goodwill.

Written task: model answer
Under U.S. GAAP, development costs of 240 must be expensed, so they reduce net income. The inventory write-down reversal of 30 is not allowed, so it is removed. The fleet passes the U.S. recoverability test, so the IFRS impairment of 50 is added back, and the truck lease cost is 6 lower as a U.S. operating lease. The land revaluation is not allowed either, but it was in OCI, so it reduces equity, not net income. In total, net income falls by 214 to 900, the figure Orontes uses to consolidate Levant.
A good answer: expenses development costs; removes the inventory reversal; adds back the impairment because the recoverability test is passed; adjusts the lease cost; keeps the revaluation out of net income but removes it from equity.
