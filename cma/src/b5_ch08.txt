Chapter 8
Solvency, and the Two Leverages
الملاءة المالية والرافعتان
Liquidity asks whether a company can pay what falls due in the coming year. Solvency asks whether it can meet all its obligations over their whole lives, and whether it would survive a bad year. The two are different questions and a company can pass one and fail the other: a business with large cash balances and debt it cannot ultimately service is liquid and insolvent, and a business with no cash and no debt is illiquid and perfectly solvent.
Between the two sits leverage, which is the word English uses for the effect of a fixed cost on a variable result. There are two kinds. Operating leverage comes from fixed costs in the business; financial leverage comes from fixed charges in the financing. Both magnify, in both directions, and both are measured.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Part 1 taught you to separate fixed from variable costs and to compute a contribution margin. This chapter uses both, and adds the financing side. If the contribution margin is unfamiliar, it is sales less variable costs: what each extra unit contributes towards covering the fixed costs.
8.1  Solvency against liquidity
Liquidity is about the next twelve months and about current assets against current liabilities. Solvency is about the whole balance sheet and the whole life of the obligations.
The two are measured with different ratios and answer to different people. A supplier granting 30-day terms cares about liquidity. A bondholder lending for ten years cares about solvency. Figure F508-02 sets them apart.

Two combinations are worth naming because the exam sets them.
Liquid and insolvent. A company sells assets, holds the proceeds in cash, and still owes more than the business can ever earn. Its current ratio is excellent on the day the receiver is appointed.
Illiquid and solvent. A company with no debt, substantial plant and a cash squeeze caused by one slow customer. It is in difficulty and it is not insolvent, and the remedy is a credit line rather than a restructuring.
Orontes is comfortably both. Its liquidity ratios are in Chapter 6; its solvency is this chapter and the next.
EXAM TRAP   The two words are not interchangeable
A question about paying debts "as they fall due" over the coming year is liquidity.
A question about the ability to meet obligations over the long term, or about the debt-to-equity ratio, is solvency.
Insolvency has a second, legal meaning — liabilities exceeding assets — and the exam sometimes uses it. Read which is meant.

8.2  Operating leverage
Operating leverage is the effect of fixed operating costs on operating income. A company whose costs are mostly fixed sees its operating income swing far more than its sales; a company whose costs are mostly variable sees the two move together.
The measure is the degree of operating leverage, written DOL. It is the percentage change in operating income divided by the percentage change in sales, and at a given level of sales it can be computed directly as the contribution margin divided by operating income.
Orontes's total operating cost in 2025 was cost of goods sold of $27,300,000 plus operating expenses of $10,524,000, which is 27,300,000 + 10,524,000 = 37,824,000. Its cost accountants split that into variable costs of $25,200,000 and fixed costs of $12,624,000.
Contribution margin is therefore 42,000,000 − 25,200,000 = 16,800,000, and operating income is 16,800,000 − 12,624,000 = 4,176,000, which is the figure on the income statement.
The degree of operating leverage is 16,800,000 ÷ 4,176,000 = 4.02.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: what a DOL of 4.02 means
Step 1. Read it as a multiplier. A 1 per cent change in sales produces a 4.02 per cent change in operating income, in the same direction.
Step 2. Test it upwards. Sales rise 10 per cent to $46,200,000. Variable costs rise 10 per cent to $27,720,000. Contribution is 46,200,000 − 27,720,000 = 18,480,000; fixed costs are unchanged, so operating income is 18,480,000 − 12,624,000 = 5,856,000. The change is 5,856,000 − 4,176,000 = 1,680,000, which on 4,176,000 is 40.2 per cent — ten times 4.02, as predicted.
Step 3. Test it downwards. Sales fall 10 per cent and operating income falls 40.2 per cent, to $2,496,000. The multiplier works both ways, and that is the risk.
Check. 4.02 is high. A company at this level of operating leverage loses a quarter of its operating income on a 6 per cent fall in sales. For Orontes the cause is visible on the balance sheet: 46.7 per cent of its assets are plant, and plant produces depreciation whether or not anything is sold.

EXAM TRAP   DOL moves as sales move
The degree of operating leverage is computed at one level of sales and changes as sales change. It is highest near breakeven and falls as sales rise above it.
DOL uses the contribution margin, not gross profit. Gross profit deducts all cost of goods sold, including the fixed part.
A high DOL is not bad. It is a description of a cost structure, and it rewards growth exactly as much as it punishes decline.

8.3  Financial leverage
Financial leverage is the same idea applied to the financing. Interest is a fixed charge: it is owed whether or not the company earned anything. So a change in operating income produces a larger change in what is left for the shareholders.
The measure is the degree of financial leverage, written DFL. It is the percentage change in net income, or in earnings per share, divided by the percentage change in operating income. At a given level it is computed as earnings before interest and taxes divided by earnings before interest and taxes less interest.
Orontes's earnings before interest and taxes is income before income taxes of $3,958,800 plus interest expense of $390,000, which is 3,958,800 + 390,000 = 4,348,800.
The degree of financial leverage is 4,348,800 ÷ 3,958,800 = 1.10.
A DFL of 1.10 is very low, and it says what Chapter 2 already implied: Orontes borrows very little, so the fixed charge is small and the magnifying effect is small with it. A company financed half by debt might carry a DFL of 1.8 or 2.0.
Note what does not appear in the formula. Tax does not, because tax is proportional and a proportional charge does not magnify anything. Repayments of principal do not, because they are not an expense. Only the interest matters.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: DFL at two capital structures
Step 1. As it is. EBIT $4,348,800, interest $390,000. DFL = 4,348,800 ÷ 3,958,800 = 1.10. A 10 per cent change in EBIT changes pre-tax income by 11.0 per cent.
Step 2. If Orontes borrowed $12,000,000 more at 6 per cent, interest would rise by 12,000,000 × 0.06 = 720,000 to 390,000 + 720,000 = 1,110,000.
Step 3. Then DFL = 4,348,800 ÷ 3,238,800 = 1.34. The same 10 per cent change in EBIT would now move pre-tax income by 13.4 per cent.
Check. The business did not change; only the financing did. More debt, more magnification, in both directions. Chapter 9 measures the same decision from the balance sheet side.

8.4  The two together
The two leverages multiply. A change in sales is magnified first by the fixed operating costs and then by the fixed financing charge, and the product is the degree of total, or combined, leverage.
For Orontes: 4.02 × 1.10 = 4.42. A 1 per cent change in sales produces about a 4.42 per cent change in income before tax.

Ten per cent off the top becomes forty-two per cent off the bottom. That is the arithmetic the board has to understand before it decides anything about either cost structure or borrowing, and it is why Chapter 9 treats the capital structure decision as a decision about risk and not only about cost.
The exam's point is usually the asymmetry of the two choices. Operating leverage is largely given: a manufacturer has plant and a distributor does not, and changing it means changing what the business is. Financial leverage is chosen: the board can borrow or not, this quarter, by resolution. So two companies with the same operating leverage can carry very different total leverage, and the difference is a decision somebody made.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Leverage" and "gearing"
American English says leverage; British English often says gearing, and the exam uses leverage throughout. Arabic الرافعة and French levier are the same metaphor: a lever magnifies a small force. Note that English also uses leverage as a verb in business writing, meaning to make use of something. That sense never appears in the exam.

8.5  What the leverages do not say
Three limits, and the exam uses all three.
A degree of leverage is a point estimate. Both are computed at one level of sales and both change as sales change. A DOL of 4.02 is true at $42,000,000 of sales and not at $30,000,000, where it would be far higher. The exam sometimes gives two sales levels precisely to test this.
A leverage figure says nothing about probability. A DOL of 4.02 tells you what happens if sales fall 10 per cent; it does not tell you whether they will. The risk is the product of the magnification and the likelihood, and the statements give only the first.
A leverage figure assumes the cost split holds. Fixed costs are fixed only within a range of activity. A fall in volume large enough to close a line turns a fixed cost into an avoidable one, and a rise large enough to need a second shift adds a step. Part 1 called this the relevant range, and it bounds everything in this chapter.

Chapter summary
Figure F508-08 brings the chapter together.
Figure F508-08. Chapter 8 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Liquidity asks about the coming year; solvency asks about the whole life of the obligations, and a company can be liquid and insolvent or illiquid and solvent. Between them sits leverage, the effect of a fixed cost on a variable result. Operating leverage comes from fixed costs in the business and is measured as contribution margin over operating income: for Orontes, 16,800,000 ÷ 4,176,000 = 4.02, so a 10 per cent movement in sales moves operating income by 40.2 per cent in either direction, and the cause is visible as 46.7 per cent of assets held in plant. Financial leverage comes from the fixed interest charge and is measured as EBIT over EBIT less interest: 4,348,800 ÷ 3,958,800 = 1.10, very low, because Orontes borrows very little. The two multiply to a combined leverage of 4.42, so ten per cent off sales is forty-two per cent off pre-tax income. Operating leverage is largely given by what the business is; financial leverage is chosen by the board. And every one of these figures is a point estimate, says nothing about how likely the movement is, and holds only within the relevant range.
