Chapter 3
Budgeting Concepts: Roles, People and Behavior
مفاهيم الموازنة: الأدوار والأفراد والسلوك
In September, the Beverages sales manager sent his first budget for 2028: 820,000 cases of juice. The controller's market data pointed to 900,000. The gap is not a mistake in arithmetic; it is about people, targets and bonuses. This chapter explains what budgets are for and how people shape them.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 1 set the strategic goals and Chapter 2 chose the strategies. Book 2 built the standard cost of a case of juice. The budget now turns all of these into next year's numbers.
3.1  What budgets are for
A budget is a quantitative plan for a coming period, usually in money. It turns the strategic plan into short-term objectives: how many cases to sell, what to spend and what profit to earn. It also tells every unit what the others plan to do.
The budget cycle in Figure F303-02 runs from the strategic plan to guidelines, preparation, approval, monitoring and revision. At the end of the year, the budget becomes the yardstick for judging results.
Figure F303-02. The Orontes budget cycle.
Figure F303-03 lists the roles of the budget. For planning and control, it sets the targets each month. For coordination, it makes sure that sales, production and purchasing plan for the same volume.
Figure F303-03. The roles of the budget at Orontes

A budget measures performance only if the targets were realistic and agreed. When a Beverages manager exceeds a budget that everyone understood, the result means something; when the budget was imposed without discussion, it means much less.

3.2  The economy, the industry and the budget
Every budget starts from assumptions about the world outside: economic growth, inflation, exchange rates and the state of the industry. If the assumptions are wrong, every number built on them is wrong too.
The Beverages sales budget starts from the forecast size of Jordan's juice market, 11,250,000 cases, and Orontes's share of 8.0% from Chapter 2. That gives an evidence-based volume of 900,000 cases. Figure F303-04 lists the main assumptions.

3.3  Allocating resources through the budget
Resources are always scarce. The budget is where the company decides who gets what: money, people, machines and time. The decision should follow the strategy, not the size of each request.

3.4  Time frames and successful budgeting
Each budget has its own time frame. A capital budget follows the life of the project; the master budget covers one year, split into quarters or months; the cash budget may be weekly. Figure F303-06 lists the usual frames.
Figure F303-06. Time frames of the main budgets

A budget that is too long becomes a guess; one that is too short misses the effect of decisions. The right frame matches the decision it supports.
Successful budgeting is linked to strategy, realistic, participative, flexible within a clear policy, and followed by timely feedback. Figure F303-07 pairs these characteristics with the role of top management.

3.5  People: participation and controllable costs
In an imposed budget, top management sets every figure. In participative budgeting, the managers who must carry out the budget help prepare it. Figure F303-08 compares the two.
Figure F303-08. Imposed and participative budgeting.
The best results usually come from participation with review. Managers who know the market and the plant propose the figures, and top management checks them against the evidence and the strategy.
A controllable cost is one a manager can significantly influence in the period. A manager should be judged on controllable costs only; other costs can be reported for information but should not affect the evaluation.

3.6  Budgetary slack, revisions and monitoring
Budgetary slack is the gap between a manager's easy figure and the figure the evidence supports. Sales are budgeted too low or costs too high, so the target is easy to beat.
Slack damages goal congruence: the manager earns a bonus while the company plans for less than it could achieve. Figure F303-10 shows the slack in the Beverages proposal, worth $656,000 of contribution margin.
Figure F303-10. Budgetary slack in the Beverages sales proposal.
Budgets sometimes need revising during the year, but only under a clear policy. A good policy revises for large changes outside managers' control, and keeps the targets for things managers can control.
In July, the concentrate price rose 10%. Figure F303-11 applies the policy: the materials budget is revised, but the usage target is not.

EXAM TRAP   Slack is measured from the evidence
Slack is the evidence-based figure minus the padded figure. Measuring it against the manager's own proposal, or as a share of the proposal, gives the wrong answer.

Chapter summary
Figure F303-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F303-14. Chapter 3 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Budgets turn the strategic plan into short-term objectives, coordinate and communicate between units, allocate scarce resources and provide the yardstick for performance. They rest on economic and industry assumptions. Each budget needs a suitable time frame, and top management must lead the process. Participation with review gives the best results, and managers are judged only on controllable costs. Slack, measured from the evidence, harms goal congruence; revisions follow a clear policy; and spending is monitored against the budget to date.

Case question: the Tahini & Spreads budget meeting
It is October, and the Tahini & Spreads division is preparing its budget for 2028. The division manager, Samir, has proposed sales of 500 g tahini jars that are well below what the controller, Rana, expects from signed orders and market data.
Samir says a new competitor may enter the Gulf market and that he does not want to promise too much. His bonus is paid when actual contribution margin beats the budget. Rana notes that the orders already signed with Barada and the Gulf importer support a higher figure.
Rana is also reviewing the plant manager's cost report. It lists the sesame used per jar, the price paid for sesame, the hours worked on the filling line, the depreciation of the plant, and head-office costs allocated to the division. The division's cost reports are prepared monthly.
The chief executive wants the meeting to end with a budget that is realistic and that the division accepts. She has asked Rana to measure the gap between the two figures and to suggest how to agree the budget without damaging the division's commitment.
