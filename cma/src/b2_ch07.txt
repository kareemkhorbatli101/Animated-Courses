WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 6 collected costs by job. Process costing uses the same accounts, raw materials, work in process and finished goods, but it keeps one work-in-process account for each department instead of one cost sheet for each job.
7.1  Process costing and departments
Process costing collects costs by department when identical units move through a continuous flow. It suits oil, juice, cement and chemicals. Each department has its own Work-in-Process account.
The Oils division has two departments. Crushing turns olives into extra-virgin oil, virgin oil and pomace. Bottling fills extra-virgin oil into bottles. Figure F207-02 shows the flow.
Figure F207-02. Oils process flow: crushing, split-off and bottling, each department with its own WIP account.
Unit cost in process costing is an average: department cost divided by the units it worked on. Every liter carries the same cost, which is fair when every liter is the same.
EXAM TRAP   Exam scope: process costing is concept only
The exam asks you to understand process costing and equivalent units, not to calculate them (LOS D.2.f). It tests spoilage calculations in job order and activity-based costing settings (Section 7.5, the case set and Chapter 12). The process numbers in this chapter show how the ideas work.

7.2  Transferred-in costs
When units leave a department, their cost goes with them. The entry is: debit Work in Process of the next department, credit Work in Process of the previous one. The next department records these as transferred-in costs.
For the next department, transferred-in cost behaves like a material added at the start. Bottling received extra-virgin oil at $8.06 a liter, the cost set by the joint cost allocation in Chapter 11. It then added bottles and conversion. Figure F207-03 shows the three layers.
Figure F207-03. Bottling cost layers: transferred-in cost, bottles and conversion.
Forgetting transferred-in cost is a common error. The bottling cost of $11.66 a liter includes the oil itself, not only the bottle and the work.

7.3  Equivalent units: the idea
At the end of a month some units are only partly finished. To share costs fairly, process costing expresses partly finished work as a number of whole units: equivalent units.
Each cost element has its own count. Materials added at the start are complete for every unit; conversion added evenly may be only partly done. Figure F207-04 shows a tank of 1,000 liters that is 60% through conversion.
Figure F207-04. Equivalent units: the idea behind costing partly finished work.
The CMA LOS ask you to understand the concept, not to calculate equivalent units. The Oils example avoids the issue: the batch was complete when it was inspected.
EXAM TRAP   No equivalent-unit arithmetic
LOS D.2.f says 'no calculations required'. Know what equivalent units mean and why they exist; do not spend exam time on weighted-average or FIFO schedules.

7.4  Spoilage: the ideas, shown in a process
Spoilage is units that fail inspection and are thrown away or sold cheaply. Normal spoilage is expected even when operations are efficient. Abnormal spoilage is not expected and could be controlled, for example after a machine failure.
In process costing, the cost of normal spoilage becomes part of the cost of good units. The cost of abnormal spoilage is taken out and recorded as a loss in the period. Figure F207-05 shows where each goes.
Figure F207-05. Decision chart: where normal and abnormal spoilage costs go.
Measure the normal rate on good output, not on input. If the policy allows 2% of good units, the normal amount is 2% × good units.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Concept illustration: normal or abnormal?
A process finishes 10,000 good units and finds 350 spoiled units at the final inspection. Normal spoilage is 200 units, so 150 units are abnormal. Figure F207-06 turns the units into dollars: a loss of $600, and good units at $4.08 each.

7.5  Spoilage, rework and scrap in job costing
Job costing has three cases. Normal spoilage caused by one job's special needs stays in that job. Normal spoilage common to all jobs goes to Manufacturing Overhead Control and is spread through the overhead rate. Abnormal spoilage is a loss. In every case, the disposal value of the spoiled units is recorded first.
Figure F207-07 records a spoiled lot on Tahini job T-0305 in each case. Rework, fixing units so they can be sold, follows the same three cases. Scrap, leftover material with a small value, reduces overhead or the job that caused it.
This is the setting in which the exam asks for spoilage calculations: the official statement on spoilage (D.2.c) covers job order and activity-based costing. The case set at the end of this chapter practises it with a month of Tahini jobs.

7.6  The strategic value of cost information
Product costs are not only for the balance sheet. Managers use them to set prices, choose the product mix and decide where to improve. A good bottle of extra-virgin oil costs $11.75; any price must cover that and the selling costs.
Job costing gives the same kind of information for each order. Tahini job T-0303 cost $346,000 in March (Chapter 6). When Barada asks for a repeat order, that job cost sheet is the starting point for the quote, and it shows which cost items to negotiate. Chapter 12 refines its overhead with activity-based costing.
Cost information also shows where value is lost. Each batch with abnormal spoilage like this one loses $11,660. Tracking spoilage by cause lets the Oils manager fix the line rather than accept the loss.
Poor overhead allocation can mislead every one of these decisions. Chapter 8, Chapter 10 and Chapter 12 show better ways to assign overhead. Figure F207-09 summarizes the main uses.

7.7  Extended Example 7A: olive batch H-2711 through two departments
This example follows one batch from the olives to the finished bottles, records every entry by department and handles spoilage at the final inspection. It is for depth: the exam does not test process calculations, but the same spoilage rules are tested in job settings (Section 7.5 and the case set).
1. Scenario
Crushing: olives $1,000,000, labor $120,000, overhead applied $280,000; total $1,400,000.
Split-off: pomace by-product $100,000; virgin oil $252,200, sold in bulk; extra-virgin oil $1,047,800 to bottling (Chapter 11 explains the split).
Bottling: bottles $312,000; labor $52,000; overhead applied $104,000.
Inspection: 130,000 liters in; 128,000 good; 1,000 normal and 1,000 abnormal spoilage; no disposal value.
2. Plan
Record crushing, split the joint cost, record bottling, compute the cost per liter, and move good output and abnormal spoilage out of Work in Process – Bottling.
3. Calculations
Bottling cost: $1,047,800 + $312,000 + $156,000 = $1,515,800; ÷ 130,000 liters = $11.66 per liter.
Abnormal loss: 1,000 liters × $11.66 = $11,660.
Finished goods: $1,515,800 − $11,660 = $1,504,140, which includes the normal spoilage; ÷ 128,000 good liters = $11.75 per liter.

Chapter summary
Figure F207-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F207-14. Chapter 7 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Process costing collects costs by department for identical units, and transferred-in cost moves with the product like a material added at the start. Equivalent units express partly finished work as whole units; the exam tests only the idea. Normal spoilage is part of the cost of good units, abnormal spoilage is a period loss, and the normal rate is based on good output. In job costing, spoilage goes to the job, to overhead or to a loss. Accurate costs support pricing, product mix and quality decisions.
