WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 16 described culture, the code and core values, and showed a company whose espoused values and underlying assumptions had come apart. Chapter 14 identified rationalisation as the side of the fraud triangle that culture attacks and noted that organisations manufacture their own pressure. Chapter 9 made culture a component of the COSO framework. This chapter is about the people at the top of all three.
17.1  Tone at the top
Tone at the top is the ethical signal that senior management and the board send through what they say, what they do and what they tolerate. The outcome calls it leadership by example, which is the mechanism: people take their standard from what they observe at the top of the organisation, not from what they are told.
Four reasons it is decisive, and an answer that gives them is answering the outcome rather than quoting the phrase.
It sets the reference point for what is normal. Chapter 16 made normality the strongest predictor of behaviour; the top of the organisation is where normality is defined.
It is the only defence against management override. Chapter 15 showed that no control prevents a sufficiently senior person from overriding it; what prevents the override is a leadership that does not do it and a culture in which it is questioned.
It determines whether bad news travels. A chief executive who reacts badly to problems will be told fewer of them, and the information on which every system in this book depends dries up at its source.
And it is read from small things. Expenses, punctuality, how a junior employee is spoken to, whether the leader takes the parking space, what happens to the person who disagrees in a meeting. Employees are extremely good at reading these signals and extremely quick to discount formal statements that conflict with them.
Three failures of tone are worth naming, because situations are written around them.
The inconsistent leader, whose statements are correct and whose decisions are not. This is the most damaging case: the gap itself becomes the lesson, and people conclude that the statements are a performance.
The absent leader, who says nothing about conduct at all, leaving every manager to infer a standard. Silence is not neutral; it is read as indifference, and in a company under pressure it is read as permission.
And the leader who sets targets that cannot be met honestly and then speaks about integrity. That is Chapter 14's manufactured pressure, delivered with a disclaimer, and it produces precisely the conduct it warns against.

17.2  Ethical leadership and its traits
Ethical leadership is leadership that is both ethical in itself and directed at making others' conduct ethical — the leader is a moral person and a moral manager, and the exam expects both halves. A leader who behaves impeccably and says nothing about standards has done only the first, and the organisation learns little from it.
The traits the outcome asks for, with what each looks like in practice.
Integrity and consistency. The same standard in private and in public, with customers and with suppliers, in a good quarter and a bad one. It is the trait everything else rests on, because inconsistency makes every other signal unreliable.
Honesty, including about uncertainty and about bad news. A leader who admits not knowing gives everybody else permission to do the same, which is how accurate information starts moving.
Fairness. Decisions made on stated criteria, consistently applied, with the three dimensions of Chapter 12 — outcome, process and treatment.
Courage. Making the costly decision and standing behind the person who made it. This is the trait on which Chapter 15's facilitation payment turns: somebody must bear the 230,135 of delay.
Concern for people and for those affected by the company's decisions, which is what makes the stakeholder reasoning of Chapter 12 something other than a rhetorical exercise.
Openness and accessibility. Inviting challenge, listening to it and visibly changing a decision occasionally as a result — because a leader who invites challenge and never accepts any teaches that the invitation is formal.
Accountability. Accepting responsibility for failures, including those of subordinates, and not permitting a cost to fall on the person who reported a problem.
And explicit communication about ethics. Talking about the awkward cases, naming the standard, explaining a difficult decision: the moral manager half of the definition, and the half most commonly missing in otherwise decent leadership.
Two further points carry marks.
Ethical leadership is not confined to the board. A supervisor is the tone at the top for the people who report to them, and the research is consistent that the immediate manager influences conduct more than the chief executive does. The board sets the possibility; the supervisor sets the practice.
And leaders are judged on the difficult decisions only. Everything is easy in a good year.

17.3  A climate where doing the right thing is expected
The outcome asks why it matters that doing the right thing is expected, and the answer is in the word expected. Where ethical conduct requires courage, it depends on the character of whoever happens to be in the room. Where it is expected, it is the default, and the courage is needed by the person who departs from it. Moving that burden is the whole objective.
The outcome names three mechanisms and each has content.
Hiring the right people. Checking conduct as well as competence: references that are actually taken, consistency in what a candidate says about past employers, how they describe a situation where they disagreed with a manager, and — for senior and sensitive roles — background verification proportionate to the position. Chapter 16 noted that values attract and repel people over time; hiring is where that works fastest.
Training them, which section 17.4 takes in detail.
Practising consistent, values-based leadership, which means the decisions of section 17.1 taken visibly and explained, and consistent consequences at every level.
Four more measures belong in a complete answer.
Set achievable targets, or accept that misses will be reported. Chapter 14's point stands: a company that creates impossible pressure and then relies on conduct standards has designed its own failure.
Make the right thing easy. A clear policy, a named person to ask, a threshold that decides, a process that supports a refusal. Most people do the right thing when it is available; the organisation's job is to make it available.
Reward the behaviour as well as the result. A sales manager who loses an order rather than make a side agreement, a plant manager who stops a line for a quality problem, a controller who reports a deficiency: if these cost those people something, every observer learns the real standard.
And protect those who raise concerns, visibly, which Chapter 18 treats in full.
A word the literature uses is worth knowing: psychological safety, the shared belief that one can speak up — raise a problem, admit an error, disagree with a superior — without being punished or humiliated. It is the single environmental condition most strongly associated with problems being reported early, and it is not the same as comfort or as low standards: the combination that produces good conduct is high standards with high safety. High standards with low safety produce concealment, which is the environment in which every large corporate failure has occurred.

17.4  Training
The outcome asks why employee training matters to an ethical culture, and the honest answer has two halves: what it does and what it cannot do.
What it does.
It makes the standard known, which is a precondition for enforcing it and a defence for the organisation.
It supplies recognition. Most ethical failures begin with somebody not noticing that a situation is an ethical one — a request that sounds administrative, a favour that sounds like courtesy. Training's main value is pattern recognition: this is what it looks like when it starts.
It supplies language and scripts. Training that gives people the actual sentence to say — "I'm not able to do that; our policy requires it to go through the register" — raises the likelihood of refusal more than any amount of principle, because the difficulty is rarely knowing that something is wrong and usually knowing what to say.
It communicates the route: whom to ask, whom to tell, what happens next.
And it signals that the organisation takes the subject seriously, provided it is delivered seriously. Training delivered by a senior leader who stays for the discussion says something that an online module does not.
What it cannot do.
It cannot substitute for tone. Training that contradicts what people observe is discounted immediately, and it does harm, because it demonstrates that the organisation says things it does not mean.
It cannot make an impossible target possible.
And it cannot be measured by completion. Chapter 16 made the point: a hundred per cent completion rate measures that a form was signed.
What makes it effective, in five points.
Situations, not principles. Real cases from this business — the shipment at the port, the gift at the new year, the invoice with the wrong date, the request to delay a write-down — discussed, with disagreement allowed.
Role-specific. The finance team, the sales team and the warehouse face different situations and need different training.
Delivered by managers, not only by a function, because the person delivering it is part of the message.
Repeated, short and timely — at induction, at promotion, before the budget cycle, when a new market opens — rather than annually and at length.
And with feedback: what people raised, what the organisation did about it, what changed as a result.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: what the training costs, and against what
Step 1. The programme. Two hours a year for all 400 employees, delivered in groups by their own managers, using six situations drawn from the company's own history.
Step 2. The cost. 400 × 2 = 800 hours of time, at an average fully-loaded 35 an hour, is 800 × 35 = 28,000, plus the preparation and the managers' time.
Step 3. Against what. The fictitious supplier of Chapter 12 took 261,000, which at Orontes's margin needs 261,000 ÷ 0.0707 = 3,691,655 of sales to replace. One prevented incident of that size pays for the programme nine times over.
Step 4. The honest qualification. Nobody can demonstrate that the training prevented it, which is Chapter 4's measurement problem exactly. The defensible argument is not that the training pays for itself but that the cost is small, the standard must be communicated somehow, and the alternative — an organisation where nobody has discussed these situations before meeting one — is indefensible.

17.5  Groupthink and diversity of thought
Two outcomes close the chapter and they are the same subject from opposite ends.
Groupthink is the tendency of a cohesive group to reach agreement at the expense of realistic appraisal — to converge on a decision because the group is converging, with dissent suppressed from inside as much as from outside. Its symptoms, which the exam asks for, are these.
An illusion of invulnerability, producing excessive optimism and risk-taking.
Collective rationalisation, in which warnings are explained away rather than examined.
Belief in the group's inherent morality, which removes the ethical question from consideration entirely — "we are good people, so what we are doing must be acceptable."
Stereotyped views of outsiders: regulators who do not understand the business, competitors who are reckless, critics with an agenda.
Direct pressure on dissenters, framed as loyalty or as being constructive.
Self-censorship, where members suppress their own doubts because they believe they are alone in them.
An illusion of unanimity, produced by that silence, in which everybody believes everybody else agrees.
And self-appointed mindguards, who protect the group from inconvenient information before it arrives.
Its impact on ethical behaviour, which the outcome asks you to analyse, is severe and specific. The third and sixth symptoms do the damage: a group that believes in its own morality does not examine the ethics of its decision at all, and a member who doubts says nothing because they believe they are the only one. The result is that a group of individually decent people reaches a decision that none of them would have taken alone, and every member later reports having had misgivings. That pattern is present in almost every major corporate ethical failure, and it explains why ethical failure so rarely requires a bad person.
The conditions that produce it are worth knowing, because they are the ones a board should watch for: high cohesion, a directive leader who states a preference early, insulation from outside views, homogeneous membership, no procedure for considering alternatives, and stress with a deadline.
The remedies are the other outcome, and the first is structural.
The leader states a preference last, or not at all, so that the group's view is not formed before it is examined.
Somebody is assigned to argue the other side — the devil's advocate — as a role rather than as a personality, so that disagreement is not disloyalty.
Independent subgroups consider the same question separately and then compare.
Outside views are brought in: advisers, the people affected, a function with a different interest.
A second-chance meeting is held after an apparent agreement, specifically to voice remaining doubts.
And anonymous input is collected where the hierarchy makes speech costly.
Diversity of thought is the broader answer. It means a group whose members bring genuinely different perspectives, experience, expertise and assumptions — which correlates with, but is not the same as, diversity of background, function, tenure, nationality and demographics. Its contribution to good ethical decisions runs through four mechanisms.
It supplies the dissent groupthink suppresses. A person who has not spent fifteen years in the same company asks the question everybody else stopped asking.
It brings the perspectives of people affected into a room they are otherwise absent from, which is the practical form of the stakeholder reasoning of Chapter 12.
It slows the group down. Homogeneous groups agree faster and more confidently; diverse groups take longer, feel less comfortable and are measurably more accurate, and that discomfort is the mechanism rather than a side effect.
And it widens the range of options considered, which matters because many ethical failures are failures of imagination — the group could see only two courses, one of which was unacceptable.
Two qualifications that a strong answer includes. Diversity delivers nothing unless the different perspectives are actually heard, which returns to the psychological safety of section 17.3: a diverse group with a directive leader produces the same decision as a homogeneous one, more slowly. And the test is not representation in the room but influence on the outcome — whether a decision has ever been changed by a view that came from outside the dominant group.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Tone", "dissent" and "team player"
Tone at the top is an unusual metaphor, borrowed from music: a tone is what everything else is tuned to, and the image is right, since what leaders signal sets the pitch for everything below. English also uses tone for manner of speech, which makes the phrase sound as though it were about how leaders talk; it is about what they do, and an answer that treats it as communication has missed the point. Dissent is the word to be careful with, because in most organisations it has acquired a negative colour — dissent suggests obstruction, where disagreement is a contribution. Groups that handle this well give disagreement a role and a name, which is what the devil's advocate device does: it converts a personality trait into a job. And team player is the most effective phrase in the language for suppressing a concern, because it praises the behaviour it is asking for while making the alternative sound like a character flaw. Arabic روح الفريق and French esprit d'équipe carry the same flattery. Whenever somebody is invited to be a team player, the question worth asking is what the team is about to do.

Chapter summary
Figure F817-03 brings the chapter together.
Figure F817-03. Chapter 17 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Tone at the top is the ethical signal senior people send by what they say, do and tolerate; it sets the reference point for what is normal, is the only real defence against management override, determines whether bad news travels, and is read from small things — failing through the inconsistent leader whose decisions contradict their statements, the absent leader whose silence is read as permission, and the leader who sets impossible targets and then speaks about integrity. Ethical leadership requires a moral person and a moral manager, with consistency, honesty, fairness, courage, concern for others, openness to challenge, accountability and explicit communication about ethics — and it belongs to every supervisor, since the immediate manager influences conduct more than the chief executive. A climate where right conduct is expected shifts the burden of courage onto the person who departs from it, built by hiring for conduct, training, consistent values-based leadership, achievable targets, making the right thing easy, rewarding the behaviour as well as the result, and protecting those who raise concerns — with psychological safety the condition most associated with early reporting, and high standards without it producing concealment. Training makes the standard known, supplies recognition and the actual words to say, communicates the route and signals seriousness, at a cost of 400 × 2 = 800 hours and 800 × 35 = 28,000 against a single fraud of 261,000 — but it cannot substitute for tone, make an impossible target possible, or be measured by completion. And groupthink, through its eight symptoms and above all the belief in the group's own morality and the self-censorship that manufactures unanimity, lets decent people reach decisions none of them would take alone — answered by a leader who states a preference last, an assigned devil's advocate, independent subgroups, outside views, a second-chance meeting, anonymous input and genuine diversity of thought, which supplies dissent, brings absent perspectives into the room, slows the group down and widens the options, provided those perspectives are actually heard.

Case-style practice: the board that agreed
Orontes's board met for ninety minutes to approve entry into a new market. The chief executive opened by saying the decision was "effectively made" and that the only question was timing. The finance director raised the distributor's request for a 4 per cent payment to a nominated consultancy, described in Chapter 15, and was told that the local team understood the market and that the board should not second-guess them. No other director spoke on the point. The decision was unanimous. Two directors told the company secretary afterwards that they had been uneasy.

Case-style set: the board that agreed
What I see. This is a textbook groupthink meeting and nearly every condition for it is present. The leader stated a preference at the outset — "effectively made", with only timing open — which section 17.5 identifies as the first and most powerful cause, because the group's position is fixed before the examination begins. The single dissenting voice was answered not on the merits but with an appeal to deference and to the local team's understanding, which is direct pressure on a dissenter dressed as respect for expertise. No other director spoke, which produced an illusion of unanimity, and the two who told the company secretary afterwards confirm the self-censorship behind it: each believed they were alone. The appeal to the local team's judgment is also a collective rationalisation of exactly the kind that removes an ethical question from the agenda — and the question raised was not a commercial one but a payment with every indicator of corruption described in Chapter 15.
Ninety minutes for a decision to enter a new market is itself evidence, and the finance director's position is the one to note: having raised the point and been overruled, they now carry an obligation under Chapter 13's Credibility standard that the meeting did not discharge.
What should be done before implementation. Reopen the decision, which is the only remedy once a meeting has gone this way, and reopen it with the structure that was missing. The chief executive should not state a preference. The payment should be examined on its own, as a separate decision, with the facts of section 15.5 established: who the consultancy is, what it would do, under what contract, paid where and how. Independent advice should be taken on the anti-bribery exposure, since the conduct reaches Orontes wherever it occurs. A director should be asked to argue the case against entry, as a role rather than as a position. And the two uneasy directors should be asked directly, in the meeting, what their concerns are — which is the second-chance mechanism, and which only works if somebody with authority asks.
Finally, the minute should record the concern that was raised and how it was resolved. A unanimous decision with no recorded discussion of the one objection is the document that will be read later, and at present it misrepresents what happened in the room.
