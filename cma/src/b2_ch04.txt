WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapters 2 and 3 treated direct labor as a variable cost with a constant rate per unit. That holds for mature products. Learning curves adjust the labor estimate for new ones.
4.1  What learning is
Workers who repeat a task become faster. They find better methods, make fewer mistakes and need less supervision. A learning curve shows how labor time per unit falls as cumulative output grows.
Learning is measured each time cumulative output doubles. On an 80% curve, the average time per unit after a doubling is 80% of the average before it. The learning rate is 80%; the time saved is 20%.
Learning affects labor and the costs that follow labor hours. It does not affect materials: each halva batch still needs the same sesame and sugar. Figure F204-02 shows the halva curve.
Figure F204-02. The halva learning curve: cumulative average hours per batch on an 80% curve.
EXAM TRAP   The rate is what remains, not what falls
An 80% learning curve does not cut time by 80%. After each doubling, the new average is 80% of the old average, a fall of 20%.

4.2  The doubling table
The CMA exam uses the cumulative average-time learning model. At each doubling of cumulative output, the average time for all units so far is the learning rate times the previous average. Total time is the average times the number of units.
The table works only at doublings: one, two, four, eight, sixteen units and so on. To find the time for a group of later units, subtract the totals: batches 5 to 8 need the total for 8 minus the total for 4.

4.3  The learning curve formula
For quantities that are not doublings, use the formula Y = a X^b. Y is the cumulative average time per unit, a is the time for the first unit, X is cumulative units, and b = log(learning rate) ÷ log(two).
For an 80% curve, b = -0.3219. Three halva batches average 702.1 hours, a total of about 2,106 hours. Figure F204-04 shows the calculation for three and six batches, and checks it at eight.

4.4  Using learning curves
Learning curves improve three decisions. In pricing, a later order costs less than the first, so a lower price may still cover cost. In budgeting, labor hours fall over the year. In setting standards, a standard based on one early batch is too loose, and one based on later batches is too tight for new staff.
Always cost an order with its own incremental hours. The average for the whole run mixes the slow early batches with the fast later ones.

4.5  Benefits and shortcomings
Both regression and learning curves turn past data into better estimates. Regression gives a fixed cost and a variable rate, with tests of reliability. Learning curves correct the labor estimate when the task is new.
Both have limits. Regression needs enough past data and holds only inside the relevant range; a good fit does not prove a causal link. A learning rate is itself an estimate, learning may stop once the task is routine, and it does not apply to machine-paced work or materials.

4.6  Extended Example 4A: pricing Barada's second halva order
This example uses the learning curve to cost two orders, sets a price floor for the second one and shows what the curve means for a labor standard. There are no journal entries: the output is a quote and a standard.
1. Scenario
Barada ordered 4 batches of halva, which Orontes has made. Barada now asks for 4 more. Direct labor costs $12 an hour and variable overhead $6 per labor hour, because the manual line uses little machinery. Materials cost $3,000 a batch and do not learn.
2. Plan
Use the doubling table to find the hours for each order. Apply the labor and overhead rates to the hours, and add materials at a flat amount per batch. Compare the two orders and think about the standard.
3. Calculations
Order 1 hours = total for 4 batches = 2,560.
Order 2 hours = total for 8 batches − total for 4 = 4,096 − 2,560 = 1,536.
Order 2 cost = 1,536 × ($12 + $6) + 4 × $3,000 = $39,648.
4. The quote
Figure F204-08 costs both orders. The second order costs $39,648, or $9,912 a batch, against $14,520 for the first. Any price above $39,648 covers the incremental cost; fixed costs and profit come on top (Chapter 9).

Chapter summary
Figure F204-11 brings the chapter together.
Figure F204-11. Chapter 4 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Labor time per unit falls as workers repeat a task. Under the cumulative average-time model, each doubling of cumulative output makes the new average equal to the learning rate times the old one. The doubling table gives totals at doublings; the formula Y = aX^b gives any quantity. Cost later orders with their incremental hours, and apply learning only to labor and labor-driven costs. Learning curves and regression both improve estimates, but both rest on assumptions that must be checked.

Case question: a new juice-blend crew
In 2027 the Beverages division launched a new juice blend. A new crew mixes the blend in lots. The crew needed time to learn the recipe and the equipment, so the first lots took many hours and later lots were faster.
The production engineer has timed the first lot and measured the crew's learning rate. She believes the crew follows the cumulative average-time model. The crew is paid the same hourly rate for every lot, and the juice concentrate and bottles used per lot do not change with learning.
The Beverages controller is preparing the labor budget for the second quarter, when the crew will make the next four lots. She needs the labor hours and the direct labor cost for those lots. She must also decide which costs will fall as the crew learns and which will not, because the budget for materials must not assume any learning.
The sales team has promised Levant Distribution a steady supply of the new blend, so the controller cannot afford a labor budget that is too high or too low. She also knows that the crew's hourly rate is fixed by contract for the year. The engineer warns that learning slows over time but does not stop inside the budget period.
