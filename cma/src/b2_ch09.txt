9.1  Two ways to cost inventory
Absorption costing, also called full costing, treats all manufacturing costs as product costs, including fixed manufacturing overhead. Variable costing, also called direct costing, treats only variable manufacturing costs as product costs; fixed manufacturing overhead is a period cost.
That is the only difference. Both methods treat materials, labor and variable overhead as product costs, and both expense all selling and administrative costs. Figure F209-02 follows the $1,800,000 of Beverages fixed overhead under each method.

9.2  The contribution margin
The contribution margin is sales minus all variable costs, both manufacturing and selling. It is the amount left to cover fixed costs and then provide profit. Per unit, it is price minus variable cost per unit.
A case of juice sells for $20.00. Variable costs are $10.60 to make it and $1.20 to sell it, so each case contributes $8.20. The contribution margin ratio is $8.20 ÷ $20.00 = 41%.

9.3  Two income statements for the same year
The absorption statement uses the gross margin format. Cost of goods sold is at full cost, and the production-volume variance is added when production falls short of the denominator. Selling and administrative costs follow.

9.4  Why the two incomes differ
The difference comes only from fixed overhead held in inventory. When inventory rises, absorption costing defers some fixed overhead to next year, so its income is higher. When inventory falls, it releases fixed overhead from earlier years, so its income is lower.
In 2027 Beverages' inventory rose from 40,000 to 60,000 cases. The difference in income is the change in inventory times the fixed rate: $40,000, the extra fixed overhead left in ending inventory.

EXAM TRAP   Difference = change in units × fixed rate
Absorption income − variable income = (ending − beginning inventory units) × fixed manufacturing overhead per unit, when the rate is the same in both years. Do not use variable costs or selling costs in this formula.

9.5  Capacity levels and product cost
The fixed overhead rate depends on the capacity level in the denominator. Theoretical capacity assumes the plant never stops. Practical capacity allows for normal breaks and maintenance. Normal capacity is average use over several years. Master-budget capacity is this year's plan.
The smaller the denominator, the higher the product cost. For a case of juice, full cost ranges from $12.10 at theoretical capacity to $12.85 at master-budget capacity. Figure F209-08 shows all four.
Figure F209-08. Full product cost per case at four denominator levels.
The choice also changes the statements. A larger denominator applies less fixed overhead per case, which gives a larger production-volume variance and lower inventory values. For the published statements Orontes uses normal capacity, as ASC 330 requires.

9.6  Practical capacity and capacity management
Many managers use practical capacity for internal reports. The product carries only the cost of the capacity it uses, and the cost of idle capacity is shown on its own as unused capacity.
In 2027 the bottling line could fill 1,000,000 cases but filled 850,000. At $1.80 of fixed overhead per case of practical capacity, 150,000 idle cases cost $270,000. That figure tells managers what the spare capacity costs, so they can seek more sales or reduce capacity.
Practical capacity also protects pricing. If prices follow full cost at a falling budget volume, unit cost rises, prices rise and demand falls again: a downward demand spiral. Figure F209-09 shows the spiral.
Figure F209-09. A downward demand spiral when prices follow full cost at a falling budget volume.
Practical capacity has limits. It must be estimated, and the published statements still need normal capacity. So Orontes keeps practical capacity for management reports and adjusts to normal capacity for the statements.

1. Scenario
Units: opening inventory 40,000 cases; produced 850,000; sold 830,000; closing 60,000.

Chapter summary
Figure F209-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F209-14. Chapter 9 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Absorption costing includes fixed manufacturing overhead in inventory; variable costing expenses it in the period. The contribution margin is sales minus all variable costs. Income differs only by the fixed overhead that enters or leaves inventory: the change in inventory units times the fixed rate. The capacity level in the denominator changes unit cost, inventory values and pricing; practical capacity shows the cost of unused capacity and helps avoid a downward demand spiral.
