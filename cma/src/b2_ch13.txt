WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 12 assigned factory overhead to products with activity rates. This chapter uses the same method for selling, delivery and service costs, and assigns them to customers instead of products.
13.1  ABC in service companies
A service company has no inventory of products, but it still performs activities: it takes orders, moves goods, answers calls and sends invoices. Most of its costs are overhead, so one volume rate can distort cost even more than in a factory.
Levant Distribution, the Orontes subsidiary that distributes juices, is a good example. Its main activities are picking order lines, delivering to stops and invoicing. Figure F213-02 shows how these activities reach two very different customer groups.
Figure F213-02. ABC in a service company: a distributor's activities and customers.
The steps are the same as in Chapter 12: build activity pools, choose a driver for each, compute a rate and assign cost by use. The cost objects are services or customers instead of products.
EXAM TRAP   ABC is not only for factories
Banks, hospitals, airlines and distributors all use ABC. If an answer option says ABC applies only to manufacturing, it is wrong.

13.2  Measuring profitability: the issues
Profit can be measured for a product, a customer or a business unit. Each measure raises the same questions: which revenue, which costs, how to allocate shared costs, and which assets and values to use. Figure F213-03 lists them.
Figure F213-03. The issues in measuring profitability
Issue
Question to answer
Cost measurement
Which costs to include: product cost only, or also the cost to serve each customer
Cost allocation
How to assign shared costs: by activity drivers, not by revenue
Revenue measurement
Revenue net of rebates, listing fees and other payments to customers (ASC 606)
Investment measurement
Which assets each business unit uses, for return measures
Valuation
Historical cost or current value of those assets; transfer prices between units
Revenue comes first. Under ASC 606, rebates and fees paid to a customer for shelf space reduce revenue; they are not selling expenses. A customer's revenue is therefore its net revenue.
Costs come next. Product cost alone hides the cost to serve, and allocating selling costs by revenue hides it again. For a business unit, the investment base and its valuation also matter, because they decide the return.
FALSE-FRIEND ALERT   Rentabilité, الربحية
French 'rentabilité' and Arabic الربحية mean profitability, not rent. A 'marge client' is a customer margin, measured after the cost to serve.

13.3  The customer cost hierarchy and the cost to serve
Customer-related costs sit at different levels. Some are caused by each order or delivery; some by keeping one customer, such as a key account manager; some by a whole channel; and some by the company as a whole. Figure F213-04 shows the customer cost hierarchy.
Figure F213-04. The customer cost hierarchy.
The cost to serve is the cost of the activities a customer causes. Tahini & Spreads uses four activity rates: $50 an order, $120 a delivery, $200 a rush order and $80 a return.

Chapter summary
Figure F213-14 brings the chapter together.
Figure F213-14. Chapter 13 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Service companies use ABC to trace their mostly indirect costs through activities. Profit by customer starts from net revenue, after rebates and listing fees, and deducts product cost, the cost to serve and customer-sustaining costs. The whale curve shows that a few customers earn more than the total profit. Managers are judged on controllable margin, and a customer or product is dropped only when the lost revenue is smaller than the costs that would really go away.

Case question: Levant's two customer groups
Levant Distribution delivers Orontes juices to two groups of customers. A large supermarket chain orders in big lots and takes a few deliveries a week to its central stores. Hundreds of small shops order a few cases at a time and each shop needs its own delivery.
Levant earns a service fee on every case it handles. Until now the controller has shared warehouse and delivery costs between the two groups in proportion to their fees. On that basis both groups look profitable.
The new finance manager, Nadia, suspects that the small shops cost far more to serve than their fees suggest. She has measured three activities for 2027: picking order lines in the warehouse, delivery stops and invoices. She has also calculated a rate for each activity.
Nadia must present an activity-based analysis to the board. She needs the cost to serve each group, the margin of each group under both methods and a recommendation for the small shops. The board chair has warned that dropping the shops would damage Levant's reputation with Orontes. The board meets at the end of the month, so the analysis must be clear enough for directors who are not accountants.
