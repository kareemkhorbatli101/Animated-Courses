WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 1 used the three risks to explain what control risk is, and Chapter 4 described the top-down approach the auditor follows. This chapter defines the risks formally and takes the testing further.
5.1  The three risks, defined
Chapter 1 introduced the three risks in passing. Figure F405-02 defines each one, and says who can do anything about it.
Figure F405-02. The three risks and who can change each
Risk
Definition
Who can change it
Inherent risk
Susceptibility to material misstatement BEFORE considering any control
Management, by changing what the business does
Control risk
The risk that internal control will not prevent or detect it in time
Management, by designing and operating better controls
Detection risk
The risk that the auditor's procedures fail to find it
The auditor, by changing the nature, timing and extent of testing
Audit risk
inherent x control x detection
The risk of giving a wrong opinion
Inherent risk is susceptibility before any control is considered. Control risk is the risk that the system fails to prevent or detect the misstatement in time. Detection risk is the risk that the auditor's own procedures miss it. Together they give audit risk, the risk of an unmodified opinion on statements that are materially misstated.
The practical point is who holds each lever. Management acts on inherent risk by changing the business and on control risk by changing the system. The auditor acts only on detection risk, by testing more, differently, or closer to the year end.

5.2  Preventive and detective controls
A preventive control acts before an error or fraud can occur. A detective control finds it afterwards. Figure F405-03 sets them side by side.
Figure F405-03. Preventive against detective controls
Preventive
Detective
When it acts
Before the error or fraud occurs
After it has occurred
What it does
Stops it happening
Finds it so it can be corrected
Examples
Authorization limits, segregation of duties, input validation, locked stores
Reconciliations, exception reports, stock counts, supervisory review
Cost of failure
The loss never happens
The loss happens but is recovered or corrected
Why both
Prevention is cheaper but cannot catch everything
Detection proves prevention is working
Both are needed, and for different reasons. Prevention is cheaper, because the loss never happens. Detection is what proves prevention is working: without a reconciliation nobody knows whether the authorization limits are being respected.
EXAM TRAP   Classify by when, not by how important
A control is preventive or detective according to when it acts, not how valuable it is. A stock count is detective even though it may be the single most useful control in the warehouse.
Some controls do both. A supervisor who reviews a payment before it is released is preventing an error, and the same review across a month's payments detects the ones that slipped through. The exam asks when the control acts in the case described, not what it could do in principle.

5.3  The five components in full
Chapter 1 named the five components. Figure F405-04 shows them with the number of principles each carries, seventeen in all.
Figure F405-04. The five COSO components and their principles.
The framework sets a demanding test for effectiveness. All five components must be present and functioning, and they must be operating together in an integrated manner. A company with excellent control activities and no monitoring does not have effective internal control; it has four fifths of a system.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'COSO issued 2023 guidance on internal control over financial reporting'
COSO's 2023 supplement concerns internal control over sustainability reporting, not financial reporting. A separate publication on generative AI followed in 2026. The 2013 framework itself has not been revised.
The order of the five is not arbitrary. The control environment comes first because it decides whether the rest function at all, and monitoring comes last because it looks back at everything above it. A question that asks which component a failure belongs to is usually asking where in that order the breakdown began.

5.4  The external auditor's responsibilities and opinions
The external auditor expresses an opinion on whether the financial statements are fairly presented. For an accelerated filer the auditor also expresses an opinion on internal control over financial reporting, as Chapter 4 explained. Figure F405-05 shows the opinions available on each.
Figure F405-05. The opinions available on the statements and on internal control
Opinion
When it is given
On the statements
On internal control
Unqualified (unmodified)

Available
Available: control is effective
Qualified

COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'The auditor can give a qualified opinion on internal control'
On internal control the opinion is unqualified or adverse only. There is no qualified opinion: internal control either is effective or it is not, and a single material weakness makes it adverse. A scope limitation leads to a disclaimer or to withdrawal.
The two opinions are independent of each other. A company can receive an adverse opinion on internal control and an unqualified opinion on its financial statements in the same report, because the auditor may have done enough extra substantive work to be satisfied about the numbers despite the weak controls.
The auditor is responsible for obtaining reasonable assurance that the statements are free from material misstatement, whether caused by error or fraud. That responsibility does not transfer to the auditor what management owes: preparing the statements and maintaining the controls behind them remain management's work.

5.5  Testing the adequacy of controls
Four methods are available, and the standard ranks them by the strength of the evidence they give. Figure F405-06 shows the ranking.
Figure F405-06. The four testing methods, ranked by the evidence they give.
Inquiry asks someone whether the control is performed. Observation watches it being performed. Inspection examines the document the control produced. Re-performance does the control again and compares the result. Each gives more evidence than the one before.
Inquiry alone is never sufficient. The person asked may be mistaken, may be describing what should happen rather than what does, or may be the person responsible for the failure. A walkthrough follows a single transaction from start to finish and typically uses all four methods at once.

5.6  From deficiency to remediation
Not every failure is equally serious. Figure F405-07 sets out the three levels of severity and what each one means in practice.
Figure F405-07. Three levels of deficiency
Severity
Definition
Consequence
Control deficiency
The control does not allow staff, in the normal course of their work, to prevent or detect misstatements on a timely basis
Management decides whether to fix it
Significant deficiency
Less severe than a material weakness, but important enough to merit the attention of those overseeing reporting
Reported to the audit committee
Material weakness
A reasonable possibility that a material misstatement will not be prevented or detected on a timely basis
Internal control cannot be called effective; disclosed
A material weakness is the one that matters most, because its existence prevents management from concluding that internal control is effective. The test combines likelihood and magnitude: a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement would not be prevented or detected on a timely basis.
COMMON ERROR IN OTHER BOOKS   'A material weakness is a misstatement above a percentage of income'
There is no numeric threshold. The test is whether there is a reasonable possibility that a material misstatement would go undetected. A weakness can exist where no misstatement has yet occurred at all.
Figure F405-08 sets out the six steps of remediation.
Figure F405-08. The six steps of remediation
Step (E.1.y)
What it involves
At Orontes

A control that was never assigned to anyone
2. Redesign
Change the control, or add one that addresses the cause
Name supplier approval in a job description

5.7  Extended Example 5A: assessing and mitigating the Orontes control risk
This example assesses the level of control risk across Orontes and recommends what to do about it. E.1.v is a Level C outcome, one of only six in this book, so the exam will ask for a judgement and a recommendation rather than a definition.
1. Where the risk is highest
Figure F405-09 sets out the assessment. Three areas stand out. Purchasing, because of the failure in Chapter 1. The Levant depot, where one person holds custody and the records. And the Gulf export channel, where terms are unusual and oversight is distant.
Figure F405-09. Extended Example 5A: control risk assessed area by area
Area
Control risk
Why
Recommended mitigation
Purchasing
Moderate
Duties now segregated and reviewed independently; collusion remains possible
Keep the design and test it each quarter
Levant depot
High
One person holds custody of the stock and maintains the records; nothing has changed
Move the records to another person and reconcile depot records to plant dispatches
Gulf export channel
Moderate
Unusual terms and distant oversight, but amounts pass through the group's systems
Require a second approval on non-standard terms; review the customer master file quarterly
2. Assessing each one
Purchasing now has segregated duties and an independent review, so control risk has fallen from high to moderate, limited mainly by the possibility of collusion. The depot remains high: nothing has changed there yet. The Gulf channel is moderate, because the risk is real but the amounts pass through the group's own systems.
3. Recommending mitigation
For the depot, move the stock records away from the custodian and have group finance reconcile depot records to plant dispatches. For the Gulf channel, require a second approval on non-standard terms and review the customer master file quarterly. For purchasing, keep what is in place and test it.
4. Judging the severity
The depot weakness is a material weakness if the stock held there is material to the group, because one person can remove stock and adjust the record without anyone comparing the two. If the depot is small, it is a significant deficiency: still reported to the audit committee, but not fatal to the assessment.
5. Remediating it
The root cause is not dishonesty but design: nobody separated the duties when the depot was set up. Redesign moves the records; implementation means telling both people and updating the procedure; the control then runs for a quarter before anyone tests it; and only after testing can management say the weakness is remediated.

Chapter summary
Figure F405-11 brings the chapter together.
Figure F405-11. Chapter 5 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Inherent risk exists before controls, control risk is the system failing, and detection risk is the auditor's own; only the last is the auditor's to change. Preventive controls act before an error and detective controls after, and both are needed. The COSO framework has five components and seventeen principles, all of which must be present, functioning and operating together. On internal control the auditor's opinion is unqualified or adverse only. Controls are tested by inquiry, observation, inspection and re-performance, in rising order of evidence, and inquiry alone is never enough. A deficiency is remediated only when the new control has operated for a period and been tested.

Case question: the weakness that was announced but not fixed
At the end of 2028 the external auditor reported a material weakness at Orontes: one purchasing clerk could both approve suppliers and release payments. Management responded in January by issuing a revised procedure that moved supplier approval to the purchasing manager and named the duty in his job description.
In February the chief financial officer told the audit committee that the weakness had been remediated and asked for it to be removed from the register. The revised procedure had been circulated, the purchasing manager had acknowledged it, and no further losses had been found.
The controller, Rania, disagreed. She pointed out that the new control had operated for six weeks, that nobody had yet tested whether the purchasing manager was in fact approving suppliers, and that the only evidence offered was the manager's own statement that he was.
The audit committee has asked Rania to set out what remains to be done, what kind of evidence would be sufficient, and what the position would be in the annual report if the work is not finished before the year end. Orontes is an accelerated filer, so its auditor reports on internal control as well as on the financial statements.
