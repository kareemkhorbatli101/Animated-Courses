Chapter 14
ROA, ROE and the DuPont decomposition
العائد على الأصول والعائد على حقوق الملكية وتحليل دوبونت
A margin says how much of each sales dollar is kept. It says nothing about how much had to be invested to make that sale. A grocer keeping two cents of every dollar and turning its assets over five times a year is doing better than a jeweller keeping twenty cents and turning its assets over a quarter of a time, and no margin in Chapter 13 can show that.
A return does. A return divides profit by the money that is tied up to produce it, which makes it the one ratio in Section A that can be compared across industries with some hope of meaning. Two of them matter: the return on assets, which measures the business, and the return on equity, which measures what the owners get out of it. The gap between the two is leverage, and the DuPont decomposition is the piece of arithmetic that opens both of them up into the things a manager can act on.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Chapter 9 computed Orontes's equity multiplier as total assets over total equity and found 1.55 at the end of 2025. Chapter 11 computed total asset turnover as 1.45 times. Chapter 13 computed a net margin of 7.1 per cent. Those three figures are the whole of the DuPont decomposition, and this chapter does nothing more than multiply them together and read the result.
14.1  Return on assets
Return on assets is profit over the assets employed to earn it. Orontes's net income for 2025 is $2,969,100. Its assets, though, were not one figure during the year: they began at $26,445,000 and ended at $31,672,600, because the company raised equity and bought plant as the year went on.
Dividing a flow by a closing balance compares a whole year's earnings with the assets of the last day. The convention that answers this is the average: 31,672,600 + 26,445,000 = 58,117,600, and half of that is $29,058,800. Return on assets is therefore 2,969,100 ÷ 29,058,800 = 10.22 per cent, or 10.2 to one decimal.
Ten cents of profit a year for every dollar of assets. That is the whole of what the ratio says, and it is enough to compare Orontes with another food manufacturer, with its own last year, and with the cost of the money that funds those assets — which at the 6 per cent Orontes pays Levant Commerce Bank means the assets earn comfortably more than they cost to finance, and that is the test that matters.

Now the examinable half of this outcome: what happens to the return when one element of the statements changes. Work it by asking which of the two parts of the fraction the change touches, and in which direction.
A change that reduces profit alone lowers the return. A rise in the cost of sesame, a larger credit loss expense, a higher effective tax rate.
A change that increases assets alone lowers the return. Buying plant, building inventory, letting receivables run.
A change that touches both may do either, and the arithmetic decides. Writing an asset off reduces assets, which helps the ratio, and reduces profit, which hurts it; which effect wins depends on the size of the write-off against the size of each total.
A change that touches neither leaves the return alone. Paying a dividend that was already declared, refinancing debt at the same amount, issuing shares and holding the cash — the last of these raises assets, so it does lower the return, and candidates who answer otherwise have forgotten that cash is an asset.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: a further write-off, and what it does to both returns
Step 1. The charge. Suppose Orontes impairs a further $1,000,000 of goodwill at the very end of 2025, deductible at its 25 per cent effective rate: 1,000,000 × 0.25 = 250,000 of tax relief, so net income falls by 750,000 to 2,969,100 − 750,000 = 2,219,100.
Step 2. The assets. Ending assets fall to 31,672,600 − 1,000,000 = 30,672,600, so the average becomes half of 26,445,000 + 30,672,600 = 57,117,600, which is 28,558,800.
Step 3. The return. Return on assets becomes 2,219,100 ÷ 28,558,800 = 7.77 per cent, against 10.22 per cent before.
Check. Both parts of the fraction fell and the return still fell sharply, because profit fell by a quarter while average assets fell by less than two per cent. That is the general rule for a write-off: the numerator is always the smaller number, so a charge of a given size moves it proportionately more. An exam answer that says "assets fall, so the return rises" has the mechanism right and the magnitudes wrong.
EXAM TRAP   Average or closing
Where the question gives two balance sheets, use the average, and say that you have. Where it gives one, use the closing figure and say that too.
The average of two year ends is itself a rough measure. A company that bought its plant in December has carried those assets for a month, and the average treats them as carried for six.
Return on assets uses total assets, not net assets and not operating assets, unless the question says otherwise. Section 14.5 is about what happens when it does.

14.2  Return on equity
Return on equity is profit over the owners' money. Orontes's equity began the year at $16,375,000 and ended at $20,392,975, so the average is half of 20,392,975 + 16,375,000 = 36,767,975, which is $18,383,988. Return on equity is 2,969,100 ÷ 18,383,988 = 16.15 per cent.
On the closing balance instead it is 2,969,100 ÷ 20,392,975 = 14.56 per cent. The two differ by more than a point and a half, and the reason is the $1,500,000 share issue and the $1,400,000 of additional paid-in capital that came with it: equity grew by a quarter during the year, so the question of which equity to divide by is not a quibble.
Why is the return on equity higher than the return on assets? Because some of the assets are funded by somebody else. Orontes's $31,672,600 of assets are funded by $11,279,625 of liabilities and $20,392,975 of equity, so the owners put up about two-thirds of the money and keep all of the profit that the whole of it earns, after paying the lenders their fixed interest. That is the arithmetic of leverage, and Chapter 9 named the factor that measures it.

EXAM TRAP   Return on equity rewards debt until it does not
A company can raise its return on equity by replacing equity with debt, and nothing about the business improves when it does. Chapter 8 priced the risk that comes with it.
Return on equity for a loss-making company with negative equity is a positive number and is meaningless. Always look at the two parts before quoting the ratio.
Preferred dividends are deducted from net income before computing the return to common equity, and preferred stock is excluded from the equity. Orontes has no preferred stock; many exam questions do.

14.3  The DuPont decomposition
The DuPont decomposition is a piece of cancelling. Write the return on equity as three fractions multiplied together and every intermediate term cancels, leaving net income over equity, which is where you started. What the three fractions buy is a diagnosis.
Net income over sales is the net profit margin: how profitable each sale is.
Sales over average assets is the total asset turnover: how hard the assets work.
Average assets over average equity is the equity multiplier: how much of the assets the owners had to fund.
For Orontes, the net margin is 7.07 per cent, the asset turnover is 42,000,000 ÷ 29,058,800 = 1.4453 times, and the equity multiplier on average balances is 29,058,800 ÷ 18,383,988 = 1.5807. Multiply them: 7.07 × 1.4453 × 1.5807 = 16.15 per cent, which is the return on equity computed directly in section 14.2.
The first two factors multiply to the return on assets: 7.07 × 1.4453 = 10.22 per cent. So the two-factor version is return on assets times the equity multiplier: 10.22 × 1.5807 = 16.15 per cent. The exam asks for either.

Three identical returns and no two of these companies would be run the same way. The supermarket lives on turnover and would be destroyed by a week of empty shelves. EXAM TRAP   The multiplier is not a target
Raising the equity multiplier always raises the return on equity in the arithmetic. It does so by adding debt, which adds the fixed interest Chapter 10 measured and the risk Chapter 8 measured. The exam treats a return raised this way as a different kind of return.
The three factors must use consistent bases. If the multiplier uses average assets, the turnover must use average assets too, or the cancelling fails and the product will not equal the return.
An equity multiplier computed on closing balances is 31,672,600 over 20,392,975, which is 1.55 — the figure Chapter 9 reported. It is not wrong; it is a different basis, and it belongs with a closing-basis return.

14.4  The five-factor decomposition
The three-factor version hides two things inside the net margin: the interest the company pays and the tax it pays. Splitting them out gives the five-factor decomposition, which the exam also names, and which is the version a finance director actually uses.
Start at the top of the income statement and work down. The operating margin on an earnings-before-interest-and-taxes basis is 4,348,800 ÷ 42,000,000 = 10.35 per cent. The interest burden ratio is pre-tax income over EBIT, which measures how much of the operating profit survives the lenders: 3,958,800 ÷ 4,348,800 = 91.03 per cent. The tax burden ratio is net income over pre-tax income, which measures how much survives the tax authority: 2,969,100 ÷ 3,958,800 = 75.0 per cent. The remaining two factors are the turnover and the multiplier, unchanged.
Multiply the operating side first: 10.35 × 1.4453 × 1.5807 = 23.65 per cent. That is what the owners would earn if there were no interest and no tax. Then apply the two burdens: 23.65 × 0.9103 × 0.7500 = 16.14 per cent, the return on equity again, give or take the rounding.

Read the five together and the leverage question becomes visible in a way the three-factor version cannot show. Debt enters twice and in opposite directions: it raises the equity multiplier, which helps, and it lowers the interest burden ratio, which hurts. That is the same break-even Chapter 8 described through the degree of financial leverage, reached from the other end.

14.5  Whose assets, whose equity, whose return
A candidate who computes a return on assets of 10.22 per cent and an analyst who computes 14.97 per cent for the same company in the same year may both be right. The ratio has three parts and each of them is defined by convention rather than by a standard.
The return. Net income is the usual numerator and it is not the only one. Net income is struck after interest, which is a payment to one of the two groups that funded the assets, so dividing it by all of the assets mixes two bases. Adding back the after-tax interest repairs that: 390,000 × 0.75 = 292,500, so the numerator becomes 2,969,100 + 292,500 = 3,261,600 and the return is 3,261,600 ÷ 29,058,800 = 11.22 per cent. An analyst who wants a pre-financing measure uses EBIT instead: 4,348,800 ÷ 29,058,800 = 14.97 per cent. All three are called return on assets in print.
The assets. Average or closing is the first choice, and it moved Orontes's return on equity by a point and a half in section 14.2. Then: total assets, or only the assets used in the business? Orontes holds $1,807,000 of equity-method investments, $115,500 of securities and $46,000 of assets held for sale, none of which produces any of its operating profit. Taking them out gives a return on operating assets, which is higher and is a better measure of the trade. Gross or net of accumulated depreciation is a third choice, and it matters: Orontes's plant cost $26,790,000 and stands at $14,802,000, so a return computed on gross plant is barely half the one computed on net.
The equity. Total equity includes accumulated other comprehensive income, which for Orontes is a negative $1,125 and so changes nothing, but for a company with large currency or pension reserves can swing the denominator by a tenth. Preferred stock is normally excluded, with its dividends taken out of the numerator. Where a group has subsidiaries it does not wholly own, the non-controlling interest is part of consolidated equity and no part of the parent shareholders' equity, and the two returns differ. Treasury stock reduces equity and therefore raises the return, which is a point Chapter 15 returns to.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Return"
English return does duty for three ideas that the exam keeps apart: a rate of profit, as in return on assets; the act of giving something back, as in a return of capital; and a filed document, as in a tax return. Arabic uses العائد for the rate and الإقرار for the document, so the ambiguity disappears. French separates them further: rentabilité for the rate of profit on assets or equity, rendement for a yield on an investment, and déclaration for the filing. Note too that French distinguishes rentabilité économique, the return on assets, from rentabilité financière, the return on equity — two settled terms where English says return twice and relies on the preposition.

Chapter summary
Figure F514-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F514-09. Chapter 14 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Return on assets is net income over average total assets, which for Orontes is 2,969,100 over 29,058,800, or 10.22 per cent, and it falls when profit falls or assets rise; a $1,000,000 write-off takes it to 7.77 per cent because the numerator is the smaller number and moves proportionately more. Return on equity is the same profit over average equity of $18,383,988, or 16.15 per cent, and it exceeds the return on assets by 5.93 points because lenders funded a third of the assets and take only their interest. The DuPont decomposition explains the whole of that: a net margin of 7.07 per cent, an asset turnover of 1.4453 and an equity multiplier of 1.5807, which multiply to 16.15 per cent, and which can be identical in total for a supermarket and a software company whose businesses have nothing in common. The five-factor version splits the margin into an operating margin of 10.35 per cent, an interest burden of 91.03 per cent and a tax burden of 75.0 per cent, which shows debt entering twice and in opposite directions. And the definitions of return, assets and equity are conventions, not standards: the same company and the same year give 9.37, 10.22, 11.22 and 14.97 per cent, so state the definition, use it on both sides, and look for a definition before you look for an error.

Case-style set: the board wants twenty per cent
Each factor alone would have to move a long way.
Through the margin. Holding turnover and the multiplier, the net margin would have to rise from 7.07 to about 8.76 per cent — a quarter higher. On 2025 sales that is roughly $710,000 more net income, which is almost exactly the $840,000 of gross margin the company has given away since 2023.
Through turnover. The asset turnover would have to rise from 1.4453 to about 1.79, which on unchanged sales means running the business on about $23,500,000 of assets instead of $29,058,800. Chapters 11 and 12 found about $4,500,000 of trade working capital and no obvious slack in the plant, so this route cannot deliver on its own.
Through leverage. The equity multiplier would have to rise from 1.5807 to about 1.96, which means replacing roughly a quarter of the equity with debt. The arithmetic works and nothing in the business improves.
I would take the margin route, because the margin is where the company has demonstrably lost ground and the lost ground is worth more than the gap to the target. Recovering two points of gross margin would carry most of the distance by itself, and it improves the return on assets rather than only the return on equity.
The risk of the leverage route is that interest coverage of 11.15 times would fall towards 6, the fixed charges Chapter 10 measured would rise, and the degree of financial leverage of 1.10 would climb — a higher return bought entirely with risk.
