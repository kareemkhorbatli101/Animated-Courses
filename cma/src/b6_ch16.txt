Chapter 16
Receivables and credit policy
الذمم المدينة وسياسة الائتمان
Orontes is owed $4,144,000 by its customers. That money was earned, has been recognised as revenue, has left the warehouse as goods, and is not in the bank. It is the second-largest asset on the current side of the balance sheet, it takes 34.3 days to arrive, and every one of those facts is the result of a decision somebody made about who may buy on credit and on what terms.
This chapter is about that decision. Selling on credit is not an administrative inconvenience; it is an investment, financed like any other, undertaken because it buys sales that would otherwise go to a competitor. The question is never whether to grant credit but how much, to whom, and at what price — and the exam's version of the question is always the same incremental calculation.

WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW   What you already know
Book 1 computed Orontes's receivables turnover of 10.63 times, its days sales outstanding of 34.3 days, its allowance for credit losses of $156,000 against a gross book of $4,300,000, and its credit loss expense of $63,000. It also found receivables growing 10.21 per cent against sales growth of 15.38, which it called the healthy direction. This chapter asks what the company should do next.
16.1  What sets the level of receivables
Receivables are the product of two things only: how much is sold on credit, and how long it takes to collect. Everything that influences the level works through one of those two.
Sales volume. More sales on the same terms means more receivables, in proportion. This is the largest factor and the least interesting, which is why the ratio measures — days outstanding, turnover — strip it out.
The credit terms offered. Terms of net 45 produce half as much again in receivables as net 30, on the same sales. This is the company's own decision and the subject of section 16.4.
The mix of customers. Large retailers pay slowly and reliably; small shops pay quickly or not at all; government bodies pay slowest of all and never default. A change in the customer mix changes the collection period without any change in policy.
The collection effort. How quickly an overdue account is chased, by whom, and with what consequence. Two companies with identical terms can have collection periods a fortnight apart.
Seasonality and industry practice. A supplier to a harvest-driven business is paid after the harvest, whatever the invoice says. A company whose competitors all offer 60 days cannot offer 30 and keep its customers.
Discounts for early settlement. A discount buys days with margin, and the proportion of customers who take it determines how many days are bought.
The economy. In a downturn, every customer pays more slowly and more of them fail, and the collection period lengthens without anybody deciding anything.

16.2  The four parts of a credit policy
A credit policy has four elements, and a question that asks about "an optimal credit policy" is asking which of them to move.
Credit standards decide who is granted credit at all. They are set by the risk the company will accept, and assessed by the five Cs: character, meaning the customer's willingness to pay as shown by its record; capacity, its ability to pay out of cash flow; capital, the equity behind it; collateral, what could be taken if it failed; and conditions, the state of its industry and the economy. The sources are trade references, bank references, published statements, a credit agency's report, and the company's own experience.
Credit terms decide how long and on what conditions. The standard notation is read as follows: "2/10, net 30" means two per cent may be deducted if payment is made within ten days, and the full amount is due in thirty. "Net 45" means the full amount in forty-five days with no discount. "2/10, net 30, ROG" dates the terms from receipt of goods rather than from the invoice.
Collection policy decides what happens when an account goes past due: the statement, the reminder, the telephone call, the stop on further supply, the collection agency, the lawyer. Each step costs money and each risks the customer relationship, so the sequence is a policy and not an improvisation.
The discount decides how much margin the company will give up to be paid sooner, and Chapter 18 computes what a discount costs the customer who does not take it — which is the same number from the other side.
The optimum, which is the examinable idea, is where the marginal benefit of relaxing a policy equals its marginal cost. Relaxing standards wins customers and adds bad debts. Lengthening terms wins sales and adds financing cost. Softening collection keeps customers and lengthens the period. Every move has a gain and a cost, and the analysis of section 16.4 is how they are compared.

16.3  Default risk
Default risk is the risk that a customer does not pay what it owes, in full or at all. It is the price of selling on credit, it can be reduced and never removed, and the exam wants it measured rather than described.
Three instruments measure it.
The ageing schedule sorts the receivable book by how long each balance has been outstanding: current, 1 to 30 days past due, 31 to 60, 61 to 90, over 90. It is the instrument Book 1 said a days-outstanding figure cannot replace, because an average of 34.3 days is consistent with everybody paying on time and with one large account at 120 days. The pattern of movement between the columns is the early warning.
The allowance for credit losses is the company's estimate of what will not be collected, charged to the income statement as a credit loss expense. Orontes's allowance is $156,000 against a gross book of $4,300,000, which is 156,000 ÷ 4,300,000 = 3.63 per cent — held steady against 3.59 per cent a year earlier, which Book 1 read as consistent.
Credit scoring assigns a number to a customer from its characteristics and its history, and sets a credit limit from the score. It makes decisions consistent and fast, and it is only as good as the data behind it.
Four ways to reduce the risk, each with a cost.
Tighter standards, which lose sales.
Security: a personal guarantee, a letter of credit, a retention of title clause, a deposit. Each costs the customer something and may lose the sale.
Credit insurance, which transfers the risk for a premium.
Factoring, which sells the receivable outright. Book 1 found a $15,000 loss on sale of receivables in Orontes's income statement, which is this decision already taken for part of the book, and Chapter 18 computes what factoring costs.

16.4  Testing a change in credit policy
This is the calculation the outcome names and the exam sets. The structure never varies: compare the incremental gain with the incremental cost, counting only what changes.
WORKED EXAMPLE   Worked example: lengthening the terms from 30 to 45 days
Step 1. The proposal. Orontes's sales director proposes moving from net 30 to net 45. Sales are expected to rise from $42,000,000 to $45,000,000. The contribution margin on the extra sales is 40 per cent, so variable costs are 60 per cent. Receivables are expected to run at the new 45 days. Bad debts on the new business are expected at 4 per cent of those sales. The carrying cost of money is the company's cost of capital of 9.04 per cent.
Step 2. The gain. The extra sales are 45,000,000 − 42,000,000 = 3,000,000, and at a 40 per cent contribution margin they add 3,000,000 × 0.40 = 1,200,000 of contribution. Fixed costs do not change, so that is the gross benefit.
Step 3. The extra investment in receivables. At 45 days, the book becomes 45,000,000 × 45 ÷ 365 = 5,547,945 against the present $4,144,000, an increase of 5,547,945 − 4,144,000 = 1,403,945. The company's own money in that increase is its cost, not its selling price, so at a 60 per cent variable-cost ratio the investment is 1,403,945 × 0.60 = 842,367.
Step 4. The cost of carrying it. 842,367 × 0.0904 = 76,150 a year.
Step 5. The bad debts. Four per cent of the new sales: 3,000,000 × 0.04 = 120,000.
Step 6. The decision. 1,200,000 − 76,150 − 120,000 = 1,003,850 of net benefit a year. The proposal should be accepted.
Check. Notice which numbers did the work. The gain is large because the contribution margin is high and fixed costs do not change; the costs are small because money is cheap relative to a 40 per cent margin. Reverse either and the answer reverses: at a 10 per cent contribution margin the gain would be $300,000 and the costs would still be nearly $200,000, and at a 20 per cent cost of money the carrying cost alone would be $168,473. The exam's version of this question is always a test of whether the candidate knows which figures to vary.

16.5  Collection, and a verdict on Orontes
Collection policy is the part of credit management that is executed rather than decided, and three principles govern it.
Act early and consistently. An account that is three days overdue is a clerical matter; the same account at ninety days is a dispute. The cost of the first contact is trivial and its effect is large, which is why the ageing schedule is reviewed weekly rather than monthly.
Separate the collection decision from the sales relationship. A sales representative paid on revenue is the wrong person to stop a customer's supply, and a credit function that reports to sales will not do it either. The exam treats this as a control question, which it is.
Escalate by steps that are agreed in advance. Statement, reminder, call, stop on supply, final demand, agency, legal action — with the authority for each step and the days at which it triggers written down, so that the decision to continue supplying a slow payer is taken deliberately.
Now the verdict on Orontes. Its days sales outstanding is 34.3 against terms the company describes as about 30: four days of slippage, which on daily sales of $115,068 is 115,068 × 4 = 460,272 of money the terms say should be in the bank. Its allowance ratio is steady and its credit loss expense of $63,000 is modest. Its receivables grew more slowly than its sales. The book is well run.
The finding is therefore not a problem but an opportunity, and it is the one section 16.4 quantified: a company with a 35 per cent gross margin, a cost of money near 9 per cent and well-controlled bad debts is a company that should probably be selling more on credit rather than less. The $1,003,850 of net benefit in the worked example is larger than the $840,000 of lost gross margin that Book 1 ranked as the company's first finding — and it is the first idea in two books that would actually address that finding, because it buys volume without cutting price.
That is the note on which the working-capital chapters turn from controlling cost to creating value, and Chapters 17 and 18 complete the set.

LANGUAGE FOCUS   "Credit"
English credit is one of the most overloaded words in the whole syllabus. It is the facility a seller grants a buyer, as in credit terms. It is the right-hand side of a double entry, as in debit and credit. It is a reduction in tax, as in a tax credit. It is a rating, as in credit risk. And to credit an account is to increase a liability or reduce an asset, which is the opposite of what a customer means by getting credit. Arabic separates the senses firmly: ائتمان for the facility and دائن for the bookkeeping side. French does the same with crédit for the facility and the entry but avoir for a credit note, and note that a French crédit in a bank statement means money coming in — from the bank's point of view, not the customer's, which is the source of endless confusion.

Chapter summary
Figure F616-06 brings the chapter together.
Figure F616-06. Chapter 16 summary map: the key rule for each section.
Receivables are the product of what is sold on credit and how long collection takes, and everything that moves them — volume, terms, standards, collection effort, discounts, customer mix, industry practice and the economy — works through one of the two. A credit policy has four parts: standards, assessed by the five Cs; terms, written as 2/10 net 30; a collection policy escalating by agreed steps; and a discount, which buys days with margin. Default risk is measured by the ageing schedule, which shows the spread an average hides, by the allowance ratio, which for Orontes is 156,000 ÷ 4,300,000 = 3.63 per cent and steady, and by credit scoring — and it is reduced by tighter standards, security, insurance or factoring, each at a price. A change in policy is tested by one template: extra sales of 3,000,000 × 0.40 = 1,200,000 of contribution, against a receivable book rising to 45,000,000 × 45 ÷ 365 = 5,547,945, an investment of 1,403,945 × 0.60 = 842,367 costing 842,367 × 0.0904 = 76,150 to carry, and 3,000,000 × 0.04 = 120,000 of bad debts, giving 1,200,000 − 76,150 − 120,000 = 1,003,850 of net benefit. And Orontes's own book is well run at 34.3 days with a steady allowance, which means the opportunity is to sell more on credit rather than less — the first proposal in two books that addresses the margin finding without cutting price.

Case-style set: a customer asks for terms
The calculation. The additional contribution is 2,400,000 × 0.35 = 840,000. The receivable this customer would carry is 2,400,000 × 60 ÷ 365 = 394,521, and the company's money in it is 394,521 × 0.65 = 256,439, which costs 256,439 × 0.0904 = 23,182 a year to carry. Bad debts at 3 per cent are 2,400,000 × 0.03 = 72,000. The net benefit is 840,000 − 23,182 − 72,000 = 744,818 a year.
Whether I would accept. On these figures, yes, and comfortably: the contribution is more than eight times the two costs together, which is what a 35 per cent margin against a 9 per cent cost of money produces. The order would also absorb capacity, and at an operating leverage of 4.02 the contribution falls almost entirely to operating income.
The risk the calculation does not capture. The existing customers. Orontes's other customers pay in 34.3 days on terms of about 30, and a supermarket chain's 60-day terms will not stay confidential. If a quarter of the existing $42,000,000 of sales migrated to 60-day terms, the additional receivable would be roughly 10,500,000 × 26 ÷ 365 = 747,945 at selling price, and the carrying cost on its variable element would be several times the $23,182 computed above — before any argument about fairness with customers who are told no.
I would therefore accept, and structure it so that the terms are defensible: a documented volume threshold at which 60-day terms apply, available to any customer who meets it, with a credit limit for this customer at the level of the quarterly order and a review if the rating falls below investment grade.
