17.1  Expected value
The expected value of an uncertain quantity is each possible outcome multiplied by its probability, and the results added together. Figure F417-02 does this for the Gulf export expansion.

The expected volume of 237,500 cases is not the middle scenario and not the simple average of the three, which would be 236,667. The probabilities do the work: the most likely case carries half the weight, and the two extremes a quarter each.
The same arithmetic estimates a future cash flow. At a margin of 8.20 a case and fixed costs of 900,000, the expected contribution is 1,047,500. Each scenario's own contribution is shown beside it, and they range from 330,000 to 1,724,000.
Where cash flows fall in different years, each year's expected flow is computed this way and then discounted. That is how Book 1 Chapter 17 handled an investment appraisal. The two steps must not be combined.

17.2  What expected value cannot tell you
Figure F417-03 pairs the benefits of the technique with its shortcomings.
Figure F417-03. The benefits and shortcomings of expected value
Expected value (B.3.h)
Benefit
Shortcoming
Uses all the information
Every outcome and its likelihood enter the figure
Nobody actually receives the expected value
Comparable across options
One number per option, so options can be ranked
It hides the spread entirely
Correct over many repetitions
The long-run average of a repeated decision
A once-only decision is not a long run
Simple to compute and explain
Multiply and add
The probabilities are usually estimates, not facts
It ignores the decision-maker's attitude to risk
Two of those shortcomings decide real cases. The first is that nobody receives the expected value: the outcomes here are 330,000, 1,068,000 and 1,724,000, and 1,047,500 is not among them. It is a weighted average, not a forecast of what will happen.
The second is that it is correct over many repetitions. A company choosing between two suppliers every week is in a long run; a company deciding once whether to enter the Gulf is not. For a single large decision, the spread of outcomes matters as much as their average, which is what the rest of this chapter addresses.

17.3  Sensitivity, what-if and goal seeking
Three related techniques vary the assumptions deliberately. Figure F417-04 distinguishes them.
Figure F417-04. Sensitivity, what-if, goal seeking and simulation
Technique
What it does
The question it answers
Varies
Sensitivity analysis
Change ONE assumption and see how far the answer moves
Which assumption deserves the most work?
One at a time
What-if analysis
Change an input and read the new answer
What happens if volume falls by a fifth?
Any number of inputs

Set the answer you need and solve for the input
What volume is needed to break even?
Works backwards
Simulation

What is the whole range of outcomes?

sensitivity analysis changes one assumption at a time and records how far the answer moves. Its purpose is not to forecast but to rank: it tells you which assumption deserves the most effort in estimating, and which can be left rough.
Figure F417-05. Sensitivity: how far each assumption moves the answer.
Figure F417-05 applies it to the expansion. A ten per cent change in margin or in volume moves contribution by +18.6%, while the same change in fixed cost moves it by +8.6%. Margin and volume therefore deserve the careful estimate, and the fixed cost can be approximate.
what-if analysis is the everyday use of the same model: change an input and read the new answer. goal seeking runs it backwards. It sets the answer required and solves for the input that would produce it, which is how a break-even volume is found.
Both are everyday spreadsheet work rather than specialist techniques, and that is their value. A manager who can ask the model a question and get an answer in a minute will ask more questions. The assumptions then get tested far more often than any formal review would test them.

17.4  Simulation and the Monte Carlo technique
Sensitivity moves one assumption at a time, which is its limitation: in reality they all move at once, and sometimes together. A simulation model varies them all, repeatedly, each draw taken from a distribution rather than a single value.
The Monte Carlo technique is the standard method. Each trial draws a volume, a margin and a fixed cost at random from their ranges, computes the outcome, and records it. Doing that thousands of times builds up the distribution of outcomes rather than a single number.
Figure F417-06. The distribution of simulated outcomes.
Figure F417-06 shows 1,000 trials of the expansion. The mean of the trials is 1,027,152, close to the single expected value of 1,047,500, which is reassuring rather than surprising. The information the expected value could not give is the spread: 539,260 to 1,503,517 covers nine trials in ten.
That range is what a board needs. It converts a decision from a single number into a question about whether the worst plausible outcome is survivable. That is the question that ought to be asked.
Simulation has costs of its own. It needs a distribution for every assumption rather than a single figure, which is more work and more judgement. It is harder to explain to a board than one number. And its apparent sophistication can lend unearned authority to inputs that were guessed.

17.5  From prediction to recommendation
The syllabus asks how predictive techniques are used to draw insights and make recommendations, and the answer is a sequence rather than a technique. Predict the outcome, test which assumptions it depends on, simulate the range, and then state what should be done and under what conditions.
A recommendation that names no condition is weaker than one that does. Compare it with the Chapter 15 analyst. Saying demand will be about 226,273 cases is a prediction; saying the plant should schedule to forecast temperature is a recommendation.
The honest form adds the conditions under which the recommendation would change. Here that is a volume below the fifth percentile, a margin materially below 8.20, or a change in the Gulf market that breaks the pattern the model was fitted to.

17.7  Extended Example 17A: the Gulf expansion under uncertainty
This example works the calculation the exam sets and then shows what the single figure conceals. It is exam-core.
1. The expected volume
WORKED EXAMPLE   Weighting the three scenarios
Pessimistic: 150,000 × 0.25
Most likely: 240,000 × 0.50
Optimistic: 320,000 × 0.25
Expected volume: 237,500 cases
2. The expected cash flow
At 8.20 a case the expected contribution before fixed cost is 1,947,500. Deducting fixed costs of 900,000 gives 1,047,500, against an investment of 2,500,000.
3. The commonest error
Averaging the three volumes without their probabilities gives 236,667 cases rather than 237,500. The difference is small here because the probabilities are nearly symmetrical, and it would not be if they were not.
4. Which assumption matters
Sensitivity analysis says margin and volume equally, at +18.6% for a ten per cent change, and fixed cost half as much at +8.6%. Estimating effort should follow that ranking.
5. What the simulation adds
1,000 trials give a mean of 1,027,152, a median of 1,035,130, and a fifth-to-ninety-fifth range of 539,260 to 1,503,517. No trial in this run produced a loss, which is itself worth reporting.
6. The recommendation
Proceed, because even the fifth percentile of 539,260 covers the fixed cost comfortably. Review if the margin falls materially below 8.20, because margin is one of the two assumptions the answer depends on most.

Chapter summary
Figure F417-09 brings the chapter together.
Figure F417-09. Chapter 17 summary map: the key rule for each section.
The expected value is each outcome multiplied by its probability and summed, and the same arithmetic estimates a future cash flow. It uses all the information and allows options to be ranked, but nobody receives it, it hides the spread, and it is correct only over many repetitions. Sensitivity analysis changes one assumption at a time to find which matters most; what-if changes inputs freely; goal seeking sets the answer and solves for the input. Simulation, usually by the Monte Carlo technique, varies every assumption together across thousands of trials and produces the whole distribution of outcomes. A recommendation names the action and the conditions under which it would change. And analytics has limits. They are data quality, association rather than cause, the assumption that the past continues, what is never measured, estimated probabilities, privacy, and the fact that it informs a decision rather than making one.
