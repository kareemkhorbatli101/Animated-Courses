1.1  Who uses financial statements, and why?
================================================================================

A company collects thousands of facts every day: sales, purchases, salaries and
loans. Financial statements summarize these facts in a standard form for people
outside the company. The four main statements are the balance sheet, the income
statement, the statement of changes in equity and the statement of cash flows.

Most companies prepare general-purpose financial statements. These are reports for
many readers at the same time, not for one special reader. The U.S. conceptual
framework names the most important readers. The primary users are existing and
potential investors, lenders and other creditors. They give resources (money, goods
or credit) to the company. They usually cannot ask the company for special reports,
so they depend on the published statements.

Each user makes a different decision, so each user looks for different information.

FIGURE F01-02. Users of financial statements and what they need
User | Decision | Key question | Statements used most
Investors (shareholders) — PRIMARY | Buy, hold or sell shares | Will the company
  create future cash flows and returns? | Income statement, cash flows, balance sheet
Lenders (banks, bondholders) — PRIMARY | Lend, renew or stop a loan | Can the
  company pay interest and repay the principal on time? | Cash flows, balance sheet
Other creditors (suppliers) — PRIMARY | Sell on credit, set credit terms | Will the
  company pay its bills when due? | Balance sheet (short-term items), cash flows
Employees and unions | Stay, negotiate pay | Is the company stable and profitable? |
  Income statement
Customers | Sign long-term contracts | Will the company continue to supply us? |
  Balance sheet, income statement
Governments and regulators | Tax, oversight, statistics | Does the company follow
  the rules and pay its taxes? | All statements and notes
Managers (internal) | Plan and control operations | Not a primary user: can use
  internal reports | Internal management reports

Other groups also read financial statements: employees, customers, governments, tax
authorities and the public. Managers read them too. However, managers are not
primary users of general-purpose statements, because they can get any internal
information they need. Reporting for managers is management accounting, the subject
of most of the CMA exam.

What do all these users need? In simple terms, they ask three questions.
• What does the company have, and what does it owe? The balance sheet answers this.
• How did the company perform in the period? The income statement answers this.
• Where did cash come from, and where did it go? The statement of cash flows answers this.

Users also want to judge stewardship: how well the managers used the owners' resources.

WHAT MAKES INFORMATION USEFUL?
The conceptual framework lists the qualities of useful information. There are two
fundamental qualities. Relevance means the information can make a difference to a
decision. It helps users predict the future (predictive value) or check their past
predictions (confirmatory value). Relevance includes materiality: an item is
material if leaving it out or stating it wrongly could change users' decisions.

Faithful representation means the information shows what it claims to show. It is
complete, neutral and free from error. Four enhancing qualities make information
even more useful: comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability.
Finally, there is a cost constraint: the benefit of reporting information should be
greater than its cost.

BOX — EXAM TRAP: Who is a primary user?
Primary users are investors, lenders and other creditors. Managers are users, but
not primary users of general-purpose statements. Also remember: relevance and
faithful representation are the two fundamental qualities. Comparability,
verifiability, timeliness and understandability are only enhancing qualities.

BOX — LANGUAGE FOCUS: Five accounting verbs
These verbs have exact meanings in exam questions. Learn the difference.
recognize | include an item in the statements, with a name and an amount
measure   | decide the amount of the item
record    | enter the item in the accounts (journal and ledger)
present   | show the item on the face of a statement
disclose  | explain the item in the notes to the statements
Example: Orontes recognizes revenue when it delivers the goods. It measures the
revenue at the invoice price. It presents revenue in the income statement, and it
discloses its revenue policy in the notes.

1.2  The building blocks: elements and the accounting equation
================================================================================

Every amount in the financial statements belongs to an element. The FASB conceptual
framework defines ten elements. Three of them describe the balance sheet at one date.
• An asset is a present right of an entity to an economic benefit. Examples: cash,
  accounts receivable, inventory and equipment.
• A liability is a present obligation of an entity to transfer an economic benefit.
  Examples: accounts payable, notes payable and wages payable.
• Equity is the residual interest in the assets after the liabilities are deducted.
  It is the owners' claim.

The other elements describe changes during a period.
• Revenues and expenses come from the company's central, ongoing operations. When
  Orontes sells olive oil, it earns revenue. When it pays plant workers, it has an expense.
• Gains and losses come from peripheral or incidental events. If Orontes sells an old
  delivery truck for more than its carrying amount, it has a gain.
• Investments by owners, such as buying new shares, increase equity. Distributions to
  owners, such as dividends, decrease equity.
• Comprehensive income is the total change in equity from sources other than owners.
  You will meet it in Chapters 3 and 15.

The accounting equation connects the three balance sheet elements:
    Assets = Liabilities + Equity
The equation always holds, because creditors (liabilities) or owners (equity) finance
every asset. Equity has two main parts. Contributed capital is what owners paid in:
common stock at its par value, plus additional paid-in capital (APIC). Retained
earnings (RE) are the past net income that the company kept instead of paying it out
as dividends.

FIGURE F01-03. The accounting equation A = L + E, with equity split into contributed
capital and retained earnings.

Look at the lower part of Figure F01-03. Revenues and gains increase retained
earnings. Expenses and losses decrease it. Dividends also decrease it, but a dividend
is not an expense. It is a distribution to owners, so it never appears in the income
statement.

BOX — IFRS CONTRAST: Same idea, different words
The IFRS Conceptual Framework (2018) defines an asset as a present economic resource
controlled by the entity as a result of past events. The idea is the same as in U.S.
GAAP; only the wording is different. IFRS also uses the word income for both revenue
and gains, while U.S. GAAP keeps revenues and gains apart. You may also know these
IFRS names: statement of financial position (balance sheet), share capital (common
stock) and share premium (additional paid-in capital).

BOX — FALSE-FRIEND ALERT: Words that look familiar
• stock — In U.S. English, stock means shares: common stock, a stock dividend. In
  French and British English, stock often means inventory. In a CMA question,
  inventory is always inventory.
• income and revenue — Arabic دخل is not إيراد. Revenue is the gross amount from
  sales. Net income is what remains after expenses. Do not use income when the
  question means revenue.
• résultat and charges — In French, résultat is the profit or loss (net income), and
  charges are expenses. In English, charges usually means fees.
• balance — In French, la balance is the trial balance. The balance sheet is le bilan.

1.3  Double entry: debits and credits
================================================================================

Accountants record every transaction with the double-entry system. Each transaction
affects at least two accounts, and total debits always equal total credits. This is
why the accounting equation always balances.

Debit simply means the left side of an account, and credit means the right side.
Neither word means good or bad, and neither word always means increase. Whether a
debit increases an account depends on the type of account. Each account has a normal
balance: the side that increases it.

FIGURE F01-04. Debit and credit rules: which six account types increase with a debit
and which with a credit.

The expanded equation at the bottom of Figure F01-04 is a useful memory aid. Accounts
on the left side of the equation (assets, expenses and dividends) increase with a
debit. Accounts on the right side (liabilities, equity and revenues) increase with a
credit.

A contra account reduces a related account, so it has the opposite normal balance.
For example, accumulated depreciation reduces equipment. It has a credit balance, but
it belongs with the assets. The allowance for credit losses reduces accounts
receivable in the same way (Chapter 6).

Orontes Foods, Inc. makes olive oil and tahini in Amman, Jordan, and frozen pastries

            Issued 100,000 new shares of $1 par common stock for $15 per share, in cash.

3 | Jan 10 | Equipment 1,200 / Cash 1,200
            Bought a new bottling line for the Amman plant and paid cash.
4 | Jan 18 | Accounts receivable 300 / Sales revenue 300
            Cost of goods sold 180 / Inventory 180
            Sold olive oil to GreenBasket Supermarkets on credit. The goods had cost
            Orontes less than the selling price.
5 | Jan 31 | Wages expense 40 / Cash 40
            Paid January wages to plant workers in cash.
6 | Jan 31 | Retained earnings (dividends declared) 50 / Dividends payable 50
            The board declared a cash dividend, payable on February 15.
Totals 4,070 = 4,070

Check each entry: the debits equal the credits.

FIGURE F01-05. Effect grid for six Orontes Foods transactions in January 2025 (USD 000).

Read the grid row by row. Three points matter for the exam.
• Rows 1 and 2 bring in cash of 2,300 in total, but no revenue. Money from owners and
  lenders is financing. It is not income.
• Row 4 creates revenue of 300 but no cash. Orontes records revenue when it delivers
  the goods, not when the customer pays.
• Row 6 reduces retained earnings by 50 and creates a liability. No cash moves until
  February, and net income does not change.

The totals prove the equation. Assets rose by 2,380, liabilities rose by 850 and
equity rose by 1,530.

BOX — EXAM TRAP: Three classic mistakes
• A dividend is not an expense. It reduces retained earnings, not net income.
• Cash from borrowing or from issuing shares is not revenue. It is financing.
• Buying equipment for cash is not an expense today. One asset (cash) becomes another
  asset (equipment). The cost becomes an expense later, through depreciation.

1.4  The accrual basis and the matching principle
================================================================================

When should a company record revenues and expenses? U.S. GAAP financial statements
use the accrual basis. Under the accrual basis, a company records revenue when it
earns it, that is, when it delivers the goods or services. It records expenses when
it incurs them. The timing of the cash does not decide the period. The cash basis
records items only when cash is received or paid. The cash basis is simple, but it is
not acceptable under U.S. GAAP.

Figure F01-07 shows why the difference matters. On December 20, 2025, Orontes ships
olive oil to GreenBasket Supermarkets and sends an invoice for 90. The goods cost
Orontes 54. GreenBasket pays on January 15, 2026.

FIGURE F01-07. Timeline of a December sale collected in January: accrual basis versus
cash basis.

Under the accrual basis, Orontes records the revenue and the cost of goods sold in
fiscal 2025, the year it delivered the goods. The gross profit of 36 belongs to 2025.
In January, the cash receipt only changes one asset (the receivable) into another
asset (cash). Under the cash basis, the revenue would fall in 2026, which is the
wrong period.

THE MATCHING PRINCIPLE
The matching principle says: record expenses in the same period as the revenues they
help to produce. There are three ways to apply it.
• Cause and effect. Some costs link directly to a sale. Cost of goods sold is the
  classic example: Orontes records it at the same time as the sales revenue.
• Systematic and rational allocation. Some costs help many periods, such as a bottling
  line. Depreciation spreads the cost over the years of use.
• Immediate recognition. Some costs have no clear link to future revenue, such as most
  advertising and office salaries. They are expenses in the period in which they are incurred.

The accrual basis creates four common types of adjustment. You met three of them in
the February transactions.
Prepaid expense (deferral) | Cash is paid before the expense is incurred. |
  Rent paid in advance (February, row 3) | an asset
Contract liability, or unearned revenue (deferral) | Cash is received before the
  revenue is earned. | Hotel pays in advance (February, row 4) | a liability
Accrued expense | The expense is incurred before cash is paid. | Interest owed on the
  bank note (February, row 6) | a liability
Accrued revenue | Revenue is earned before cash is received or billed. | Studied in
  Chapter 11 | an asset

Two of these names are important for the exam. A prepaid expense is an asset: the
company has paid for a future benefit. A contract liability (unearned revenue) is a
liability: the company must still deliver the goods or services.

BOX — EXAM TRAP: Cash is not the trigger
• Cash received in advance is a liability, not revenue.
• Cash paid in advance is an asset, not an expense.
• Matching means matching expenses to revenues, not to cash payments.

1.5  Who writes the rules? U.S. GAAP and IFRS
================================================================================

In the United States, the accounting rules are called U.S. GAAP (generally accepted
accounting principles). The FASB (Financial Accounting Standards Board) writes them.
The FASB is a private, independent organization. It keeps all U.S. GAAP in one place:
the Accounting Standards Codification (ASC). The ASC is the single source of
authoritative nongovernmental U.S. GAAP. It is organized by topic numbers. For
example, ASC 606 covers revenue and ASC 842 covers leases. When the FASB changes a
rule, it issues an Accounting Standards Update (ASU), which amends the Codification.

The SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) is a U.S. government agency. It has
legal authority over the financial reporting of public companies, and it recognizes
the FASB as the standard setter. For companies that report to the SEC, SEC rules are
also part of authoritative GAAP. Do not confuse these bodies with the PCAOB, which
oversees the audits of public companies. The PCAOB sets auditing standards, not
accounting standards.

Outside the United States, many countries use IFRS (International Financial Reporting
Standards). The IASB (International Accounting Standards Board) issues them, under
the IFRS Foundation. Many countries in the Middle East and North Africa, for example
Jordan and the United Arab Emirates, require IFRS for listed companies. In this book,
Cedar Retail S.A.L. is our IFRS company.

FIGURE F01-08. Who writes the rules: SEC, FASB and the ASC for U.S. GAAP; IFRS
Foundation and IASB for IFRS.

The CMA exam tests U.S. GAAP. However, Section A also asks about the main differences
between U.S. GAAP and IFRS (Chapter 17). If you learned accounting under IFRS, this is
good news: most concepts are the same. Every chapter has an IFRS Contrast box, so
Chapter 17 will be revision for you.

Both systems rest on a conceptual framework: the basic ideas behind the standards,
such as the users, the elements and the qualities of useful information. You studied
the U.S. version in Sections 1.1 and 1.2.

• IASB: writes IFRS.

1.6  A first look at the four statements
================================================================================

Each statement answers a different question for users. Chapters 2 to 5 study each
statement in detail. Here is the overview.
Balance sheet | What does the company have and owe, and what is the owners' claim? |
  at a date | Chapter 2
Income statement | How did the company perform? | for a period | Chapter 3
Statement of changes in equity | Why did each equity account change? | for a period | Chapter 4
Statement of cash flows | Where did cash come from, and where did it go? | for a period | Chapter 5

The four statements are linked. Figure F01-09 shows the two main links. Net income
flows into retained earnings, and the net change in cash explains the cash balance on
the balance sheet.

FIGURE F01-09. How the four statements connect through net income, retained earnings
and cash.

You can see both links in the January transactions of Orontes. Net income was 80:
revenue of 300, minus cost of goods sold of 180, minus wages of 40. The dividend
declared was 50, so retained earnings rose by 30. Cash rose by 1,060: an operating
outflow of 40, an investing outflow of 1,200 and a financing inflow of 2,300.

Notice that net income and the change in cash are very different numbers. This is the
accrual basis at work.

1.7  Extended Example 1A: from a trial balance to a set of statements
================================================================================

This example takes the Barada trial balance of Figure F01-10 and builds the statements
from it, in the order a company prepares them. It is exam-core: the exam often gives a
trial balance and asks for one figure from one statement.

1. THE PLAN
• Sort every account by element: asset, liability, equity, revenue or expense.
• Prepare the income statement first, because net income is needed by the next statement.
• Prepare the statement of changes in equity, which produces closing retained earnings.
• Prepare the balance sheet last, using that closing figure.
• Check that the balance sheet balances.

2. THE INCOME STATEMENT
Revenue of $3,100 less cost of goods sold of $1,860 gives a gross profit of $1,240.
Operating expenses are wages of $520 and depreciation of $300, so operating income is
$420. Interest of $90 leaves net income of $330.

Cost of goods sold | (1,860)
Gross profit | 1,240
Wages expense | (520)
Depreciation expense | (300)
Operating income | 420
Interest expense | (90)
Net income | 330

3. THE STATEMENT OF CHANGES IN EQUITY
Retained earnings open at $1,000, rise by net income of $330 and fall by dividends
declared of $50, closing at $1,280. Common stock of $600 and additional paid-in capital
of $1,900 do not change this year, so total equity is $3,780.

4. THE BALANCE SHEET
Current assets of $1,774 and non-current assets of $4,236 give total assets of $6,010.
Current liabilities are $430 and the long-term note is $1,800.

Two contra accounts matter here. Receivables appear net of the allowance at $280, and
equipment appears net of accumulated depreciation at $3,300. Reporting either one gross
would overstate assets.

5. TIE LINE — BOX: WORKED EXAMPLE: Everything ties
• Assets $6,010 = liabilities $2,230 + equity $3,780.
• Operating income $420 − interest $90 = net income $330.
• Opening retained earnings $1,000 + $330 − $50 = $1,280, the figure on the balance sheet.

6. READING THE RESULT
Working capital is $1,344 and the current ratio is 4.13, so Barada can meet its
short-term obligations comfortably. A lender would also note that the long-term note of
$1,800 is large against equity of $3,780.

7. WHERE THE FOURTH STATEMENT COMES FROM
A statement of cash flows needs last year's balance sheet as well as this one, so it
cannot be built from a single trial balance. Chapter 5 prepares it for Orontes, which
has both years.

8. HOW THE EXAM ASKS IT — BOX: EXAM TRAP: Exam view
A trial balance question usually asks for one number: total current assets, closing
retained earnings or net income. Sort the accounts by element first, and watch the
contra accounts, which belong with the asset they reduce rather than with liabilities.

CHAPTER SUMMARY
================================================================================
FIGURE F01-13. Chapter 1 summary map: the key rule for each of the seven topics.

General-purpose financial statements serve investors, lenders and other creditors.
Useful information is relevant and faithfully represented. The statements are built
from elements that the accounting equation links: assets equal liabilities plus equity.
Every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits. The accrual basis records
revenues when they are earned and expenses when they are incurred, and it matches
expenses to the revenues they help to produce. The FASB writes U.S. GAAP in the ASC,
and the IASB writes IFRS. The CMA exam uses U.S. GAAP and tests the main IFRS
differences. The balance sheet shows one date, while the other three statements cover
a period, and net income and cash link them.

CASE QUESTION — Barada Wholesale, Inc.
================================================================================
Barada Wholesale, Inc. is a food wholesaler in Amman and one of Orontes's largest
customers. It buys tahini, halva and oils from Orontes and sells them to shops across
Jordan. Barada prepares its statements under U.S. GAAP because its bank and its main
supplier ask for them.

At the end of 2025 Barada's bookkeeper prepared a trial balance. It lists every account
with its debit or credit balance, and total debits equal total credits. The list
includes cash, receivables, inventory, prepaid rent, equipment and accumulated
depreciation, loans, dividends payable, revenue and several expenses.

Barada's bank is reviewing its loan and wants three figures from the statements before
it renews the credit line: net income for the year, total assets and total liabilities
at the year-end. The bank also wants to know how the year's results changed retained
earnings after closing.

Barada's owner, Mr Haddad, asks you to help. He is not an accountant, so he wants each
account placed in the right category before any total is calculated. Amounts in the
trial balance are in thousands of U.S. dollars. He also wants to know whether dividends
reduce net income, because a friend told him they do.

How to enter answers: Enter amounts in thousands of U.S. dollars (USD 000), without the
$ sign or commas (for example, 1250).

C1-1  Match each account to its category. (On the exam screen you would drag each
      account into a box.)
      Categories: Asset · Contra-asset · Liability · Equity · Revenue · Expense
      Prepaid rent = Asset
      Land = Asset
      Dividends payable = Liability
      Additional paid-in capital = Equity

      Interest expense = Expense
      Allowance for credit losses = Contra-asset

      Note: Watch the two contra-asset accounts: they sit with the assets but have
      credit balances. Dividends payable is a liability; dividends declared (in the

      280 is wrong: this answer subtracts the dividends declared as if they were an expense.
      1,240 is wrong: this answer stops at gross profit (revenue minus cost of goods sold).
      3,100 is wrong: this answer reports revenue instead of net income.
