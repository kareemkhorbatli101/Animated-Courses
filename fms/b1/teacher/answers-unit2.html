W-AExercise 1 in the book’s key

Pair Discussion – Behind a Successful Meeting

Question 1

B1 targetThe hardest part is managing changes at the last minute. A room becomes unavailable, a flight is delayed, or an extra participant joins. You must inform everybody quickly and keep the visiting side calm.

B2 extensionThe hardest part is managing late changes without the visiting delegation ever feeling the disruption. A conference room becomes unavailable, a flight is delayed by two hours, or two additional participants need interpretation. The coordination work is invisible when it is done well, and painfully visible when it is not. The second difficulty is that almost none of this work is visible in a job description, so a liaison officer is judged on the one occasion when something is not absorbed.

Question 2

B1 targetPoor preparation signals that the partner is not important to us. If the agenda arrives late or the room is wrong, the visiting delegation starts the meeting with doubts. That doubt is hard to repair later.

B2 extensionPoor preparation signals, however unintentionally, that the partnership is not a priority. A late agenda, an unprepared protocol brief, or a missing interpreter tells the visiting delegation something about the institution before a single substantive word is spoken — and that impression travels back to their capital in the post-visit report. The repair cost is also asymmetric: a good impression takes a year to build and a late agenda takes an afternoon to undo.

Question 3

sampleA visiting delegation feels well-coordinated when they arrive and find the room ready, their names printed correctly on the seating cards, and a liaison officer waiting for them at the entrance. The seating cards matter most — because a misspelled name is the one error a visitor cannot forget. It shows whether the detail work was done.

W-BExercise 2 in the book’s key

Group Brainstorm – The Liaison Officer's Work

Question 1

B1 targetBefore the meeting, the liaison officer must confirm the date, time, and venue with both sides, circulate the agenda, arrange security clearances for all foreign participants, prepare the protocol brief, and confirm the room setup.

B2 extensionBefore the meeting, the officer confirms date, time, and venue with both sides, circulates the agenda in time for the visiting delegation to prepare, arranges security clearance for every foreign participant, prepares the protocol brief for the delegation lead, and confirms the room setup — seating, AV, and translation booths. Every one of these has a deadline that is earlier than people expect. The second hardest part is that several of them depend on the other side responding, so the officer's deadline is always earlier than the real one.

Question 2

B1 targetDuring the meeting, the officer monitors the agenda to keep the discussion on track, coordinates with the interpreters, and takes careful notes for the official record. The officer also watches the time and signals the delegation lead discreetly when a session is running over.

B2 extensionDuring the session, the officer monitors the agenda, coordinates with the interpreters, takes careful notes for the official record, and manages the clock discreetly. None of this should be visible to the visiting side: the officer intervenes through the delegation lead, never over them. The second during-meeting task is watching the clock, which means knowing which agenda items can be deferred before the session starts rather than deciding it under pressure.

Question 3

B1 targetAfter the meeting, the officer writes the post-meeting summary within 24 hours and follows up on the action items agreed in the session. The summary becomes the official record, so it must be accurate and complete.

B2 extensionAfter the session, the officer distributes the post-meeting summary within 24 hours and tracks every action item to its owner and deadline. The summary is the institutional memory of the meeting — if an agreement is not in it, it effectively did not happen. The second failure point is the action items, which have owners in the summary and no owner in practice unless the officer tracks them personally.

Spoken sample answer
Salma: Let us sort these tasks into the three stages. I will start with the ones that clearly come before the meeting — confirming the date, the venue, and the agenda.
Youssef: Agreed. Security clearances belong there too. All foreign nationals must submit the clearance form 48 hours before entry, so that is a before-meeting deadline — not a same-day task.
Salma: And the protocol brief? That is for the delegation lead.
Youssef: Before, definitely. The lead needs to read it the evening before — not in the car. It should include names, titles, nationalities, and the key positions of the other side.
Salma: What about the interpreters?
Youssef: Both. You arrange them before — and you coordinate with them during the session — because the booth needs to know when the technical presentation starts and whether there is a handout to follow. The during-meeting work is monitoring the agenda, managing the clock, and keeping an accurate note for the record.
Salma: And after the session?
Youssef: The post-meeting summary within 24 hours, as required by protocol, and then following up on the action items. That is where most coordination work quietly fails — the summary goes out and nobody tracks the commitments.
Youssef: So: before is confirmation and preparation, during is monitoring and recording, and after is reporting and follow-up. All three are the same job.

Scenario Cards – Liaison Officer in Action

Question 1

B1 targetI am arranging Conference Room B and the AV equipment for the Morocco–Japan bilateral working session. Twelve participants will attend, and three require Japanese interpretation. The Japanese side expects to present slides, so I have confirmed that the projector and laptop connection are tested.

B2 extensionI am arranging Conference Room B and the full AV setup for the Morocco–Japan bilateral working session. Twelve participants will attend in total, and three of them require Japanese interpretation, so I have requested a booth and two interpreters from the Language Services Unit. The Japanese side will present slides, so I have confirmed that the projector, the laptop connection, and a spare adapter are all tested the day before — not on the morning.

Question 2

B1 targetI am coordinating catering for a six-person Gulf Arab ministerial delegation attending a full-day session with a working lunch. All food must be halal, and I have confirmed this in writing with the catering service. I have also arranged Arabic coffee and dates for the welcome in the protocol lounge.

B2 extensionI am coordinating the dietary arrangements for a six-person Gulf Arab ministerial delegation attending a full-day session that includes a working lunch. All catering must be halal and I have confirmed that in writing with the service provider rather than by telephone. I have also arranged Arabic coffee and dates for the welcome in the protocol lounge, and I have asked the embassy whether any member of the delegation has an additional dietary requirement — it is better to ask than to assume.

Question 3

B1 targetI am preparing the one-day visit schedule for a three-person Swedish environmental NGO team. They are coming to discuss the National Coastal Restoration Program. The schedule includes a morning session, a working lunch, and a site briefing in the afternoon. Because the team is small and informal, I have not arranged a formal welcome ceremony — that would read as excessive rather than respectful.

B2 extensionI am preparing the one-day visit schedule for a three-person Swedish environmental NGO team coming to discuss the National Coastal Restoration Program. The schedule covers a morning working session, a lunch with the technical directorate, and an afternoon briefing with the coastal management unit. Because the team is small and informal, I have kept the protocol light — no formal welcome ceremony — but I have built a thirty-minute buffer into the afternoon — because site briefings always run over. I have also confirmed that the programme documents are available in English. And I have asked the technical directorate to send one person who can answer coastal questions directly — because referring a visiting team back to the ministry after the meeting wastes the visit.

1CExercise 6 in the book’s key

Group Discussion – What Must a Protocol Brief Include?

Question 1

B1 targetA useful protocol brief must include the full names, correct titles, and nationalities of everyone attending. It should also state the key positions of the other side and any sensitive points to avoid. The delegation lead needs it the evening before the meeting — not on the morning.

B2 extensionA useful protocol brief must give the full names, exact titles, and nationalities of every participant on the visiting side, the seniority order for seating and for speaking, the other side's known positions on each agenda item, and any subject that should be handled carefully. It must be short enough to be read in ten minutes — a brief nobody reads is worse than no brief — because it creates a false sense of preparation.

Question 2

B1 targetThe agenda should be sent at least five days before the session, and the final version at least 48 hours before. If it arrives too late, the visiting delegation cannot prepare their technical positions and the meeting becomes a general discussion instead of a working session.

B2 extensionThe agenda should be circulated at least five days ahead, with the final version confirmed no later than 48 hours before the session. A late agenda forces the visiting delegation to arrive unprepared, which turns a working group into a general exchange of positions — and it also signals that the host side was itself disorganized — which is the more lasting damage.

Question 3

B1 targetCommon last-minute failures are room changes, interpretation that was not booked, and security clearances that were not processed in time. The officer should inform the other side immediately, always with a proposed solution, and then confirm in writing.

B2 extensionThe most common last-minute failures are a room that becomes unavailable, interpretation that was requested too late, and a clearance that was not processed. The professional response is the same in all three cases: inform the other side at once, never let them discover it themselves, always present a solution alongside the problem, and confirm the agreed fix in writing so there is a record.

Spoken sample answer
Youssef: Salma, what do you actually put in a protocol brief? I have seen some that are twenty pages and some that are half a page.
Salma: The half-page one is usually better. The lead needs four things: who is in the room, exactly how their names and titles are spelled and pronounced, the order of seniority for seating and speaking, and what the other side wants out of each agenda item. Everything else is background you can attach.
Youssef: And the sensitive points?
Salma: Those go in the brief, but carefully. Not "do not mention X" — that reads badly if the brief is ever seen. I write it as what the other side's position is and where they have been cautious before. The lead can draw their own conclusion. And it goes to them the evening before — because nobody reads a brief in a car.
2EExercise 12 in the book’s key

Speaking Practice

Question 1

B1 targetAll foreign visitors must submit a security clearance form 48 hours before entry. Visitors must not enter the ministry building without a visitor badge. They may use the side entrance if the main entrance is being prepared for a ceremony.

B2 extensionAll foreign visitors must submit the security clearance form at least 48 hours before entry, and they must present official identification at the security desk on arrival. Visitors must not carry recording equipment into the meeting rooms without prior written authorization. They may be escorted through the side entrance when the main entrance is in ceremonial use.

Question 2

B1 targetFirst, you should confirm the date, the venue, and the participant list in writing — a phone call is not enough. You need to circulate the agenda at least five days before. You should also prepare the protocol brief early and send it to the delegation lead the evening before the session.

B2 extensionFirst, you should confirm the date, venue, and participant list in writing — because a telephone agreement leaves no record if a detail is later disputed. You need to circulate the agenda at least five days ahead and the final version 48 hours ahead. You should prepare the protocol brief early rather than the night before, and you need to build a buffer into the schedule — every bilateral session I have coordinated has run over somewhere. And confirm the interpretation requirement in the first call — not the last — it is the single question that most often arrives too late to solve properly.

Question 3

B1 targetA liaison officer can move a meeting to a different room, arrange interpretation, and change the catering. The officer cannot change the agenda, agree to a new participant from the other side, or move the meeting to a different day without authorization from a senior official.

B2 extensionA liaison officer can decide room allocation, AV arrangements, interpretation booking, catering, and the practical schedule of arrivals. The officer cannot alter the agreed agenda, accept an additional participant on the visiting side, commit the ministry to a new meeting date, or agree to any change with substantive content — all of those require authorization, and acting alone on them is the fastest way to lose a delegation lead's confidence.

3BExercise 14 in the book’s key

Listening: Four Coordination Calls

Question 1

sampleDialogue 1: The original conference room is unavailable because of an urgent ministerial meeting; Salma informs the German side proactively and confirms that the new room still seats twelve, and she commits to an updated logistics note by midday. Dialogue 2: Dr. Braun's flight is delayed, so Thomas asks to move the session to three o'clock; Salma checks with the Director and confirms, with a revised logistics note within the hour. Dialogue 3: Two German-speaking specialists need interpretation; Salma says she should be able to arrange it through the Language Services Unit and will call back within the hour. Dialogue 4: Dr. Hoffman's security clearance is incomplete; Salma states the 48-hour rule and proposes a video link as a fallback. In all four calls the pattern is identical: the problem is reported by whoever discovers it, a solution is proposed in the same breath, and a written note follows.

Question 2

sampleSalma uses must to state the non-negotiable institutional rules: "All foreign nationals must submit the clearance form 48 hours before entry" and "interpretation must be requested at least four hours before the session." She uses should for what she expects to be possible but has not yet confirmed: "We should be able to arrange German interpretation." She uses can to ask for and to offer practical possibilities: "Can Dr. Hoffman submit the form today?" And she uses will for her own firm commitments: "I will send written confirmation before noon." The pattern is consistent: must for the rules, should for the likely, can for the possible, will for what she personally undertakes.

Question 3

B1 targetSalma communicates problems early and never waits for them to become worse. She always offers a solution with the problem. She is careful to separate what she can confirm now from what she needs to check, and she sets a clear deadline for every call-back.

B2 extensionSalma demonstrates four professional qualities. She is proactive: the German side learns about the room change from her — not on arrival. She is solution-oriented: every problem arrives with a proposed fix attached. She is precise about her own authority — she never promises what she has not confirmed, but she commits to a specific time by which she will have an answer. And she closes every verbal agreement with a written note, which protects both sides if a detail is later remembered differently. None of the four calls ends without a stated deadline — which is what distinguishes coordination from correspondence.

3CExercise 15 in the book’s key

Choose the Correct Response

Question 1

sampleCorrect answer: (b) I will check the room capacity immediately and confirm within the hour. If needed, we can consider an alternative room setup. — Why (a) is wrong: It tells the embassy it is at fault, which settles nothing. A liaison officer's first move is to find out what is actually possible - the room's capacity - and come back with an answer and a time. Note that the correct reply commits to both.

Question 2

sampleCorrect answer: (b) Of course. I will coordinate with our translation team and send you the German version by tomorrow morning — the meeting is in three days, so this is very manageable. — Why (a) is wrong: It refuses without offering anything instead. "We cannot change it now" ends the exchange; the professional answer accepts the request, names who will do the work, and says when it will arrive.

Question 3

sampleCorrect answer: (a) Yes, all delegation leads must bring their signed authorization letter — this is required for the official record. I will include a reminder in the logistics note I send you today. — Why (b) is wrong: It waives a protocol requirement that a liaison officer has no authority to waive. Telling a delegation that nothing official is needed produces exactly the problem it was trying to avoid - at the door, on the morning, in front of the delegation lead.

Question 4

sampleCorrect answer: (b) Certainly. The Morocco–Germany Infrastructure Working Group is confirmed for tomorrow, Tuesday the fourteenth, at ten o'clock in Conference Room B on the second floor of the main ministry building. I will send you the final logistics note by end of today. — Why (a) is wrong: "I believe it is in the morning" is not a confirmation. Coordination language has to be exact - the name of the session, the date, the time - because the other side will book flights and interpreters on the strength of it.

4CExercise 19 in the book’s key

Group Discussion: Managing a Meeting Professionally

Question 1

B1 targetThe visiting delegation knows a meeting is well managed when the room is ready, the agenda is followed, and the interpretation works. They also notice whether someone is clearly in charge of the time. Small failures are what they remember.

B2 extensionA: The visiting delegation reads the quality of management from details they are not supposed to notice — the room ready before they arrive, their names spelled correctly, the interpretation booth tested, the agenda actually followed. B: They also notice whether someone is visibly responsible for the clock — because a session that drifts tells them the host side has not thought about outcomes. C: What they remember afterwards is never the substance of agenda item three; it is whether the day felt controlled. D: And they read it from the parts nobody prepared — whether the coffee arrived during the break, whether their names were spelled correctly on the cards, whether somebody met them at the entrance. The agenda is assumed; the details are the evidence. E: Which is why the liaison officer's work is invisible when it succeeds.

Question 2

B1 targetFirst, confirm room readiness and the AV setup early in the morning — not just the day before. Second, check that the protocol brief and the seating plan reflect the final participant list. Third, never forget the interpretation — if it fails, nothing else in the day works. And confirm the final participant list on the morning — because a late addition changes the seating, the badges, and the catering at once.

B2 extensionA: First, confirm room readiness and test the AV in the morning — not only the day before — overnight is when projectors stop working. B: Second, reconcile the seating plan and the protocol brief against the final participant list — because a late addition on either side changes both. C: Third, confirm the interpretation arrangement personally with the booth — because interpretation is the one failure that stops the meeting entirely rather than merely embarrassing the host. D: The fourth thing, which is not on most checklists, is to know who on the host side can authorize a change during the session — because if the answer is nobody, the officer will be making decisions they do not have the standing to make.

Question 3

B1 targetThe liaison officer monitors the time and signals the delegation lead discreetly, never interrupting the discussion directly. The lead can then propose moving an item to the next session. The language should be neutral: "I suggest we return to the agenda."

B2 extensionA: The liaison officer watches the clock and signals the delegation lead privately — a note — not an interruption. B: The lead then makes the intervention — because only the lead has the standing to redirect a counterpart: "I propose we take the remaining point on water infrastructure at our next session, so that we can conclude today with the committee framework agreed." C: The phrasing matters: a neutral, level tone makes it a scheduling decision rather than a criticism of whoever was speaking. D: What does not work is the officer intervening directly. However polite the wording, a liaison officer stopping a visiting delegation lead mid-sentence is a protocol error that will be remembered longer than the overrun would have been.

5BExercise 21 in the book’s key

Reading Comprehension: Discussion Questions

Question 1

B1 targetThe six weeks of preparation made the session successful. The agenda was circulated five days in advance, so both sides came prepared. The most important factor was that the coordination problems were solved before the delegation arrived. Preparation here means the unglamorous part — the agenda, the clearances, the room — rather than the substantive briefing.

B2 extensionA: The six weeks of preparation is what made the session work, and the clearest evidence is that the agenda went out five days ahead — both sides arrived with technical positions rather than opening statements. B: I would point to the pre-meeting briefing in the protocol lounge: thirty minutes that let the German team settle before the formal opening. C: For me the decisive factor is that two real coordination failures — the room and the interpretation — were absorbed before the visitors ever saw them. A session is well managed not when nothing goes wrong but when the guests never find out. D: The counter-argument is that six weeks for one session is not scalable, and the ministry will have to find a way to do this in three.

Question 2

B1 targetSalma's contribution was to solve both coordination problems quietly and to distribute the summary within 24 hours. Her role required attention to detail, quick decisions, and the ability to communicate problems professionally without alarming the other side. She also had to keep the Director informed without escalating every detail to him.

B2 extensionSalma's contribution was to absorb two failures that would each have been visible to the visiting delegation, and then to close the session properly with a summary distributed inside the protocol deadline. The role required precision with detail, the judgement to know what she could decide alone and what needed the Director, and the professional calm to present every problem with a solution already attached. What the role did not require, and what inexperienced officers often attempt, was solving everything personally rather than routing a decision upward when it genuinely belonged there.

Question 3

B1 targetI would have confirmed the interpretation requirements in the very first coordination call instead of waiting. I would also have built a one-hour buffer into the schedule for overruns. And I would have prepared a contingency room plan from week one rather than after the problem appeared.

B2 extensionA: I would have asked about interpretation requirements in the first coordination call rather than discovering them late — it is a question that costs nothing to ask early and a great deal to ask late. B: I would have built a one-hour buffer into the afternoon — because the water infrastructure item was always going to run over. C: And I would have held a second room provisionally from week one. A contingency is cheap when nobody needs it; it is impossible to create two days before a session. D: What I would not change is the six weeks. The preparation time is what made all three of those corrections possible, and a session arranged in two weeks fails on the first of them.

Question 4

B1 targetThis session signals that the ministry has trained professionals who can manage high-level bilateral sessions. The country is engaging substantively on infrastructure priorities — not just exchanging declarations. The signed document with a timeline signals institutional follow-through. It also signals that the ministry can absorb a problem without the partner noticing.

B2 extensionIt signals several things at once. The ministry has staff capable of running a technical bilateral session to international standards. The engagement was substantive — rail, water, and a monitoring committee — not a general statement of friendship. And the session closed with a signed aide-mémoire and a commitment to meet again within three months, which is the clearest available evidence of institutional follow-through. Partners read that signal carefully when they decide how much to invest in a relationship. The absence of any of those three signals is read just as carefully — which is why a session that produces only a photograph is worse than no session at all.

7BExercise 29 in the book’s key

Intonation in Meeting Room Language

Question 1

sampleFalling intonation on BEGIN — this is the key point. "Shall we BEGIN?" is grammatically a question but functionally a decision, and the falling tone communicates that. A rising tone turns it into a genuine request for permission, which weakens the chair's authority at the exact moment it is needed.

Question 2

sampleLevel, calm intonation — neither rising nor falling. "I suggest we return to the AGENDA." The neutral tone is what makes this a professional redirect rather than a reprimand. Any rise sounds uncertain; any sharp fall sounds like criticism of the person speaking.

Question 3

sampleWarmth on 'I thank you' — slight emphasis and a little more time on 'thank.' Then a clear falling tone on MEETING to signal finality. The combination is what a closing statement needs: genuine appreciation followed by an unambiguous end. Do not rush either half.

7DExercise 31 in the book’s key

Practice and Record

Question 1

sampleStress: your INSTITUTION, the PARTNER'S name, the MEETING title, and the word BEGIN. Link: 'on_be-half_of.' Tone: authoritative and welcoming at the same time. Pace: slower than conversation. One breath per sentence, with a clear pause before the invitation to begin.

Question 2

sampleDeclarative statements should go DOWN at the end. This is the most common mistake for speakers whose first language uses rising patterns: a welcome delivered with a rising tone sounds like a question and undercuts the authority of the chair. Record yourself, listen only to the final word of each sentence, and check that it falls.

Question 3

sampleAuthority comes from a steady pace, clear stress on content words, and falling endings — not from volume. Check three things in the recording: did you rush the meeting title, did any sentence end with a rise, and did you pause before inviting the delegation to begin? Warmth and authority are not in tension; both come from control.

8AExercise 32 in the book’s key

Understanding Cultural Differences in Meeting Behavior

Question 1

B1 targetScandinavian teams expect the meeting to start exactly on time and any member may speak, regardless of seniority. Gulf Arab delegations are strictly hierarchical — the delegation lead speaks first and junior members follow. Scandinavian teams take their own notes; in a Gulf session, note-taking is usually done by a designated official.

B2 extensionSpeaker A: Scandinavian teams treat the published start time as binding — arriving late is read as disrespect rather than as a minor logistical fact — and their speaking order is relatively flat, so a junior technical specialist may contradict a director without any awkwardness. Speaker B: A Gulf Arab ministerial delegation operates on a strict order of seniority: the delegation lead opens, and juniors speak when invited. The start may be flexible — because the relationship conversation that precedes the agenda is itself part of the meeting. Speaker C: Note-taking differs too. Scandinavian participants take their own notes openly on laptops, whereas in a Gulf session the record is usually kept by one designated official, and visible note-taking by several people can look like surveillance rather than diligence. Discussion: Adapting Without Losing Your Professional Identity

Question 1

B1 targetAdaptation is not performance if the respect behind it is genuine. You are changing the form — not your values. Learning how a partner expects to be addressed is the same as learning their language — it is professional preparation.

B2 extensionA: Adaptation is not performance when the respect behind it is real. You are adjusting the form of courtesy — not inventing a personality. B: The test I use is whether I would be comfortable if the other delegation saw me working with both partners in the same week. If yes, the adaptation is professional; if it would look like two different people, I have gone too far. C: It also helps to remember that the partner is adapting to you as well. A Swedish team using more formal English than they would at home is doing exactly what you are doing, and neither side finds it dishonest. D: Where the line genuinely is, in my view, is content rather than form. Adapting how you say something is professional; adapting what you say to each audience is not.
