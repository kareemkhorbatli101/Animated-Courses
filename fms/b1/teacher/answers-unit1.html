W-AExercise 1 in the book’s key

Pair Discussion – A Wider World, New Visitors

Question 1

B1 targetFive years ago, the ministry mainly received officials from a small number of neighbouring countries. Now we expect many more types of visitors: diplomats, investors, journalists, and UN staff. The biggest change is the variety.

B2 extensionFive years ago, visits were mostly limited to officials from a narrow group of regional partners and a handful of development agencies. Now we expect a much wider range: European diplomats, EU officials, investors from Japan and Korea, journalists from international broadcasters, World Bank representatives, citizens returning from abroad, and UN agency staff.

Question 2

B1 targetEvery staff member should be professional, welcoming, and calm. We represent the country's international face. A visitor's first impression of the ministry is very important.

B2 extensionProfessional, welcoming, and calm. We represent the whole institution in the first thirty seconds. A visitor's first interaction at the ministry shapes their impression of the entire institution — and, in a sense, of the country's professionalism as a whole.

Question 3

sampleA ministry feels welcoming without being informal when it has clear English signs, friendly staff, and short waiting times. For example, clear signs help visitors feel confident immediately. This is important because visitors form their impression very quickly.

W-BExercise 2 in the book’s key

Group Brainstorm – Visitor Types and Their Needs

Question 1

B1 target1. Diplomat — Protocol Department — formal reception and escort. 2. Journalist — Press Office — press accreditation. 3. Investor — Economic Affairs — meeting with the relevant officer. 4. UN official — Humanitarian Liaison — a coordination meeting. 5. Citizen living abroad — Consular Section — passport or document service.

B2 extension1. Diplomat or Ambassador — Protocol Department — formal reception, credential presentation, escorted meeting with a senior official. 2. Journalist — Press and Information Office — press accreditation, access to the spokesperson, official press materials. 3. Investor or Businessperson — Economic Affairs Office — a regulatory overview and an introduction to the right government bodies. 4. UN or NGO official — Humanitarian Liaison, under Political Affairs — programme coordination and field access. 5. Citizen living abroad — Consular Section — passports, certified documents, and guidance on legal status.

Spoken sample answer
Linh: Okay, let us go through the five visitor types. Let us start with diplomats and ambassadors.
Huy: That is the Protocol Department. When a new ambassador arrives, the most important event is presenting credentials to the minister. Everything about that visit — the ceremony, the timing, the seating — is managed by the protocol team.
Linh: What about journalists?
Huy: The Press and Information Office. Journalists need official press accreditation before they can cover ministry events. They also need clear access to the spokesperson for official quotes and statements.
Linh: And investors?
Huy: Economic Affairs handles them. Investors coming to Viet Nam now are usually interested in manufacturing, energy, and infrastructure. The ministry helps connect them with the right government bodies and provides the regulatory overview.
Linh: UN officials and NGO workers?
Huy: They usually meet with Political Affairs or the Humanitarian Liaison team. They coordinate their field programmes with the government, share updates on their operations, and work with the relevant administrative offices to ensure safe and effective access to the areas where their programmes operate.
Linh: And citizens living abroad?
Huy: Consular Section. They may need a new passport, a document certified, or guidance on their legal status if they want to return and resettle or invest. This section deals with very personal situations and needs a particularly professional and empathetic approach.

B2 extensionEconomic Affairs Office. The officer checks his name against the appointment log, requests his embassy ID, issues a visitor badge, and either calls an escort from Economic Affairs or accompanies him to the correct floor. French Embassy credentials are noted in the visitor register.

B2 extensionHumanitarian Liaison, under Political Affairs. As a UN official, her security procedure involves ID verification and a visitor badge. Question 3

B1 targetMr. Watanabe visits the Press and Information Office. He must submit his press credentials and a letter from NHK. The reception officer gives him the accreditation form and directs him to the Press Office.

B2 extensionPress and Information Office. Journalists require a specific accreditation process — he must submit his press credentials, a letter from NHK confirming his assignment, and a list of the events he intends to cover. The reception officer should give him the press accreditation form and direct him to the second floor. He should also be told clearly that accreditation is processed by the Press Office itself — not at reception, so he knows where the decision will be taken.

Question 3

sampleLinh does not turn Mr. Chaiyaporn away or show any sign of frustration. 4CExercise 19 in the book’s key

Group Discussion: What Makes a Good First Impression?

Question 1

B1 targetThe best interactions were the ones where the officer gave the visitor a clear next step immediately. Using the visitor's name — for example, "Thank you, Ms. Martin" — sounded much more professional than just "Okay." Offering to call the contact person right away removed all uncertainty.

B2 extensionA: The interactions that worked best were the ones where the officer made the visitor feel expected and welcomed immediately — even in the case of Mr. Chaiyaporn, who had no appointment. The best officers offered a clear next step right away rather than leaving the visitor to guess. B: Using the visitor's name and title consistently was the single most professional habit. C: Offering to notify the relevant officer immediately removed all uncertainty from the interaction and signalled that the institution was organized. D: What all three have in common is that none of them required extra time. The officer who used the visitor's name and offered a next step was not slower than the one who said "Okay" — only better prepared.

Question 2

B1 target"Welcome to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. How may I help you?" sounded professional because it names the institution and offers help immediately. Phrases like "Okay, go ahead" sounded unprofessional because they give no useful information. The most effective phrases showed both warmth and structure.

B2 extensionA: "Welcome to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. How may I help you?" sounds professional because it names the institution and immediately offers assistance. B: "Okay, go ahead" sounds unprofessional — it gives no useful information and seems dismissive of the visitor. C: The most effective phrases combine institutional warmth with a concrete next step, which is exactly what a diplomatic visitor expects. D: The phrases that failed were not rude; they were simply empty, and an empty phrase in a diplomatic setting is read as indifference rather than as brevity.

Question 3

B1 targetIf a reception officer looks at a screen and does not make eye contact, the visitor feels unwelcome immediately. Good posture and a direct greeting show the officer is ready and glad to help. In a diplomatic context, this first impression is very important.

B2 extensionA: If a reception officer looks distracted or keeps looking at a screen, the visitor immediately feels like an inconvenience. B: Good posture, a genuine greeting, and direct eye contact communicate that the officer is present, ready, and glad to help. C: In a diplomatic context the visitor reads these signals as evidence of how the whole ministry works, which is why body language matters before a single word is spoken.

5BExercise 21 in the book’s key

Reading Comprehension: Discussion Questions

Question 1

B1 targetI think Huy's role is most important right now. Viet Nam needs foreign investment to keep growing, and his work helps make that possible. Without economic diplomacy, political progress does not have a real impact.

B2 extensionA: I think Huy's role is most critical at this moment. Viet Nam's economic strategy absolutely depends on attracting and retaining foreign investment, and his work directly creates those pathways. Without economic diplomacy, political engagement means very little in practical terms. B: I would argue for Mai's role — because protocol is what makes high-level engagement possible at all. C: Duc's work is the most visible to ordinary people, and a ministry is judged by how it treats them. D: What is striking is that none of us chose the role with the highest formal rank, which suggests we are all judging importance by what the work makes possible rather than by where it sits on the organigram. E: And all four roles depend on the other three — which is probably the real answer.

Question 2

B1 targetMai manages the formal arrival and protocol — the physical and logistical side of official visits. Ha manages what gets reported and communicated about those same events. They work together to make sure visits are both well-organized and well-presented to the international public.

B2 extensionMai manages the formal arrival and protocol — the physical, logistical, and ceremonial side of official visits. Ha manages the media dimension — what gets photographed, reported, and communicated about those same events. A visit that is perfectly organized by Mai but badly communicated by Ha achieves only half of its diplomatic purpose, and the reverse is equally true. Their two calendars are effectively one calendar, which is why protocol and press sit in adjacent offices in most ministries.

Question 3

B1 targetAll four people need strong organizational skills and professional communication in English. They all work with time-sensitive tasks — a delayed visa or a poorly organized event can have real diplomatic consequences. They also need attention to detail and the ability to stay calm.

B2 extensionAll four require strong organizational skills, the ability to communicate professionally in English with foreign counterparts, exceptional attention to detail, and the ability to remain calm and effective under time pressure. All four also work with time-sensitive processes where a single missed step carries a diplomatic cost, so discretion matters as much as competence. What is less obvious is that all four also need the confidence to say "I will confirm that and come back to you" rather than guessing — because in each of their jobs a wrong detail is more expensive than a short delay.

Question 4

B1 targetThese profiles show that the ministry's international engagement is real and active — it is not just political speeches. The ministry is doing professional work every day, from visa processing to investment facilitation. This sends a strong message that the country has capable institutions.

B2 extensionThese four profiles show that the ministry's international engagement is operational and active — not just political announcements but real, day-to-day professional work carried out by trained and dedicated staff. From visa processing to investment facilitation to press coordination, the institutional capacity is visible to every partner who deals with it. The cumulative message is that the institution does not depend on any one person in it, which is the only kind of capacity a partner can plan around.

8AExercise 32 in the book’s key

Understanding Cultural Differences in First Meetings

Question 1

B1 targetFrench diplomats are usually formal but direct. They appreciate titles like "Mr." or "Ms." and move to professional conversation fairly quickly. Gulf Arab officials expect more formality and ceremony — using "Your Excellency" is required — not optional.

B2 extensionSpeaker A: French diplomats are usually formal but not ceremonial. They appreciate being addressed by their full title or name — "Mr. Moreau" or "Ms. Martin" — but they generally move to professional conversation relatively quickly. They value directness combined with correct form. Speaker B: Gulf Arab officials expect a far more ceremonial opening, and rank is central: "Your Excellency" is obligatory, and the social exchange before business is part of the relationship — not a delay. Speaker D: The practical consequence for a reception officer is that the French visitor measures you by efficiency and the Gulf visitor measures you by attentiveness, and neither reading is wrong — they are simply looking at different evidence of the same professionalism.

Question 2

B1 targetWith the French attaché, a direct greeting works well: "Good morning, Mr. Moreau. Welcome to the Ministry." With the Gulf Arab official, the language is much more ceremonial: "Your Excellency, it is a great honor to welcome you." The language shows more respect for rank.

B2 extensionSpeaker A: With the French attaché, a confident, direct English greeting works well: "Good morning, Mr. Moreau. Welcome to the Ministry. We are pleased to have you." It is formal but efficient. Speaker B: With the Gulf Arab deputy minister, you would use far more elevated language — honour, privilege, gratitude — and you would convey the minister's own greetings before anything practical is mentioned. Speaker C: What is identical in both cases is the information conveyed — who you are, that they are expected, and what happens next. Only the quantity of ceremony around it changes, and judging that quantity correctly is the whole skill.

Question 3

B1 targetWith a French visitor, a firm handshake and direct eye contact show confidence and professionalism. With a Gulf Arab official, the handshake may be gentler and longer — this shows warmth and respect. A male officer should not extend his hand to a female guest first.

B2 extensionSpeaker A: With the French visitor, a firm handshake and direct eye contact are expected and signal confidence and professionalism. Good posture is important. Speaker B: With the Gulf Arab official, the handshake may be gentler and can last longer — in Gulf culture a longer handshake communicates warmth rather than hesitation, and the hand placed briefly over the heart afterwards is a respectful gesture. Follow the guest's lead where gender is concerned. Speaker C: The common rule is to follow the visitor's lead rather than to apply a remembered formula — because individuals vary far more than cultural generalizations suggest, and a visitor who is treated as a type rather than as a person notices immediately.
