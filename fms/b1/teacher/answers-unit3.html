W-AExercise 1 in the book’s key

Pair Discussion – Your Country's Story in English

Question 1

B1 targetThe most important change is that our international engagement has become practical. Agreements are being signed, committees are meeting, and programs are being implemented. Five years ago we talked about intentions; now we report on results.

B2 extensionThe most important change is that our international engagement has moved from declarations to implementation. Diplomatic contacts have been established or upgraded with more than twenty countries, bilateral frameworks have been signed in infrastructure, education, and trade, and joint technical committees are now required to report quarterly. The difference a journalist should be able to verify is that there is now a paper trail of obligations — not only a record of good intentions.

Question 2

B1 targetHonesty and forward momentum are not in conflict. You name the difficulty precisely, then describe what is being done about it and by when. A vague answer sounds like evasion; a precise one sounds like competence.

B2 extensionHonesty and forward momentum are not in conflict — in fact the forward-looking part is only credible if the acknowledgment is specific. The formula I use is three steps: name the difficulty in concrete terms, state the mechanism that is addressing it, and give a timeline or a reporting point. "Implementation is slower than we projected; the joint committees now report quarterly and the first public report is due in March" is both honest and constructive. A general reassurance is neither.

Question 3

sampleMy country's most important message to the world right now is that its institutions are working and verifiable. For example, implementation of the bilateral frameworks is overseen by joint technical committees that are required to report on progress every quarter — which means a partner does not have to take our word for it. Verifiability is the message.

W-BExercise 2 in the book’s key

Group Brainstorm – Questions Foreign Visitors Ask

Question 1

B1 target1. "What is the security situation in the regions?" — Political Affairs — a region-specific, factual answer, never a generalization. 2. "Are the reforms real or only on paper?" — Press and Information Office — name signed agreements and established committees. 3. "What protections exist for foreign investors?" — Economic Affairs — the legal framework and dispute resolution, with a referral to qualified advisers. 4. "How does the returning-citizens program actually work?" — Consular Affairs — who it covers, how applications are processed, what support is provided. 5. "Who monitors the use of international funds?" — Political Affairs — the oversight mechanisms and the reporting requirements.

B2 extension1. "What is the current security picture, region by region?" — Political Affairs Directorate — the answer must be region-specific and sourced to official advisories — because a generalization is both inaccurate and, to a journalist, evidence of evasion. 2. "Are the reforms substantive or presentational?" — Press and Information Office — the credible answer cites specific signed instruments, named committees, and reporting obligations, and acknowledges honestly where implementation has not yet begun. 3. "What legal protection does a foreign investor actually have?" — Economic Affairs Office — explain the investment framework and the dispute resolution mechanism, be clear about which sectors are covered, and direct the visitor to confirm their own position with qualified advisers. 4. "How does the returning-citizens program work in practice, and how many people have actually returned?" — Consular Affairs Section — give the application figure and the return figure separately — because conflating them destroys trust. 5. "Who audits the international funding?" — Political Affairs with Economic Affairs — name the multilateral coordination committee, the quarterly reporting requirement, and the international partners who sit on it. The sixth question, which nobody expects, is what happens to a partner's money if a project stops.

Spoken sample answer
Valentina: Let me give you the five questions you will actually be asked, in the order you will be asked them. First, security — and the only professional answer is region-specific, sourced to the official advisory, with an offer to arrange a detailed briefing. Second, whether the reforms are real: name the instruments, name the committees, name the reporting dates. Third, investor protection: the framework, the dispute mechanism, the sectors, and then a clear recommendation that they take their own legal advice. Fourth, the returning-citizens program, where you must separate applications from completed returns. Fifth, oversight of international funds.
New colleague: And if I do not know the answer to one of them?
Valentina: Then you say so, and you say what you will do about it. "I do not have that figure in front of me. I will have it sent to you by the end of the day, and I will confirm who the right official is for the detail." That is a professional answer. An invented figure is the one mistake you cannot recover from.
New colleague: Does that not look weak in front of a journalist?
Valentina: The opposite. Journalists test figures. The moment one of yours fails, nothing else you say is usable, and they stop coming to you. Being the official whose numbers are always right is worth more than being the official who always has an answer.

Role Cards – Presenting Your Ministry's Programs

Question 1

B1 targetGood morning. I am a Political Affairs Officer at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. My topic today is our regional integration progress. Three agreements have entered into force in the past eighteen months, and customs procedures have been aligned with three neighbouring states. Frameworks on energy and transport have been signed but are still in the early implementation phase. We would value the European Union's technical support in the monitoring stage.

B2 extensionGood morning. On behalf of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, thank you for this first coordination meeting. I am a Political Affairs Officer and my topic is regional integration. Three agreements have entered into force in the past eighteen months, and customs procedures have been aligned with three neighbouring states — that is the part that is operational today. Frameworks on energy interconnection and transport corridors have been signed but implementation has only begun, and I would rather tell you that plainly than present signature as completion. Two areas remain in discussion: labour mobility and mutual recognition of professional qualifications. We would value the European Union's technical support in building the monitoring capacity — because that is where our institutional gap is widest.

Question 2

B1 targetThank you for this opportunity. I am a Consular Affairs Officer at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. My topic is the returning-citizens program. The program covers nationals residing abroad who wish to return, and it is being administered in partnership with the International Organization for Migration. More than forty thousand applications have been received, with priority given to skilled professionals. We would value IOM's support in expanding our document-replacement capacity.

B2 extensionThank you for this opportunity. I am a Consular Affairs Officer and my topic is the returning-citizens program. The program covers nationals residing abroad who wish to return, and it is being administered by the Consular Affairs Section in partnership with the International Organization for Migration. More than forty thousand applications had been received by mid-2025, with priority given to applicants whose professional expertise is needed in medicine, engineering, education, and public administration. I should be clear that applications and completed returns are different figures, and the gap between them is our principal operational challenge. What the ministry needs from international partners is capacity for legal assistance to citizens whose documents were lost abroad — that single bottleneck accounts for most of the delay.

Question 3

B1 targetGood morning, distinguished guests. I am an Economic Affairs Specialist at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. My topic is our national infrastructure investment priorities. Investment frameworks have been established in transport, water, and energy, and a joint technical committee oversees implementation. Legal protections for foreign investors are guaranteed by the investment framework, which includes a dispute resolution mechanism. We look forward to a technical briefing with your delegation.

B2 extensionDistinguished guests, welcome. I am an Economic Affairs Specialist and my topic is national infrastructure investment. Priority sectors are transport, water systems, and energy, and investment frameworks have been established in all three. Implementation is overseen by joint technical committees that report quarterly, which matters to an investor because it means there is a documented record rather than a verbal assurance. Legal protections are guaranteed by the national investment framework and include a defined dispute resolution mechanism; I would encourage your legal advisers to review it directly rather than rely on my summary. A new investment facilitation unit has been created within the ministry as a single point of contact. We look forward to a full technical briefing with your delegation.

1CExercise 6 in the book’s key

Group Discussion – Sensitive Topics and Professional Language

Question 1

B1 targetCareful language acknowledges the question and then gives the most accurate answer available, even when that answer is incomplete. Evasive language avoids the question and fills the space with general statements. The test is whether the listener learns something specific.

B2 extensionCareful language acknowledges the question, names what is known precisely, and is explicit about where knowledge ends. Evasive language substitutes general reassurance for specific content and hopes the questioner will move on. The practical test is simple: after the answer, can the listener write down one new verifiable fact? If yes, the answer was careful. If no, it was evasive, however polite the phrasing — and an experienced journalist will identify that within one sentence. The same test applies to your own answers before you give them.

Question 2

B1 targetThe cleanest redirect is to name the right authority and offer to connect the person to it: "That question falls to our spokesperson's office, and I can facilitate that for you today." This is not evasion because it gives the questioner a route to the answer rather than closing the subject. What makes a redirect look evasive is giving no route at all.

B2 extensionThe cleanest redirect names the correct authority and provides the route: "For a question of that scope I would direct you to the Political Affairs Directorate, and I can arrange that introduction today." Three elements make it professional rather than evasive. You acknowledge the question as legitimate. You identify who can answer it, which shows you know your own institution. And you take on the work of connecting them. A redirect that stops at "that is not my area" is an evasion in everything but wording — because the questioner is left exactly where they started.

Question 3

B1 targetSeveral formulations work well. "That is a real challenge, and here is what is being done about it." "Progress has been slower than we projected; the mechanism to address it is now in place." "I would rather give you the accurate picture than an optimistic one." Each acknowledges the difficulty and then moves forward.

B2 extensionSeveral formulations do this well, and they share a structure: acknowledgment, mechanism, timeline. "That is a genuine challenge — the joint technical committee was established precisely to address it and reports for the first time in March." "Implementation has been slower than projected; the delay is in document replacement and the legal assistance capacity is being expanded now." "I would rather give you the accurate picture than an optimistic one — because you will check it." What makes all three credible is that the acknowledgment is specific enough to be uncomfortable. A difficulty that costs nothing to admit does not buy any trust.

Spoken sample answer
Valentina: This is the phrase you need to master: "That is an important question — let me give you the most accurate and current answer I have." Then give the answer. The phrase does four things: it validates the question, it signals that you are going to answer rather than dodge, it commits you to accuracy, and it commits you to currency, which means you have been following developments. If you then give a substantive answer, the journalist or diplomat registers that you are a serious professional worth engaging with.

3AExercise 13 in the book’s key

Before You Listen: Predict and Discuss

Question 1

sampleThe tone will be formal, measured, and institutional. Passive voice will be used for the achievements — "has been signed," "has been established" — because the point is the event and the institution behind it — not the individual who acted. A spokesperson avoids the first person singular almost entirely, which is itself a signal that the ministry — not the person, is speaking.

Question 2

sampleThe UN coordinator will bring a global, operational perspective and will speak in figures: tonnages, offices opened, households processed. Passive voice will dominate the reporting of results, but the coordinator will switch to active voice for the appeal — "we are currently processing," "cannot succeed without" — because the request needs an agent.

Question 3

sampleThe Ambassador's opening remarks will be the most formal of the four, and the most careful. Expect passive constructions for the agreement itself and active, first-person-plural statements of commitment. The register will be ceremonial but the content will be conditional: support tied to institutional frameworks and transparency.

Question 4

sampleThis speaker will use a warmer, more personal register and almost entirely active voice: "I speak," "I left," "we want to come back." The vocabulary will be concrete rather than institutional, and the effect is urgency. It will sound like a citizen rather than an institution — and that contrast is exactly what gives the excerpt its force.

3BExercise 14 in the book’s key

Listening: Four Official Excerpts

Question 1

sampleExcerpt 1: The ministry spokesperson announces that a comprehensive bilateral cooperation framework has been signed with Germany, covering rural infrastructure, water systems, and educational exchange, with a joint technical working committee established to convene within sixty days. Excerpt 2: The UN Resident Coordinator reports measurable progress in the southern region — forty thousand tonnes delivered, four coordination offices opened, fifteen thousand household applications in process — and states that the programs cannot succeed without continued international funding and political support. Excerpt 3: The German Ambassador frames the agreed framework as a long-term commitment, conditional on strong institutional frameworks, transparent implementation, and civil society participation. Excerpt 4: The community leader speaks for citizens abroad who are ready to return with skills and capital, but who need clarity on the process and protection of their rights.

Question 2

sampleExcerpt 1 (Spokesperson): "has been signed," "has been established," "was reached" — the passive foregrounds the institutional event and avoids naming individuals, which is standard for an official announcement. Excerpt 2 (Coordinator): "progress has been made," "have been delivered," "have been opened," "are being implemented" — passive for results, then active for the appeal. Excerpt 3 (Ambassador): "has been agreed," "can only be achieved when it is supported" — passive to present the agreement as a joint act with no single author. Excerpt 4 (Community leader): almost no passive at all — "I speak," "I left," "we want," "we are ready" — because the speaker is claiming agency rather than reporting an institution's work. The contrast is deliberate on the part of whoever assembled the briefing — because three institutional voices followed by one personal one is far more effective than four of either.

Question 3

B1 targetThe community leader uses active voice and the first person, which creates a striking contrast with the institutional passive voice of the other three speakers. The effect is that this sounds like a real citizen rather than another institution. The direct language also creates more urgency than a passive construction would. It also makes the institutional speakers sound more credible by contrast, rather than less.

B2 extensionThe community leader inverts the grammar of the other three excerpts. Where they say "progress has been made," he says "I left," "we want to come back," "we are ready." Three effects follow. First, agency: he is not reporting on a process, he is a party to it. Second, urgency — "it needs to become something bigger and faster" is a demand, and a passive construction could not carry it. Third, and most important diplomatically, credibility of a different kind: the institutional speakers establish that the machinery exists, and he establishes that it matters to someone. A briefing that contains only the first kind of voice is incomplete, which is presumably why he was invited. D: The reverse is also true: four citizen voices and no institutional one would establish urgency without establishing that anything is being done.

3CExercise 15 in the book’s key

Choose the Correct Official Response

Question 1

sampleCorrect answer: (b) That is an important question. A joint technical monitoring committee has been established, which includes both government representatives and international partner observers. Quarterly implementation reports are published and available through the ministry's official communications. We welcome accountability — it strengthens the programs. — Why (a) is wrong: Refusing the question outright reads as concealment, which is the opposite of what it intends. The passive exists so that a ministry can state what has been established without naming individuals; being careful is not the same as saying nothing.

Question 2

sampleCorrect answer: (b) We take that feedback seriously. The return program has been established and is being expanded, but we acknowledge that processing times need to improve. Applications are handled by the Consular Section, and anyone who has experienced a difficulty is welcome to contact our return assistance office directly — I can provide that contact information. — Why (a) is wrong: A flat denial invites the journalist to produce the evidence, and the exchange is then about the evidence rather than the policy. Acknowledging the concern and describing the mechanism answers the question without accepting the premise.

Question 3

sampleCorrect answer: (a) That is an important and legitimate question. The official position on transparency processes has been stated by the Foreign Minister — I am happy to direct you to that statement. For details beyond the official position, I would recommend submitting a formal inquiry to the spokesperson's office, which I can help facilitate. — Why (b) is wrong: Telling a correspondent to ask something else is itself the story. A legitimate question gets the official position - even when the position is that the matter is still under procedure.

Question 4

sampleCorrect answer: (b) Ratification follows established parliamentary procedure, and that process is ongoing. I cannot speculate on timelines — that would be premature. What I can say is that we are committed to demonstrating through concrete actions — the programs you have heard about today — that the conditions for a durable partnership are being built. — Why (a) is wrong: It promises a date the ministry does not control. Ratification follows parliamentary procedure, and "this year", once said on the record, is a commitment somebody will quote back when the year ends.

4CExercise 19 in the book’s key

Discussion: Staying Honest and Forward-Looking

Question 1

B1 targetDiplomatic language acknowledges a difficulty without exaggerating it and keeps the focus on what is being done. Dishonest language denies a difficulty that exists or claims results that do not. The difference is factual — not stylistic. In practice the difference is also easy to test — because only one of the two survives being checked.

B2 extensionA: Diplomatic language chooses which true thing to say first; dishonest language says something untrue. That is the whole distinction, and it is factual rather than stylistic. B: An example of diplomatic language from our role-play: "Capacity is being built alongside the programs — and that is a genuine constraint — here is the reporting mechanism." It is uncomfortable but accurate. C: An example of dishonest language would be "the institutional capacity is fully in place," which is simply false and would be disproved by the first quarterly report. D: The practical danger is that the second one is easier in the moment and destroys the relationship permanently. A journalist who catches one false claim will never use you as a source again, and a partner who catches one will discount everything in the file. D: The practical discipline, then, is to decide before the meeting which uncomfortable facts you are prepared to state plainly, so that you are not making that judgement under pressure with a microphone in front of you.

Question 2

B1 targetA: "Progress has been slower than we projected, and the mechanism to correct it is now in place." B: "That is a real constraint — here is what is being done about it and by when." C: "I would rather give you the accurate picture than an optimistic one." Each names the difficulty and then moves forward without minimizing it.

B2 extensionA: "Progress has been slower than we projected, and the correction mechanism is now in place with a reporting date." B: "That is a real constraint. The joint technical committee was established precisely to address it and reports for the first time in March." C: "I would rather give you the accurate picture than an optimistic one — because you will check it and I would rather you checked and found me right." Each has the same three parts — specific acknowledgment, named mechanism, verifiable date — and each is usable in a press conference without further authorization.

Question 3

B1 targetIt is correct when the question is genuinely outside your authority or when you do not have an accurate figure. It becomes evasion when you do know the answer, or when you give no route to someone who does. The professional version always includes a next step: who can answer, and by when. What converts it from evasion to professionalism is the second half — not the first.

B2 extensionA: When the question is outside your authority, or when the honest answer is that you do not have an accurate figure in front of you, "I do not have that information" is the only professional response available. An invented figure is unrecoverable. B: It becomes evasion the moment it is used to close a subject you could in fact address, or when it is offered with no route attached. C: The complete form therefore has two halves: the admission and the commitment — "I do not have that figure; I will have it sent to you by the end of the day and I will confirm who the right official is." D: Used that way it actually strengthens your position — because it establishes that the figures you do give are reliable. E: It also protects your colleagues — because an invented figure becomes the ministry's figure the moment it is published.

5BExercise 21 in the book’s key

Reading Comprehension: Discussion Questions

Question 1

B1 targetI think the establishment of the joint technical committees is the most significant development — because it is the only one that creates an obligation. Agreements can be signed and forgotten, but a committee that must report every quarter generates a record. That record is what partners and journalists can check.

B2 extensionA: The re-establishment of diplomatic contacts with more than twenty countries is the headline, but I would argue the quarterly reporting requirement is the substantive change — because it converts political goodwill into a documented obligation. B: I would choose the investment facilitation unit — because a single point of contact is what actually determines whether a pledge becomes a contract. C: My own choice is the returning-citizens program. The others are state-to-state; that one is the ministry dealing directly with individual citizens, and institutions are ultimately judged on how they treat people who have no leverage. D: What all three answers have in common is that none of us chose a signing ceremony, which is probably the right instinct. E: The second common feature is that all three choices are mechanisms rather than events — which is probably what a year of this kind should be judged on.

Question 2

B1 targetThe journalist is partly right, and saying so is the strongest answer available. Signature is not construction. But the frameworks created committees with reporting obligations and timelines, and the first reports are due. The honest answer is: judge us on the March reports — not on the ceremonies — and here is where to find them. What makes this answer work is that the concession comes first; leading with the defence invites them to test it.

B2 extensionA: The honest answer begins by conceding the point: signature is not construction, and a year of framework-building is exactly what the text describes. B: But the frameworks are not empty, and the evidence is specific — joint technical committees were established, quarterly reporting was made mandatory, and an investment facilitation unit was created inside the ministry. Those are the mechanisms by which paper becomes concrete. C: I would then offer the verification route: the first quarterly reports are due in March, and we should be judged on them. D: What I would not do is list the agreements again more emphatically. A skeptical journalist has already counted the agreements; what they are testing is whether we know the difference between signing and delivering. D: And I would give them the reporting date unprompted — because a journalist who has to ask for it writes a different story than one who is handed it.

Question 3

B1 targetThe passive voice creates a tone of institutional gravity. "Frameworks have been signed," "committees have been established" — each sentence emphasizes the event rather than the person responsible. It is almost certainly intentional — because it lets a ministry describe achievements without appearing to claim exclusive credit. The cost is that the same construction can read as evasive to a reader outside the institution.

B2 extensionA: The passive voice gives the text an institutional, almost impersonal gravity — these read as systemic changes rather than as things particular officials did. B: It is certainly intentional, and it serves two purposes at once. It allows the ministry to report achievements without appearing to claim personal credit, which would be unseemly, and it quietly avoids attributing anything to a named individual whose position might change. C: There is a cost, though. Sustained passive voice can read as evasive to an outside audience — which is why the text still needs active sentences at the points where someone must be accountable. D: The community leader's excerpt in Part 3 shows the contrast exactly: one active, first-person voice in a document of passives carries disproportionate force. E: The practical lesson for a drafter is to use the passive for the record and the active for anything where someone has to be accountable.

Question 4

B1 targetThe next phase should focus on demonstrating that the frameworks work. The returning-citizens program needs to be expanded because demand clearly exceeds capacity. And the investment facilitation unit needs to convert pledges into signed contracts.

B2 extensionA: Implementation over signature. The text describes a year of framework-building and program establishment; the next phase must produce evidence that the frameworks actually deliver, which means the quarterly reports have to be substantive and public. B: The returning-citizens program needs capacity expansion most urgently — forty thousand applications against the current processing capability is an unsustainable ratio, and the legal assistance bottleneck is identified in the text itself. C: The investment facilitation unit needs to convert pledges into signed contracts — because a pledge that is never contracted eventually becomes a reputational liability rather than an asset. D: And one thing that is not in the text at all: a public reporting channel for citizens — not just for partners. E: And a published figure for completed returns — not only applications — because that gap is the thing a sceptical reader will look for first.

7DExercise 31 in the book’s key

Practice and Record

Question 1

8EExercise 36 in the book’s key

Discussion: Diplomatic Language and Personal Integrity

Question 1

B1 target"I don't have that information" is the most professional response when the question is genuinely outside your authority, or when you do not have an accurate figure and the alternative is guessing. It becomes evasion when you do know the answer, when you use it to close a subject you are simply uncomfortable with, or when you offer no route to someone who can answer. The complete version always has a second half: who can answer this, and when you will come back.

B2 extensionA: "I don't have that information" is the only professional answer available when the question falls outside your authority, or when you do not have an accurate figure in front of you. An invented number is the one error from which a press relationship does not recover. B: It becomes evasion at the point where it is used to close a subject you could address, or where it is offered with nothing attached. C: The complete form has two halves: the admission and the commitment — "I do not have that figure; I will have it sent to you by the end of the day and I will confirm who the right official is." D: Used consistently it is actually a source of authority rather than a confession of weakness — because it establishes that everything you do assert is checkable. The officials journalists trust are not the ones who always have an answer. E: The habit also protects the ministry — because a figure you invent becomes institutional once it is quoted.

Question 2

B1 targetDiplomatic language is choosing which true thing to say, and in what order. Dishonest language is saying something untrue. A concrete example: "Implementation has been slower than projected and the correction mechanism is now in place" is diplomatic; "implementation is fully on schedule" when it is not is dishonest. The first one is uncomfortable; only the second one is a lie. The test is whether a reader could disprove it with a document.

B2 extensionA: Diplomatic language is a choice about emphasis and sequence among true statements. Dishonest language asserts something false. That is the entire distinction, and it is factual rather than a matter of tone. B: A concrete pair: "Capacity is being built alongside the programs, which is a real constraint — here is the reporting mechanism" is diplomatic. "The institutional capacity is fully in place" is dishonest, and will be disproved by the first quarterly report. C: What makes the distinction hard in practice is that the dishonest version is easier in the room and only expensive later. D: The discipline is to accept the uncomfortable moment. Every official who has lost a press relationship lost it for a sentence that felt safer at the time. E: And the sentence is always short — which is why it slips out before the judgement catches up.

Question 3

B1 targetA: "That is a genuine challenge — the committee established to address it reports for the first time in March." Use this when the difficulty is real and a mechanism exists. B: "Progress has been slower than we projected." Use this when the gap is already visible and denying it would be absurd. C: "I would rather give you the accurate picture than an optimistic one." Use this to open a difficult answer — because it tells the listener what kind of answer is coming.

B2 extensionA: "That is a genuine challenge, and the joint technical committee was established precisely to address it — it reports for the first time in March." Use it when a mechanism genuinely exists; it fails badly if you cannot name one. B: "Progress has been slower than we projected, and the bottleneck is document replacement." Use it when the gap is already visible from outside — because naming it yourself is the only way to keep control of how it is described. C: "I would rather give you the accurate picture than an optimistic one — because you will check it." Use it to open a difficult answer — it signals the register of what follows and buys you the space to be precise. D: All three work because they are specific. The vague equivalents — "there are challenges" — sound like the same move and achieve the opposite.
