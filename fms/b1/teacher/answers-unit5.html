W-AExercise 1 in the book’s key

Pair Discussion – Opening Doors

Question 1

B1 targetMy country has opportunities in infrastructure, energy, and agriculture. Agriculture is the clearest: we have land, water, and a growing domestic market, and processing capacity is still very limited.

B2 extensionMy country has significant potential in infrastructure and transportation, energy — both conventional and renewable — and agriculture and food security. Agriculture is the strongest case: the land and water exist, domestic demand is growing, and almost no value is added locally, so a processing investment captures a margin that currently leaves the country entirely.

Question 2

B1 targetThe biggest challenge is legal certainty. Investors want to know what happens if something goes wrong — not only what is promised. The ministry can address it by providing the legal framework in writing, early, before being asked.

B2 extensionThe most significant challenge is not opportunity but predictability — an investor's first question is what happens if something goes wrong. The ministry can address it concretely: provide the investment law, the free zone regulations, the profit repatriation provisions, and the arbitration clause in writing at the first meeting rather than on request. Sending the documents before they are asked for changes the whole character of the conversation — because it signals that we expect to be checked.

Question 3

sampleMy country has the potential to attract significant investment in agriculture, energy, and transport infrastructure because the demand is domestic and growing, the inputs already exist here, and the regulatory framework now has an arbitration route that an international investor can rely on.

W-BExercise 2 in the book’s key

Group Brainstorm – Your Country's Investment Partners

Question 1

B1 target1. Infrastructure companies — looking for legal certainty and a clear approval timeline. 2. Sovereign funds — looking for scale and a long horizon. 3. Development banks — looking for oversight and measurable impact. 4. Technology companies — looking for a digital regulatory framework. 5. UN agencies — looking for co-design — not commercial return.

B2 extension1. Infrastructure and energy companies — they want legal certainty, a documented approval timeline, and a dedicated case officer; speed of process matters more to them than incentives. 2. Gulf sovereign funds — they want scale, a long investment horizon, and a relationship with the institution rather than with an individual officer. 3. Multilateral development banks — they want oversight mechanisms, measurable development impact, and co-financing partners. 4. Technology and telecommunications companies — they want a digital regulatory framework and clarity on data and licensing. 5. UN agencies — not seeking commercial return at all; they want to co-design facilitation frameworks, which is often handled by the wrong office.

Spoken sample answer
Ambassador Tesfaye: Let us list the partners who will actually come this year, and what each of them is looking for. Dawit, start with the Europeans.
Dawit: Infrastructure and energy.
Dawit: Companies like Richter. What they want is not incentives — it is certainty. A documented timeline, a named case officer, and an arbitration clause they can show their board.
Selamawit: And the Gulf funds? They are the ones asking about agriculture.
Dawit: Scale and horizon. A sovereign fund is thinking in fifteen-year terms, so what they are really assessing is whether the institution is stable — not whether this year's numbers are good.
Selamawit: Development banks?
Dawit: Oversight and impact. They need to show their own boards a measurable development outcome, so they care about the monitoring framework as much as the project.
Selamawit: Technology companies?
Dawit: The digital regulatory framework — licensing, data rules, spectrum. We lose those conversations because nobody in the building can answer the licensing question in one meeting, and they do not come back for a second.
Selamawit: And the UN agencies?
Meron: Those are a different category altogether. UNDP is not investing; it wants to co-design a facilitation framework with us. We keep routing them to Economic Affairs, and they should go to a policy desk.
Dawit: That is a fair point, and it is our own process failing — not theirs.
Selamawit: So five types, five different offices, and five different first documents.
Meron: Which is exactly why the single-window process matters more than any incentive we could offer.

Group Discussion – Matching Investors to Ministry Resources

Question 1

B1 targetKlaus Richter should be handled by the Economic Affairs Office with the Investment Promotion Agency. He needs the investment law, the free zone regulations, the profit repatriation provisions, and the arbitration clause — in writing. He also needs a named case officer and a processing timeline.

B2 extensionKlaus Richter goes to the Economic Affairs Office working directly with the Investment Promotion Agency. What he needs is documentary: the investment law, the free zone regulations, the profit repatriation guarantee, and the arbitration clause, supplied in writing and in advance. He also needs a named case officer and a stated processing target — because his board will not approve against a verbal assurance. His concern is not whether the opportunity is real but whether the process is predictable, and the answer to that is paperwork rather than persuasion.

Question 2

B1 targetMs. Al-Sayed also goes to Economic Affairs, but with the Ministry of Agriculture involved from the first meeting. She needs the water rights framework and the land tenure position in law. Her concerns will be political stability over a long horizon and whether the water guarantee survives a change of government.

B2 extensionMs. Al-Sayed's visit should be handled by Economic Affairs with the Ministry of Agriculture present from the first meeting — because her question is sectoral and nobody in Economic Affairs can answer it alone. She needs the water rights framework, the land tenure position, and the agricultural investment incentives — all in law rather than in policy. Her concerns will be the fifteen-year horizon: political stability, whether the water guarantee is statutory, and whether the institution is reliable independently of the officials she meets. The relationship-building is not a courtesy in her case; it is part of the due diligence.

Question 3

B1 targetMr. Chen is different because UNDP is not investing. He needs a policy counterpart — not an investment officer, and a conversation about framework design rather than about sectors. Routing him through the commercial process wastes his time and ours.

B2 extensionMr. Chen's visit is categorically different: UNDP brings design capacity and convening power — not capital, so the commercial pathway is the wrong one entirely. What he requires is a policy counterpart — someone with the authority to discuss how a facilitation framework would be structured and who would govern it — and a willingness on our side to treat the framework itself as the deliverable. The risk is that we route him through the Investment Promotion Agency, which is built to process applications, and he concludes that the Ministry has not understood the offer.

2EExercise 12 in the book’s key

Speaking Practice

Question 1

B1 targetIf my country completes its first major infrastructure joint venture with a European company, other investors will follow. The first one is the hardest — because it has no precedent to point to.

B2 extensionIf my country successfully completes its first major infrastructure joint venture with a European partner, the effect on everyone else's risk assessment will be larger than the project itself. The first investor has to price the absence of precedent; the second can point to a working case. That is why the first joint venture should be chosen for its chance of visible success rather than for its size.

Question 2

B1 targetIf I could change one thing about the investment process, it would be the number of offices an investor has to visit. A single window would make the biggest difference — because delay is what loses investors.

B2 extensionIf I could change one thing it would be the number of separate offices an investor must deal with before a decision. A fully functional single-window centre would cut processing time more than any tax incentive would increase interest — because what loses an investor is not cost but uncertainty about how long a decision takes. If we had that centre, I believe we would attract substantially more mid-sized investors, who are the ones least able to absorb delay.

Question 3

B1 targetIf Gulf sovereign funds committed to agricultural projects here, the scale would change everything. Their horizon is long enough to build processing capacity — not only to export raw product.

B2 extensionIf Gulf sovereign funds committed seriously to agriculture here, the character of the sector would change rather than just its size. Their investment horizon is long enough to justify processing and storage infrastructure, which commercial investors on a five-year view will not build. The risk — which we should be honest about, is that a land-and-production joint venture at that scale raises water allocation questions that have to be settled in law before the first agreement — not after it.

Question 4

B1 targetIf I prepare a clear briefing document before every investor meeting, the conversation will be shorter and better. I will also send the legal framework before it is requested.

B2 extensionIf I prepare a clear sector briefing and send the legal framework before the meeting rather than after it, the meeting itself becomes a discussion of specifics instead of an exchange of generalities. I am also going to stop estimating processing times. If I do not know the current figure I will say so and send it the same day — because an investor who discovers that one of my numbers was approximate will discount all of them.

sampleThe language will be formal, positive, and reciprocal, with conditional structures used for commitment rather than for hedging: "if you invest here, you will find a partner that respects its commitments." 3CExercise 15 in the book’s key

Choose the Correct Response

Question 1

sampleCorrect answer: (b) If you submit through the Investment Promotion Agency, we will fast-track the process and target a 30-day review. — Why (a) is wrong: It gives a timescale with no condition attached, so the investor cannot act on it. The first conditional is the tool the question is asking for: name the route, and the timescale that follows from taking it.

Question 2

sampleCorrect answer: (b) If you sign a registered agreement, you are protected by an international arbitration clause that guarantees full legal recourse regardless of any domestic changes. — Why (a) is wrong: The investor is not asking for a prediction - he is asking what protects him. "We cannot predict the future" declines a question that has a concrete answer: the instrument that applies if he signs.

Question 3

sampleCorrect answer: (b) If you are investing above a certain threshold, additional incentives may apply. I would like to arrange a meeting with our Investment Promotion Agency to discuss your specific situation. — Why (a) is wrong: It states a flat rule where the book states a condition, and it is inaccurate as well as unhelpful: incentives do vary with the size of the investment, which is precisely what the investor needs to hear.

Question 4

sampleCorrect answer: (b) A letter of intent is a statement of serious intention — it does not commit you to a financial obligation, but it confirms your intent to proceed to feasibility study and signals that both sides will work in good faith toward a final agreement. — Why (a) is wrong: It restates the investor's own words instead of defining the instrument. What he needs to know is what a letter of intent does - and, just as importantly, what it does not bind him to.

4CExercise 19 in the book’s key

Group Discussion: What Makes an Investment Pitch Work?

Question 1

B1 targetThe briefings that worked were the specific ones. A named case officer, a processing target, and a document the investor could take to a board are worth more than any description of potential. The pitches that failed described opportunity without describing process. The difference was always specificity rather than confidence, and specificity is the part that can be prepared in advance.

B2 extensionThe most persuasive briefings answered the question the investor actually asked, which was almost never about opportunity. They named the case officer, stated the processing target, and offered a document that could be shown to a board without translation. The weakest ones described the country's potential at length — which every competing country also does, and left the investor with nothing to take away. An investor who leaves with nothing in their hands has effectively not had the meeting.

Question 2

B1 targetConditional language shows that you understand the investor's decision has conditions attached. "If you submit the application, we will fast-track it" commits to something precise without over-promising. It also makes clear what depends on them and what depends on us.

B2 extensionConditional language lets you make a firm commitment without claiming to control what you do not control. "If the feasibility study confirms viability, we will assign a case officer within thirty days" is precise, honest, and checkable. It also divides the obligations visibly: this is your step, this is ours. An unconditional promise sounds stronger in the room and is weaker on paper — because the investor knows it cannot be guaranteed and begins to discount everything else.

Question 3

B1 targetThe biggest mistake is over-promising to a hesitant investor. A hesitant investor is testing your reliability, so an answer that is too good confirms their doubt rather than removing it. The right response to hesitation is more specificity — not more enthusiasm.

B2 extensionThe biggest mistake is responding to hesitation with enthusiasm. A hesitant investor is not short of encouragement; they are testing whether what you say can be relied on, so an answer that is better than they expected increases their suspicion rather than reducing it. The correct response is more specificity: a date, a clause, a name, an honest acknowledgment of what has not yet been tested. Meron's answer in Part 3 is the model — she conceded that the arbitration framework had never been challenged — and that concession is precisely what made the rest of her answer credible.

5BExercise 21 in the book’s key

Reading Comprehension: Discussion Questions

Question 1

B1 targetTwenty-four countries sending delegations suggests the diplomatic re-engagement is working. Eighty-seven companies means the interest is commercial — not only political. And the presence of the World Bank and the regional development banks signals that multilateral institutions now treat the country as a serious partner.

B2 extensionThe scale of participation tells us three different things. Twenty-four national delegations is a diplomatic result — it reflects the ministry's bilateral work rather than the investment case. Eighty-seven companies and financial institutions is a commercial result, and a more demanding one — because companies send people only when there is a plausible transaction. And the attendance of the World Bank, the African Development Bank, the Islamic Development Bank, and seven UN agencies is an institutional endorsement that private investors read carefully — because multilateral due diligence is slower and more sceptical than their own.

Question 2

B1 targetLetters of intent are significant because they commit both sides to a next step with a named counterpart. They are not investment agreements, but they move a conversation into a process. They also create a record that someone can be held to. And they create an obligation on our own side — because a letter of intent with no study six months later is a visible failure rather than a quiet one.

B2 extensionA letter of intent is not an investment agreement and should not be presented as one. Its significance is procedural: it names the parties, defines the next step — in all three cases a study rather than a commitment — and puts a date against it. That converts a conversation into a process with an owner, which is exactly the stage at which most investment interest is lost. It also creates an internal obligation on our side — because a letter of intent that produces no study six months later is a visible failure rather than a quiet one.

Question 3

B1 targetThe Ministry of Foreign Affairs is not only hosting an event — it is facilitating investment. It brings the investors, the other government ministries, and the development banks into one room. That makes the Ministry a bridge between the country's diplomacy and its development.

B2 extensionThe Ministry's role here goes well beyond representation. It convenes the parties that cannot easily convene themselves — foreign investors, the domestic line ministries, and the multilateral banks — and in doing so it takes on an economic facilitation function rather than a purely diplomatic one. That is a significant institutional shift, and it carries a corresponding risk: a ministry that convenes investment is also accountable for what happens to it afterwards.

Question 4

B1 targetI would recommend that every delegation leave with a named case officer and a scheduled follow-up date. The Forum generates interest well; what it does not do is convert it. A site visit programme is useful, but follow-up is what turns a letter of intent into a study.

B2 extensionI would recommend deliberate conversion rather than more content. Every delegation should leave the Forum with a named case officer, a scheduled follow-up date, and the sector legal framework already in their hands. The 2026 additions — a third day and a site visit programme — will raise interest, but interest is not the constraint; the constraint is what happens in the eight weeks afterwards, when a company's attention moves to the next opportunity. One measurable target would be worth more than a third day: of the letters of intent signed, how many produced a study within six months.

7BExercise 29 in the book’s key

Rhythm in Conditional Sentences — Sound Natural and Confident

Question 1

sampleIF-clause: slight rise on 'application.' Pause. RESULT-clause: firm fall on 'review.' Stress: sub-MIT, ap-pli-CA-tion, FAST-track, re-VIEW. The pause between the clauses is what makes this sound like a commitment rather than a sentence read aloud.

Question 2

sampleIF-clause: slight rise on 'Monday.' Pause. RESULT-clause: fall on 'Tuesday.' Stress: ap-PROVES, MON-day, ar-RANGE, TUES-day. The two days are the content of the sentence, so give each one its own beat and do not run them together.

Question 3

sampleSecond Conditional — hypothetical. IF-clause: rise on 'package.' RESULT-clause: fall on 'interest,' slightly softer than a First Conditional landing — because this is a projection rather than an offer. Stress: re-STRUC-tured, PACK-age, at-TRACT, IN-ter-est.

Question 4

sampleIF-clause: slight rise on 'office.' Pause. RESULT-clause: fall on 'half.' Stress: DED-i-cat-ed, OF-fice, PRO-cess-ing, HALF. Keep the final figure clear — 'by half' is the whole point of the sentence and is the part a listener writes down.

7DExercise 31 in the book’s key

Practice and Record

Question 1

sampleStress: WEL-come, your NAME, your ROLE, the SEC-tor, and the conditional verb. Warm on the welcome, falling on the declarations, rise-then-fall on the conditional. Pace: steady, with a real pause before the next step you are offering.

Question 2

sampleReview each conditional sentence in the recording separately. Did the if-clause rise? Was there a pause before the result? Did the result fall firmly? A conditional delivered flat sounds like a hedge rather than an offer, which is the opposite of the intended effect. Fix one sentence at a time rather than re-recording the whole thing.

Question 3

sampleRhythm test: tap lightly at the start of each stressed syllable while you listen back. If the taps are evenly spaced, the rhythm is working. If they bunch together in the middle of the conditional and then stop, you are rushing the if-clause to get to the result — which is the most common fault and the easiest to fix. Slow the if-clause — not the result.

8AExercise 32 in the book’s key

Understanding Business Culture Differences in Investment Meetings

Question 1

B1 targetGerman business culture is direct and data-driven: specific questions expect specific answers, and decisions follow documentation. Gulf business culture is relationship-driven: trust is established before commitment, and decisions involve senior consultation over a longer period.

B2 extensionGerman business culture is direct, precise, and documentary. The meeting moves to the agenda quickly, specific questions expect specific answers, and the decision follows a review of legal analysis rather than a conversation. Gulf business culture is relationship-driven on a longer timescale: trust must be established before any commitment, decisions involve consultation at senior levels that the visitor may not describe, and the quality of the hospitality is read as evidence about the institution. Neither is slower in substance — a Gulf fund can move very fast once the relationship is settled — but the order of the stages is reversed.

Question 2

B1 targetWith Klaus, the meeting opens briefly and moves to the agenda within two minutes; the language is precise and the answers are specific. With Ms. Al-Sayed, the opening is ceremonial, refreshments come before business, and the specifics arrive only when she signals she is ready. Rushing the second is as damaging as padding the first.

B2 extensionWith Klaus, the meeting opens briefly, moves to the agenda within a few minutes, and succeeds or fails on the precision of the answers. Long courtesies would read as time-wasting. With Ms. Al-Sayed, the opening is ceremonial, hospitality precedes business, and the specifics arrive when she indicates readiness rather than when the agenda says so. The mistake in each direction is symmetrical and equally costly: padding a German meeting looks evasive, and rushing a Gulf meeting looks transactional. The same facts are delivered in both; only the sequence changes.

Question 3

B1 targetIn German business culture, "yes" is a firm, binding commitment and is expected to be honoured precisely. In Gulf business culture, "yes" often signals interest or acknowledgement rather than a final commitment, so you follow up to confirm. Silence in a German meeting usually means the point is being considered; in a Gulf meeting it may signal a reservation expressed politely.

B2 extensionIn German business culture a "yes" is a binding commitment and will be honoured to the letter, which also means it is given carefully and later than you might expect. In Gulf business culture a "yes" frequently signals interest, goodwill, or acknowledgement rather than a final decision — not out of evasiveness but because the decision genuinely rests with a wider circle — so the professional response is a written follow-up that confirms what was understood, phrased as a courtesy rather than a challenge. Silence differs too: in a German meeting it generally means the proposal is being weighed, whereas in a Gulf meeting it may be carrying a reservation that politeness prevents stating, and the right move is to open a door rather than to repeat the proposal.
