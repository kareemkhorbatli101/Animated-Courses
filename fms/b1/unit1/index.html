Part 8 - Can-Do and glossary

1 card
  • I can describe the kinds of foreign visitor my ministry now receives, say what first impression every staff member should aim to make, and carry out a short reception sequence - greeting a visitor, asking who they have come to see, and directing them to the right office.
  • I can use this unit's seven reception and protocol terms correctly, read a visitor's role card to work out which department should receive them and what they need before they proceed, and complete a professional sentence with the right term.
  • I can tell a permanent fact about the ministry from a temporary situation, choose present simple or present continuous for each, correct the most common mistakes between the two, and use both to answer questions about my own work.
  • I can predict what a visitor at the reception desk will say, follow four arrival conversations closely enough to identify each visitor, their purpose and the department they need, choose a correct staff response to an opening line, and write down five reception phrases I can use myself.
  • I can perform a complete reception script from greeting to handover, introduce myself and my role at a formal reception, and say what makes a good first impression and what damages one.
  • I can read a set of ministry staff profiles for each person's role, length of service and current work, discuss who is responsible for what, and use this unit's professional collocations in my own writing.
  • I can recognise the five parts of a professional introduction email and what each one is for, write a short professional bio, and write my own introduction email of 80 to 100 words in a formal register.
  • I can place the stress correctly in this unit's diplomatic vocabulary, link words so that my speech sounds natural rather than word by word, and record a reception dialogue using my own name and position.
  • I can describe how first meetings differ across the cultures my ministry receives, compare greeting, small talk and business-card norms in a table, and adapt my welcome for a European cultural attache and for a senior Gulf Arab official without losing my own professional manner.

The directions and every line of the acted conversations carry Arabic. The questions, the options and the answers are in English on purpose — they are the part of the exercise that has to be done.
