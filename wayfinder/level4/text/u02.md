### 📚 ACADEMIC LANGUAGE

| Word | Meaning | In this unit |
|---|---|---|
| **to allege** | to state without proof | It was alleged that the figures were wrong. |
| **ostensibly** | according to what is claimed, though perhaps not truly | ostensibly a cost-saving measure |
| **to corroborate** | to support with other evidence | No second source corroborated it. |
| **account** *(noun)* | a description of what happened | two accounts of the same meeting |
| **to omit** | to leave out | The vote was omitted from both reports. |

> **Reader's Tip** — A finding and its explanation are two different things, and the explanation
> is usually the writer's. Underline the sentence that reports what was measured. Everything
> around it is argument, and argument can be wrong without the measurement being wrong.

> *Portmere Library Service · media literacy briefing, sheet 1*
>
> 1  We run a weekly session here for people who want to know whether what they are reading is
> 2  true. It is the most-attended thing in the building. This sheet gives the three figures we
> 3  are asked about most, and one warning about how to use them.
>
> 4  **Trust.** Across the markets surveyed, **40 percent** say they trust most news most of the
> 5  time. That figure has not moved for three years. It is not collapsing and it is not
> 6  recovering; it is flat, which is a different story from the one usually told about it.
>
> 7  **The range inside the average.** The aggregate hides enormous variation. Trust runs from
> 8  **67 percent in Finland** to **22 percent in Greece and Hungary**. A single global figure
> 9  for trust in news is about as useful as a single global figure for rainfall.
>
> 10 **Avoidance.** Four people in ten now say they sometimes or often avoid the news, up from
> 11 **29 percent in 2017**. That is the figure that has moved. Note that this is selective
> 12 avoidance — turning away from particular subjects or at particular times — and not a
> 13 withdrawal from information altogether.
>
> 14 **The warning.** These are survey answers. They measure what people say about their own
> 15 behavior, which is not the same as their behavior, and people are consistently poor
> 16 witnesses to their own media use. The direction of travel is reliable. The exact figure is

> 18 What the figures do establish is this: distrust is not rising, and avoidance is. Those are
> 19 not the same problem, and the second one is the one this library can do something about.

### 1 · The past passive

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W5-02-06%%

> **Priya:** "The library was closed." Closed by whom?
> **Linh:** By the council.
> **Priya:** The council is nine people and it was four to three. Two of them weren't there.
> **Linh:** So who closed it?
> **Priya:** Four people closed it. One of them changed her mind in the room. That's the story and
> it isn't in either report.

*Is a sentence that is true but leaves out who acted a form of dishonesty, or just shorthand?*

**1B  Underline the passive forms in 1A. Which sentence restores the agent?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Past passive** | was / were + past participle | The decision **was taken** on Tuesday. |
| **Negative** | wasn't / weren't + participle | The vote **wasn't recorded**. |
| **Question** | Was / Were + subject + participle | **Was** the figure **checked**? |
| **With the agent** | … **by** + person | It **was agreed by four members**. |
| **Continuous** | was / were being + participle | It **was being discussed** when I left. |

> **Grammar Note** — The past passive is the natural tense of institutional writing because it
> puts the event first and the actor last, or nowhere. That is often reasonable: *the meeting was
> postponed* rarely needs a name. It stops being reasonable at the moment somebody has to answer
> for the decision, and that is exactly when the passive is used most.

> **Printed:** "The library was closed to save money."

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Real Lives — Priya
>
> I was at the meeting. I have read both reports of it and I cannot point to a false sentence in
> either, and I would still not let either of them stand as an account of what happened.
>
> The first said the library was closed by the council. That is true in the way that *the country
> voted* is true. The council is nine people. Four were in favor, three were against, two were
> not there, and one of the four said out loud, in the room, that she had come in intending to
> vote the other way. None of that is secret. All of it is in the minutes, which nobody had read
> because the minutes were not published for ten days and both pieces were written on the
> Wednesday.
>
> The second report was worse, and it took me a day to see why. It said a reduction in opening
> was agreed as part of a review. Every word of that can be defended and not one word of it can
> be checked. No number, no names, no date for the review, no way to go and look.
>
> So I wrote one sentence and sent it to both of them: *Saturday opening was ended by four votes
> to three, with two members absent.* One printed it as a correction, which it was not, and the
> other did not reply. I have stopped minding about that. What I mind about is that I was the
> only person in the room who was asked nothing, and I was the only person in the room who was
> not being paid to be there.

> **Writer's Tip** — When you want to show that a report is inadequate, do not argue with it.
> Write the sentence it should have contained, in the same number of words, and put the two side
> by side.
