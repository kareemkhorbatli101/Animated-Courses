### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — The right answer to a question about a figure is sometimes *we cannot tell
> from this*. That is not a failure to understand; it is the understanding. Practice saying it,
> because it is the sentence that stops bad numbers traveling.

%%FIG y11-f5 | A four-page briefing: a cover, a page with one figure printed very large and a blank space beneath it where a base line would go, a page with three alternative bases worked out side by side, and a final page with a method note and a source line.%%

> ## A number we will not repeat
> *Portmere Advice Center · consumer briefing, sheet 7*
>
> 1  Last sheet we said we would not quote one figure from a large consumer survey. We have been
> 2  asked about it eleven times since, which is more than we were asked about the ones we did
> 3  quote, so this sheet is about that figure and about why *we cannot tell* is a real answer.
>
> 4  **The figure.** Published summaries report that unfair practices were common, with **fake
> 5  reviews at 66 percent** and misleading discounting at 61 percent.
>
> 6  **The missing half.** Sixty-six percent **of whom**? The summaries we can reach do not say.
> 7  There are at least three candidates and they are not close to each other:
>
> 8  *If the base is all consumers surveyed*, then two people in three have met a fake review and
> 9  the problem is near-universal.
>
> 10 *If the base is consumers who shop online*, the finding is narrower and says nothing about
> 11 anybody else.
>
> 12 *If the base is consumers who reported encountering any unfair practice*, then it is a
> 13 ranking — fake reviews are the most common unfair practice **among people who met one** — and
> 14 it tells you nothing at all about how many people that is.
>
> 15 **Why we care.** The third reading is the weakest and it is the one most consistent with how
> 16 such surveys are usually built. If it is right, every poster using this figure to say that
> 17 two thirds of shoppers see fake reviews is making a claim the survey never made.
>
> 18 **What to do.** When somebody quotes it, ask what it is a percentage of. We have asked eleven
> 19 times and been told eleven times that the person does not know. That is not an argument we
> 20 have won. It is a sentence we have stopped being afraid of.
>
> *Figures: European Commission, Consumer Conditions Scoreboard 2025. The base for the 66 percent
> figure is not stated in the published summaries available to us.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Public complaint works differently in different places. In some, a bad review is ordinary
feedback and neither side takes it personally; in others it is a public shaming and the response
is expected to be a defense of honor. Platforms are built on the first assumption and used by
people living in the second, and the result is replies that read as wildly disproportionate to
the people who wrote the policy.

**Discuss:** in your experience, what is a public complaint for? What would a reasonable reply
look like where you come from?
