Ms Nkemelu has put four sentences on the board. They say the same thing. One of them would get
you a formal warning, one would get you ignored, one would get you an answer, and one would get
you laughed at — and which is which depends entirely on who you are speaking to.

### 1 · Four registers

**1A  Read the four sentences. Then say each one aloud, in the voice it belongs to.**

> **1** Oi — this is broken.
> **2** Sorry, I think there's something wrong with this.
> **3** This isn't working. Could somebody take a look at it?
> **4** The unit appears to be defective and I should be grateful if it could be inspected.

**1B  Listen. Four speakers, one situation. Match each to a sentence above.** %%AUD W5-00-01%%

| | Who is speaking | Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| a | to a close friend | |
| b | to a stranger at a counter | |
| c | to a colleague they know well | |
| d | in a written complaint | |

**1C  Discuss in threes.**

1. Which of the four would you use most often? Which have you never used?
2. Sentence 4 is grammatically perfect and would be strange in a shop. Why?
3. Is there a register in your first language that English has no equivalent for?

### 2 · What register is made of

**2A  Study the chart.**

%%FIG y00-f2 | A four-column comparison chart of register markers: informal, neutral, formal and academic, with rows for typical verbs, typical nouns, contractions, and who the sentence is addressed to.%%

| | Informal | Neutral | Formal / written |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Verb** | get, put up with, find out | receive, tolerate, discover | obtain, tolerate, ascertain |
| **Noun** | a go, a look | an attempt, an inspection | an attempt, an examination |
| **Contractions** | always | usually | never |
| **The person** | you, I | you, I | the applicant, this office |

**2B  Rewrite each sentence one step more formal.**

1. I'll have a go at it tomorrow. → ______________
2. We never found out who did it. → ______________
3. They put up with it for months. → ______________
4. Can you have a look? → ______________

**2C  Talk about it.** *With a partner.* The formal column removes the person from the sentence.
When is that useful, and when is it a way of avoiding responsibility?

### 3 · The tense system, reviewed

**3A  Say what each sentence means, and when it happened.**

1. I work there.
2. I'm working there until June.
3. I worked there for two years.
4. I was working there when it closed.
5. I've worked there since 2024.
6. I've been working there all morning.
7. I had worked there for a year before they told me.
8. I'd been working there for a year when they told me.

**3B  Listen. Eight speakers. Write the number of the sentence each one matches.**
%%AUD W5-00-02%%

**3C  Numbers 7 and 8 are new.** Both put one past event before another past event. With a
partner, say which of the two emphasizes the *activity* rather than the *fact*, and how you can
tell.

> **Grammar Note** — The past perfect (*had worked*) is not a more past past. It exists to fix
> the order of two events when the order is not obvious from the sentence. If you say *When I
> arrived, they left*, they left after you got there. If you say *When I arrived, they had left*,
> they were already gone. Both are correct English and they describe different evenings.

### 4 · What this book will ask of you

**4A  Read, then discuss in your group.**

> This book will ask you to pass things on. Somebody will tell you something and you will have to
> tell somebody else, accurately, without adding what you assumed and without dropping the part
> that was inconvenient.
>
> It will also ask you to read three numbers that are harder to use than they look. One has a base
> that nobody states. One is six years old and is still the best figure anybody has. One is
> repeated everywhere by people who have never asked what it counts.

1. Has anybody ever repeated something you said in a way that was accurate and still wrong?
2. What did they do to it?
3. What would you have needed them to add?
