### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Very large numbers are built, not counted. When you meet one, look for the
> word that tells you how it was made — *estimated*, *modeled*, *projected* — and then ask what
> it was built out of. A number with no visible construction is a number you cannot defend.

%%FIG y09-f5 | A four-page briefing: a cover, a page with one very large figure and beneath it a short list of what it was built from, a page with a part-whole diagram of working-age mental disorder, and a final page with a method and date note and a source line.%%

> ## Twelve billion days
> *Portmere Occupational Health Partnership · briefing note 2*
>
> 1  This is the figure that appears on every poster in every staff room, and most of the people
> 2  quoting it could not tell you what it counts. It is worth knowing, because it is a good
> 3  figure and a badly used one.
>
> 4  **The figure.** An estimated **12 billion working days** are lost every year to depression
> 5  and anxiety, at a cost put at about **one trillion US dollars** a year in lost productivity.
> 6  Separately, **15 percent of working-age adults** were estimated to have a mental disorder in
> 7  2019.
>
> 8  **What it is built from.** It is a modeled estimate: prevalence data, employment data and
> 9  assumptions about days lost per case, combined. Nobody counted twelve billion days. The
> 10 construction is published and it is reasonable; it is still a construction.
>
> 11 **The date.** The underlying work was published in **2022** and the prevalence figure refers
> 12 to **2019**. It is the current official figure. It is not a figure for this year, and the
> 13 period it describes includes the year before a pandemic that changed a great deal.
>
> 14 **What it does not tell you.** It does not tell you how many of those days were caused by
> 15 work. Depression and anxiety have many causes and the figure counts days lost from work, not
> 16 days caused by it. A poster that puts this number under the heading *workplace stress* has
> 17 made a claim the figure does not support.
>
> 18 **What we use it for.** One thing only: to establish scale. When a manager says they have
> 19 never had a case on their team, this figure is how you say, politely, that they have.
>

**1B  Underline the three advice forms in 1A. Which one has a consequence attached?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Weight | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **should** | ordinary advice | You **should** mention it. |
| **ought to** | the same, slightly more moral | You **ought to** mention it. |
| **had better** | advice **with a consequence** | You'**d better** mention it, or it goes on the review. |
| **shouldn't** | advice against | You **shouldn't** leave it. |
| **had better not** | warning against | You'**d better not** leave it. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Had better* is not a politer *should*. It carries an implied *or else*, and
> it is always about the near future. Used to a stranger or upwards in a hierarchy it sounds like
> a threat, because it is one. Between friends it is what friends are for.

### 2 · Gerund or infinitive, when both are possible

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Verb | + gerund | + infinitive |
|---|---|---|
| **stop** | stop **working** = give it up | stop **to work** = pause in order to |
| **remember** | remember **locking** it = a memory | remember **to lock** it = don't forget |

| **regret** | regret **telling** you = I told you | regret **to tell** you = bad news coming |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Whether you tell an employer about a health problem is a calculation, not a value. In systems with
strong protection and a culture of disclosure, telling is sensible and withholding looks like
concealment. In systems where disclosure costs you shifts, telling is how careful people lose
work. Advice that assumes the first and is given to somebody living in the second is worse than
no advice, because it sounds authoritative.

**Discuss:** what happens where you work to somebody who discloses a health problem? How do you
know?
