### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Every percentage is a percentage of something. When a figure is quoted
> without its base — *66 percent reported fake reviews* — the first question is not whether it is
> true. It is *sixty-six percent of whom*.

%%FIG y08-f5 | A four-page briefing: a cover, a page of three figures each with a base line printed beneath it, a page with one figure whose base line is blank and marked with a query, and a final page with a method note and a source line.%%

> ## Sixty-six percent of whom?
> *Portmere Advice Center · consumer briefing, sheet 4*
>
> 1  A large consumer survey was published this year and three of its figures are already being
> 2  quoted at us across this desk. Two of them are usable. The third is the one everybody quotes
> 3  and it is the one we cannot use, and the reason is worth a page.
>
> 4  **The usable ones.** **Seventy percent** of consumers agree that retailers and service
> 5  providers respect consumer rights — a higher figure than most people in this building
> 6  expected. Separately, problems with online purchases are reported as **over sixty percent
> 7  more likely** than with buying in person, and **45 percent** of consumers say they have
> 8  encountered an online scam.
>
> 9  **The one we cannot use.** The published summaries say that unfair practices were common,
> 10 with **fake reviews at 66 percent** and misleading discounting at 61 percent. Sixty-six per
> 11 cent of what? Of all consumers surveyed? Of consumers who shopped online? Of consumers who
> 12 reported encountering any unfair practice at all? The summaries we can reach do not say, and
> 13 those three bases would give three completely different findings from the same number.
>
> 14 **Why this is not pedantry.** If the base is all consumers, two people in three have seen a
> 15 fake review and the problem is universal. If the base is people who already reported seeing
> 16 something unfair, the figure tells you what the most common unfair thing is among people who
> 17 saw one, and says nothing about how many people that is.
>
> 18 **What we do.** We quote the first two figures and we do not quote the third. When somebody
> 19 quotes it at us, we ask them what it is a percentage of, and so far nobody has known.
>
> *Figures: European Commission, Consumer Conditions Scoreboard 2025.*

<sub>**base** — the group a percentage is calculated from · **self-selecting** — made up of people
who chose to take part · **pedantry** — excessive concern with small details</sub>

### 2 · Adverbs of degree, and *so* / *such … that*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **absolutely / completely** + extreme adjective | It was **absolutely useless**. |
| **very / really** + ordinary adjective | It was **very slow**. |
| **quite / fairly / rather** | It was **rather** worse than I expected. |
| ***so* + adjective + *that*** | It was **so slow that** I rang twice. |
| ***such* + (a) + adjective + noun + *that*** | It was **such a slow process that** I gave up. |

> **Grammar Note** — *So* goes with an adjective or an adverb; *such* goes with a noun phrase.
> *So slow that …* and *such a slow process that …* say the same thing, and you cannot mix them.
> *Absolutely* does not go with ordinary adjectives: *absolutely slow* is not English, because
> *absolutely* intensifies words that are already absolute.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How you complain is read very differently in different places. In some systems a raised voice is
how you signal that something is serious, and staying calm means you are not really bothered. In
others a raised voice ends the conversation and the quiet person gets the remedy. Neither is
better; both are learned; and the person who learns the first system and then moves gets a
reputation before they get a refund.

**Discuss:** how does complaining work where you come from? What did you have to unlearn?
