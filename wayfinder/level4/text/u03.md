### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A rights summary has two halves: what you are entitled to, and the exception
> that removes it. Find the exception first. It is usually one sentence, usually near the end,
> and it decides everything.

%%FIG y03-f5 | A four-page passenger information leaflet: a cover, two pages of numbered clauses with one sentence boxed, and a final page with a short figure panel and a source line.%%

> ## If your service is canceled
> *Portmere Transport Partnership · passenger information, sheet 3*
>
> 1  **What you are entitled to.** If your service is canceled or delayed beyond the published
> 2  threshold, you may choose between a refund of the unused portion, rerouting at the earliest
> 3  opportunity, or rerouting on a later date of your choosing. That choice is yours and not the
> 4  carrier's, and staff are not permitted to make it for you.
>
> 5  **Duty of care.** Where you are stranded overnight, the carrier must provide meals and
> 6  accommodation in proportion to the wait, and transport between the station and the
> 7  accommodation. This applies regardless of the cause. It is not discretionary and it is not
> 8  a gesture of goodwill.
>
> 9  **Compensation.** Compensation for the delay itself is separate from a refund and is paid in
> 10 addition to it. It is not paid automatically by every carrier. You generally have to claim,
> 11 and the time limit for claiming is shorter than most people assume.
>
> 12 **The exception.** Compensation may be refused where the cancellation was caused by
> 13 extraordinary circumstances outside the carrier's control. Duty of care is **not** removed by
> 14 that exception. Carriers and passengers disagree most often about what is deemed
> 15 extraordinary, and the answer is narrower than carriers usually suggest.
>
> 16 **Why we print this.** Awareness of these rights rose from **14 per cent in 2019 to 30 per
> 17 cent in 2024**. Even now, only **5 per cent** of people say they feel very well informed, and
> 18 a further 25 per cent fairly well informed. Seven people in ten do not know what they are
> 19 owed, which is a figure the industry is aware of and has had five years to improve.
>
> *Figures: Eurobarometer survey on passenger rights, 2024.*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Direct | Reported | What shifts |
|---|---|---|
| "There **is** a coach." | She said there **was** a coach. | present → past |
| "It **left** at six." | She said it **had left** at six. | past → past perfect |
| "We **will** rebook you." | He said they **would** rebook me. | will → would |
| "**Come** back at nine." | She told me **to come** back at nine. | imperative → to + base |
| "**Are** you traveling on?" | He asked **whether I was** traveling on. | question → statement order |

> **Grammar Note** — *Say* takes no person: *she said that*. *Tell* takes a person: *she told me
> that*. You cannot *say me* anything and you cannot *tell* without a listener. This is the most
> frequent error at this level and it survives because the meaning is never in doubt.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

In some places, passing on what you have heard is how a crowd takes care of itself, and the
person who says *I'd rather you heard that from staff* is being unhelpful. In others, repeating
unverified information is seen as the thing that makes a bad evening worse. Both views come from
real experience: one of a system that tells you nothing, and one of a system that does.

**Discuss:** which system did you grow up with? What do you do now when somebody in a queue tells
you something?
