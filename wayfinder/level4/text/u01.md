### 📚 ACADEMIC LANGUAGE

These nine appear constantly in reports, policies and appraisals, and almost never in
conversation.

| Word | Meaning | In this unit |
|---|---|---|
| **to constitute** | to be; to make up | Three facts do not constitute a judgement. |
| **criterion / criteria** | the standard a decision is measured against | Which criterion is being applied? |
| **to demonstrate** | to show by evidence | She demonstrated the skill twice. |
| **attribute** *(noun)* | a quality somebody has | Reliability is an attribute, not an achievement. |
| **to perceive** | to see or understand in a particular way | How is the behavior perceived? |
| **subsequent** | coming after | in subsequent conversations |
| **to assert** | to state firmly | She asserted that the record was wrong. |
| **inherent** | built in; part of the thing itself | an inherent difficulty in the form |
| **nonetheless** | in spite of that | The record is accurate; nonetheless it misleads. |

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A forecast about the future is always a statement about the present: it tells
> you what the people surveyed currently believe. Read the method line before the headline. If
> the method says *employers were asked*, the finding is about employers, not about jobs.

%%FIG y01-f5 | A four-page report summary: a cover with a title and a year, a page carrying three large figures with short captions, a page with a grouped bar chart of rising and falling skills, and a final page with a method note and a source line.%%

> ## What employers say they will need
> *Portmere Employment Partnership · briefing for advisers · annual skills note*
>
> 1  Every January we are asked the same question by clients and by our own board: what skills
> 2  should people be learning? The most useful public answer comes from a survey of more than a
> 3  thousand large employers across fifty-five economies, and it is worth reading carefully,
> 4  because what it establishes is narrower than what it is usually quoted as establishing.
>
> 5  **The headline.** Employers expect that **39 per cent** of workers' core skills will have
> 6  changed by 2030. That is a large figure and it is **down** from 44 per cent in the previous
> 7  edition two years earlier, which is not how it was reported anywhere.
>
> 8  **What is rising.** Analytical thinking remains the single most-named core skill: seven
> 9  employers in ten call it essential. The largest increases in rated importance since the
> 10 previous survey are leadership and social influence, up 22 percentage points, and resilience,
> 11 flexibility and agility, together with AI and big data, each up 17 points.
>
> 12 **What is falling.** Physical abilities show a net decline in rated importance for the first
> 13 time since the survey began. That is a sentence worth sitting with if your working life has
> 14 been built on what your body can do.
>
> 15 **What this does not establish.** The survey asked employers what they expect. It did not
> 16 measure what happened. Employers have been wrong before, in both directions, and a forecast
> 17 that falls from 44 to 39 in two years is evidence that the forecasters are revising, not that
> 18 the world is calming down. Use it to decide what to learn. Do not use it to tell somebody
> 19 their job is safe.
>
> *Figures: World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025.*

**to establish** — to prove or settle beyond doubt</sub>

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Action verb** | She **is working** late. | happening now, or around now |
| **Stative verb** | I **know** the answer. | a state, not an activity |
| **Stative, no continuous** | *I am knowing* ✗ | states do not progress |
| **Some do both** | She **is being** difficult. | behaving that way **today** |
| | She **is** difficult. | that is what she is like |

> **Grammar Note** — Common stative verbs: *know, believe, understand, mean, belong, own, need,
> want, prefer, seem, contain*. Verbs of the senses — *see, hear, taste, smell* — are stative
> when they describe perception and active when they describe an action: *I see it* (perception)
> against *I'm seeing the consultant on Tuesday* (an appointment).

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

In some workplaces a review is a conversation and you are expected to argue your corner; silence
is read as agreement and agreement is binding. In others the review is a ceremony around a
decision already taken, and arguing marks you as somebody who does not understand how things
work. People who learn the first system and then move into the second are often described as
difficult; people who do the reverse are often described as having no ambition.

**Discuss:** which is it where you work now? What happened the first time you got it wrong?
