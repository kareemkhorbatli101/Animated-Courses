### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A report usually contains a forecast and a recommendation, and they are
> written by the same people in the same voice. The forecast can be checked one day. The
> recommendation cannot, and it is always the part that gets quoted.

%%FIG y12-f5 | A four-page report summary: a cover, a page showing a hundred small figures with fifty-nine shaded and eleven of those marked differently, a page with a bar of employer responses, and a final page with a method note and a source line.%%

> ## Fifty-nine in a hundred
> *Portmere Employment Partnership · briefing for advisers, sheet 3*
>
> 1  The most-quoted figure in workforce planning this year is a projection, and the way it is
> 2  constructed is unusually clear, so it is worth reading as an example of how to read one.
>
> 3  **The projection.** Of every **hundred workers**, **fifty-nine** are projected to need
> 4  reskilling or upskilling by 2030. Of those fifty-nine, **eleven** are judged unlikely to
> 5  receive it — which the report translates as over **120 million workers** at medium-term risk
> 6  of redundancy.
>
> 7  **What has already happened.** Half of workers have now had some training, reskilling or
> 8  upskilling, up from **41 per cent** two years earlier. That is a measurement, not a
> 9  projection, and it is the number we would quote if we could only have one.
>
> 10 **What employers say they will do.** **Eighty-five per cent** name upskilling as their main
> 11 response to change, and **63 per cent** name skill gaps as the largest barrier to
> 12 transformation. Those are statements of intention and perception, and they have the status
> 13 of intentions.
>
> 14 **The three kinds of sentence.** This briefing contains a projection, a measurement and a
> 15 statement of intention, and they are not equally useful to somebody sitting in front of you.
> 16 The measurement tells you what is happening. The projection tells you what a large group of
> 17 employers currently expect. The intention tells you what they would like to be seen to say.
>
> 18 **What we do with it.** We use the measurement to tell people that half their peers have had
> 19 training and they may ask for it too. We do not use the projection to frighten anybody,
> 20 because a projection is not a thing that has happened to them.
>
> *Figures: World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025.*

### 1 · Gerunds after prepositions

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W5-12-06%%

*Why is the useful sentence the fourth one, and not the first?*

**1B  Underline every preposition followed by a verb in 1A. What form does the verb take?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **preposition + gerund** | before **finishing** · without **asking** · after **reading** |
| **adjective + preposition + gerund** | good **at finishing** · interested **in learning** |
| **verb + preposition + gerund** | think **about applying** · insist **on knowing** |
| **noun + preposition + gerund** | the point **of asking** · a way **of putting** it |
| ***to* as a preposition** | committed **to doing** · look forward **to hearing** |

> **Grammar Note** — The last row is the trap. In *committed to doing*, *look forward to seeing*
> and *object to being asked*, *to* is a preposition, not part of an infinitive, so it is
> followed by *-ing*. The test: can you put a noun after it? *Committed to the plan* works, so
> *committed to doing* is right.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Asking your manager for criticism is normal in some workplaces and alarming in others. In systems
where feedback flows downward only, asking upward can look like a complaint about the manager's
communication. In systems where it is expected, not asking looks like a lack of interest in your
own development. The same four-word question is read as initiative in one building and as a
challenge in the next.

**Discuss:** what happens where you work? Would you ask the question in this unit, and of whom?
