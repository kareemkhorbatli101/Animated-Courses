### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A participation figure is always a fraction, and both halves can move. When
> turnout falls, find out whether fewer people voted or whether more people became eligible. They
> are different events and they are reported identically.

%%FIG y10-f5 | A four-page briefing: a cover, a page with a line chart falling from 65.2 to 55.5 per cent, a page with a short comparison of in-country and diaspora turnout, and a final page with a definitions box and a source line.%%

> ## Fifty-five point five
> *Portmere Civic Forum · briefing for community organizations*
>
> 1  We are asked this at every meeting, usually by somebody who has just been told that nobody
> 2  cares any more. The figure is real, the fall is real, and the conclusion people draw from it
> 3  is not supported by it.
>
> 4  **The figure.** Average global voter turnout fell from **65.2 per cent in 2008 to 55.5 per
> 5  cent in 2023** — a fall of almost ten percentage points in fifteen years. That is a large
> 6  movement for a measure of this kind, and it is consistent across most regions.
>
> 7  **What a turnout figure is.** It is votes cast as a share of those eligible. Both halves can
> 8  change. Where the eligible population grows faster than the number of people voting — younger
> 9  populations, expanded franchise, more registration — turnout falls even if the same number of
> 10 people turn up. Some of this fall is that.
>
> 11 **The figure underneath.** Across 29 elections in 2024, turnout among voters living abroad
> 12 was about **55 per cent**, against an in-country average of **60 per cent**. People who had
> 13 to post a form from another country voted at close to the rate of people who walk to a school
> 14 hall. That is not the behavior of a population that has stopped caring.
>
> 15 **What follows for us.** If participation has fallen partly because the denominator grew, the
> 16 response is registration and access, not exhortation. Telling people their vote matters is
> 17 cheap and has been tried. Finding out which half of the fraction moved is harder and tells
> 18 you what to do.
>
> *Figures: International IDEA, Global State of Democracy.*

others · **denominator** — the bottom half of a fraction</sub>

### 1 · Indirect questions

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W5-10-06%%

> **Adeel:** Has the decision already been taken?
> **Officer:** I can't answer that.
> **Adeel:** Could you tell me whether the decision has already been taken?
> **Officer:** … I can't answer that either, but I notice you asked it twice.

*Did Adeel learn anything? If so, what, and would it stand up anywhere?*

**1B  Underline the direct question and the indirect one. What changes in the word order?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|
| **When does** it close? | Could you tell me **when it closes**? |
| **Has** it **been** decided? | Do you know **whether it has been decided**? |
| **Who reads** the responses? | I'd like to know **who reads** the responses. |
| **Why is** it online only? | Can I ask **why it is** online only? |
| **How many have** responded? | Do you know **how many have** responded? |

> **Grammar Note** — After an indirect opener the question becomes a statement: no inversion, no
> *do* or *does*. *Could you tell me when does it close* is the commonest error at this level and
> it comes from translating the direct question whole. The giveaway is a second auxiliary in one
> sentence.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Asking an official a question in public ranges from ordinary to dangerous depending on where you
have lived. In some places it is what public meetings are for and officials expect it. In others

sits silently through a consultation may be uninterested, or may be behaving exactly as they
learned to.
