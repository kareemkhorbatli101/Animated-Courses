# Unit 6 · My Week
%%SUB Four jobs, two shifts and one evening class%%
%%GOALS

Mei's week has no normal days in it. She has placement shifts at the hospital, lectures at the
college, and a job at the weekend. When somebody asks what she does on Tuesdays, the honest answer
takes four sentences and usually ends with *it depends*.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — In a staff notice, look for the words **must**, **may** and **will**. *Must*
> is a rule, *may* is permission, and *will* is a promise the employer is making to you.

%%FIG v06-f5 | A staff notice about shifts and breaks: four short paragraphs about the rota being published, swapping shifts, breaks, and recording hours, with a line at the bottom about who to ask.%%

> ## Shifts, breaks and hours
> *Portmere General Hospital · Student placements*
>
> The rota **will** be published **two weeks** in advance. Once it is published it does not change
> without your agreement.
>
> You **may** swap a shift with another student. Both of you **must** tell your supervisor before
> the swap, not after.
>
> On a shift of six hours or more you **must** take a **30-minute** break. The break is unpaid. If
> you cannot take it, write it on your sheet that day.
>
> Record your hours **on the day**. We cannot pay for hours that appear three weeks later with no
> note.
>
> Questions: ask your supervisor, not another student.

### 1 · The simple present

**1A  Listen to the conversation.** %%AUD W2-06-05%%

> **Priya:** What do you actually do on a Tuesday?
> **Mei:** I start at seven at the hospital. I finish at three.
> **Priya:** And then?
> **Mei:** Then I sleep for an hour and I go to the library.
> **Priya:** Every Tuesday?
> **Mei:** Most Tuesdays. Sometimes the shift runs late and I don't go.
> **Priya:** When do you eat?
> **Mei:** I eat on the bus. Don't tell my mother.

**Your opinion:** Priya asks *"When do you eat?"* — not *"Are you tired?"* Which is the better
question, and why?

**1B  Look at the conversation again. Underline every simple present verb.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| I / you / we / they | **start** / **don't start** | at seven. |
| he / she / it | **start*s*** / **doesn't start** | at seven. |
| **Do** you **finish** at three? | Yes, I do. / No, I don't. | |
| **What time does** she **finish**? | At three. | |

> **Grammar Note** — The *-s* is only for *he*, *she* and *it*, and it disappears after *doesn't*
> and after *does … ?* — because the *s* has moved into *does*.

**1D  Practice with a partner. Change the subject.**

2. They finish at three. → (he) ______________
3. We don't work on Sunday. → (she) ______________
4. Do you study in the evening? → (he) ______________
5. He eats on the bus. → (they) ______________

### 2 · Adverbs of frequency

**2A  Study the chart.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **always · usually · often · sometimes · rarely · never** | before the main verb: *I **often** work late.* |
| but **after** *be* | *I am **always** tired.* |
| **every day · twice a week · once a month** | at the end: *I cook **most days**.* |
| **How often** do you …? | Every day. / About twice a week. |

> **Grammar Note** — *never* is already negative. *I don't never go* is not English; *I never go*
> is.

**2C  Practice with a partner.** Say six sentences about a third person. Your partner listens for

> **Why this matters:** dropping the *-s* is the single most common thing that makes fluent
> speakers sound like learners. It carries almost no meaning, and people notice it anyway.

### 3 · The conversation

**3A  Read and listen.** %%AUD W2-06-10%%

%%FIG v06-f9 | Mei and her placement supervisor in a hospital staff room, standing at the wall planner. The supervisor has a pen and is pointing at a block; Mei has her own notebook open with her hours written in it.%%

> **Supervisor:** Mei, you've got three earlies and a late next week.
> **Mei:** I have a lecture on Thursday evening.
> **Supervisor:** Which one is the late?
> **Mei:** Thursday.
> **Supervisor:** Ah. Does anybody swap with you?
> **Mei:** Adeel usually does, but he works Thursdays now.
> **Supervisor:** Then I'll move it to Friday. Tell me earlier next time — the rota goes up two
> weeks ahead for a reason.
> **Mei:** I know. I didn't look.

### 💼 ON THE JOB

**Asking to change a shift.** %%AUD W2-06-12%%

> **1** — **Mei:** Can I ask about next Thursday? I have a lecture at six.
> **Supervisor:** How long have you known?
> **Mei:** Since the term started.
> **Supervisor:** Then tell me when the rota goes up, not the week before.
>
> **2** — **Mei:** Can I swap with somebody instead?
> **Supervisor:** You can, if you both tell me first.
> **Mei:** Before, not after.
> **Supervisor:** Before. Always before.
>
> **3** — **Supervisor:** Mei, your hours sheet. There are two shifts with no break written on it.
> **Mei:** I didn't take one.
> **Supervisor:** Then write that. Otherwise the system takes the time anyway.

**A  Practice all three in pairs.**

**B  Think about it.**

1. The supervisor asks *"How long have you known?"* What is she really asking about?
2. Why does *before, not after* matter so much for a swap?
3. You have a clash between work and study. What do you say, and when? Say it out loud.

> **Need help?** Can I ask about …? · I have … on … · Can I swap with …? ·
> I'll tell you as soon as the rota goes up.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

In some workplaces the rota is fixed weeks ahead and changing it is a formal request. In others it
appears on a Friday for the following Monday, and everybody is expected to be available.

**Talk about it in groups.**

1. How far ahead do people know their hours where you are from?
2. Is it normal to swap shifts with a colleague? Who has to agree?
3. What do you do if work and study are on the same evening and neither will move?

> No right answer — but the person who asks early is the person who gets the Friday.
