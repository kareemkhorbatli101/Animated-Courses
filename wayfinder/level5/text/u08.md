### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A breakdown by group is the most quoted and least read part of any survey.
> Before you use one, check three things: how large the subgroup is, how large the stated gap is
> compared with the margin of error, and whether the groups overlap with each other.

%%FIG z08-f5 | A six-page survey extract: a cover with 2025 results, a page of three gap figures in large type with small sample notes beneath, a double page with three grouped bar charts by party, gender and age, and a final page with a margin-of-error table and a note on overlapping categories.%%

> ## Twenty-six, seven and five
> *Portmere Civic Forum · note for tomorrow's debate*
>
> 1  Two speakers tomorrow will use the same survey to argue opposite things, and both will be
> 2  quoting accurately. It is worth having the actual breakdown in front of us.
>
> 3  **The partisan gap.** In the OECD's 2025 trust results, trust in national government runs
> 4  about **26 percentage points higher** among people who say they support a governing party than
> 5  among those who do not. This is the largest single gap in the breakdown, it is found across
> 6  almost every country surveyed, and it is far larger than any plausible margin of error. It
> 7  means that a national trust figure is, to a substantial degree, a measure of who happens to
> 8  have won the last election.
>
> 9  **The gender gap.** Women are about **seven percentage points less likely** than men to report
> 10 trusting the national government. Seven points is a real difference and a modest one. It is
> 11 consistent across waves, which is what makes it interesting; a one-off seven-point difference
> 12 would be worth very little.
>
> 13 **The age gap.** People aged 18 to 29 are about **five percentage points less likely** to
> 14 trust national government than those over 50. Five points is close to the edge of what this
> 15 kind of survey can resolve for a subgroup of that size, and we should say so when we use it.
> 16 Anybody who builds an argument about a generational collapse in trust on a five-point gap is
> 17 building on sand, and it is the figure most often quoted in headlines.
>
> 18 **Why the three cannot simply be added.** The groups overlap. A young woman who does not
> 19 support the governing party appears in all three low-trust groups, and the survey does not
> 20 report her three times — but a speaker who stacks the three gaps to produce a total is counting
> 21 her three times, and will arrive at a figure the data does not contain.
>
> 22 **What we would say if asked.** That the strongest, most robust finding here is the partisan
> 23 one, that it is uncomfortable for everybody because it suggests trust tracks political
> 24 alignment rather than performance, and that it is the finding least likely to be quoted by
> 25 either speaker tomorrow, because neither of them can use it.
>
> *Figures: OECD, Survey on Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions, 2025 results.*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **If I were …** | a position you do not hold | **If I were** against this, I'd say … |
| **Suppose / supposing** | invites the room to imagine | **Suppose** the funding fell through. |
| **It's time + past** | an overdue change | **It's time** we **looked** at the cost properly. |
| **It's high time + past** | stronger | **It's high time** somebody **said** so. |
| **I'd rather + past** | a preference about somebody else | **I'd rather** you **didn't** quote that figure. |
| **I'd sooner + bare infinitive** | a preference about yourself | I'**d sooner lose** than win on that number. |

> **Grammar Note** — *It's time*, *I'd rather* and *I'd sooner* take a past form for present and
> future meaning: *it's time we decided*, not *it's time we decide*. The past form marks the
> situation as not yet real, which is the same mechanism as the second conditional.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Opening with your own weakest point is a recognized technique in some argumentative traditions
and reads as self-sabotage in others. In some settings a speaker is expected to put the strongest
case and leave opponents to find the holes, and volunteering one looks like you have been got at.
In others, a speaker who has not named the obvious objection is assumed to be hiding something
worse. In hierarchical settings the calculation changes again: conceding in front of a senior
person may be read as disloyalty to your own side rather than as honesty.

**Discuss:** in a setting you know, what happens to somebody who concedes a point? Who is allowed
to?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Submission on the timetable proposal
>
> I support this proposal at about seventy per cent and I want to set out the thirty first, because
> it is the strongest thing anybody will say this afternoon and it ought not to arrive as an
> ambush.
>
> The evening students were not consulted. I am not saying they were inadequately consulted. Four
> sessions were held and all four ran between two and four in the afternoon, which excludes, by
> design or by carelessness, precisely the group whose timetable is being changed. If I were
> opposing this proposal, that is the entirety of what I would say, I would say it for my whole
> twelve minutes, and I would be right to.
>
> Granted all of that, I am still for it, on one ground. The proposal gives the evening class a
> fixed room for the first time since 2022, which is the single item that has appeared in every
> feedback round the evening group has submitted. The alternative in front of us today is not a
> better consultation; it is another academic year of a room that moves weekly, which the evening
> students have told us, repeatedly, is the thing they mind most. It would be a strange outcome if
> a process that failed to ask them ended by denying them the thing they asked for.
>
> What I am claiming is narrow. It is time this committee separated two decisions it habitually
> runs together: whether a proposal is good, and whether the process that produced it was adequate.
> I would ask the committee to approve the proposal and to minute the consultation as inadequate,
> with a requirement that the next review hold at least one session after six o'clock. I would
> rather carry an amendment that constrains the next review than win a vote that congratulates
> this one.
>
> If the cost comes in above the figure on page two of the paper, I withdraw this submission in
> full. I would like that recorded.

> **Writer's Tip** — Say your confidence as a number. Put the strongest objection first, at full
> strength, in the words its best advocate would use. Then make one narrow claim, and state the
> condition under which you would withdraw. A position with a stated breaking point is read as a
> position; one without is read as an advertisement.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. The writer supports the proposal at about (fifty / seventy) per cent.
2. All four sessions were between (two and four / six and eight).
3. The evening class has asked for a fixed room since (2019 / 2022).
4. The writer would withdraw if (the cost / the date) exceeded the paper.

**1E  Listen to a second submission. Circle the details.** %%AUD W6-08-15%%

1. The speaker's confidence is about (forty / eighty) per cent.
2. The concession is about (cost / staffing).
3. The narrow claim concerns (a deadline / a review date).
4. The withdrawal condition is (named / not named).

## Team Up & Review

### A · Look at the picture

%%FIG z08-f14 | The hall at the moment of the vote. Nineteen people: twelve with hands raised, four without, two still writing, one counting with a pen pointed. The chair standing. A sheet of paper left on a seat, a coat over a chair back, the jug empty. Afternoon light now lower, one strip light on.%%

**In your team, answer.**

1. How many have voted in favor, and how many have not raised a hand?
2. Who is counting, and who is still writing?
3. What has been left behind in the room?
4. How long has the meeting been going on, and what tells you?

### B · Write a team summary

> This is the hall at the moment of the vote. Nineteen people are present. Twelve have raised
> their hands, which, granted, is a majority, but two are still writing.

Write eight sentences. Use at least three concessive expressions. One person writes, everybody
checks.

### C · Report

Read your summary to another team. They name one concession that was really a dismissal.

### D · Team project — the twelve-minute speech

| Role | Who | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| **Manager** | | chooses the motion and keeps strictly to time |
| **Writer** | | drafts the speech, objection first |
| **Editor** | | makes the objection stronger, not weaker |
| **Presenter** | | delivers it; the other team tries to turn the concession into a climb-down |

Choose a real motion about your class or institution. Write and deliver a speech that opens with
its own strongest objection. The Editor has the important job, and the Presenter must hold the
narrow claim.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD W6-08-16%%

%%FIG z08-f16 | A staff kitchen at the end of the day. Two people standing, one holding a printed agenda with one item circled, the other leaning against a counter holding a mug. A dishwasher light on, a window showing a dark car park, a wall calendar with Thursday marked.%%

> Beatriz is on a committee of nine that will vote on Thursday. She has worked out, by talking to
> people, that the vote will be five to four in favor, and that she is the five. She also believes,
> having read the paper twice, that the proposal is right in substance and that the consultation
> behind it genuinely excluded the people most affected. If she votes for it, it passes and a bad
> process is rewarded, and the two colleagues who ran that consultation will learn nothing. If she
> abstains, it fails, and the people who were excluded lose the one thing they have asked for since
> 2022. She could propose an amendment, but an amendment needs a seconder and she has not found
> one, and she has until Thursday to find one or decide without it.

1. What are her three real options, and what does each cost whom?
2. Is rewarding a bad process a cost to be weighed, or a line not to cross?
3. What are three things Beatriz could do before Thursday? Which one does not require anybody
   else's agreement?
