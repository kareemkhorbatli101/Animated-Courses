> ## Thirty per cent, since 1990
> *Portmere Civic Forum · note for the Ferry Lane group*
>
> 1  The figure somebody quoted at the last meeting is real and is worth understanding properly
> 2  before we put it in a letter, because the first thing the planning officer will do is ask what
> 3  it means.
>
> 4  **The figure.** UN-Habitat's *World Cities Report 2024* states that urban green space has
> 5  declined by **nearly 30 per cent since 1990**. That is a global aggregate, across cities of
> 6  all sizes, measured over thirty-four years.
>
> 7  **What it does and does not say.** It does not say that thirty per cent of parks have been
> 8  built on. Green space in this sense includes anything vegetated and open — parks, yes, but
> 9  also verges, sports grounds, cemeteries, allotments, river banks and the kind of unnamed strip
> 10 that most cities do not record at all. Much of the decline is the quiet conversion of exactly
> 11 that sort of land: not a park with a gate and a name, but ground that was green on an old map
> 12 and is now a car park, a service yard or six houses.
>
> 13 **Why that matters for us.** A space with a name, railings and an entry in a register is
> 14 comparatively hard to lose, because losing it requires a decision somebody has to defend. A
> 15 strip of grass behind a terrace, which appears on one plan as amenity and on the next as
> 16 residual, can be lost without any single person ever having decided to lose it. The thirty per
> 17 cent is mostly made of those.
>
> 18 **The distribution, which is the part worth arguing about.** Provision per person is not
> 19 evenly spread within a city, and the gap is usually wider between neighborhoods in one city
> 20 than between whole countries. The report also notes that access matters as much as area: a
> 21 large park two kilometers away does less for a household than a small one at the end of the
> 22 street, and most measurement systems count the area and not the distance.
>
> 23 **What we should and should not claim.** We can say that urban green space has fallen by
> 24 nearly a third globally since 1990, citing the report. We cannot say anything at all about
> 25 Ferry Lane on the basis of it. For Ferry Lane we have a surveyor's wheel, two Saturdays and a
> 26 worn path, and in a planning meeting that local evidence will do more work than the global
> 27 figure, which the officer has read.

### 1 · Defining and non-defining relative clauses

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W6-06-06%%

> **Ines:** The strip, which is used by about forty people a day, has been reclassified.
> **Officer:** The strip that's used by forty people a day? Do you have a count?

**1B  Underline the two relative clauses. Which one adds information, and which one identifies
which strip?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Type | Punctuation | Pronoun | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Defining** | no commas | who / that / which / whose | The strip **that runs behind Ferry Lane** is unnamed. |
| **Non-defining** | commas | who / which / whose — **never *that*** | The strip, **which is unnamed**, runs behind Ferry Lane. |
| **With a preposition** | formal: preposition first | whom / which | the officer **to whom** I wrote |
| **Whole-clause** *which* | comma | which | It was approved in four minutes, **which tells you something**. |
| **Quantifier +** *of which* | comma | of whom / of which | forty households, **most of whom** use the path |

> **Grammar Note** — A defining clause tells you which one and cannot be removed; a non-defining
> clause adds something and can. The commas are not decoration: *my sister who lives in Lisbon*
> implies you have more than one sister. In a planning objection, this distinction decides what
> you have claimed.

### 2 · Reduced relative clauses

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Full | Reduced | Note |
|---|---|---|
| the path **that is worn** to bare earth | the path **worn** to bare earth | passive → *-ed* |
| the people **who are using** it | the people **using** it | active → *-ing* |
| the trees **which stand** at the north end | the trees **standing** at the north end | present → *-ing* |
| the land **that was left** over | the land **left** over | past passive → *-ed* |
| the officer **who is responsible** | the officer **responsible** | adjective moves after the noun |

> **Grammar Note** — A reduced clause drops the relative pronoun and the verb *be*. It only works
> on defining clauses with *who/which/that* as subject. You cannot reduce *the officer who I
> wrote to*.

### %%AUG a%% Language connection — the comma that changes the claim

Complete the rule from the examples.

> **"The strip that is used by forty people a day has been reclassified."** → *There is more than
> one strip, and this is the busy one.*
> **"The strip, which is used by forty people a day, has been reclassified."** → *There is one
> strip, and here is a fact about it.*
>
> **"Residents who objected were not consulted."** → *Only the objectors.*
> **"Residents, who objected, were not consulted."** → *All of them, and they objected.*

**Complete:** Without commas, the clause says ______________ one you mean. With commas, it adds a
fact about the one already ______________ . In the second pair, the comma changes the number of
people the sentence is about, which is why an objection letter is read with a ______________ in
hand.

**Now say what each pair claims**, and choose the version you would write:

1. The trees that are diseased will be removed. / The trees, which are diseased, will be removed.
2. The households that use the path have signed. / The households, which use the path, have signed.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Who may object to a building proposal, and how, differs enormously. In some systems any resident
can write in and every letter must be recorded; in others only adjoining owners have standing, and
a tenant has none. In some places the expected route is a letter; in others it is a visit, a
petition, or a word with a councillor at a surgery. Almost everywhere, the people least likely to
read a notice on a lamp post are the people with least time, and almost everywhere that is known
and not fixed.
