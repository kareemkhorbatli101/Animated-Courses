# Unit 2 · What Comes Next
%%SUB Four people in one room, all describing the same next five years%%
%%GOALS
Use the future continuous and future perfect to place events in a future sequence
Grade certainty, from near-certain down to barely possible
Read a skills forecast and separate what is measured from what is assumed
Disagree about the future without pretending to know it
%%

The planning meeting was supposed to take an hour. It has taken two, and the reason is not
disagreement about what to do; it is that four people are describing four different futures and
all four are using the same flat word, *will*. Nobody has said how sure they are, so nobody can
tell whether they are arguing about values or about probabilities, and those two arguments cannot
be had at the same time.

### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When a report gives you a figure and a direction, read for three things: the
> figure, the comparison it is being measured against, and the countries or groups where the
> direction is the other way. The third is usually in the paragraph nobody quotes.

%%FIG z02-f5 | A six-page report extract: a cover page with a title and December 2024, a page of key findings in bullets, a double page with two bar charts and a shaded country strip, and a final page with a methodology box and a sample-size line.%%

> ## What adults can actually do, and where it is going
> *Portmere Adult Learning Service · briefing note for tutors*
>
> 1  Every two or three years we are asked to plan provision for the next decade, and every time,
> 2  somebody in the room predicts the collapse of basic skills and somebody else predicts that
> 3  technology will handle it. In December 2024 the OECD published the second cycle of its adult
> 4  skills survey, which tested about **160,000 adults aged 16 to 65 across 31 countries and
> 5  economies**. It is the largest thing of its kind and it does not support either prediction
> 6  cleanly.
>
> 7  **The headline figure.** Across participating countries, between **18% and 19% of adults
> 8  scored at or below the lowest proficiency level in literacy and numeracy**. At that level an
> 9  adult can read brief texts and find a single piece of information, but struggles to compare
> 10 two sources or follow an argument across paragraphs. Roughly one adult in five, in some of the
> 11 wealthiest countries on earth. This is not a forecast; it is a measurement, and it is the
> 12 number our next ten years of planning has to start from.
>
> 13 **The direction.** Compared with the previous cycle, average literacy declined in a majority
> 14 of the countries that took part in both. Numeracy was more mixed: it rose in some countries
> 15 and fell in others. In Finland, Denmark, Norway and Switzerland results were stable or
> 16 improved. So the sentence *skills are falling everywhere* is false, and the sentence *skills
> 17 are fine* is also false, and anybody who tells you either has not disaggregated by country.
>
> 18 **The part that will shape our timetable.** The gap between the highest and lowest performers
> 19 within countries widened in most places. That matters more for us than the average does. An
> 20 average that falls because the bottom falls needs different provision from an average that
> 21 falls because everybody drifts down a little, and the two are indistinguishable in a headline.
>
> 22 **What we will be doing by 2030.** If this pattern holds — and it is a measurement of the
> 23 past, not a projection of the future — we will have been running the same beginner provision
> 24 for fifteen years while the population it serves grows. By the end of the decade the demand
> 25 will not have changed shape; it will have changed size. That is an easier problem than the one
> 26 people fear, and a harder one to fund.
>
> *Figures: OECD, Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC Cycle 2), published December 2024.*

### 1 · Future continuous and future perfect

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W6-02-06%%

> **Priya:** By March we'll have finished the review.
> **Tomas:** We'll be finishing it in March. That's not the same sentence.
> **Priya:** No, it isn't, and mine's the one in the funding bid.
> **Tomas:** Then we should change one of them, and I don't think it should be the review.
> **Priya:** … That's fair.

*Is it dishonest to write the confident version in a funding bid?*

**1B  Underline the two future forms. Which one says the thing is complete?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Form | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **will + verb** | a neutral future fact or decision | We **will publish** in March. |
| **will be + -ing** | in progress at a future time | By March we'**ll be finishing** it. |
| **will have + -ed** | complete before a future point | By March we'**ll have finished** it. |
| **will have been + -ing** | duration up to a future point | By March we'**ll have been working** on it for a year. |
| **be going to** | present evidence points to it | Look at the numbers — demand **is going to** rise. |

> **Grammar Note** — *By* with the future perfect means *at or before that point*. *In* with the
> future continuous means *during*. A plan that says *by March* has promised completion; a plan
> that says *in March* has promised activity. Reports, bids and contracts are read by people who
> know the difference, which is why the choice is not a matter of style.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Attaching a percentage to your own opinion is normal in some professional cultures and reads as
arrogant in others, where the appropriate move is to defer to the most senior person's estimate

and hedging is heard as refusing to commit rather than as accuracy.

> **The shape of demand will not change.** Seventy percent. Our beginner classes will still be
> beginner classes. What I expect is more of the same people, not different people, which is a
> cheaper problem to solve and a harder one to make a case for, because *more of what we already
> do* is the least persuasive sentence in any funding bid.
>
> **We will lose a room by 2027.** Forty percent, on one conversation, which is thin. I am
> including it because by the time we know, we will have committed the timetable, and a
> forty-per-cent risk with a nine-month lead time is worth a contingency even if it is worth
> nothing else.
>
> What I am not claiming is that technology will absorb any of this. I have no figure for that
> and neither, as far as I can tell, does anybody else, and in the absence of a figure I would
> rather plan for the measurement we have.

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the number next to the claim, and put what it rests on next to the
> number. A forecast whose basis is stated can be argued with, which is the whole point of
> writing it down.
