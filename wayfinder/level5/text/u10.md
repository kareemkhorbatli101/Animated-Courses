### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — Watch for figures that are reported in the direction that suits the writer. A
> survey finding of 41% can honestly be written as *four in ten believe they would be heard* or
> *a clear majority believe they would not*. Both are the same number. Only one of them is the
> number the reader will remember.

%%FIG z10-f5 | A five-page survey report: a cover with a title and a year, a page with a single large percentage and a one-line caption, a double page with a bar chart by country and a small trend line for a four-point change, and a final page with a question-wording box and a note on comparability.%%

> ## Forty-one per cent, read both ways
> *Portmere Civic Forum · note for tonight's meeting*
>
> 1  There is one figure worth taking into the hall this evening, and there is a right and a wrong
> 2  way to use it.
>
> 3  **The figure.** Across the countries in the OECD's trust survey, an average of **41% believe
> 4  they would be able to voice an opinion before local government takes a decision affecting
> 5  their community**. The same survey finds that the share of people who think they can easily
> 6  find information about an administrative service has **risen by four percentage points since
> 7  2021**.
>
> 8  **The two readings.** Forty-one per cent can be reported as *four in ten people believe they
> 9  would be heard*, which sounds like a system with a problem. It can equally be reported as
> 10 *almost six in ten believe they would not be heard*, which sounds like a system in trouble.
> 11 Nobody is lying in either case. What differs is which group the sentence puts at the front, and
> 12 the one at the front is the one the reader remembers.
>
> 13 **The four points, which are easy to misuse.** A four-point rise since 2021 is a real
> 14 improvement in one specific thing: whether people think they can find information. It is not an
> 15 improvement in whether they think they will be listened to, and the two are different
> 16 questions in the survey. A council that quotes the four points in answer to a question about
> 17 being listened to has changed the subject, and it is worth being able to say so in one
> 18 sentence.
>
> 19 **What is genuinely encouraging.** Finding information is the precondition for everything
> 20 else. You cannot object to a proposal you have not heard of, and for twenty years the
> 21 complaint about consultation has been less about refusal than about discovery. Four points is
> 22 small and it is in the right place.
>
> 23 **What we would say tonight.** That most of the people in this room came believing it would
> 24 make no difference, that the figure says they are not unusual, and that the only thing which
> 25 reliably changes that figure in a given place is a decision visibly changed by something
> 26 somebody said in a room like this one.
>
> *Figures: OECD, Survey on Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions.*

**1B  Underline the two versions. Where does the important information fall in each?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Type | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***it*-cleft** | *It is / was X that / who …* | **It was the evening class that** wasn't asked. |
| ***what*-cleft** | *What … is / was …* | **What most people believe is that** it's pointless. |
| **Reverse *what*-cleft** | *X is what …* | Discovery **is what** they complain about. |
| ***all*-cleft** | *All … is / was …* | **All I'm asking for is** one session after six. |
| ***the thing / reason*-cleft** | *The thing that … is …* | **The reason nobody came is that** nobody heard. |

> **Grammar Note** — English puts new or important information at the end of a sentence. A cleft
> rearranges the sentence so that the part you want remembered arrives last, where the voice
> falls. That is why a cleft sounds deliberate: the listener can hear that you have chosen the
> order.

### 2 · Inversion

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Trigger | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative adverbial first** | *Never / Rarely / Seldom + aux + subject* | **Never have** so few believed it. |
| ***Not only …*** | *Not only + aux + subject … but also* | **Not only was** it unadvertised, **but** it clashed. |
| ***No sooner … than*** | | **No sooner had** we asked **than** it closed. |
| ***Only after / Only when*** | the inversion falls in the main clause | **Only when** they objected **did anything** change. |
| ***Little / Hardly*** | | **Little did** we know. |
| **Place adverbial (literary)** | no auxiliary | **At the back of the hall stood** a man with a folder. |

> **Grammar Note** — Inversion after a negative adverbial is formal and emphatic, and it is easy to
> overdo. One in a short speech is striking; three is a performance. And *only when* puts the
> inversion in the **second** clause: *only when they objected did anything change*, not *only when
> did they object*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The right way to make a point in public varies sharply. In some settings understatement carries
more weight than force, and a speaker who raises their voice has conceded the argument. In others
a flat delivery reads as indifference, and emotion is the evidence that you mean it. In some
meeting cultures a junior person naming a figure the officials have not mentioned is admired; in
others it embarrasses everybody and the point is lost in the embarrassment. Length signals
seriousness in some rooms and self-importance in others.

**Discuss:** in a setting you know, what makes a speaker credible? What would make the same words
fail?

> That is the share of people, across countries like this one, who believe they would be able to
> voice an opinion before local government took a decision affecting their community. What that
> means for a meeting like tonight's is simple and uncomfortable: most of the people sitting in
> the hall arrived believing it would make no difference, and the survey says they are not unusual
> in thinking so. I include myself.
>
> I am not offering that as a complaint. It is a description, and the reason I am giving a figure
> rather than an account of my own frustration is that a figure is the one thing in the room that
> nobody can argue with, and I would rather be answered than agreed with.
>
> It was put to me during the meeting that information has improved since 2021. It has, by about
> four percentage points, and I would like that in the minutes, because it is a real improvement in
> the thing that has to come first — you cannot object to a proposal you have never heard of.
> What it is not is an improvement in whether people believe they will be listened to. Those are
> two separate questions in the survey and answering the second with the first changes the subject.
>
> All I am asking for is one consultation session held after six o'clock. Not a policy on
> consultation, not a review of engagement, not a commitment in principle. One session, at an hour
> when the people most affected by this decision are not at work. If the next review holds one, I
> will come back to this microphone and say so, in public, because the only thing that moves a
> figure like forty-one is a decision that somebody can see was changed by something said in a
> room like this.

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the number on its own line. Then say what it means for the people reading
> it, not what it means in general. End with one request that a single person could grant, and say
> what you will do if they do — a promise to come back and say so is the cheapest thing you can
> offer and the rarest thing anybody hears.
