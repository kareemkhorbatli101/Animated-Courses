### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When two reputable sources give different numbers for the same thing, the
> usual reason is that they are measuring different things, or the same thing at different dates.
> Find the definition and the date before you decide anybody is wrong.

%%FIG z03-f5 | A four-page extract from an energy report: a cover with a title and a year, a page of key numbers in large type, a double page with a stacked area chart and a note box, and a final page listing definitions and a revision history.%%

> ## Two numbers for one report
> *Portmere Energy Forum · note for the reading group*
>
> 1  A member of this group asked why she had seen two different figures for the renewable share
> 2  of world electricity in 2030 — **43%** in one place and **"almost 45%"** in another — and
> 3  whether one of them was wrong. Both come from summaries of the same International Energy
> 4  Agency report. Neither is a misprint, and the reason is worth more than the answer.
>
> 5  **Start with what has been measured.** Renewables supplied about **32% of global electricity
> 6  generation in 2024** and about **34% in 2025**, against roughly **23% a decade earlier**.
> 7  Those are observations, published annually, and nobody in the field disputes them. The
> 8  argument is only ever about the figure for a year that has not happened.
>
> 9  **Why two summaries of one report differ.** We can think of four possibilities and the group
> 10 should add more. One summary may be rounding a figure the other gives to a decimal. The two
> 11 may be quoting different scenarios from the same document, since a report of this kind
> 12 normally publishes a main case and at least one accelerated case. They may include different
> 13 technologies — whether large hydropower is counted changes the total by several points. Or one
> 14 may have been written from a later revision than the other. We do not know which of those it
> 15 is, and we are not going to pretend to; what we can say is that the gap between 43 and 45 is
> 16 smaller than any of those four effects.
>
> 17 **The trap underneath all of it.** Capacity and generation are not the same thing and the two
> 18 words are used interchangeably in almost every newspaper report. A solar farm's **capacity**
> 19 is what it produces at noon in June. Its **generation** is what it actually produced over the
> 20 year, which is a fraction of that, and the fraction differs by technology and by latitude. A
> 21 country can double renewable capacity and raise its renewable share of generation by a much
> 22 smaller amount, and both statements will be true and will appear in different headlines on the
> 23 same day.
>
> 24 **What this means for reading any energy figure.** If a number has no date, it cannot be
> 25 checked. If it does not say capacity or generation, it cannot be compared. If the share has
> 26 risen but the absolute amount of fossil generation has also risen — which happens when total
> 27 demand grows — then the share is doing something different from what most readers assume it is
> 28 doing. None of this makes the figures unreliable. It makes them figures, which must be read
> 29 rather than quoted.
>
> *Figures: International Energy Agency, Renewables 2025 and Global Energy Review 2026. The two
> 2030 figures are reproduced here as they were published in summaries of the same report,
> including their disagreement.*

**vintage** — the date a projection was made</sub>

### 1 · Modals of deduction — present

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W6-03-06%%

> **Priya:** The cleaning shift starts at 6:30, so it must be that.
> **Adeel:** It can't be — they don't have a key to that door.

> **Adeel:** By whom? There's nobody in before seven.

*Is "one of us is wrong about X or Y" a concession or a counter-attack?*

**1B  Underline the three modals. Which one expresses the strongest conclusion?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Modal | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **must** | the only explanation I can see | It **must** be the cleaners. |
| **can't / couldn't** | this explanation is excluded | It **can't** be them — no key. |
| **may / might / could** | one of several open possibilities | It **might** have been propped by accident. |
| **should** | expected, if things are normal | The log **should** show who opened up. |
| **will** | confident prediction from habit | That door **will** be open again tomorrow. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Must* here is not obligation; it is the speaker's conclusion. The negative
> of deductive *must* is **can't**, not *mustn't*: *It must be the cleaners* / *It can't be the
> cleaners*. *Mustn't* means prohibition and belongs to a different sentence.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Saying *I was wrong* in front of colleagues is read very differently across workplaces. In some,
it is the move that restores your authority, because it shows your conclusions track evidence. In
others, it hands a weapon to whoever wants one, and the local technique is to let the correction
happen silently, without a sentence. In some hierarchies, a junior person correcting a senior one
in public is the error, regardless of who was right.

**Discuss:** in a workplace you know, how does a correction get made? Who is allowed to make one
out loud?

>
> **What follows from that.** Somebody must have propped the door between the last secure check on
> Monday evening and 6:40 on Tuesday, and somebody closed it before 7:55. The key to that door
> went onto the cleaning ring when the lock was changed in August, so the cleaning shift, which
> begins at 6:30, could have been in the building; this is consistent with the photograph and does
> not establish it. I had previously stated that the cleaners could not have had access. That was
> wrong, and the error was mine rather than theirs.
>
> **What we do not have.** Anybody's whereabouts. The camera clock has run fast before, by up to
> four minutes, which does not matter at this resolution but would matter if we were comparing the
> photograph with a signed entry.
>
> **What I would act on.** Not the identity of whoever propped it, which on current evidence
> cannot be established and may well be nobody's fault. Two things instead: the log has not been
> signed for two days, which is a procedure that has stopped working; and the August lock change
> was not circulated, which is why four people spent twenty minutes last Thursday reasoning from a
> fact that expired in the summer.
>
> I would rather close this with a procedure fixed than with a name attached.

> **Writer's Tip** — Separate *what we have*, *what follows*, and *what we do not have* into
> different paragraphs, and the inferences stop leaking into the observations. Then say what you
> would act on, which is almost never the thing people wanted a name for.
