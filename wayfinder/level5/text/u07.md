> **Reader's Tip** — When a survey splits one population into two groups and the two groups differ
> enormously, the interesting sentence is never the average. It is the split, and the question of
> which way the arrow runs between the two things being measured.

%%FIG z07-f5 | A five-page survey report: a cover with a title and 2025 results, a page of two large contrasting percentages side by side, a double page with a split bar chart and a country strip, and a final page with a methodology box and a note on question wording.%%

> ## Sixty-nine and twenty-two
> *Portmere Civic Forum · note for the reading group*
>
> 1  Last month somebody in this group said that trust in government is really about whether
> 2  people feel they are listened to. It is a satisfying sentence and the survey evidence on it is
> 3  more striking than most of us expected.
>
> 4  **The two figures.** In the OECD's 2025 trust results, among people who believe that people
> 5  like them have a say in what the government does, **69% report trusting the national
> 6  government**. Among those who feel they do not have a say, the figure is **22%**. Same
> 7  countries, same survey, same question about trust. A gap of forty-seven percentage points,
> 8  which is larger than the gap between the most and least trusting countries in the study.
>
> 9  **What this does not tell us.** Which way the arrow runs. It is entirely possible that feeling
> 10 heard produces trust. It is equally possible that people who are inclined to trust also tend to
> 11 feel heard, and that both are produced by something else — a successful dealing with a service,
> 12 a stable job, a long residence, a family history of being treated well by officials. The survey
> 13 asks both questions of the same person at the same moment, and a single interview cannot
> 14 separate those.
>
> 15 **What it does tell us.** That the two things are bound together very tightly indeed, and that
> 16 any account of falling trust which does not mention voice is leaving out the single strongest
> 17 association in the data. The report also records that an average of **41%** believe they would
> 18 be able to voice an opinion before local government takes a decision affecting their community
> 19 — which means a clear majority believe they would not.
>
> 20 **Why the reporting of this matters to us.** Two newspapers covered these results in the same
> 21 week. One reported that the OECD *found* that exclusion drives distrust. The other reported
> 22 that the OECD *claimed* a link between participation and trust. The first overstates what a
> 23 cross-sectional survey can establish; the second implies the OECD was advancing a position
> 24 rather than reporting an association. The underlying report says neither, and says so
> 25 carefully, in a paragraph about limitations that neither newspaper quoted.
>
> *Figures: OECD, Survey on Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions, 2025 results.*

### 2 · Reporting verb patterns

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Pattern | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **verb + that** | say, claim, insist, deny, point out, concede | He **claimed that** the figures were final. |
| **verb + object + that** | tell, remind, assure, inform | She **assured us that** nothing had changed. |
| **verb + to-infinitive** | agree, refuse, offer, promise, threaten | They **refused to** reopen it. |
| **verb + object + to-infinitive** | ask, tell, advise, urge, warn | She **urged us to** write in. |
| **verb + -ing** | admit, deny, suggest, recommend | He **denied having** seen the letter. |
| **verb + preposition + -ing** | apologize for, insist on, object to | She **objected to being** quoted. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Say* takes no object (*say that*, never *say me*); *tell* requires one
> (*tell me that*). *Deny* takes *-ing* or *that*, never a to-infinitive. These are the four
> mistakes that appear most often in minutes.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

What minutes are *for* differs. In some organizations they are a record of decisions only, and
recording who said what is considered improper, because it makes people cautious. In others they
are nearly verbatim and a participant can ask for their exact words. In some cultures a public
correction of the secretary is an attack on the chair who appointed them. And in some languages the
choice of reporting verb carries far less judgment than it does in English, so a speaker
translating directly may produce *he claimed* where they meant nothing by it at all.

**Discuss:** in a meeting you know, who writes the record and who may change it?
