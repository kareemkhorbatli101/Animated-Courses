### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A study's number depends on three choices: what was in the sample, how
> "average" was calculated, and what reading speed was assumed. Two honest studies of the same
> thing can differ by a factor of three for exactly those reasons.

%%FIG z09-f5 | A five-page academic paper extract: a title page with authors and an abstract, a page with a histogram of document lengths, a double page with a table of summary statistics and a diagram of linked policies, and a final page of limitations and references.%%

> ## Five point six minutes, and eighty-four point seven
> *Portmere Civic Forum · note for the plain-language group*
>
> 1  We spent most of the last meeting arguing about whether anybody reads terms and conditions.
> 2  There is a measured answer to the related question of how long it would take if you did, and
> 3  it is more interesting than either side expected.
>
> 4  **The study.** An automated audit analyzed the privacy policies of about **207,000 websites**.
> 5  The average policy ran to roughly **1,404 words**, which the authors calculate as about
> 6  **5.6 minutes** of reading. That is less than most of us guessed. A document of 1,404 words is
> 7  about three sides of typescript and would not be remarkable in any other context.
>
> 8  **The figure that changes the picture.** Policies refer to other policies. A site's own
> 9  document names the third parties it shares data with, each of which has its own policy, and
> 10 those documents are part of what you are agreeing to. Counting the policies a typical site
> 11 refers you to, the total comes to about **84.7 minutes**. The thing people imagine they are
> 12 declining to read takes about six minutes; the thing they are actually agreeing to takes nearly
> 13 an hour and a half.
>
> 14 **Why this is a writing problem and not only a legal one.** Nobody set out to produce
> 15 eighty-five minutes of reading. The structure produced it. Each individual document is a
> 16 defensible length, written by somebody who was told to be thorough, and the total is an
> 17 emergent property of thousands of separately reasonable decisions. This is exactly what happens
> 18 to any document that is amended rather than rewritten: every amendment is justified and the
> 19 whole becomes unreadable.
>
> 20 **What follows for us.** Length is not the measure that matters; reachability is. A
> 21 hundred-and-twenty-word summary that is read is worth more than a four-hundred-word notice
> 22 that is not, and a four-hundred-word notice is worth more than a perfect one that sits behind
> 23 two links. When we next revise our own leaflets, the question to ask of each sentence is not
> 24 whether it is accurate — they are all accurate — but what it is doing, and whether anybody
> 25 would reach it.
>
> *Figure: automated audit of 207,000 website privacy policies, arXiv 1805.01187. Other studies
> using different samples and reading speeds give substantially different averages; see Unit 11.*

### 1 · Participle clauses

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W6-09-06%%

> **Designer:** "Having reviewed the leaflet, we decided to shorten it." Twelve words.
> **Marisol:** "We reviewed the leaflet and then we decided to shorten it." Eleven.

*Is compression about length, or about which clause is subordinate?*

**1B  Underline the participle clause. What has been removed from it?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Type | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Present participle** | *-ing* — same time or cause | **Living** nearer, she arrives first. |
| **Perfect participle** | *having + -ed* — earlier action | **Having reviewed** it, we cut it. |
| **Past participle** | *-ed* — passive | **Written** in 2019, the policy is out of date. |
| **With a conjunction** | *after / before / while / when + -ing* | **After reading** it twice, I cut a third. |
| **Negative** | *not + -ing* | **Not having** the figures, I said nothing. |

> **Grammar Note** — The subject of a participle clause must be the subject of the main clause.
> *Having reviewed the leaflet, the text was shortened* is wrong: the text did not review the
> leaflet. This is the single most common error in formal writing, and it is produced by exactly
> the instinct to be concise.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Brevity is not universally admired. In some professional and academic cultures, a short document
suggests the writer has not done the work, and length signals seriousness and respect for the
reader's judgement. In others, a long document is read as discourtesy. Formal registers in some
languages require opening and closing courtesies that a direct translation strips out, so a writer
following an English style guide can produce something that reads as curt to half their audience
and plain to the other half.

**Discuss:** in your language and setting, what does a very short formal letter communicate? What
does a very long one?

> leaflet cold and a warm version was added without the cold one being removed. That duplication
> has been through four print runs. Forty words were legal cover; asked directly, our legal adviser
> needed one clause of eleven words. Thirty words nobody could account for, including the four
> people I asked, and they are gone.
>
> The real failure was not length. The first sentence of the short version is: *You will not be
> charged for this and nobody will ask about your immigration status.* In the long version that
> sentence was on page two. Everything above it was accurate, and nobody who needed that sentence
> was ever going to reach it.
>
> I would propose one change to how we work. Before anybody writes, we agree the one sentence a
> reader must reach, and we write it first. Everything else is then measured against whether it
> delays that sentence or supports it. On this leaflet that rule would have saved three drafts and
> about three years.

> **Writer's Tip** — Don't cut by length; diagnose by function. Mark each sentence as information,
> tone, repetition, cover, requested or unexplained, and the cuts make themselves. Then check
> where your most important sentence sits on the page, because a buried sentence has not been
