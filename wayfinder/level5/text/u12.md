> countries, and that is a limitation the careful reports state and the careless summaries do not.

> *Portmere Adult Learning Service · closing note for the year*
>
> 1  This is the last of these notes for the year and I want to put one pair of figures beside
> 2  everything else we have discussed, because it reframes most of it.
>
> 3  **The pair.** In the OECD's adult skills survey published in December 2024, **60% of adults
> 4  with tertiary education scored at or above Level 3 in literacy**. Among adults whose highest
> 5  attainment was below upper secondary, the figure was **12%**. Level 3 is the band at which a
> 6  reader can compare sources, follow an argument across paragraphs, and identify what a text
> 7  does not say. It is, more or less, the level this note assumes.
>
> 8  **What follows, and what does not.** It does not follow that education produces the skill. A
> 9  great deal sorts people into those two groups before anybody reaches a classroom, and the
> 10 survey measures adults at one moment rather than following them. What does follow is that the

> 12 3 is, in effect, addressed to the first of them.
>
> 13 **The distribution, which is the harder finding.** Over the decade covered, the
> 14 lowest-performing tenth of adults declined while the highest-performing tenth improved. The
> 15 average therefore moved very little, and the average is what was reported. Across participating
> 16 countries roughly **one adult in five** is a low performer in literacy and numeracy, and
> 17 literacy and numeracy fell or were flat in most countries, with only Finland and Denmark
> 18 improving significantly.
>
> 19 **Why this reframes the rest.** Every note this year has been about reading something
> 20 carefully: a projection, two conflicting energy figures, a trust breakdown, a clause in a

> 22 compare sources and spot what a document does not say. One adult in five cannot do that with a

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Position | Punctuation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Front** | marker + comma | **However**, the categories are not comparable. |
| **Mid** | commas both sides | The categories, **however**, are not comparable. |
| **End** | comma before | The categories are not comparable, **though**. |
| **Conjunction** | no comma before *and/but*, comma after a long front clause | **Although** the gap is large, the categories differ. |
| **Prepositional** | marker + noun | **Despite** the gap, the categories differ. |

> **Grammar Note** — *However* is an adverb, not a conjunction: *The gap is large, however the
> categories differ* is a comma splice. Use a full stop or a semicolon, or use *but*. Moving a
> marker to the middle of a sentence slows it down and is a useful way to avoid starting four
> paragraphs in a row with the same word.

### 2 · Hedging, and how much is too much

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Device | Example | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Modal** | This **may** indicate … | open possibility |
| **Lexical verb** | This **suggests** / **appears to** … | tentative |
| **Adverb** | **arguably**, **broadly**, **largely** | narrows |
| **Impersonal** | It **is possible that** … | distances |
| **Quantified** | in **most** cases, **about** a fifth | bounded |
| **Attributed** | **on the authors' own account** … | shifts the claim |

> **Grammar Note** — One hedge per claim is accuracy. Two is caution. Three or more is evasion,
> and a reader stops believing you have a position: *it could arguably be suggested that there may
> in some cases be a tendency* commits to nothing. If you need that many, the honest sentence is
> *I don't know*, followed by what would settle it.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Academic and professional writing traditions differ in where the argument goes. Some expect the
claim in the first sentence and everything afterwards in support; others build the context first
and arrive at the claim near the end, and a reader trained in the first tradition will say the
second has no thesis while a reader trained in the second will say the first is blunt and
presumptuous. Hedging norms differ too: in some fields a bare claim is a sign of confidence, and in
others it is a sign that the writer has not read enough.

**Discuss:** in your field or your language, where does the argument belong? What happens to a
writer who puts it in the other place?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Closing section — what the figures oblige us to do
>
> I have put one pair of figures beside everything in this report: sixty percent of
> tertiary-educated adults reach Level 3 in literacy, and twelve percent of those who did not
> complete upper secondary school. Level 3 is the band at which a reader can compare two sources
> and notice what a document does not say.
>
> It does not follow that education produces the skill, and I want to be exact about that rather
> than careful about it. A great deal sorts people into those two groups long before they reach a
> classroom, and a survey that interviews adults once cannot separate the sorting from the
> teaching. What does follow — and this is the claim I am making — is that the two groups live in
> different informational worlds, and that almost every document discussed in this report is
> addressed, in practice, to the first of them.
>
> Admittedly, the categories behind those percentages are assembled from school systems that are
> not comparable, and "below upper secondary" means something different in each country. I would
> not have used a five-point gap built this way. A forty-eight-point gap survives the objection,
> which is the only reason I am relying on it.
>
> The distribution matters more than the gap, however, and it is the finding that reframes the
> rest. Over the decade the lowest-performing tenth declined while the highest improved, so the
> average moved very little — and the average is what was reported. An average that holds still
> while its ends move apart is the most quotable and least informative figure a survey can produce.
> This suggests, in most cases, a reporting problem rather than a measurement one.
>
> There is one thing I do not know. Whether writing public documents differently changes outcomes
> for the lowest-performing tenth is not something this report, or any study I have found,
> establishes; what would settle it is a longitudinal trial that nobody has run. I am leaving that
> sentence in rather than hedging around it.
>
> What doing nothing costs is not neutral. Every notice, clause and policy we issue at Level 3
> transfers the work of understanding to the reader, and the readers least able to do that work
> are the ones for whom the consequences of misunderstanding are heaviest: a deposit, a tenancy, a
> shift pattern, a deadline. That is the cost, and it is already being paid.
>
> I would therefore ask for one change, and only one. Before any document leaves this service, the
> sentence that matters most should be written first and placed first. It costs nothing, it needs
> nobody's permission, and on the evidence of this year it would have saved three of the eleven
> disputes described above.

> **Writer's Tip** — Write one sentence per paragraph saying what it *does*. Delete any whose
> sentence says *gives more detail*. Keep your caveat visible and say why the finding survives it.
> One hedge per claim; where you have four, write *I don't know* and name what would settle it —
> that sentence is almost always the one a reader quotes.
