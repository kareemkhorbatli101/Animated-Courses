### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — A survey can tell you what people say. It can tell you that two answers go
> together. It cannot, by itself, tell you which one caused the other, and the sentence that
> claims it did is almost always added by somebody who is not the researcher.

%%FIG z04-f5 | A five-page survey report: a cover with a title and a year, a page of headline percentages, a double page with a grouped bar chart by country and a correlation scatter, and a final page with a methodology box naming the sample size and the mode of interview.%%

> ## What people say about trust, and what that does and does not tell us
> *Portmere Civic Forum · reading group note*
>
> 1  Somebody at the last meeting said that trust in government has collapsed. Somebody else said
> 2  it has always been low. The OECD runs a survey on exactly this across member countries, and
> 3  it supports the second sentence better than the first, which surprised most of the room.
>
> 4  **The headline.** Across the countries surveyed, roughly **four in ten people say they trust
> 5  their national government**, and a similar share say they do not. The remainder sit in the
> 6  middle. That is not a collapse and it is not healthy; it is a long-standing split that moves
> 7  by a few points between waves and gets reported as a crisis in whichever direction it last
> 8  moved.
>
> 9  **The part that does the work.** Trust is consistently higher in local government than in
> 10 national government, in almost every country surveyed. People who have recently had a
> 11 successful dealing with a public service — a license renewed, a benefit processed, a complaint
> 12 answered — report higher trust than those who have not. That relationship is strong and it is
> 13 found repeatedly.
>
> 14 **What it does not establish.** Whether good service produces trust, or trusting people
> 15 experience the same service as better, or some third thing produces both. All three are
> 16 consistent with the finding. The survey measures what people report, in a single interview,
> 17 about their own recent experience; it cannot follow the same person through a transaction and
> 18 out the other side. If local government were simply more competent than national government,
> 19 we would see this pattern. We would also see it if people found the local more legible, or
> 20 nearer, or simply less often in the news.
>
> 21 **Why we should care about the distinction.** Because the room divided, last month, into
> 22 people who wanted to conclude that services must be improved and people who wanted to conclude
> 23 that expectations must be managed, and the survey supports an intervention from neither camp.
> 24 It supports one sentence: a considerable number of people's trust is attached to an experience
> 25 they have had, rather than to a view they hold. What follows from that sentence is a political
> 26 question and the figures do not answer it.
>
> *Figures: OECD, Survey on Drivers of Trust in Public Institutions. Percentages are rounded and
> vary by country and wave; the direction of the local–national gap is consistent.*

### 1 · Mixed conditionals

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W6-04-06%%

> **Leila:** If you'd taken it, I wouldn't be sitting here.

**1B  Underline the two conditional clauses. Which half of each is in the past, and which in the
present?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Type | Condition | Result | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Third** | past | past | If I'd taken it, I **would have moved** in 2015. |
| **Mixed (past → present)** | past | present | If I'd taken it, I **wouldn't be** here now. |
| **Mixed (present → past)** | present | past | If I **were** the kind of person who moves, I **would have gone**. |
| **Second** | present / unreal | present | If I **had** the offer now, I **wouldn't take** it. |

> **Grammar Note** — The mixed conditional is the grammar of a consequence that is still going on.
> *If I'd taken it, I would have moved* describes a past event. *If I'd taken it, I wouldn't be
> here* describes the present you are standing in. Most real regret and most real relief are
> mixed conditionals, which is why people reach for them and get them wrong.

### 2 · *Wish*, *if only* and *otherwise*

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **wish + past** | a present state you want different | I **wish** I **lived** nearer. |
| **wish + past perfect** | regret about the past | I **wish** I **had asked** more questions. |
| **wish + would** | irritation at a repeated habit | I **wish** he **would stop** bringing it up. |
| **if only + past perfect** | stronger regret | **If only** I'**d said** something at the time. |
| **otherwise** | states the alternative outcome | I turned it down; **otherwise** I'd be in another city. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Wish* takes *would* for other people's habits, not for your own: *I wish he
> would listen*, but *I wish I listened* — or, more naturally, *I wish I were better at
> listening*. And *I wish I would* is almost always wrong.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Whether an alternative life is a reasonable thing to discuss aloud varies a great deal. In some
families it is ordinary conversation and nobody is hurt by it. In others, speaking about the life
you did not have is heard as a complaint about the people who are in the one you did, and the
custom is to say *everything happened as it was meant to* and change the subject. A migration
decision carries extra weight, because the people who were left behind can hear any version of it
as a verdict on them.

**Discuss:** in your family, is this a conversation people have? Who is it safe to have it with?

> once a month ever since, which is four times a year more than I admit to. The version I think
> about is specific: a department, a house with stairs, a particular walk to work that I made up
> entirely, since I visited the place once for six hours.
>
> What that version does not contain is eleven years of ordinary weather. It has no bad manager in
> it, no winter when I would have been ill in a flat with nobody in it, no two years of being new
> somewhere at thirty-eight, which is a much worse age to be new than twenty-five. If I'd taken
> it, I would have had all of that, and I would also, by now, have a story about the job I turned
> down here, told once a month, with a house in it that had no stairs.
>
> My daughter made me notice something last month. She said that when I tell it, I describe
> something I lost and nothing I got, and that she is nineteen and sitting at the table while I
> do it. If I'd gone, she would not be somewhere else; she would be somebody else, which is a
> different sentence and a much harder one. I had been telling a story in which the whole of the
> last eleven years was the cost side of a transaction, in front of the part of it that can hear
> me.
>
> I am keeping the habit, I think — I do not believe the monthly version does any harm, and I
> would not trust somebody who claimed never to run it. What I am changing is the telling. If I
> describe the other city again, I will describe the winter in it as well, and I will say what was
> on this side. She has the letter now. She asked for it and I could not think of a reason to
> keep it that would have survived being said out loud.

> **Writer's Tip** — When you write about a road not taken, write the bad weather on both roads.
> A comparison between a real life with its dull Tuesdays and an imagined one without them is not
> a comparison, and the reader can feel the cheat even when they cannot name it.
