### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When two studies of the same thing disagree, read the method before you pick
> a side. Different samples, different years and different assumed reading speeds can produce a
> threefold difference without either study being wrong about anything.

%%FIG z11-f5 | A six-page academic extract: a title page with authors and dates, a page showing a line chart of policy length from 1996 to 2021, a double page with a table of means and medians and a boxplot, and a final page of method notes naming the reading speed assumed.%%

> ## Seventeen minutes, or five point six
> *Portmere Civic Forum · note for the plain-language group*
>
> 1  In Unit 9 we used a figure of 5.6 minutes for the average privacy policy. A member has since
> 2  sent us a different academic study giving about 17 minutes for the same thing, and has
> 3  reasonably asked which of us is wrong. Neither. This is worth working through carefully,
> 4  because it is the commonest way a reader is misled by honest research.
>
> 5  **The second study.** A longitudinal analysis of privacy policies from **1996 to 2021** found
> 6  that, at an assumed reading speed of **250 words a minute**, the average policy took about
> 7  **17 minutes** to read in 2021. The longest document in its sample took about **152 minutes**.
> 8  The authors calculate that reading the policies of the services an average user actually uses
> 9  would exceed **400 hours a year**.
>
> 10 **Why the two figures differ.** At least three reasons, all visible in the methods. The
> 11 samples are different: one audited a very large number of ordinary websites, the other followed
> 12 a smaller set over twenty-five years, weighted towards major services whose policies are longer.
> 13 The years are different, and policies have grown steadily over the period. And the assumed
> 14 reading speeds differ, which alone can move a figure by half. None of these is an error. They
> 15 are different questions with the same headline.
>
> 16 **What both studies agree on.** That the documents have grown; that the growth is driven by
> 17 the number of separate matters each policy must address rather than by verbosity in any single
> 18 sentence; and that no plausible reading speed makes the total obligation achievable for any
> 19 person.
>
> 20 **The figure that does not depend on the dispute.** Four hundred hours is ten working weeks.
> 21 Even if the true figure were a quarter of that, it would be two and a half working weeks a
> 22 year, and the conclusion would be the same. When two studies disagree by a factor of three and
> 23 the argument survives the lower figure, the disagreement does not matter and we should say so
> 24 rather than pretending to adjudicate it.
>
> 25 **What this means for our own documents.** Every clause in them was added by somebody with a
> 26 reason. Not one of them was added by somebody who had read the whole thing afterwards.
>
> *Figures: Privacy Policies Across the Ages, 1996–2021 (arXiv 2201.08739). The 5.6-minute figure
> in Unit 9 comes from a different study with a different sample and method; the two are not
> comparable and neither is in error.*

### 1 · Articles: *a*, *the* and zero

**1B  Underline the articles. Which one points at something already identified, and is there

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Article | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **a / an** | one of a class, not yet identified | You must obtain **a** receipt. |
| **the** | a particular one, identified or unique | **The** receipt must be dated. |
| **zero** | a general class, or an abstract noun | **Carpets** must be left clean. |
| **the** + singular, generic | the class as a type (formal) | **The tenant** shall be responsible. |
| **a** + superlative-like standard | names a required standard | left to **a professional standard** |

> **Grammar Note** — In a contract, *a* creates a thing that must be obtained; *the* presupposes a
> thing already defined elsewhere; zero article describes a class or a state. "The cleaning of the
> carpets" uses *the* for something the document never defines, which is why it can be read two
> ways. Where a standard matters, it must be named: *to a professional standard*, *to the standard
> recorded in the inventory*.

### 2 · Quantifiers and scope

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Quantifier | With | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **much / little** | uncountable | **little** evidence |
| **many / few** | countable plural | **few** deductions |
| **a few / a little** | positive, some | **a few** marks |
| **plenty of / a great deal of** | both | **a great deal of** wear |
| **each / every** | singular countable | **each** room *(singly)* / **every** room *(no exceptions)* |
| **either / neither** | two | **neither** reading is excluded |
| **all / both / whole** | sets | **all** receipts / **the whole** carpet |
| **any** | free choice, or in negatives | **any** reasonable cost |

> **Grammar Note** — *Few* and *little* are near-negative: *few deductions* means hardly any; *a
> few deductions* means some. In a clause that difference is money. And *any* in a permission
> ("may deduct any reasonable costs") gives the other side the choice, while *the reasonable
> costs* implies a defined set.

### %%AUG a%% Language connection — the clause that specifies its own evidence

Complete the rule from the examples.

> **Ambiguous:** "The tenant shall be responsible for the cleaning of the carpets."
> **The question:** *What evidence would satisfy you that this obligation has been met?*
>
> **Answer A:** "A receipt from a professional cleaner." → *They have just told you the clause
> means a purchase, in writing.*
> **Answer B:** "That they're clean on inspection." → *They have just told you it means a state.*
> **Answer C:** silence. → *They cannot say, and a clause whose own author cannot state its
> evidence is one you can ask to have amended.*

**Complete:** You cannot settle an ambiguity by arguing about the ______________ , because both
readings are available to both sides. What you can do is ask what ______________ would satisfy the
obligation, in ______________ . Any answer specifies the clause; no answer shows that it specifies
______________ , which is itself the thing to put in your next letter.

**Now write the evidence question** for each clause:

1. The tenant shall keep the garden in good order.
2. The employee shall be available outside normal hours as required.
3. Students must attend regularly.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How a written contract relates to what was agreed varies. In some legal traditions the document is
the whole agreement and anything said at the viewing has no standing; in others, custom and the
parties' conduct count, and a clause that nobody has ever enforced may be held to have lapsed. In
some countries an ambiguity is construed against whoever drafted it, which is a powerful thing for
a tenant to know; in others it is not. And in many places asking for something in writing is
normal, while in others it is read as an accusation of bad faith.

**Discuss:** where you have lived, what counts — the document, or what was said? Who does an
ambiguity favor?

> ### Letter about a deposit deduction
>
> I am writing about the deduction of 185 pounds for cleaning from my deposit, and I would like to keep
> this to one question.
>
> The clause in the agreement reads, in full: *the tenant shall be responsible for the cleaning of
> the carpets at the end of the tenancy.* I asked your office what evidence would satisfy that
> obligation, and I was told by email on the 14th that it means the carpets are to be left *clean
> to the standard recorded in the check-in inventory*. I am grateful for that, and I am relying on
> it.
>
> The check-in inventory, which I have attached, records the carpets as *fair, light staining to
> the hallway*. I have also attached four photographs taken on the day I handed back the keys,
> dated by the file information, which show light staining to the hallway and nothing else. I am
> not claiming the carpets improved during the tenancy. I am claiming only that their condition at
> check-out is the condition the inventory records at check-in, which is the standard your own
> email names.
>
> I would add that no receipt or invoice for the cleaning accompanies the deduction statement.
> Under the scheme's rules a deduction should be itemized and evidenced. I mention this once and I
> would rather resolve the matter with you than refer it, which is why I am writing before the
> fourteen days run out rather than after.
>
> I would therefore ask for the 185 pounds to be returned. If any part of it is retained, I would ask for
> the invoice and a statement of which part of the inventory standard was not met.
>
> One final point, which is not about my deposit. Within a fortnight this clause has been read two
> ways inside your own office. If it read *the carpets shall be left clean to the standard recorded
> in the inventory*, this correspondence would not exist. I would be glad to think the next tenant
> gets that version.

> **Writer's Tip** — Quote the clause in full and italicize it. Quote back what you were told, with
> the date. Then make the narrowest possible claim, attach the evidence, and ask one question that
> can be answered yes. Mention a third party once, without threatening, and never as your opening.
