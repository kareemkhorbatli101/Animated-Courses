### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When a statistic surprises you, look for the definition of the thing being
> counted. A very large number often means the definition is very wide, and a wide definition is
> not a trick — but it changes what the number is evidence of.

%%FIG x05-f5 | A four-page report briefing: a cover with a title and a year, a page of three findings with a small world map, a page carrying a definition box in a tinted panel, and a final page with a short list and a source line.%%

> ## The largest workforce nobody counts
> *Portmere Voluntary Sector Forum · briefing note 11*
>
> 1  New global estimates put the number of people doing volunteer work at around **2.1 billion
> 2  a month** — about **34.5 per cent** of the world's working-age population. If that figure is
> 3  right, roughly one working-age adult in three does unpaid work for somebody outside their own
> 4  household every month, and almost none of it appears in any economic measure.
>
> 5  **What counts.** Under the international standard, volunteer work is non-compulsory work
> 6  performed for others without pay. The statistical threshold is **one hour in a four-week
> 7  period**. One hour. That is deliberately low, and it is why the number is so large: it counts
> 8  the neighbor who collects a prescription as well as the person who runs a project.
>
> 9  **What it does not count well.** Fewer than seventy countries collect official statistics on
> 10 volunteering, most of them wealthy ones, and they use different definitions and different
> 11 reference periods. The global figure is therefore modelled rather than counted. It is the
> 12 best estimate available and it is an estimate.
>
> 13 **Why it matters here.** Services in this city are underpinned by people doing one or two
> 14 hours a week. That is a strength and a risk at once: it is cheap, it is local, and it depends
> 15 on individuals whose circumstances change. A project built on goodwill has no notice period.
>
> *Figures: ILO and UNV, State of the World's Volunteerism Report 2026; ILOSTAT.*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Yes/no** | Have + subject + past participle | **Have you had** the training? |
| **Short answer** | | Yes, I have. / No, I haven't. |
| **With *ever*** | Have + subject + **ever** + participle | **Have you ever done** the course? |
| **Wh-** | How long / How many times + have | **How long have you** been here? |
| **Negative** | haven't / hasn't | I **haven't** signed it. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Have you ever …?* asks about a whole life. *Have you …?* asks about now,
> or about this period. "Have you ever worked nights?" is about your history. "Have you worked
> nights?" is probably about this month, and the listener will decide which from the situation.

### 3 · Present perfect or past simple?

**3A  Study the chart.**

| Situation | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time period **not finished** | present perfect | I've done three rounds **this week**. |
| Time period **finished** | past simple | I did three rounds **last week**. |
| **When?** is asked or implied | past simple | I **started** in March. |
| The result is what matters | present perfect | I'**ve lost** the key. (it is still lost) |

> **Grammar Note** — *This morning* is present perfect if it is still morning and past simple if
> it is not. The tense depends on the clock, not on the words.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

In some places, helping is a family and neighbor matter and the idea of signing an agreement to
help somebody is faintly insulting. In others the agreement is what protects the volunteer, and
refusing to sign one is the warning sign. Both views are reasonable; the difference is whether you
expect the trouble to come from inside the relationship or from outside it.

**Discuss:** have you ever been asked to sign something to do a favor? How did it feel?
