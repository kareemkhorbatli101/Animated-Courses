### 2 · Read

> **Reader's Tip** — When a statistic sounds impossibly good, find the definition before you find
> the argument. Nine times out of ten the definition is doing something you did not expect, and it
> is printed, in small type, right there.

%%FIG x02-f5 | A two-page magazine spread: a headline, three columns of text, a pull-quote in large type reading fifteen hours a day, and a boxed definition at the foot of the second column with a source line under it.%%

> ## The fifteen-hour day that nobody has
> *Portmere Review · Numbers we repeat without checking*
>
> 1  You will have seen the figure. People in wealthy countries, it is said, have around
> 2  **fifteen hours a day** for leisure and personal care. It gets repeated in articles about
> 3  work-life balance, usually followed by a sentence about how we should be happier than we are.
>
> 4  The figure is real and it is not what it sounds like. The category is called *leisure and
> 5  personal care*, and the second half of that name is doing most of the work. Personal care
> 6  includes sleeping. It also includes eating, washing and getting dressed. Bundled together
> 7  with actual leisure, the aggregate comes to about fifteen hours in a twenty-four hour day.
>
> 8  Take out sleep, at a conservative seven hours, and eating and washing, at around two, and
> 9  what is left is nearer **five or six hours** — and that is before you subtract commuting,
> 10 childcare and the forty minutes most people spend on jobs around the house.
>
> 11 The figures vary between countries by about two hours, from just over fourteen to about
> 12 sixteen and a half. That is a smaller spread than you would expect, and it is smaller than
> 13 the difference between two people living on the same street.
>
> 14 None of this means the statistic is dishonest. It means it is an aggregate, and an aggregate
> 15 answers a question nobody asked. The question people actually have is *how much of today is
> 16 mine*, and no national average can answer it.
>
> *Figures: OECD Time Use Database, country data to 2024.*

<sub>**aggregate** — several things combined into one total · **bundled** — counted together with
something else · **conservative** — deliberately cautious; the low end of an estimate</sub>

### 1 · *be going to* and *will*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the opinion question.** %%AUD W4-02-06%%

> **Priya:** We're going to do the coast. I booked the coach this morning.
> **Tomás:** And if it rains?
> **Priya:** Then it'll rain and we'll be at the coast in the rain.
> **Tomás:** I'll bring an umbrella.
> **Priya:** You'll forget.
> **Tomás:** I won't forget. I promise.

*Priya has already decided. Was she right to book before the group agreed?*

**1B  Underline every future form in 1A. Which ones were decided before this conversation?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Use | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A plan already made | **be going to** | We're **going to** do the coast. |
| A decision made now | **will** | I'**ll** bring an umbrella. |
| A promise | **will** | I **won't** forget. I promise. |
| A prediction with evidence | **be going to** | Look at that sky — it'**s going to** rain. |
| A prediction, your opinion | **will** | You'**ll** forget. |

> **Grammar Note** — Both forms predict, and the difference is where the prediction comes from.
> *It's going to rain* points at the sky. *It will rain* points at the speaker. When you can see
> the evidence, *going to* sounds right; when you are only giving an opinion, *will* does.

### 3 · *very* and *too*

**3A  Study the chart.**

| | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **very** | a large amount — and that may be fine | The hall is **very** small. |
| **too** | more than is acceptable — a problem | The hall is **too** small. |
| **too … to** | with the consequence | It's **too** small **to** fit us all. |
| **enough** | the amount needed | It isn't big **enough**. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Very* describes. *Too* complains. "The coach is very full" is an
> observation; "the coach is too full" means somebody has to get off.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> ### Real Lives — Priya
>
> I was given eighty-four pounds and told to organize something, and I learned more about this
> group in two weeks than in the two years before it. Four wanted the coast. Four wanted the
> gallery. Nobody would say which until somebody else had, because the first person to speak
> becomes the person who ruined it for the other four.
>
> So I stopped asking what people wanted and started asking what they would mind least. That is a
> different question and it gets a different answer. Seven of the eight said they would mind the
> gallery least, including three who had voted for the coast.
>
> We went to the gallery. It rained all day, which made it the right answer for a reason that had
> nothing to do with the reason I chose it. Two people have told me since that they would have
> preferred the coast. Both of them were in the seven.
>
> Next time I am going to book first and announce second, and take the blame, because the blame
> was always going to be mine and at least this way it is quicker.

> **Writer's Tip** — A text that ends with what you will do differently is stronger than one that
> ends with what you learned. "I learned that groups avoid deciding" is a lesson; "next time I am
> going to book first" is a person.
