*If you serve sixty people something they did not expect, do you tell them in advance or let them

**1B  Underline the six phrasal verbs in 1A. Which ones have the object in the middle?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| Type | Can you separate it? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Separable** | yes — object between or after | put the crate **down** / put **down** the crate |
| **Separable, pronoun** | **must** separate | put it **down** (never *put down it*) |
| **Inseparable** | no — object always after | run **out of** potatoes |
| **Three-part** | no | get **on with** the job |

> **Grammar Note** — There is no rule that tells you which verbs separate. There is one rule that
> always holds: if the object is a pronoun — *it*, *them*, *him* — and the verb separates, the
> pronoun goes in the middle. *Use it up*, never *use up it*.

### 3 · Possessive pronouns

**3A  Study the chart.**

| Adjective + noun | Pronoun alone |
|---|---|

> **Grammar Note** — There is no apostrophe in *hers*, *ours*, *yours* or *theirs*. *It's* is
> always *it is* or *it has*; the possessive *its* has no apostrophe either. This is the single
> most common written error in English and it is made by everybody.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Offering food and refusing food are both governed by rules that are invisible until somebody
breaks them. In some places a first refusal is expected and the offer must be repeated twice. In
others, repeating the offer after a no is pressure. Serving staff who get this wrong are read as
rude in one direction or cold in the other, and almost nobody explains it to them.

**Discuss:** where you come from, how many times should an offer be repeated? What happened the
first time somebody here accepted the first offer?

> ### Real Lives — Hana
>
> I have cooked the Tuesday lunch for two years and I have never once cooked what I planned to

> not eat something that arrives with an apology attached to it, and they are right not to.
