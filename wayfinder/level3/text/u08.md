> **Reader's Tip** — When a health page gives you a guideline, find out what it is measured in
> before you decide whether you meet it. Most guidelines are in minutes per week, and most people
> estimate in days per week, which is why so many people are wrong about themselves.

> *Portmere Health Partnership · patient information, sheet 7*
>
> 1  The guideline is **150 minutes of moderate activity a week**, or 75 minutes of vigorous
> 2  activity, or a mixture. Moderate means you can talk but not sing. That is the whole test.
>
> 3  **How common is it not to meet it?** About **31 per cent of adults worldwide** — roughly
> 4  **1.8 billion people** — do not. That share has risen from 23.4 per cent in 2000 to 31.3 per
> 5  cent in 2022, and on current trends it is projected to reach about 35 per cent by 2030.
>
> 6  **Who is most affected.** Inactivity is more common among women, at 34 per cent against 29
> 7  per cent for men. It increases with age and it increases with the number of hours a job
> 8  requires you to sit. Most of the change over twenty years has come from work and transport
> 9  becoming more sedentary, not from people choosing to exercise less.

**1B  Underline the two *used to* forms in 1A. One is negative. What happens to the *d*?**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Statement** | used to + base verb | I **used to** smoke. |
| **Negative** | didn't use to | I **didn't use to** take the stairs. |
| **Question** | Did you use to …? | **Did you use to** walk? |
| **Still true?** | no — always finished | *Used to* always means **not now**. |

> **Grammar Note** — In the negative and the question, *used* loses its *d*, because *did* is
> already carrying the past. *Didn't used to* is extremely common in speech and is still written
> *didn't use to*.

### 2 · The present perfect continuous

**2A  Study the chart.**

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Started in the past, **still going** | I'**ve been walking** to work since June. |
| **How long**, with a continuing action | **How long have you been** waiting? |
| Explains a **present result** | I'm tired because I'**ve been standing** all day. |
| **Not** for finished, counted things | I'**ve walked** 300 km. (not *been walking*) |

> **Grammar Note** — *I've worked here for a year* and *I've been working here for a year* are
> both correct and mean almost the same. The continuous leans on the activity and the simple
> leans on the fact. The difference only becomes sharp with a number: *I've read three books* is
> finished and counted; *I've been reading* is not.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

How much you say to a doctor varies enormously. In some places you give the facts and wait to be
asked; volunteering more is seen as wasting a professional's time. In others, the consultation is
a conversation and the doctor is waiting for you to lead. Patients trained in the first style are
routinely recorded as having "no concerns", because nobody asked the question that would have
found them.

**Discuss:** which style were you brought up in? What would help you say more, or less?
