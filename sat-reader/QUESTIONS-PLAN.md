# Questions, Answer Key and Arabic Summaries — the plan

*Reading the Five Fields*, second edition. Ten SAT-style questions after every
passage, an Arabic summary after every question set, a complete answer key at the
back. 2,000 questions, 20,000 per-question check passes, 100 book-level check
passes, 200 Arabic summaries.

This plan is written before the writing, on disk, because a plan held only in a
conversation does not survive the conversation. Section 12 is reserved for the
record of what measurement changes in it, on the same principle as `PLAN.md` §11.

---

## 1. What the questions have to be

Not comprehension questions about a passage. **SAT questions**, in the forms the
test actually uses, built from a passage the student has just read.

The Reading and Writing section of the current SAT has four content domains. The
published domain weights, as the College Board describes the section, are roughly:
Information and Ideas about a quarter, Craft and Structure about a quarter,
Expression of Ideas about a fifth, Standard English Conventions about a quarter.
Two things follow for this book.

**First**, a set of ten questions can cover all four domains, and must, or the
book trains a third of the test and leaves the rest. Most reading-comprehension
question sets in circulation cover Information and Ideas only.

**Second**, two SAT question types do not look like comprehension questions at
all. Transitions questions present a sentence with a connective removed.
Rhetorical Synthesis questions present bulleted research notes and a stated goal.
Standard English Conventions questions present a sentence with a punctuation or
structure choice. These can all be built honestly from a 300-word passage — the
notes come out of the passage, the carrier sentence is the passage's own — and
building them is what makes this a test-preparation book rather than a reader with
questions attached.

## 2. The ten slots

Fixed order, the same ten in every one of the 200 sets. A fixed order is not a
stylistic choice: it is what makes "no omissions" a mechanical claim rather than an
assurance. A set is complete when slots 1 through 10 are present, each with its
prescribed type, and a check can say so.

| # | Type | SAT domain | The form |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `central_idea` | Information and Ideas | Which choice best states the main idea of the text? |
| 2 | `detail` | Information and Ideas | According to the text, … |
| 3 | `evidence` | Information and Ideas | Which quotation from the text most strongly supports the claim that …? All four options are verbatim quotations. |
| 4 | `inference` | Information and Ideas | Which choice most logically completes the text? |
| 5 | `words_in_context` | Craft and Structure | As used in the text, what does *word* most nearly mean? |
| 6 | `structure` | Craft and Structure | Which choice best describes the function of … / the overall structure of the text? |
| 7 | `cross_text` | Craft and Structure | Two texts: this passage and its sibling one level away in the same strand. How would one author respond to the other? |
| 8 | `transitions` | Expression of Ideas | Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition? |
| 9 | `boundaries` | Standard English Conventions | Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English? |
| 10 | `synthesis` | Expression of Ideas | Notes, a goal, and four candidate sentences. |

Three of these slots exist only because of how this book is built, and they are
the reason the questions are worth more than a generic set.

**Slot 5 is the cross-link to Book 1.** The target word is the passage's declared
`vocab_link` word — one of the 200 target words of *Words in Context*. A student
meets `attenuate` as a vocabulary item in Book 1, meets it in running prose here,
and is then asked about it in the SAT's own words-in-context form. Three
encounters, one word, two books.

**Slot 7 uses the strands.** Every passage has a sibling one level away in the same
strand: the same subject treated as phenomenon, mechanism, evidence or dispute.
That is a genuine paired-text relationship, which is what the SAT's Cross-Text
Connections questions test and what a set of unrelated passages cannot provide.
The sibling is named by passage number and its position is summarized in the stem,
so the question stands alone for a student working one page at a time.

**Slot 9 keeps the content tie.** Standard English Conventions questions on the
real test are about sentences on any subject. Here the carrier sentence is built
from the passage's own content, so a student practising punctuation is also
rehearsing the material.

## 3. How difficulty ladders

The SAT labels items easy, medium and hard. This book assigns difficulty **by
slot, fixed per level**, so that the ladder is visible and both the per-question
check and the level total are exact.

| Level | easy | medium | hard | The map, by slot |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Foundation | 5 | 4 | 1 | easy 1,2,5,8,9 · medium 3,4,6,10 · hard 7 |
| 2 Developing | 3 | 5 | 2 | easy 1,2,9 · medium 3,4,5,6,8 · hard 7,10 |
| 3 Target | 2 | 5 | 3 | easy 2,9 · medium 1,3,4,5,8 · hard 6,7,10 |
| 4 Stretch | 1 | 4 | 5 | easy 9 · medium 1,2,4,5 · hard 3,6,7,8,10 |

Book totals: 275 easy, 450 medium, 275 hard, plus 1,000 at the level-dependent
positions — in full, 550 easy, 900 medium, 550 hard across 2,000 questions.

Three of the ten slots are **anchors** and hold one difficulty at every level:
slot 7, the cross-text pair, is hard throughout, because reading two passages
against each other is the hardest thing the book asks; slot 9, the Standard
English carrier, is easy throughout, because a comma splice is a comma splice at
any level; and slot 4, the inference, sits at medium throughout. The other seven
slots move with the level, and that movement is what produces the ladder in the
table above. An earlier draft of this plan stated the check in section 10 as
"every slot carries at least one easy and one hard somewhere", which the map
above cannot satisfy and was never meant to; the check reads as it does below
instead.

Difficulty here is a **design position, not a measurement**. A real SAT difficulty
label comes from how students performed on the item. Nothing in this book can
produce that, and §11 says so plainly rather than implying otherwise.

## 4. How the four options are built

One key, three distractors, and the distractors are the whole craft. A question
whose wrong answers are obviously wrong teaches nothing.

**The distractor taxonomy.** Every set of three draws from these, and no two
distractors in one question use the same move:

- **True but not asked.** A statement the passage supports which does not answer
  the stem. The commonest real-SAT distractor and the hardest to resist.
- **Right scope, wrong direction.** Reverses a cause, a comparison or a sign.
- **Overreach.** The key's claim pushed to always, never, proves, the main reason.
- **Underreach.** True and trivial, so it answers less than was asked.
- **Imported.** Plausible on the subject and absent from the passage.
- **Detail swap.** One name, number or date exchanged for another from the passage.

**Mechanical rules, all checked.** Four options labelled A to D. Exactly one key.
No duplicate option, and no option contained inside another. No *all of the
above*, *none of the above*, *both A and B*. No negative stem, because a negative
stem tests the reading of the stem. Option lengths within a ratio of 2.4, so that
length is not a tell, and the key is the longest option in no more than 30 per cent
of questions and the shortest in no more than 30 per cent.

**Key-letter balance.** Each of A, B, C and D takes 22 to 28 per cent of the 2,000
keys, 18 to 32 per cent within every level, and 15 to 35 per cent within every
field. No more than three identical keys in a row inside a set. No slot carries the
same letter in more than 40 per cent of its 200 questions. A student who learns
that the answer is usually C has learned something about the author, not the test.

**Self-containment.** Every question is answerable from the passage on the facing
page, and nothing else. The check for this is mechanical: the content words of the
key appear in the passage or in the stem.

## 5. The answer key

Appendix E, 2,000 rows, in passage order. Each row carries the question number,
the slot type, the domain, the difficulty, the key letter, and two short pieces of
writing:

- **`why`** — 8 to 45 words on what makes the key right, naming its content rather
  than its letter. "B is correct" is not an explanation.
- **`trap`** — 5 to 30 words on the most attractive wrong answer and the move it
  makes, naming its letter.

A key that says only which letter is right is a scoring sheet. The `trap` line is
the part a student learns from, because the distractor that nearly worked is where
the lesson is.

## 6. The Arabic summaries

After each question set, before the next passage: a summary in Modern Standard
Arabic, in four declared parts, 90 to 200 Arabic words in all.

| Part | Field | What it carries |
|---|---|---|
| خلاصة | `khulasa` | What the passage says, in three or four sentences. |
| المعنى | `maana` | What it means — the idea under the facts, not a restatement. |
| الأهمية في المجال | `ahammiyya` | Why it matters in its field, and where it sits in that field. |
| الصلة بالاختبار | `sila` | What this is likely to look like on the test: which question types it feeds, which trap it sets. At least 15 words. |

This is not a translation of the passage. A translation would let a student avoid
the English, which is the opposite of the purpose. It is a summary of the content
and its significance, written so that a student whose first language is Arabic can
confirm that the material landed, and can see what the test will want from it.

The fourth part is the one that justifies the other three. It names the relation
to the test — that this passage's strand supplies paired texts, that its vocabulary
word is a likely words-in-context item, that its structure is the kind the test
asks about.

**Checked mechanically**: presence, the four parts, word count, at least 92 per cent
Arabic script by character, no Latin leakage outside a declared allowlist of names
and terms, the field named in Arabic, the level's move named in Arabic, the test
named in Arabic, no two summaries identical, and right-to-left marking in the
document build.

## 7. The 10 check passes per question

Ten per question, 2,000 questions, 20,000 passes. Each pass is a named, mechanical
test that can fail.

| | Pass | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | **identity** | Id well-formed, unique, and agreeing with its passage and slot index. |
| Q2 | **slot** | The type is the canonical type for that slot, and every field that type requires is present. |
| Q3 | **options** | Exactly four, labelled A to D in order, none empty, none a fragment of another. |
| Q4 | **key** | Exactly one key, a letter in A to D, resolving to a real option. |
| Q5 | **distinctness** | No duplicate option, no containment, none of the banned option forms, no two distractors using the same taxonomy move. |
| Q6 | **shape** | Option length ratio inside the cap; the key not a length outlier; option punctuation uniform. |
| Q7 | **stem** | Form and length; ends as its type prescribes; no second person, no contractions, no negative stem, no British forms. |
| Q8 | **grounding** | Every quoted span verbatim in the passage, plus the type-specific tie: the words-in-context target is the passage's `vocab_link` word and appears in it; the transitions carrier is a real sentence pair; every synthesis note is traceable; the cross-text sibling exists, in the same strand, one level away; the boundaries carrier is built from passage content. |
| Q9 | **rationale** | `why` and `trap` present and inside their word bands; `trap` names a letter; neither contains British forms or second person. |
| Q10 | **difficulty** | The label is valid and is the one the level's map assigns to that slot. |

## 8. The 100 book-level check passes

Ten families of ten. Reported separately because none of them is a property of a
single question.

**A. Completeness (1–10).** 200 sets present · every set exactly ten questions ·
2,000 ids unique · every id agrees with its passage · slot order canonical in every
set · all ten types present and none repeated in a set · 200 Arabic summaries · 20
question files, one per field and level · ten sets in each file · global question
numbering 1 to 2,000 contiguous.

**B. Option integrity (11–20).** Four options everywhere · labels A to D everywhere ·
one key everywhere · key letters valid · no duplicate option inside a question ·
no option contained in another · no banned option form anywhere · length ratio

**C. Key balance (21–30).** A, B, C and D each 22 to 28 per cent book-wide (four

each 15 to 35 per cent within every field (two checks) · no more than three

**D. Difficulty (31–40).** Each level's easy, medium and hard totals exactly as

**E. Stem quality (41–50).** Every stem ends as its type prescribes · stem length 6

repeated inside a strand · no second person · no contractions · no British forms ·

**F. Grounding (51–60).** Every quoted span in a stem verbatim in its passage ·

**H. Arabic (71–80).** 200 summaries · four parts in each · 90 to 200 Arabic words ·

**I. Document (81–90).** One passage to a page still · questions begin on the page

**J. No regression (91–100).** The 600 passage-level passes still green · the 8

round-trips through YAML without loss.

## 9. What the checks cannot do

They cannot tell whether a question is **good**. A question can satisfy all ten
passes and still be dull, or ambiguous between two options in a way no mechanical
test detects. The defence against that is the same as for the passages: the
document is read end to end before it is called finished, and the reading is where
defects of judgment get caught.

They cannot establish **difficulty**. The labels are design positions, as §3 says.

They cannot verify that an Arabic summary is **well written**. The checks establish
that it is Arabic, that it is the right length, that it has its four parts, and that
it names its field, its move and the test. Whether the Arabic reads naturally is a
judgment, and a native reader should make it.

## 10. The document

The existing layout is unchanged: one passage to a page, with its running head,
margin code, title, 300 words and anchor box. After it:

- a **questions page** for that passage — ten questions, numbered `n.1` to `n.10`,
  each with its type and difficulty in a small label, four options, and nothing
  that reveals the key;
- an **Arabic summary block** closing the set, right-to-left, in a serif Arabic
  face, with the four parts headed in Arabic.

At the back, after Appendix D: **Appendix E, the answer key**, 2,000 rows in
passage order, grouped by passage, each row carrying the number, the type, the
difficulty, the key letter, the `why` and the `trap`. Expected total length about
900 to 1,000 pages: one page of passage, two of questions and one of Arabic summary for
each of the 200 passages, plus the front matter, the four original appendices and about a
hundred and ten pages of answer key.

## 12. What the measurement changed in this plan

On the same principle as `PLAN.md` §11: a claim in a plan that does not survive
contact with measurement is recorded, not quietly corrected.

**The no-duplicate-stem rule contradicted the test it imitates.** Four of the ten
slots have a fixed stem on the real SAT, word for word: *Which choice best states
the main idea of the text?*, *Which choice most logically completes the text?*,
*Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?*, and *Which
choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard
English?* Writing two hundred variations of those would make the book less like the
test. The rule now applies to the six passage-specific slots, and §2's canonical
wording is **required** for the other four and checked.

**The containment rule failed every Standard English Conventions item.** Those four
options differ only in punctuation by design, so one is always inside another.
Boundaries items are now held to their own rule: the options must differ *only* in
punctuation.

**The stem minimum of eight words forbade the real inference stem**, which is seven.
Lowered to six.

**Self-containment cannot mean that every content word of a key appears in the
passage.** A central-idea or inference key must paraphrase; that is what it is. The
check now forbids importing a *particular* — a name, a number or a date the text
never supplies — which is what an outside-knowledge question actually smuggles in.

**The words-in-context target is tied to the word family, not the exact form.**
Eleven passages use an inflection of their Book 1 link word and one, HIS-S04-L1,
uses a cognate: *opposite* for *opposed*. Demanding the exact Book 1 form would make
the question unanswerable from the text. 188 of the 200 links are exact; the check
now requires the target to appear verbatim in the passage **and** to share a stem
with the link word.

**The negative-stem rule flagged ordinary English.** It rejected *the sentence
reporting that Douglass did not reject the founding documents*. A negative stem is
one whose **task** is negated, so the rule now requires the negation to govern the
interrogative, and still catches NOT, EXCEPT, *least*, *neither* and *which choice …
does not*.

**The distractor taxonomy was too small.** Six moves could not cover evidence items
or Standard English items without repeating within a question. It now runs to
thirteen, with `near_miss`, `restatement`, `comma_splice`, `run_on`, `wrong_mark`,
`misplaced` and `unpaired` added.

**The one-passage-a-page check had the wrong scope.** Appendix E prints every
passage's field and strand code beside its answer rows, so the rule applies to the
body only.

**Arabic is verified against the .docx, not the PDF.** `pdftotext` reorders the
lam-alef ligature, so an exact match against the text layer fails on correctly
rendered Arabic. The render is held to the weaker claim that one page per set carries
Arabic script.

**The expected page count rose from 640–700 to 900–1,000.** Measured: 233 pages of
passages, plus two pages of questions and one of Arabic summary for each of the 200,
plus about a hundred and ten pages of answer key.

**The key-letter plan was added, and it earned its place.** One base pattern rotated
by a letter for each successive set in a file gives every letter exactly 25 of the
100 keys in that file — and so a quarter of every field, every level and the book —
while holding each slot to at most 30 per cent on one letter. `qemit` reports any
departure from it, and caught fourteen drifted keys in the first twenty sets.
