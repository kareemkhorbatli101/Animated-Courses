@PART 2 | Grammar in the newsroom | The present. Half the practice is controlled; half is your own words.
@LABEL MODEL | The four structures
@GRAMMAR 1 · the verb be — am / is / are
{{I am}} a correspondent.     {{We are}} journalists.
{{She is}} the world editor.     {{They are}} at the desk.
Negative: She [[isn't]] a photographer.     Question: [[Are]] you a correspondent?

@GRAMMAR 2 · present simple — daily work and facts
{{I work}} for Atlas Wire.     {{He files}} at six. (he/she/it + -s)
Correspondents {{report}} from many countries.     She {{writes}} the news.
Negative: I [[don't]] work Sundays.     Question: [[Do]] you work here?

@GRAMMAR 3 · have got — for things you have
{{I have got}} a notebook.     {{She has got}} a press card.
Question: [[Have]] you got a visa?

@GRAMMAR 4 · there is / there are
{{There is}} a bureau in Nairobi. (one)     {{There are}} three correspondents. (more than one)
Negative: There [[isn't]] a photographer today.     Question: [[Is there]] a deadline?

@WATCHOUT With he / she / it, add -s: *she files* (not *she file*). Use *have got / has got* (not *haves*). Use *there is* for one, *there are* for more than one.

@READING A free press, around the world
Journalism crosses borders. A story that starts in one country is read in fifty. This is big news for journalists, and for everyone.
Every year, Reporters Without Borders publishes the World Press Freedom Index. It ranks 180 countries and territories. Some countries rise; some countries fall. The Index does not say that one country is good and another is bad. It asks a simpler question: can a journalist do the work, and is the journalist safe?
In many places, the answer is still no. Every year on 1 December, the Committee to Protect Journalists counts the journalists who are in prison for their work. In recent years, that number has been more than three hundred. UNESCO reports that when a journalist is killed, nobody is punished in about nine of every ten cases.
There is also good news. In 1991, journalists met in Windhoek and wrote a declaration for a free press. In 1993, the United Nations named 3 May World Press Freedom Day. In 2012, the United Nations agreed a Plan of Action on the Safety of Journalists. And 2 November is now the International Day to End Impunity for Crimes against Journalists.
Journalists also work together. Reporters in many countries share documents and check each other's facts. One story, forty newsrooms, one deadline: this is normal now.
The work is the same in every language. Find the story. Check the facts. Name the source. Be fair, and be kind. A free press is not a building. It is a habit — and journalists keep it, together.
