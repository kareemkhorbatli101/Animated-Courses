@PART 2 | Grammar: the past | Past simple for the events, past continuous for the background.
@FIG u6-gA | Past simple — the events, one after another.
@GRAMMAR 1 · Past simple — what happened
Regular: add {{-ed}}: open → {{opened}}, return → {{returned}}, start → {{started}}.
Irregular: come → {{came}}, go → {{went}}, be → {{was / were}}, say → {{said}}, build → {{built}}.
The crossing {{opened}} last spring.  Families {{came}} back.  It {{was}} a happy day.

@FIG u6-gB | Past continuous — what was happening in the background.
@GRAMMAR 2 · Past continuous — what was happening
Form: {{was / were}} + verb{{-ing}}.
When Mira arrived, people {{were crossing}} and children {{were running}}.
Use it for the background — a longer action that was going on.

@GRAMMAR 3 · Time words
{{ago}}: seven years {{ago}}.   {{last}}: {{last}} week / {{last}} spring.   {{yesterday}}.
{{when}} + past simple: {{When}} the barrier went up, people were waiting.
{{while}} = at the same time: {{While}} the workers repaired the road, families returned.

@PART 5 | Reading: the feature | Two readings. Read for the events and the order.
@FIG u6-read | A feature tells the whole story — from how it was, to how it is.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · a feature about the seven years
@READING The year the crossing came back
Seven years ago, the bridge at Kalina closed. The barrier came down one evening, and it stayed down. The road was empty. The stalls disappeared. The old gate at the end of the bridge rusted in the rain, and for a long time the only sound on the river was the water.
Then, last spring, things began to change. First, a small team of workers arrived and looked at the bridge. While they were measuring the rail, people on both sides watched them from their windows. Nobody said very much, but everybody understood.
The repairs took four months. Workers rebuilt the road surface and painted the rail. In April, the barrier went up. "I opened my stall for the first time in seven years," one stall-holder remembered. "While I was setting up, families started to arrive. Everyone was smiling."
When the school bus crossed the bridge in the autumn, forty children returned to class on the other side. Their teacher said that it was the happiest day of the year. After that, more stalls opened, and a young family started a small café at the end of the bridge.
Today, the crossing is busy again. According to the Crossing Office, a hundred families have now returned to the valley. "For seven years, we were waiting," Elena Marku, the café owner, said. "Now the door is open, and the coffee is hot. The valley came back."

@SUBHEAD Reading B | · writing a story about the past — four tips
@READING Four tips for a past story
**1. Use the past simple for the events.** What happened, one thing after another: 'The crossing opened. Families came back.'
**2. Use the past continuous for the background.** What was going on at the time: 'While the workers were painting, families were arriving.'
**3. Use time words to show the order.** 'Seven years ago… Then… When… After that…' The reader follows the story.
**4. Keep it accurate.** Check the dates and the numbers. Name a source. Use the eyewitness's exact words in a quote.

@PART 8 | Ethics: a true story | A story about the past must still be accurate and kind.
@FIG u6-ethA | Report what you can confirm — not what sounds bigger.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · the number grows
@CASE Mira is writing the story of the day the crossing reopened. One stall-holder tells her, "I think ten thousand people crossed that day — maybe more!" It makes a great line, and the story is due soon. But nobody counted, and the Crossing Office says the real number was much smaller. Mira cannot confirm ten thousand. She is a journalist, and she knows the Code: report what you can confirm, and do not make a past event bigger than it was. What does Mira write?
