@PART 2 | Grammar: relative clauses | who for people, which/that for things, where for places.
@FIG u10-gA | who = people · which / that = things.
@GRAMMAR 1 · who / which / that
{{who}} for people: The correspondent {{who}} wrote the story is Mira.
{{which}} / {{that}} for things: The story {{that}} everyone read.  The photograph {{which}} we chose.
We use them to say which person or thing we mean.

@FIG u10-gB | where — for places.
@GRAMMAR 2 · where
{{where}} for places: Kalina is the town {{where}} the families came back.
This is the desk {{where}} the team works.
Use *where* to join a place to what happened there.

@GRAMMAR 3 · Joining two ideas
Two sentences: Mira is a correspondent. She wrote the story.
One sentence: Mira is a correspondent {{who}} wrote the story.
Joining ideas makes your writing flow, and is better to read.

@SUBHEAD Reading A | · the story that brought a town home
@READING A crossing comes home
**By Mira Sandoval, Atlas Wire · KALINA**
Kalina, the town that was quiet for seven years, is a home again. The bridge, which reopened last April, is busy every morning, and the families who left have come back.
The correspondent who followed the story all year is Mira Sandoval. "I have watched this valley change," she said. "The road that was empty is full of children, and the stalls that disappeared have come back."
Much of the work was done by the volunteers who live here, on both sides of the river. Elena Marku, who owns the café at the end of the bridge, is one of them. "It is our home," she said — words that many people in the valley now repeat.
According to the Crossing Office, a hundred families have returned. There is still work to do: the clinic, which is closed, has not reopened yet. But the change is easy to see.
This is the front page that tells the story — a story about a place where, after seven quiet years, the people came home. As Elena said, and as the headline now reads: the crossing has come home.

@SUBHEAD Reading B | · making a front page — the parts
@READING The parts of a front page
**1. Masthead and headline.** The masthead is the paper's name at the top. The headline is the big title that people read first.
**2. Byline and dateline.** The byline names the writer. The dateline says where the report comes from — on an international page, it is the first thing a reader looks for.
**3. Lead, photograph and caption.** The lead is the first sentence, which gives the five Ws. A strong photograph, and a caption which says what the photograph shows.
**4. Edit and check.** Before you publish, edit the page: check every fact, every name, and every quote. A front page that has a mistake loses trust.

@PART 8 | Ethics: the whole Code | The front page brings the whole Code together: accurate, fair, and kind.
@FIG g-code | Five words that have held this course together, and now hold one page together.
@FIG u10-ethA | The lead story is the important one — not the loudest one.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · which story leads?
@CASE The team has two stories. One is the town that has come home — an important, true story that helps readers in many countries understand the year. The other is a small, shocking claim that would be shared more widely, but that nobody has checked. An editor says, "Put the shocking one on the front — it will travel further." But it is a rumour, and it is not the story that matters most. The Code says: choose the story that is important and true, not the one that is loud. Which story does the team put on the front page?

Before you publish, edit it. Check every fact, every name, and every quote. Be accurate, fair, and kind. Lead with the story that is important and true — not the one that is loud. That is the whole Code, on one page.

It has a masthead, a headline, a byline and a dateline.
The lead gives the five Ws.
It has one exact quote, with a named source.
It uses the past, the passive, or the present perfect correctly.
It uses at least two relative clauses.
It is edited: every fact, name, and quote is checked.
It is accurate, fair, and kind.
