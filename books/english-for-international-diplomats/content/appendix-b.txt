@ANSHEAD Exercise 19: Group Discussion: What Makes a Good First Impression?
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): The best interactions were the ones where the officer greeted the visitor by name, explained what would happen next, and did not rush. Naming the next step is what made them feel professional rather than merely polite. The visitors who were made to wait without explanation reacted to the silence rather than the delay.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): A: The interactions that worked best shared three features. First, the officer used the visitor's name and title correctly from the beginning, which signals that the visit was expected and prepared. Second, the officer explained the sequence — identification, register, notification, escort — so the visitor was never left wondering what came next. Third, the pace was calm. Rushing communicates that the visitor is an interruption rather than the reason the desk exists. A fourth feature is harder to name but just as visible: the officer never looked at the computer while the visitor was speaking. Attention is the one part of a reception that cannot be delegated to a procedure.
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): "Welcome to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs" and "May I see your identification, please?" sounded genuinely professional. Phrases like "Okay, go ahead" or "Just wait there" sounded too informal for a ministry reception desk. The register of a government reception desk is closer to that of a formal letter than of a conversation.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): A: "Welcome to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs," "May I see your identification, please?" and "I will notify the office right away" all sound professional because they are complete, polite, and specific. What sounded awkward was anything abbreviated — "Okay," "Go ahead," "Wait there" — because it borrows the register of a shop counter rather than a government institution. A ministry desk is not a counter, and the language should make that difference audible.
@QHEAD Question 3 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): If a reception officer looks up, makes eye contact, and stands to greet the visitor, the visitor already feels expected. If the officer continues typing and does not look up, no polite phrase afterwards can repair that first impression.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): A: If a reception officer makes eye contact, stands, and turns fully towards the visitor, the visitor feels expected before a single word is spoken. The reverse is equally powerful: continuing to type, answering a phone first, or speaking without looking up communicates that the visitor is an interruption. In a ministry, where the officer represents the state, posture and attention are part of the professional message. Everything else the officer says afterwards is heard through the impression those first seconds created.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 31: Understanding Cultural Differences in First Meetings
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): French diplomats are usually direct and comfortable with a firm handshake and early first-name use. Japanese officials expect formal titles, a slight bow, business cards exchanged with both hands, and a slower, more ceremonial opening.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): Speaker A: French diplomats generally open with a firm handshake, direct eye contact, and a brief exchange of pleasantries before moving to substance; first names may follow quickly once the relationship is established. Japanese practice is more ceremonial: titles are used throughout, a slight bow accompanies or replaces the handshake, business cards are offered and received with both hands and read before being put away, and the opening conversation is deliberately unhurried. Treating the second as a slower version of the first is the common mistake. The error is almost always one of pace rather than of politeness, which is why it is easy to make.
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): With the French attaché I would be warm and efficient: welcome her by name, confirm the appointment, and move quickly to the purpose of the visit. With the Japanese vice-minister I would use "Your Excellency" throughout and speak more slowly and formally.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): Speaker A: With the French attaché the language can be warm and economical — "Welcome, Ms. Martin. Your eleven o'clock with the Cultural Relations Division is confirmed." With the Japanese vice-minister the register shifts: "Your Excellency, it is a great honor to welcome you to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs." The second opening expresses honor and gratitude explicitly, avoids abbreviation, and leaves space for His Excellency to set the pace before any practical matter is raised. The practical rule is to open with honor and gratitude and let the business arrive when he raises it.
@QHEAD Question 3 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): With a French visitor I expect a firm handshake, direct eye contact, and a fairly brisk pace. With a senior Japanese official I expect a bow, more physical distance, cards exchanged with both hands, and a slower, calmer rhythm throughout.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): Speaker A: With the French attaché: a firm handshake, direct and sustained eye contact, an upright but relaxed posture, and a brisk, businesslike pace. With the Japanese vice-minister: a slight bow at the right depth, a respectful distance rather than physical contact, business cards presented and received with both hands, eye contact that is present but less insistent, and an unhurried rhythm. Standing when His Excellency enters and remaining standing until he is seated matters more than any phrase. The underlying principle is that in the second context the ceremony is the substance rather than a preliminary to it.

@H2 Unit 2: Unit 2: Facilitating Meetings — Coordinating with Foreign Counterparts
@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Pair Discussion – Behind a Successful Meeting
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): The hardest part is managing changes at the last minute. A room becomes unavailable, a flight is delayed, or an extra participant joins, and everything else has to be adjusted quickly without the delegation noticing.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): The hardest part is managing late changes without letting them become visible to the visiting delegation. Rooms fall through, flights slip, interpretation is requested at the last moment, and a participant's clearance is missing. Each of these is solvable alone, but they tend to arrive together, and the officer has to re-sequence everything while still sounding calm on the phone.
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): Poor preparation signals that the partnership is not taken seriously. A delegation that waits in the wrong room or receives the agenda the night before will remember that more clearly than anything agreed in the session.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): Poor preparation signals disrespect even when none is intended. A late agenda means the visiting side cannot brief its own minister properly; a missing interpreter means the technical specialists cannot contribute. The damage is cumulative: partners who experience two badly run sessions begin to route substantive work through other channels, and that is very difficult to reverse. Recovering a partner's confidence after that takes considerably longer than the preparation would have taken.
@QHEAD Question 3 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Sample: A visiting delegation feels well-coordinated when they arrive and find their names printed correctly, a room that is already set up, an agenda they received days earlier, and someone waiting at the entrance who knows who they are. The name cards matter most: getting a title wrong in front of a delegation lead is the one error that is noticed immediately.
@ANSHEAD Exercise 2: Group Brainstorm – The Liaison Officer's Work
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): Before the meeting, the liaison officer must confirm the date, time, and venue with both sides, prepare and circulate the agenda, arrange security clearances, prepare the protocol brief for the delegation lead, and confirm the room setup.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): Before the meeting, the liaison officer confirms date, time and venue with both sides; prepares and circulates the agenda far enough in advance for both ministries to brief their own principals; arranges security clearances for every foreign participant; prepares the protocol brief covering names, titles and nationalities; and confirms the room setup, including seating by seniority, audiovisual equipment and interpretation booths. The officer also confirms who on the host side will take the official notes, which is easily forgotten.
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): During the meeting, the liaison officer monitors the agenda to keep the discussion on track, coordinates with the interpreters, and takes careful notes for the official record. The officer also watches the time and signals the delegation lead discreetly when a session is running over.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): During the meeting, the liaison officer monitors the agenda and keeps the discussion on track, coordinates with the interpreters so that technical terms are handled consistently, and takes careful notes for the official record. The officer also manages the clock, passes notes discreetly, and resolves practical problems without interrupting the session. None of this is visible to the delegation, which is the measure of whether it was done well.
@QHEAD Question 3 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): After the meeting, the officer writes and circulates the post-meeting summary within twenty-four hours and follows up on the action items that both sides agreed, confirming who is responsible for each one.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): After the meeting, the officer writes the post-meeting summary and circulates it within twenty-four hours, then tracks the agreed action items until each one has an owner and a date. An unfollowed action item is how a good session quietly turns into nothing. The summary is also the only record if the two sides later remember the session differently.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 6: Group Discussion – What Must a Protocol Brief Include?
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): A useful protocol brief includes the full names, correct titles, and nationalities of everyone attending, the seating plan, the agenda with timings, and a short note on each visitor's key positions. It should fit on one or two pages so the delegation lead can actually read it.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): A useful protocol brief includes the full names, exact titles and nationalities of every participant, the seating plan by seniority, the agenda with timings, and a short summary of each visiting principal's known positions and sensitivities. It should also list forms of address, pronunciation of difficult names, and the one or two points the delegation lead must not omit. Length matters: a brief that runs to six pages will not be read before a ten o'clock session.
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): The agenda should be sent at least four or five days before the meeting, and the final version no later than forty-eight hours before. If it arrives too late, the visiting delegation cannot brief its own minister or prepare technical material properly.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): The agenda should be circulated four to five days in advance, with the final version confirmed no later than forty-eight hours before the session. Sent too late, it prevents the visiting side from briefing its own principal, from bringing the right specialists, and from clearing positions internally — so the session becomes exploratory rather than decisive. A session that becomes exploratory wastes the principals' time, which is the one resource nobody can add to.
@QHEAD Question 3 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
Level 1 (B1 Target): Common last-minute failures are room changes, missing audiovisual equipment, an absent interpreter, and an incomplete security clearance. The professional response is to inform the other side immediately and always offer a solution at the same time as the problem.
@SEP
@ANS
Level 2 (Extended): The most common last-minute failures are: room changes, missing equipment, absent interpreters, incomplete security clearances, and late additions to the participant list. The professional response is always the same shape — communicate early, state what has changed, and present the alternative in the same sentence. What damages trust is not the problem itself but discovering it on arrival. A problem announced early is a logistics note; the same problem discovered on arrival is an incident.
