@PART 2 | Grammar: comparing | Comparatives and superlatives; as…as and not as…as; too and enough.
@FIG u8-gA | -er and more · the -est and the most.
@GRAMMAR 1 · comparatives — more than one other thing
Short words: busy → {{busier}}, small → {{smaller}} ({{than}}). Longer words: {{more}} important {{than}}.
Irregular: good → {{better}}, bad → {{worse}}, far → {{further}}.
Make it bigger or smaller: {{far}} busier, {{much}} smaller, {{slightly}} higher, {{a little}} worse.

@GRAMMAR 2 · superlatives — the one above all
Short words: {{the busiest}}, {{the smallest}}. Longer: {{the most}} important.
Irregular: {{the best}}, {{the worst}}, {{the furthest}}.
Careful: "{{one of the}} busiest" is a claim you can prove; "{{the}} busiest" is a claim you must prove.

@FIG u8-gB | as…as · not as…as · too small · small enough.
@GRAMMAR 3 · as…as · too · enough
Equal: "Veren is {{as}} old {{as}} Kalina." Not equal: "It is {{not as}} busy {{as}} Kalina."
{{too}} + adjective = more than is wanted: "The figure is {{too small}} to publish alone."
adjective + {{enough}} = as much as is needed: "The case is {{clear enough}} to lead the piece."

@PART 5 | Reading: seven crossings, compared | Two readings. Read for the comparisons, and for the rules that keep them honest.
@FIG g-reach | An explainer is read by people who will never see the place. Scale is all they have.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · the basin, in proportion
@READING Is a hundred and twelve a lot? The Vara basin in proportion
There are seven crossings on the river Vara, and almost nobody outside the valley could have named a single one of them a year ago. That is exactly the problem an explainer exists to solve.

Kalina is the busiest. Roughly two thousand people cross it in a week — more than the next two crossings together, and about twenty times more than the quietest. "Busiest is a claim, so we checked it against the office's weekly counts," says Mira Sandoval of Atlas Wire. "If we could not prove it, we would have written *one of the busiest*."

A hundred and twelve families have returned to Kalina since it reopened. On its own the figure means nothing. Last year, eleven families came back; over the quiet years, about nine hundred left. So the return is roughly ten times larger than last year's and roughly an eighth of what was lost. "Give both comparisons or neither," says Daniel Okoye, the world editor. "One of them alone is an argument, not a fact."

Some comparisons were left out. The partners could not show that the valley is poorer than it was before the closure, because the figures are not good enough. "We can say the market is not as busy as it was," Mira says. "We cannot put a number on poverty here, so we do not."

The smallest crossing is Veren: eleven households, and the only one of the seven that has not reopened. Eleven is too small for a chart, and the partners nearly cut it. In the end it led the piece. "The smallest number in the basin is the most important one," the explainer says, in the voice of Dren Markaj of Kalina Daily. "It is a whole village, and nothing has changed for it."

@SUBHEAD Reading B | · comparing honestly
@READING Four rules for comparison
**1. Give both directions.** *Ten times more than last year, an eighth of those who left.* Either one alone is an argument.
**2. Prove every superlative.** *The busiest* needs a source you can name. If you cannot prove it, write *one of the busiest* — almost as useful, and true.
**3. Compare like with like.** Not a village against a city, or this April against a dry March. If they are less similar than they look, say so.
**4. Never let size decide significance.** A number can be too small for a chart and large enough to lead. Eleven households is a whole village.

@PART 8 | Ethics: comparisons that mislead | Every comparison is an argument. A true number inside a false comparison is still a false sentence.
@FIG g-code | Accuracy includes the size of what you claim, not only the figures in it.
@FIG u8-ethA | 'The worst-hit valley in the region' is a superlative nobody has counted.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · the superlative nobody can prove
@CASE The Post's editors want a stronger line: 'the worst-hit valley in the region'. It would travel well, it would be quoted, and nobody could easily disprove it. The reporting supports something smaller, and firmer: Kalina is one of the worst-hit crossings on this river, by the office's own weekly counts. Nobody has counted the other valleys, so the bigger claim is not false exactly — it is unmeasured, which is worse, because a reader cannot tell the difference. The Code says: a superlative is a claim, and a claim needs a source. What line should run?

@CASE Veren has eleven households, and the data team says it cannot be charted: the bar would be invisible beside Kalina's. The simplest decision is to cut it, and the argument is about scale, not politics — the piece is long, and eleven is a rounding error beside two thousand. But Veren is the only crossing of the seven that never reopened, which makes it the clearest case in the basin. Cutting it would leave a reader thinking the river had reopened everywhere. The Code says: do not let the size of a number decide whether people exist. What leads the piece?
