@PART 2 | Grammar: possibility and advice | might, may and could for what is possible; should and ought to for what should be done.
@FIG u6-gA | might · may · could — three ways to say 'possible, not certain'.
@GRAMMAR 1 · might · may · could — it is possible
Form: {{might / may / could}} + verb (no *to*, no *-s*). "The gates {{might be}} opened." "It {{may be}} genuine."
All three say *possible*. {{May}} is a little more formal; {{could}} is often weaker.
[[Not 'may to be']]: say *it may be true*, NOT *it may to be true*.

@GRAMMAR 2 · saying it is not likely
Negative: "The valley {{may not}} flood." "It {{might not}} be genuine."
[[Careful]]: *could not* usually means *was unable*, not *is not likely* — so for possibility use *may not* or *might not*.
Questions: "{{Could}} the gates {{be}} opened without notice?"

@FIG u6-gB | should / ought to — what a newsroom owes its readers.
@GRAMMAR 3 · should · ought to — advice and duty
Form: {{should}} / {{ought to}} + verb. "We {{should}} say what we do not know." "A newsroom {{ought to}} publish a correction."
For advice, and for a duty that is not a law.
Negative: "We [[should not]] repeat the claim in the headline."

@PART 5 | Reading: the claim that went upriver | Two readings. Read for what was possible, and for the rules of a careful report.
@FIG g-reach | A claim with no author reached three countries in twelve hours. The correction will not.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · twelve hours, three countries
@READING The screenshot that moved a valley
At 21:40 on Thursday, a message appeared in a group of about two hundred people upstream. It said the gates of the Vara dam would be opened within the week and that the lower valley would flood. It carried an official-looking logo and no name.

By Friday morning it had reached three countries. "It was plausible, and that is the whole problem," says Priya Nair of Atlas Wire, who spent the day tracing it. "The dam does have gates. They could be opened in an emergency. Everything around the false part was true."

Two things did not hold. The date in the message had already passed, and the logo came from a document issued four years ago. "Our best explanation is a real document used wrongly," Priya says. "It may have started as a warning between neighbours. We cannot prove that, so we have not written it."

The Crossing Office said there was no plan to open the gates. Asked whether the gates could be opened without notice in an emergency, it did not answer. "That is not a denial," says Ana Ferreira of Atlas Wire. "A reader ought to be told the difference."

Meanwhile, families upstream moved animals to higher ground, and a school kept its children indoors. Those consequences were real while the claim was not, which is why four newsrooms decided the story had to be reported — carefully, and without the claim in the headline.

The piece they published said what was claimed, since when, by nobody traceable; what might be true; what the office would not say; and what families had already done. "We should be able to say *we do not know* in a way that still helps people," says Omar Faris of the Meridian Post. "That sentence is the hardest one in journalism."

@FIG g-ladder | Four rungs from rumour to fact. You may report from the third; you can show from the fourth.
@SUBHEAD Reading B | · from rumour to fact
@READING From rumour to fact: four rungs
**1. A rumour.** Somebody says it, and nobody can say who said it first. It might be true, and it is not yet reportable as a fact.
**2. One source.** A person tells you directly, on the record. That is a claim with a name on it — still not a fact.
**3. Two sources agree.** Two independent people who did not hear it from each other. Now you may report it, and say who.
**4. A document.** A register, a notice, a dated photograph. Now you can show the reader what you saw.

@PART 8 | Ethics: giving a rumour oxygen | Repeating a claim to deny it still carries it further. So does debunking a person instead of a claim.
@FIG g-code | Accuracy includes accuracy about your own doubt.
@FIG u6-ethA | A headline that repeats a claim spreads it, even with 'claim' in front.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · the headline that carries it
@CASE The Post's traffic desk points out, correctly, that 'Fears of flooding in the Vara valley' will be read ten times more widely than 'Unverified claim moves families upstream'. More readers would then see the careful paragraphs underneath. But most people read only a headline, and the first version plants exactly the belief the piece exists to correct. The claim gets the audience; the correction gets the few who scroll on. The first version would also be quoted back at the newsroom for a year. The Code says: a true sentence that leaves a false impression is not accurate. What headline should the partners run?

@PART 11 | Assignment: a careful report | Put the unit together: report a claim you cannot prove.
@FIG g-template | The assignment template — your careful report.
@P
**Your task. **A claim is spreading and you cannot prove it. Write a careful report (6–7 sentences). Say what is claimed and since when, what you could not trace, what might or may be true, what an official body said and would not say, and what people have already done. Put the consequences in your headline, not the claim. End with one sentence about what your newsroom should do if it turns out to be wrong.

@FIG g-model | A model — use Reading A and the Writing 1 model to check the shape of your report.
@WHITE
Remember: might, may and could for possibility (no 'to'); may not or might not for 'not likely'; should and ought to for duty; the consequences in the headline; and the claim marked unverified.

@CRITERIA Success criteria
I use might, may or could correctly for possibility.
I use may not or might not for what is not likely.
I use should or ought to for what the newsroom owes its readers.
My headline is about consequences, not the claim.
I say exactly what I could not confirm.
