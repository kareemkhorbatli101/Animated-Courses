@PART 2 | Grammar: -ing and to | Verbs followed by -ing; verbs followed by to; and the ones that take both.
@FIG u10-gA | Some verbs take -ing. Some take to. A few take both.
@GRAMMAR 1 · verb + -ing (the gerund)
After {{enjoy, avoid, finish, keep, suggest, consider, risk, mind, practise}}: "We {{finished checking}} the data."
After every {{preposition}}: "before {{publishing}}", "instead of {{waiting}}", "good at {{explaining}}".
As a subject: "{{Publishing}} together is harder than publishing alone."

@GRAMMAR 2 · verb + to + infinitive
After {{decide, agree, promise, refuse, manage, offer, hope, plan, learn, intend, want}}: "We {{agreed to publish}} at seven."
After adjectives: "It is hard {{to explain}}." With {{too}} / {{enough}}: "too late {{to change}}", "ready {{enough to launch}}".
[[Not 'agree publishing']]: say *agreed to publish*, NOT *agreed publishing*.

@FIG u10-gB | stop doing · stop to do — the same verb, two different facts.
@GRAMMAR 3 · verbs that take both
No real change: {{start, begin, continue}} — "It {{started raining}}" = "It {{started to rain}}."
A real change: {{stop}} {{-ing}} = end it; {{stop to}} = pause in order to.
{{remember to}} = not forget; {{remember -ing}} = have a memory. {{try to}} = attempt; {{try -ing}} = experiment.

@PART 5 | Reading: seven o'clock, four countries | Two readings. Read for the launch, and for what a newsroom owes after publishing.
@FIG g-reach | Eleven million readers. Eleven households. Both numbers are the story.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · the launch, and the morning after
@READING Seven o'clock: publishing The Seven Crossings
At 07:00 GMT on the last Sunday of October, four newsrooms published the same investigation. The package had five parts: a long read, an audio piece, a subtitled film, a data page and seven photographs. By noon it had been republished by forty outlets, under a licence that keeps every partner's name on every copy.

"Publishing together is much harder than publishing alone, and it is the only way this story could exist," says Ana Ferreira of Atlas Wire. "No single newsroom could have reported seven crossings in four countries. We needed to be four."

The reach was eleven million. The partners had agreed to measure something else as well. "Metrics tell you who saw it," says Omar Faris of the Meridian Post. "They do not tell you what changed. We decided to publish a follow-up in the spring, before we knew whether we would want to."

The hardest part began the next morning. Veren, the village of eleven households, had led the piece, and by Monday its residents were being telephoned by broadcasters in three countries. Only one journalist was there: Dren Markaj of Kalina Daily, who had agreed to go at nine on Sunday and was still going at six on Wednesday.

"That is what aftercare means, and it is why we plan it before launching," he says. "Eleven million people finished reading and went to lunch. I live twenty minutes away."

The debrief was held on the Tuesday. The partners agreed to extend moderation to three weeks, to pay for it jointly, and to correct an award entry submitted in one outlet's name. "The work is four newsrooms' work," Ines Duarte of the River Basin Network says. "So is the credit, and so is everything that comes afterwards."

@FIG g-credit | One licence, four names. The credit travels with every copy.
@SUBHEAD Reading B | · after you publish
@READING Four rules for after publication
**1. Plan the aftercare before launching.** Who answers the phone, for how long, and who pays. The people in the story cannot move on.
**2. Keep the credit on every copy.** Write the licence so republishing means keeping every name. A credit that survives syndication is worth most.
**3. Measure what changed, not only who looked.** Metrics count readers; a follow-up asks what is different. Agree it before you know the answer.
**4. Share everything that comes afterwards.** Awards, money, follow-ups and complaints belong to all the partners. If an entry carries one name, it is the wrong entry.
