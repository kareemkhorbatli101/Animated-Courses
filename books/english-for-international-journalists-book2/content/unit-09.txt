@PART 2 | Grammar: the small words | Quantifiers; a, an, the and no article; connectors.
@FIG u9-gA | some · most · a few · several — each one is a different fact.
@GRAMMAR 1 · quantifiers — how many, how much
Countable: {{a few}}, {{several}}, {{many}}, {{most}}, {{all}}, {{none}} of the families.
Uncountable: {{a little}}, {{much}}, {{most}} of the water, {{plenty of}} time.
[[A quantifier is part of the fact]]: *some families returned* and *most families returned* are different claims.

@GRAMMAR 2 · a, an, the — and no article
First mention: {{a}} crossing, {{an}} office. Second mention, or one we both know: {{the}} crossing.
{{the}} with rivers and named things: {{the}} river Vara, {{the}} Crossing Office. No article with most place names: Kalina, Veren.
[[Not 'the' for a general plural]]: say *families returned*, NOT *the families returned*, unless you mean particular ones.

@FIG u9-gB | however · although · because · so — the joints of a paragraph.
@GRAMMAR 3 · connectors — joining ideas
Contrast: {{however}} (new sentence), {{although}} / {{whereas}} (one sentence), {{but}}.
Cause and result: {{because}}, {{since}}, {{so}}, {{therefore}}.
Adding and sequencing: {{in addition}}, {{also}}, {{meanwhile}}, {{then}}.
[[Punctuation]]: *However, the figure is unconfirmed.* — comma after *however*, not before a clause.

@PART 5 | Reading: written by three, read as one | Two readings. Read for the joins, and for the rules of co-writing.
@FIG g-reach | One piece, three newsrooms, four countries — and it has to read as though one person wrote it.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · how three drafts became one piece
@READING The seams: co-writing The Seven Crossings
Three newsrooms wrote The Seven Crossings, and a reader should not be able to tell where one stopped and the next began. Getting there took four passes over three days, and almost none of the work was about facts. The facts had agreed with each other from the start.

The first pass was structure. Veren, with eleven households, leads; Kalina follows; the middle crossings come third. "We argued for a day about the order and ten minutes about the facts," says Ines Duarte of the River Basin Network. "That is normal in co-writing."

The second pass was quantifiers, and it was the dangerous one. Dren Markaj of Kalina Daily had written that most families upstream had returned. Ines had written that a few had. Both were correct, about different crossings, and side by side they looked like a contradiction. The piece now names the crossings in both sentences.

Worse was a draft that had no quantifier at all: *families have returned*. "A missing quantifier reads as *all*," says Mira Sandoval of Atlas Wire. "Some is a fact. None is a hole, and the reader fills it."

The third pass looked small: articles and names. The river Vara and the Crossing Office take *the*; Kalina and Veren take none. Three partners, two of them writing in a second language, had made eleven different choices between them, and a reader notices the eleventh even when they cannot say why.

The fourth pass was the joins themselves, and it was the slowest. Contrast between sections is carried by *however*; contrast inside a sentence by *whereas*. Where Dren's account and Ines's disagree about a second ferry, nothing was smoothed: the piece says both, and says they disagree. "A seam you can feel is better than a seam that hides something," Ines says.

@FIG g-partners | Co-writing is an agreement about small words, made by people in different time zones.
@SUBHEAD Reading B | · co-writing with partners
@READING Four rules for co-writing
**1. Agree the structure before the sentences.** Decide what leads, and in what order. Most arguments about style are arguments about structure.
**2. Treat a quantifier as a fact.** *Most*, *some*, *a few* are not softeners; each is a different claim. Never delete one to tighten a sentence, or change one without asking its reporter.
**3. Choose one house voice, and keep every byline.** One outlet's sound; every reporter's name, including the local one nobody could replace.
**4. Do not smooth a real disagreement.** If two accounts differ, print both and say so. A visible seam is honest; a hidden one decides for the reader.

@PART 8 | Ethics: the small words | Tidying is editing. Tidying away a quantifier, a byline or a disagreement is something else.
@FIG g-code | Fairness lives in small words: which quantifier, whose name, whose account.
@FIG u9-ethA | Deleting 'most' does not shorten a sentence. It enlarges a claim.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · the quantifier that was tidied
@CASE The Post's subeditor cuts 'most' from Dren's sentence, leaving 'families upstream have returned'. The motive is length: the line was one word over, and 'most' looked like a hedge. Nothing was invented, and no figure changed. But a sentence with no quantifier is read as *all*, and upstream some families have not come back and will not. Those families are now, in print, home. The person whose sentence it was was not asked, and he is the only one of the three who will meet them. The Code says: a quantifier is part of the fact. What happens before sign-off?
