@PART 2 | Grammar: perfect and past | Present perfect for up to now; past simple for a finished time.
@FIG u1-gA | present perfect = up to now · past simple = a finished time.
@GRAMMAR 1 · Present perfect — up to now
Form: {{has / have}} + past participle.
We use it for experience and unfinished time: We {{have covered}} this story {{for}} a year. The Meridian Post {{has just}} contacted us.
Signal words: {{for, since, just, already, yet, ever, never}}.

@GRAMMAR 2 · Past simple — a finished time
Form: the past form of the verb (regular {{-ed}}, or irregular).
We use it for a finished time: The crossing {{reopened}} last spring. Omar {{wrote}} to us last week.
Signal words: [[last week, ago, yesterday, in April]].

@FIG u1-gB | just, already, yet, ever, never — all with the present perfect.
@GRAMMAR 3 · Which one?
Result now, or up to now → present perfect: "We {{have worked}} on it for a year."
A finished time → past simple: "We {{started}} it last April."
*Ever* and *never* ask and answer about experience: "{{Have}} you {{ever}} co-produced a story? — No, {{never}}."

@FIG g-reach | A year of coverage — and a story that has travelled.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · a year on: the story that travelled
@READING A year on: the story that travelled
It has been a year since Atlas Wire published its front page about the crossing at Kalina, and the story has never really stopped. It has become the desk's longest running story, and, this month, it has reached further than anyone expected.
A great deal has happened since that front page. The crossing reopened last spring, and, according to the Crossing Office, a hundred families have returned to the valley. The school reopened in the autumn, and a small café has opened at the end of the bridge. Atlas Wire has followed every step, and its correspondent, Mira Sandoval, has written most of the coverage.
"I have covered this valley for a year now," Mira said. "When we started, the road was empty. I never thought the story would travel so far. But it has — people have read it in countries that have never heard of the river Vara."
Last week, that reach became clear. An editor at the Meridian Post, a large international daily, wrote to the desk. He said he had followed the coverage for months, and he proposed working together. Atlas Wire has never co-produced a story with another newsroom before.
"We are an agency; we file, and others publish," said Ana Ferreira, the editor-in-chief. "We have not agreed anything yet. But the story has grown, and it has found a much wider audience. Now we have to decide, carefully, what to do next — and how to keep it honest."
The Post is not the only outlet that has noticed. In recent weeks, the River Basin Network has asked to run the coverage across the region, and readers have written from four continents. The audience, once a wire list, has grown far wider than before.
There is one more thing the desk has not forgotten. Kalina Daily, the small paper in the town itself, has covered the crossing every week since long before Atlas Wire arrived. "Whatever we build," Ana said, "they are in it, and their name is on it. That part is not negotiable."

@FIG g-partners | A long-running story can reach far beyond the town where it began.
@SUBHEAD Reading B | · catching up on a running story — four tips
@READING Four tips for a recap
**1. Say what has happened so far.** Use the present perfect for the whole story up to now: 'We have covered it for a year.'
**2. Say what happened at key moments.** Use the past simple for a finished time: 'The crossing reopened last spring.'
**3. Say what is new.** Use *just*, *recently* and *already*: 'A daily has just noticed our work.' Keep it short.
**4. Name your sources, and stay accurate.** A recap is still journalism: a source for each number, no more than has really happened, and the name of the newsroom that was there before you.
**5. Keep it short.** A recap is a bridge, not the story. Give enough to understand where things stand, and no more.

@PART 8 | Ethics: keeping it accurate, keeping it ours | A story that travels must stay accurate — and stay true to the place it comes from.
@FIG u1-ethA | 'Fully recovered' is a claim; 'has improved a lot' is what you can say.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · the recap that says too much
@CASE Mira writes a recap for the new partner. She wants it to sound strong, so she nearly writes: 'The Kalina valley has fully recovered, and everything is finished.' It would read well, and a big partner might like a simple, bright line. But that is not accurate. The crossing has reopened and many families have returned — a great deal has improved — but the clinic is still closed, the bus does not cross yet, and not everyone has come home. The partner has never been to Kalina; they will build their whole picture on Mira's words. A recap is still journalism, and its first reader trusts it completely. Mira knows the Code: report what has really happened, not more. What does Mira write in the recap?

@CASE The Meridian Post is interested — but the editor suggests a simpler, more dramatic angle: 'the miracle valley that came back from nothing'. It would travel well and reach millions. But it is not quite true. The recovery was slow and hard, and it was done, day by day, by the people who live there. 'Came back from nothing' erases years of ordinary work, and it makes the valley sound helpless rather than strong. There is a second problem: the Post's draft names only Atlas Wire, and Kalina Daily has covered this crossing every week for years. The Post's reach is real and welcome. But the facts, and the credit, are not the Post's to change. The Code says: independence — a partner's reach must never change the story, however large the audience. What does Atlas Wire do?
