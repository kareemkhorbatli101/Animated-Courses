@PART 2 | Grammar: talking about the future | will for decisions and promises; going to for plans already made; the present continuous for fixed arrangements.
@FIG u3-gA | will — a decision at the moment of speaking; going to — a plan made before.
@GRAMMAR 1 · will — decisions, promises, predictions
Form: {{will}} + verb. "I {{will move}} the call to four." "It {{will take}} three weeks."
For a decision made as you speak, a promise, an offer, or a prediction.
Negative: It [[won't]] be ready. Question: [[Will]] you file by Friday?

@GRAMMAR 2 · going to — a plan already made
Form: {{am / is / are going to}} + verb. "We {{are going to cover}} the whole basin."
For a plan or intention decided before now — and for a prediction you can see coming.
[[Not a decision at the moment of speaking]]: for that, use *will*.

@FIG u3-gB | going to = the plan; present continuous = the plan with a time and a place.
@GRAMMAR 3 · present continuous — a fixed arrangement
Form: {{am / is / are}} + verb{{-ing}}, with a future time. "We {{are publishing}} on Sunday."
For an arrangement that is fixed: a time, a place, and other people who know.
"Ines {{is joining}} at four." "Dren {{is travelling}} on Thursday."

@PART 5 | Reading: one basin, four newsrooms | Two readings. Read for the plan, and for how to plan across borders.
@FIG g-reach | One project, four mastheads, four audiences — and a single hour of publication.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · the project plan, explained
@READING Seven crossings, one Sunday: how the basin project will work
At the end of this month, four newsrooms are publishing one investigation at the same hour. It is called *The Seven Crossings*, and it asks a simple question: a year after Kalina reopened, what has happened to the rest of the river?

The work is divided by geography. Kalina Daily, the paper in the town itself, is reporting the two upstream crossings. The River Basin Network is taking the middle three. Atlas Wire is covering the two at the mouth of the river, and the Meridian Post is editing the English version.

"Nobody could do this alone, and nobody should," says Ines Duarte, who edits for the River Basin Network. "We are going to put seven small stories beside each other, and the picture they make is the story."

The calendar is short. First drafts are due on the 14th; the edit and translation take eight days; everything goes out at 07:00 GMT on the last Sunday. "That hour is the only thing we will not move," says Ana Ferreira of Atlas Wire. "If one partner runs early, the others lose the day."

Four time zones have their own rules. The kick-off call was moved twice: four o'clock in Geneva is ten in the morning for the Post's desk and six in the evening for Dren Markaj at Kalina Daily. "We learned to write the time zone beside every hour," Omar Faris of the Meridian Post says. "It sounds small. It saves a week."

The hardest part, all four editors say, is not the logistics. It is holding to one standard when four newsrooms have four house styles, four lawyers and four audiences. So the partners agreed their rules before their deadlines: every figure confirmed twice, every local reporter named, and nothing published that the others have not seen.

@FIG g-partners | Four newsrooms, one standard: the rules come before the calendar.
@SUBHEAD Reading B | · good planning across borders
@READING Four rules for planning with partners
**1. Name the time zone every time.** Write '16:00 Geneva' or '07:00 GMT', never just 'four o'clock'. A plan that does not say whose clock it means is not a plan.
**2. Share the calendar, not only the deadline.** Partners need the milestones between: the kick-off, the drafts, the edit, the hour.
**3. Agree the standard before the schedule.** How a figure is confirmed, who is named, what each partner may publish — all before anyone writes.
**4. Let the smallest partner choose the hour.** The newsroom closest to the story has the fewest people and the least room to move. It should not be the one getting up at dawn.
