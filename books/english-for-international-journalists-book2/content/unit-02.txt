@PART 2 | Grammar: the way things were | used to for past states and habits; would for repeated actions; the past continuous for background.
@FIG u2-gA | used to — a past state that is not true now; would — a repeated action.
@GRAMMAR 1 · used to — how things were
Form: {{used to}} + verb. The bridge {{used to be}} closed. Families {{used to live}} on both sides.
For a past state or habit that is not true now.
Negative: It [[didn't use to]] be busy. Question: [[Did]] it [[use to]] be quiet?

@GRAMMAR 2 · would — what happened again and again
Form: {{would}} + verb. Every morning, Bekim {{would open}} his stall.
For a repeated past action — something that happened again and again.
[[Not for states]]: say *it used to be closed*, NOT *it would be closed*.

@FIG u2-gB | past continuous = the longer background; past simple = the event.
@GRAMMAR 3 · past continuous — the background
Form: {{was / were}} + verb{{-ing}}. The families {{were leaving}} when the barrier came down.
For a longer background action, often interrupted by a past-simple event.
Set the scene: "It {{was raining}}, and the road {{was}} quiet."

@PART 5 | Reading: the way it was | Two readings. Read for the backstory, and for how to give good background.
@FIG g-reach | A backstory feature — the valley, before the story we know.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · the backstory: how the crossing used to be
@READING Before the quiet: a short history of the Kalina crossing
To understand the reopening of the Kalina crossing, you have to know the bridge as it used to be. For years, this was one of the busiest crossings on the river. It was never rich, but it was full of life, and everyone knew everyone on both banks.
In those days, the market on the bridge opened before the sun. Stall-holders like Bekim would arrive at dawn, set out the boxes, and sweep the boards. By seven, the crossing was loud with sellers and buyers from both sides. Children would cut across on their way to the school on the far bank, and old men would sit outside the café at the end of the bridge, as they had for thirty years.
Then, over one hard winter, things began to change. Families were leaving, quietly at first, and then in greater numbers. Stalls closed one by one. "I remember it clearly," Bekim says. "One morning I was sweeping the boards, and I looked up. The bridge was empty. I had never seen it empty before."
When the barrier finally came down, the valley went quiet, and it stayed quiet for seven years. The café shut. Grass grew between the stones at the end of the bridge. For a long time, Kalina was a place people remembered rather than a place people crossed.
That is the backstory, and it is not decoration: it matters. The crossing that reopened last spring did not open in an empty history; it opened where a whole life used to be. When a hundred families return, the number means little on its own. It means a great deal when you know how many left, and how long the valley waited. The past is not just background. It is the reason the present is news.

@SUBHEAD Reading B | · good background: what an editor abroad needs
@READING Four rules for good background
**1. Use used to for how things were.** Past states and habits that are not true now: 'The market used to be busy.'
**2. Use would for what people did again and again.** Repeated actions: 'Every morning, he would open his stall.' (But not for states.)
**3. Use the past continuous to set the scene.** A longer background, often broken by an event: 'Families were leaving when the barrier came down.'
**4. Keep it fair and in order.** Give the background in the order it happened, and never make the past worse or better than it was to make today bigger. Where you can, take it from someone who was there — and say who.

@PART 8 | Ethics: a fair backstory | The past is powerful. A fair backstory tells it as it was — not worse, and not like a fairy tale.
@FIG g-code | The Code applies to the past as much as to today: the backstory must be accurate, honest and fair.
@FIG u2-ethA | A backstory can be true, or it can be shaped to make today look bigger.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · making the past sound worse
@CASE Mira wants the reopening to feel like a triumph, so she is tempted to darken the backstory: to write that the valley 'used to be a place of nothing but fear and ruin.' It would make the recovery look bigger. But it is not true. The valley used to be poor, yes, and the quiet years were real — but before them it was an ordinary, living place, full of small joys: children crossing to school, coffee carried over the bridge at dawn, neighbours on two banks who knew each other. To paint the past as 'nothing but ruin' would erase those people and their ordinary lives, just to make a brighter headline today. The Code says: report the past as it was. What does Mira write?
