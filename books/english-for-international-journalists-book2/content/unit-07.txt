@STORY One sentence, two hundred newsrooms. | This unit is wholly cross-border. A wire report is not written for one audience; it is written to be carried, translated and cut by newsrooms that have never seen the place. That is why so much of it is in the passive: *three crossings have been reopened*, *the figure is being checked*. The passive is a tool, and it is also a hiding place. This week, Mira learns to use it for clarity and to refuse it when somebody wants to use it to hide a name. (Atlas Wire, Kalina Daily, the Meridian Post and their staff are invented, for practice.)

@PART 2 | Grammar: the passive | be + past participle, in every tense; and when to name the doer.
@FIG u7-gA | The passive puts the action first: 'three crossings have been reopened'.
@GRAMMAR 1 · the passive — the action first
Form: {{be}} + past participle. "The gates {{were opened}}." "The figure {{is checked}} twice."
Use it when the action matters most, when the doer is unknown, or when the doer is obvious.
Active: *The office reopened the crossing.* → Passive: The crossing {{was reopened}} (by the office).

@GRAMMAR 2 · the passive across tenses
present: {{is / are}} checked · past: {{was / were}} opened · present perfect: {{has / have been}} reopened
continuous: {{is / are being}} checked · future: {{will be}} carried · modal: {{can / must be}} used
[[Watch the participle]]: say *has been reopened*, NOT *has been reopen*.

@FIG u7-gB | by + the doer: say it whenever you know it.
@GRAMMAR 3 · by whom — and when you must say it
Add {{by}} + the doer when the reader needs it: "The gates {{were opened by}} the Crossing Office."
Leave it out only when it is unknown or obvious: "He {{was arrested}} yesterday."
[[Never]] use the passive to hide a name you have: *mistakes were made* says nothing.

@PART 5 | Reading: built to be carried | Two readings. Read for how a wire sentence is made, and for the rules of the passive.
@FIG g-reach | Written once, published two hundred times. Every weakness is published too.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · how a wire sentence is built
@READING Eleven words, two hundred newsrooms
A wire sentence is written to be used by people who will never meet its author. It will be translated into languages nobody in the newsroom reads, shortened to four paragraphs, and read by someone who has never heard of the river. That is why it is built the way it is.

"Most of our leads are passive, and that is not laziness," says Daniel Okoye, world editor at Atlas Wire. "What has happened is usually what the reader needs first. *Three crossings have been reopened* — then the name of the body that reopened them."

The name of the body is not optional, and never has been. A snap that says *the crossings were reopened* can be read as an accident of nature, and a sentence that can be read two ways will be, somewhere. So the authority goes into the first paragraph, because the first paragraph is sometimes all that is carried.

Unfinished work is marked in the same grammar. *The figure of 112 returning families is still being checked* tells a subscriber in another country exactly how much weight to give the number, in six words. "The continuous passive is the most honest tense we have," Daniel says. "It says the work is still going on."

Two things are never cut, in any version and at any length. The credit line names the outlet whose reporting it is, and the byline names the reporter who did it. Both were nearly lost when one subscriber's page template used agency bylines automatically.

"A credit is not a courtesy," says Omar Faris of the Meridian Post, who had it corrected within the hour. "It is the only payment some reporters get, and in some places it is also their protection. A name on a story says somebody stands behind it."

@SUBHEAD Reading B | · the passive on the wire
@READING Four rules for the passive
**1. Use it when the action comes first.** *Three crossings have been reopened* puts the news where a reader starts. The body follows at once.
**2. Name the doer whenever you have one.** Leave out *by* only when the doer is unknown or obvious. A passive with no name can be read as nobody's fault.
**3. Mark unfinished work with the continuous.** *The figure is still being checked* stops a subscriber abroad printing a number as settled.
**4. Never use it to hide.** *Mistakes were made* is grammar standing in for reporting. If you know who did it, the sentence says so.

@PART 8 | Ethics: the hiding place | The passive can put the action first, or it can make a person disappear. The grammar is the same; the journalism is not.
@FIG g-code | Independence means the grammar is chosen by the newsroom, not by whoever is named in it.
@FIG u7-ethA | 'Mistakes were made' is a sentence with nobody in it.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · the name the lawyers wanted out
@CASE Lawyers in two countries ask for the lead to read 'the crossings were reopened', with no body named. Their reasoning is reasonable: naming an authority invites a complaint, and the complaint lands on the smallest partner first. Nobody is asking Atlas Wire to write anything false. But a passive with no doer can be read as an accident, and the office's decision — taken by named people, announced on a Tuesday — becomes weather. If the sentence is wrong, nobody can even be asked about it. The Code says: the passive may order a sentence, never empty it. What goes in the lead?
