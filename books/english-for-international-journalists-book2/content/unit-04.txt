@PART 2 | Grammar: reporting what was said | Reported speech and the tense shift; say and tell; according to for facts.
@FIG u4-gA | Direct speech keeps the words; reported speech shifts the tense back.
@GRAMMAR 1 · reported speech — one step back
Direct: "The water {{is}} better." → Reported: She said the water {{was}} better.
Present → past · past → past perfect · {{will}} → {{would}} · {{can}} → {{could}}
Pronouns and times move too: *today* → {{that day}}, *tomorrow* → {{the next day}}.

@GRAMMAR 2 · say and tell
{{say}} + what was said: She [[said]] the water was better. ([[not]] *she said me*)
{{tell}} + person + what was said: She [[told me]] the water was better.
Also: *add*, *insist*, *deny*, *claim* — each carries a different weight. Choose carefully.

@FIG u4-gB | say something · tell somebody something — the difference matters in print.
@GRAMMAR 3 · according to — attributing a fact
Form: {{According to}} + a person or body + a fact. "{{According to}} the Crossing Office, 112 families have returned."
Use it for a fact you have not confirmed yourself, and for figures from an official body.
[[Not for your own reporting]]: if you counted it, say so.

@PART 5 | Reading: how a quote travels | Two readings. Read for the journey of a sentence, and for the rules that protect it.
@FIG g-reach | One sentence, four newsrooms, millions of readers. It has to arrive intact.
@SUBHEAD Reading A | · a sentence crosses four borders
@READING The long way round: how one sentence reached print
The sentence began at a stall on the Kalina bridge, early on a Tuesday. A woman selling apples was asked how the river was this year, and she answered, in her own language, that the water was a little better than last year.

Dren Markaj of Kalina Daily wrote the words down twice: once as she said them, once in English. "The first version is the evidence," he says. "The second is only my best attempt."

Mira Sandoval of Atlas Wire read the English back to the woman before they left the bridge. The woman agreed with it. That took ninety seconds, and it is the reason the sentence survived everything that came next.

The copy went to the Meridian Post's foreign desk, where a subeditor met it for the first time. She had never been to Kalina and could not call the woman. What she had was the copy, and the copy said who had spoken, in which language, and who had interpreted.

Her first instinct was to shorten. 'The water is a little better this year' is nine words; 'the river has recovered' is four, and it reads better. She queried it. Mira said no, and explained why: the woman had made a small, careful claim about one year, and the shorter version made a large claim about a river.

"A desk abroad has to be able to trust the copy, because it cannot check the stall," says Omar Faris, who edits the project at the Post. "So we do not improve quotes. We query them."

The sentence ran as she said it, with her name, her town, and a line saying the interview was conducted in her own language. Four newsrooms, four editors, two languages — and nine words that still meant what she meant.

@SUBHEAD Reading B | · reporting what was said
@READING Four rules for reported speech
**1. Shift the tense, not the meaning.** 'The water is better' becomes *she said the water was better*. The tense moves back one step; the claim stays the same size.
**2. Say who, and how you know.** *Said* and *told* for speech, *according to* for a fact you did not confirm. Name the person or body every time.
**3. Never improve a quote.** A tidier sentence is a different sentence. If a quote is long, cut it with care, or paraphrase it and say that you have.
**4. Carry the language with the words.** Say which language it was spoken in, say who interpreted, and read the English back to the speaker.

@PART 8 | Ethics: the size of a claim | A reported sentence can be made bigger or smaller without a single word being invented. That is the danger.
@FIG g-code | Accuracy is not only about facts. It is about the size of what someone said.
@FIG u4-ethA | 'A little better' and 'recovered' are both true-sounding. Only one is true.
@SUBHEAD A first case | · the tidier sentence
@CASE The Post's subeditor is not trying to mislead anybody. She has nine words that read awkwardly and four that read well, and 'the river has recovered' is the kind of line a reader remembers. She does not invent anything: she is reporting what residents say. But the woman made a small claim about one year, and the short version makes a large claim about a river. If the water falls again next summer, the woman will be the one who said it had recovered. The Code says: a paraphrase must carry the same weight as the words. What does Mira tell the desk?
