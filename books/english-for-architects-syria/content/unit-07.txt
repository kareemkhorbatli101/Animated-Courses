@PANEL
Building Design and Health Outcomes
Many illnesses are preventable through building design. Understanding how disease spreads allows architects to design against it. However, design alone is complex. A building must be arranged correctly, be maintained, and be used as the designer intended.
Design guidance teaches builders how illness spreads and how arrangement prevents it. Knowing that cross ventilation reduces airborne infection is valuable knowledge. But knowledge alone does not produce healthy buildings. A clinic must also have openable windows on two sides. The staff must believe opening them is worth the dust and noise. They must trust the design. Effective guidance addresses arrangement, specification, and how people actually use the room.
Schools are ideal places to design for health because children form habits early. Placing handwashing troughs where children pass them establishes patterns lasting into adulthood. Providing a private washroom for girls addresses the single most common reason girls leave school. Providing shaded outdoor space breaks the habit of crowding indoors. School design reaches many children efficiently. One good classroom serves hundreds of pupils over its life.
Health posts bridge the gap between hospitals and villages. A post is a small building that local masons can build and local people can maintain. It can be designed with a consulting room, a store and a waiting shade. It can be extended as needs grow. Posts cost far less than a hospital wing but meet most everyday needs. In Syria, small health posts have become vital for reaching districts far from any functioning hospital.
Private pharmacies and shops occupy many ground floors. However, without guidance, owners sometimes alter structure or block an escape route. Advice to private owners extends good practice beyond publicly funded buildings. Health posts, advised shopfronts, well-planned schools, and district clinics working together create a stronger network than any single building type.
Durability requires that communities eventually maintain their own buildings. This means specifying materials local builders can obtain and repair. It means detailing so an owner can replace a tile or clear a gutter. It means building on existing local construction practice rather than replacing it entirely.

@PANEL
Primary Care Buildings: Foundation for Healthy Districts
Primary care design is an approach to health buildings emphasizing prevention, local construction, and accessibility. It differs from hospital design that concentrates complex servicing in one large building. Primary care design builds healthy districts where illness is prevented through daylight, clean water, ventilation, drainage, and simple durable fabric.
Primary care buildings serve as the foundation of a health network. Instead of everyone travelling to a hospital, most needs are met locally in a small post or clinic designed by an architect and built by local masons. Only complex cases go to the hospital. This keeps costs lower and buildings closer. In Syria, where many districts are hours from a functioning hospital, small clinics became vital.
Prevention is central to the philosophy. Preventing illness through ventilation, water supply, sanitation, and dry construction costs much less than treating illness. A properly drained floor costs less than the illness damp causes. A window that opens costs less than a ward that spreads infection. Primary care design emphasizes these cost-effective measures.
Primary care buildings require community participation. Architects alone cannot create health. Residents must understand how the building works and use it as intended. Residents must help identify where the building should stand. Residents must take part in maintaining it. This approach views residents as partners not passive recipients.
Primary care design requires coordination among disciplines. Health cannot improve without a water engineer providing supply and drainage. A clinic cannot work if the access road floods. Good design requires structural engineers, services engineers, surveyors, and the health staff who will run the building, all working together around one plan.
Primary care design requires strong management and continuous learning. Builders must be trained, supported, and supervised. Offices must revisit buildings to see whether they work. Offices must learn from experience and adjust details. Weak management leads to wasted materials and buildings that fail early. Strong management creates effective, efficient buildings.
Primary care design creates durable health buildings because it builds local capacity. Communities maintain their own buildings with outside support decreasing over time. A hospital wing depends on expensive outside servicing. A district that can repair its own clinic becomes independent over time.

Quality School Design Standards and Indicators
A good school requires multiple components working together. A strong plan alone is insufficient if the classrooms are dark. A modern facade alone is insufficient if the acoustics are poor. Quality requires coordination of many factors: well-proportioned classrooms, daylight and ventilation, durable finishes, accessible routes, safe outdoor space, community involvement, and a layout the staff can supervise.
Classroom quality is the most important factor in school design. An excellent classroom can teach pupils in a simple building with few resources. A poor classroom cannot teach well even behind an impressive entrance. Investment in classroom proportion, daylight and acoustics creates stronger impact than investment in display facades. In resource-limited contexts, the classroom determines everything.
The layout must be relevant to how the school actually runs. An abstract plan disconnected from the timetable fails the staff. A layout connecting classrooms to outdoor teaching, to water, and to a staff room helps teachers see the building as an ally. A plan respecting local climate and construction creates stronger buildings.
Inclusive design means all children can enter and use the building regardless of gender, ability, or background. Schools with a single stepped entrance teach disabled children they are less welcome. Schools with no girls' washroom teach girls to leave. Quality design actively includes all children, providing the ramps, rails and privacy that make this real. Inclusive buildings create more equitable schools.
Community involvement determines whether a building is looked after. If residents value the school and helped shape it, more repairs get done. If residents see the building as a government object, small faults become ruins. Engaging residents to take pride in the school creates the conditions for a long life.
Review after occupation should measure whether the building actually works. Drawings are one record but not the only measurement. Practical review of whether rooms are used as intended is equally important. Review should guide the next project, not just close the file. Architects must use what they find to improve the next school.
Syria's school building challenges stem from war damage and crowding. Classrooms damaged, roofs lost, services cut, double shifts in surviving buildings. Building quality schools requires not just classrooms but attention to repair priorities, safe routes, and community support alongside new construction.

@PANEL
Syrian Values Supporting Health and Education Buildings
Syrian traditions provide a strong foundation supporting integrated health and school buildings. Understanding them helps design culturally appropriate projects gaining community support.
The idea of "aql" or intellect is central to Syrian learning. Education develops intellect, allowing people to understand the world and take responsibility within it. Learning and teaching are understood as duties: those who know are expected to share, and those who learn to pass knowledge on. Education in Syrian tradition is a community obligation, not only a personal advantage. Buildings expressing that duty, with a dignified entrance and a room for gathering, gain stronger community support.
Syrian teaching emphasizes health as a shared responsibility. Quarters have long maintained their own water channels, shaded lanes and roof terraces, and knowledge of how to keep a house cool. Health is understood as a balance between people, air, water and neighbourhood, and keeping that balance is everybody's work. Clinics aligned with these values frame design as caring for the whole quarter, not an outside agenda.
Community responsibility supports integration. The "hara" is the quarter whose households share lanes, water and shade. Members are responsible for each other. If neighbours are sick, healthy neighbours should help. If children lack schooling, those with learning should teach. A shared campus combines health and education as a single quarter responsibility.
Women's education and health are increasingly recognized as essential in Syrian communities. Women hold deep knowledge of food, water storage and child health, and increasingly lead local committees. Community leaders emphasize that educating girls strengthens the whole quarter. A private washroom and a safe route to school align with these values even where older practice restricted it.
Syrian building ethics require high standards. Masons and teachers are trusted figures accountable to the quarter. Tradition emphasizes mastery and trustworthiness in those who serve. Projects emphasizing craft integrity and competence align with these values.
The traditional courtyard shows that everything is interconnected — room, air, water, shade and family. This way of building naturally supports integrated health-school campuses. Separating the clinic from the school fragments what a quarter experiences as one place. Shared campuses honour that unity.
Aligning health and school buildings with Syrian values creates stronger community acceptance and care. Residents see the buildings as consistent with their traditions, not threatening them.
