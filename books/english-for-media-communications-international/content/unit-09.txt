@GRAMMAR

@TEXT
Ingrid: In a crisis, people remember not what you said but whether you were steady. So we have four habits. First, publish something within the hour: what has happened, what we are doing, and when the next update will come. It will be short, and that is fine. Second, never guess. If a fact has not been verified, say that it has not been verified. ‘We do not yet know’ is a complete and honest answer, and it is far better than a number you have to withdraw tomorrow. Third, update at the time you promised, even if nothing has changed, because silence is where rumours grow; if you have nothing new, say that you have nothing new. Fourth, one voice. One named person speaks for us, and everybody else sends questions to that person — not because colleagues are untrustworthy, but because five slightly different answers become the story. And afterwards we write down what we have learned, while we still remember it.

@TASK Part 5: Communicating in a Crisis
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
Crisis communication is often called a skill in writing; it is mostly a skill in deciding. The writing itself is simple: short sentences, no decoration, nothing you cannot prove. The difficulty is that every decision has to be made while the facts are still arriving and while somebody more senior is asking why there has not been a statement yet. Four habits make this bearable. The first is to publish quickly and shortly. A statement within the hour that says what has happened, what the organisation is doing and when the next update will come is worth more than a careful one three hours later, because in those three hours other people have been explaining your situation for you. The second is never to guess. ‘We do not yet know’ is a complete answer, and it is the only one that is still true tomorrow. A number that has to be withdrawn costs more trust than a silence ever did. The third is to update at the promised time whether or not there is news, because silence is read as concealment, and a team that has been steady for six hours is believed at the seventh. The fourth is the single voice: one named person answers, since five slightly different answers become the story instead of the event. Afterwards, write down what you have learned while you still remember it, because nobody remembers a crisis accurately a month later.

@TASK Part 5: A Steady Voice Across Time Zones
@RUBRIC Read about crisis work in an international team. Then mark each sentence True (T) or False (F).
@TEXT
A crisis in an organisation that works across six time zones has a particular problem: it begins while most of the team is asleep. The office nearest the event is the one that knows most and usually the one with the fewest communications staff, and by the time Geneva wakes up, six hours of conversation have already happened without it. Teams that handle this well decide three things in advance. They agree who may publish a holding statement without waiting for anybody else, and they make that authority local rather than central, because a statement four hours late is worse than a statement that a manager would have worded differently. They agree a handover: the desk moves with the working day, and the team that is going to sleep writes down what it knows, what it has verified and who it has promised to call. And they agree one voice per language rather than one voice in total, because a journalist in Dhaka will not wait for Geneva and should not have to. None of this removes the difficulty. It stops the team from spending the first three hours deciding who is allowed to speak.

@RUBRIC Write about why an organisation should answer rumours. Give reasons and an example.
@MODEL
A rumour that is not answered becomes the record. People do not usually read a correction a week later; they remember the first version they saw, and after a few days it is simply what they know. Answering quickly is therefore not about pride but about accuracy. It began on a Tuesday evening with a message in a community group: the food distribution in the north had stopped. It was not true, and it was not malicious.
