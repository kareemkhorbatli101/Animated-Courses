@TASK Warm-up: Reading — The Rules Before a Story
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
Before Meridian Relief publishes any story, it follows the same four rules in every country. First, explain clearly what the story is for and where it will appear. Second, ask for consent in a language the person reads easily, and never in a hurry. Third, check that nothing in the words or the photograph could put the person at risk. Fourth, tell the person that they may say no, now or later, and that nothing will change for them if they do. If a rule and a deadline disagree, the rule wins. The team calls this ‘do no harm’, and it is the first thing that every new officer learns.

@TASK Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAMMAR
 | Form | Use | Example
Imperative | the verb with no subject | instructions and rules
Negative imperative | do not + verb | things you must never do
Sequencers | first, then, next, after that, finally | putting steps in order

@REMEMBER Use the imperative for a rule or an instruction: ‘Ask for consent.’ Use the first conditional for a real possibility: ‘If the person says no, we will stop.’ Never use ‘will’ after ‘if’.
@WATCH Watch out: say ‘If the person says no, we will stop’, not ‘If the person will say no’. The ‘will’ goes in the second half only.

@TASK Part 3: Layla Explains Consent
@AUDIO 3.2
@PRE listen
what should a person understand before they agree to be in a story?
@TEXT
Layla: Consent is not a signature. It is an understanding, and the signature only records it. So take your time. First, explain what the story is for, where it will appear, and who will see it. Many people imagine a local newsletter and do not realise that a photograph may be seen in another country. Then explain how long we will keep the photograph, and tell them that they can change their mind later. Next, read the form aloud, in the language they read most easily, because not everybody is comfortable reading in front of a stranger. After that, answer every question honestly, even if the honest answer is ‘I do not know’. Finally, give them a copy. If somebody hesitates, that is not a small thing; stop and ask what is worrying them. If you ever feel that a person is agreeing only to be polite, do not take the photograph.

@TASK Part 5: Protecting the People in Our Stories
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
Every communications team has a rule that comes before all the others: do no harm. It sounds obvious, and it is not. Harm is rarely dramatic. It is a street sign in the corner of a photograph, a school uniform that shows which school a child attends, or a sentence that says more about a family’s situation than the family realised it was agreeing to. None of these is a lie, and none of them is unkind. Each of them can still put somebody at risk. For this reason the team works through the same steps every time. First, it asks whether the story needs this person at all, or whether the point can be made without them. Then it explains, in the person’s own language, what the story is for and where it will appear. Next, it asks for consent, and it treats hesitation as a no until it is clearly a yes. After that, it checks the words and the images for anything that could identify a home, a school or a route that somebody takes every day. Finally, it tells the person how to withdraw, and it keeps that promise. The team also knows that consent is not the same everywhere. In one country, a signature is a familiar and welcome thing; in another, it is a document that has caused people trouble before. The rule does not change, but the way the team explains it does.

@TASK Part 5: Consent and Community Across Cultures
@RUBRIC Read about consent in different places. Then mark each sentence True (T) or False (F).
@TEXT
Consent is a single principle, but it is not a single conversation. In some communities, a visitor should speak to an elder or a community leader before approaching a family, and to do otherwise would be rude rather than efficient. In others, going through a leader would feel like pressure, and the family would rather decide for themselves. In some places people sign documents every week; in others, a form brought by a stranger is a reason to be careful, and for good reason. A good communications team learns this before it arrives, usually by asking the field staff who live there. What never changes is the substance: the person must understand what will happen, must be free to say no, and must be able to change their mind afterwards. What changes is the route to that understanding — who you speak to first, whether you read the form aloud, how long you leave before you ask again. Teams that confuse the route with the rule make one of two mistakes. They either apply one country’s habits everywhere, or they decide that consent means something different here. Both are wrong, and the second is more dangerous.

@TASK Part 10: Unit 3 glossary
@TEXT
consent = permission given freely and with understanding    ·    informed consent = consent given after a full, clear explanation    ·    safeguarding = the work of protecting people, especially children    ·    do no harm = the rule that a story must never put a person at risk    ·    dignity = the respect that every person deserves    ·    anonymous = with the name kept secret    ·    anonymise = to remove the details that identify a person    ·    guardian = the adult responsible for a child    ·    withdraw = to take back a permission you gave before    ·    risk assessment = a check of what could go wrong and for whom    ·    harm = damage or injury to a person    ·    hesitation = a pause because you are not sure    ·    identify = to show who or where somebody is    ·    sequencer = a word that shows the order of steps    ·    imperative = the form of a verb used for instructions
