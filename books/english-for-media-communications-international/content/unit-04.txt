@TASK Part 5: Choosing the Right Channel
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
Communications teams spend a great deal of time arguing about channels, and most of those arguments are really arguments about audiences. A channel is only a way of carrying a message; it has no value on its own. The largest channel is not the best channel, and the newest is rarely the most useful. What matters is a simple pair of questions: who are we trying to reach, and what do those people actually use? The answers are often uncomfortable. A team that is proud of its social media may find that the families it serves are not there at all, and that the account is read mostly by colleagues in other organisations. A team that thinks print is old-fashioned may discover that a single page pinned up in a clinic is read by more people, and more carefully, than a month of posts. Cost matters too, but not in the way people expect. The cheapest channel to produce is often the most expensive to be understood through, because a message that is misread has to be sent again. Accessibility is the measure that teams forget most often: a video with no subtitles excludes anybody who cannot hear it or cannot play it, and a message in one language excludes everybody who does not read that language. The best teams therefore choose two channels rather than one, and they choose them for different audiences, not for the same one twice.

@TASK Part 5: Reaching People Where They Are
@RUBRIC Read about reaching audiences in different places. Then mark each sentence True (T) or False (F).
@TEXT
The same message often needs a different channel in each country where an organisation works. In one district, almost everybody has a smartphone and good data, and a short video is the obvious choice. Two hundred kilometres away, data is expensive and the signal is poor, and the same video would simply not be watched. There, a community radio announcement at the right hour reaches more people than any post. In a third place, neither works as well as a notice read aloud at a weekly market. None of these audiences is harder to reach than the others; they are reached differently. The mistake that international teams make most often is to design a message in one place and then send it everywhere, because that is cheaper and faster. It is cheaper only until nobody acts on it. The teams that do this well write the message centrally and choose the channel locally, and they trust the colleague who lives there to say which channel that is.

@TASK Part 8: Reading — The Newsletter and the Post
@PRE read
have you ever been surprised by which channel worked best?
@TEXT
Last year the Lima office ran the same appeal twice, by accident. Tomás had written a long piece for the monthly newsletter, which goes to eleven hundred donors, and Nadia had made a short post from the same material, which reached forty-one thousand people. On the numbers, there was no contest. The post reached nearly forty times as many people as the newsletter, and the team said so in the monthly report. Then the finance office sent through the figures. The newsletter had raised more than three times as much money as the post, from a hundred and nine people rather than from nine. Nobody had expected this, and the explanation took a while to find. It was not that donors dislike social media. It was that the newsletter arrived in an inbox that the reader had chosen to open, at a moment when they were sitting down, with enough detail to answer the question that a person always asks before they give money: what exactly will this pay for? The post had reached far more people, but it had reached most of them while they were doing something else. The team did not stop posting. But it stopped reporting reach as though reach were the result.

channel = a way of reaching people with a message    ·    audience = the people you want to reach    ·    reach = the number of people who see a message    ·    engagement = what people do with a message, not only how many see it    ·    target audience = the particular group you want to reach    ·    newsletter = a regular email or paper update    ·    press release = an official statement sent to the news    ·    community radio = a small station that serves one area    ·    bulk SMS = one short message sent to many phones at once    ·    accessibility = how easily everybody can use something    ·    subtitles = the words written on a video    ·    exclude = to leave somebody out    ·    misread = to understand something wrongly    ·    reliable = something you can trust to work    ·    broadcast = to send out a programme to many listeners
