@TEXT
A quote is a promise. When Meridian Relief puts a sentence inside quotation marks, it is promising the reader that the person said exactly those words, and promising the speaker that their words will not be improved, shortened into something sharper, or moved into a context they never agreed to. @TASK Part 3: Ingrid Explains Reporting Speech
@AUDIO 10.2
@PRE listen
when is it better to quote somebody, and when is it better to paraphrase?
@TEXT
Ingrid: There are two honest ways to use what somebody says. You quote them word for word, inside quotation marks. Or you report them in your own words, with attribution and no marks. What you may never do is the thing in between: tidy a sentence and then put marks around it. That is not a quote; it is your sentence wearing somebody else’s name. So when should you quote? When the words carry something — a way of putting it you could not improve, or a judgement that must clearly be theirs. When should you report? When the information matters more than the phrasing, or when the speaker was tired, or speaking through an interpreter, or simply not making a speech. And always attribute. A reader who sees ‘Mrs Dela Cruz said’ can decide what to think. A reader who sees a claim with no name has to trust us, and we should not ask for that when we do not need to.

@TASK Part 5: Telling the Story Truthfully
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
There are two honest ways to use what somebody has told you, and one dishonest one that is extremely common. The honest ways are to quote them word for word inside quotation marks, or to report them in your own words with attribution. The dishonest way is the thing in between: taking a sentence that was slightly rambling, tidying it, sharpening it, and then putting quotation marks around the improved version. Almost everybody who does it believes they are helping, and almost everybody it is done to notices. A quote is not a service to the reader; it is a promise that these were the words. Attribution is the second half of the same promise. A reader who sees ‘Mrs Dela Cruz said’ can weigh the claim against the person who made it; a reader who sees the same claim floating free has to trust the organisation instead, and an organisation that asks for that trust unnecessarily will eventually spend it on something that matters. There is a further problem in international work. A quote that has passed through an interpreter is not verbatim in the language it is printed in. Honest teams say ‘speaking through an interpreter’, read the final version back in the original language, and accept the changes the speaker asks for. The result is less polished than an invented sentence would be. It is also the only version the speaker would recognise as theirs.

@TASK Part 5: Whose Words Are They?
@RUBRIC Read about quotes across languages. Then mark each sentence True (T) or False (F).
@TEXT
Almost every organisation that works internationally publishes quotes that were not spoken in the language they appear in, and most of them say nothing about it. The sentence looks the same on the page as one spoken in English, and the reader assumes the speaker chose those words. They did not. Somebody chose words with the same meaning, as carefully as they could, under time pressure and usually without a chance to check. This is not dishonest in itself, and the alternative — publishing nothing that was said in another language — would silence most of the people whose stories matter. What is dishonest is hiding it. Three habits fix almost all of it. Write ‘speaking through an interpreter’ where it is true, so that the reader knows what they are reading. Read the final version back in the original language before publication, not the interpreter’s notes but the sentence that will actually be printed. And when the speaker says that a word is not quite right, change it, even when the change makes the sentence flatter. A flatter sentence that the speaker would recognise is worth more than a beautiful one that they would not.

@TASK Part 6: Why We Never Invent a Quote (80–120 words)
@RUBRIC Write about why a quote must never be invented or improved. Give reasons and an example.
@MODEL
An invented quote is the one mistake from which an organisation does not recover. Everything else we publish — a figure, a date, a caption — can be corrected, and a reader will accept a correction. A quote cannot be corrected, because the person either said it or did not, and if they did not, every other quote we have ever printed becomes a question. Improving a quote is the same offence in a smaller coat. For example, a sentence that was rambling can be paraphrased honestly with attribution; what it cannot be is tidied and then put inside quotation marks.

@TASK Part 10: End of Course — The Four Values
@RUBRIC You have reached the end of the course. Everything in this book rests on four simple values. Keep them with you.
@TEXT
Consent — never use a person’s words, face or story without permission, and remember that permission is specific to a use.  ·  Accuracy — check every fact and every quote, and never invent anything.  ·  Dignity — treat every person with respect, and protect those who could be at risk.  ·  Do no harm — if something could hurt someone, do not publish it.
