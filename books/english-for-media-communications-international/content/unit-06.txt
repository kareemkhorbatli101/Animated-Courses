@TASK Part 3: Tomás Explains Interviewing
@AUDIO 6.2
@PRE listen
what makes a good interviewer, in your opinion?
@TEXT
Tomás: People think interviewing is about asking good questions. It is mostly about listening, and about being comfortable with silence. My first question is always open and easy: could you tell me about your day? It lets the person choose where to begin, and where somebody begins tells you a great deal. After that, almost everything should be a follow-up. If an answer is short, do not move to the next question on your list; ask about the answer you just received. You should never ask a leading question — ‘That must have been frightening, wasn’t it?’ — because the person will agree with you out of politeness and you will publish your own words in their mouth. With an interpreter, look at the person, not the interpreter, and allow twice as much time. And remember that the interview can stop at any moment. If somebody looks tired or upset, put the recorder down. The story is never worth more than the person telling it.

@TASK Part 5: The Art of the Interview
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
Most new communications officers prepare for an interview by writing a list of questions, and most of them discover, somewhere around the third interview, that the list is the problem. A list makes you listen for the moment when you can ask the next thing on it, rather than listening to what is actually being said. The officers who are good at this prepare differently. They write down two or three things they genuinely want to understand, and then they put the paper away. The first question should be open and easy, because its job is not to collect information but to let the person choose where to begin. Where somebody begins is itself information. After that, almost every question should be a follow-up: ‘you said the first flood — would you tell me about that?’ Follow-ups show that you were listening, which is the only thing that makes a stranger willing to continue. There are two rules that should never be broken. Never ask a leading question, because a polite person will agree with you and you will end up publishing your own opinion in somebody else’s words. And never continue when somebody is tired, upset or simply finished. An interview that is stopped early can be continued another day. An interview that is pushed too far usually cannot be repaired, and the person is left feeling that they were a source rather than a human being.

@TASK Part 5: Listening Across Languages
@RUBRIC Read about interviewing through an interpreter. Then mark each sentence True (T) or False (F).
@TEXT
A great many interviews in international work are done through an interpreter, and they are not simply slower versions of ordinary interviews. They are a different skill. The first thing to accept is the time: everything takes at least twice as long, and an interview planned for thirty minutes should be given an hour. The second is where you look. It is natural to look at the person who is speaking your language, but if you look at the interpreter, the person being interviewed will quickly feel that they are talking to a machine. Look at them, even while you cannot understand a word. The third is the brief you give the interpreter beforehand: ask for a full translation rather than a summary, and ask them to translate hesitations and unfinished sentences too, because those carry meaning. The fourth is the quote. A quote that has travelled through two languages is not the person’s exact words, and it should not be presented as though it were. Honest teams write ‘speaking through an interpreter’ and read the final version back in the original language before they publish it.

@TASK Part 6: Open Questions or Closed Questions? (80–120 words)
@RUBRIC Write about when to use open and closed questions. Give reasons and an example.
@MODEL
Open questions and closed questions do different jobs, and a good interview uses both. Open questions invite a person to speak in their own way, and they belong at the beginning and whenever you want to understand rather than confirm. Closed questions belong at the end, when you need one clear fact: a date, a number, a spelling. The mistake is to use a closed question when you want a story. For example, ‘Were you frightened?’ can only be answered yes or no, and it also suggests the answer. ‘How did you feel?’ gives the person the whole room.

@TASK 7B: The Interview-Coaching Briefing
@AUDIO 6.5
@PRE listen
how should a manager give feedback after a difficult interview?
@TEXT
Ingrid: Before we go out tomorrow, I want to say something about how we coach each other on interviews, because it is the part of the work people find hardest to hear feedback on. Start by asking the officer how they thought it went. Almost always they will name the problem themselves, and feedback that somebody has named themselves is feedback they can use. Then name one thing that genuinely worked, and be specific: ‘your first question was open and easy’ is useful, ‘that was good’ is not. After that, offer one thing to change — one, not four — and make it concrete enough to practise. ‘Listen better’ is useless advice. ‘Count to five before you speak again’ is advice somebody can follow tomorrow. And finally, offer to go together next time. Interviewing is a craft, and nobody has ever improved at it by being told that they should have done better.

@TASK Part 10: Unit 6 glossary
@TEXT
interview = a meeting where you ask somebody questions    ·    open question = a question that invites a long answer    ·    closed question = a question answered with yes or no    ·    follow-up = a second question about what was just said    ·    leading question = a question that suggests the answer you want    ·    off the record = said on the understanding that it will not be published    ·    interpreter = a person who helps two speakers understand each other    ·    transcript = the written record of what was said    ·    deduction = a conclusion you reach from the evidence you have    ·    evidence = the facts that help you decide what is true    ·    source = a person or place that gives information    ·    hesitation = a pause because you are not sure    ·    brief = short, using few words    ·    willing = ready and happy to do something    ·    condition = something that must be agreed first
