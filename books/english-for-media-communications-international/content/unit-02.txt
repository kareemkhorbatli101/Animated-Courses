@TASK Part 2: Notice
@TEXT
There are two cameras in the Geneva office and only one in Nairobi. There are a few recorders, but there is not much storage space, so the team deletes old files every month. There has been a lot of interest in the winter campaign, and there have been many requests for photographs. Notice the difference: we use 'there is' and 'there are' for the present, and 'there has been' and 'there have been' when something started in the past and matters now. Notice too that 'much' goes with uncountable nouns and 'many' goes with countable ones.
@LINE Look at the sentences again. Which nouns are countable? Which are uncountable?

@TASK Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAMMAR
 | Form | Use | Example
much / little | with uncountable nouns | amount, not number
many / few | with countable nouns | number, not amount
there is / there are | present | There are two cameras here.
there has / have been | from the past until now | There has been a lot of interest.
@REMEMBER Use 'much' and 'little' with uncountable nouns (money, storage, time) and 'many' and 'few' with countable nouns (cameras, cards, files). 'There has been' connects the past to now.
@WATCH Watch out: 'much' is unusual in positive sentences. Say 'a lot of storage', not 'much storage', unless the sentence is negative or a question.
@GLOSS countable = something you can count one by one    ·    uncountable = something you measure rather than count

@TASK Part 5: The Tools of Visual Storytelling
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
A communications team does not need much equipment, but it needs the right equipment, and it needs to look after it. A camera, a recorder, a tripod and a laptop are enough for most of the work that a small team does. What matters more than the number of tools is the care that the team takes with them. There has been a change in the last ten years. Cameras have become cheaper and lighter, and a good phone can now take a photograph that is strong enough for a report. This is useful for teams that work far from a capital city, where there is not much money and even less time. But a lighter camera has not made the work easier in every way. There are more images than ever, so there is more to check, more to caption and more to store. Storage is the quiet problem of every newsroom: there are only a few people who enjoy sorting files, and there are many who put it off. A good team therefore agrees three simple habits. Copy every file the same day. Write the caption while you still remember the name. Delete nothing until it is in the archive. These habits are not exciting, but they save a story that would otherwise be lost.

Many communications teams work with very little. There is rarely much money for new equipment, and there are rarely enough people. Yet some of the strongest stories come from the smallest teams. The reason is simple: when there is not much equipment, a team thinks harder about what it is trying to say. A single good photograph, taken with care and with consent, can say more than twenty taken quickly. A short recording of one person speaking clearly can be stronger than a long film with poor sound. @TEXT
Ingrid: Before every field visit, we do a kit-check together, and I want to explain why. There have been three shoots this year where something small went wrong, and each time the cost was not the equipment but the people's time. First, check the batteries, and check the spares. An empty battery is the most common fault in every office. Second, check the storage. There is never much space on a card, so copy and clear before you travel, not after. Third, check the consent forms, and check that they are in a language the people can actually read. Fourth, check that two people know how to use each piece of equipment, because one person is always ill on the wrong day. And finally, report anything broken the same day. Nobody is in trouble for an honest fault. The only mistake that costs us is the one that nobody mentions until the morning of the shoot.

@TASK Part 8: Reading — The Battery That Almost Stopped the Shoot
@PRE read
what is the smallest thing that could stop a day's work?
@TEXT
Joseph had planned the day carefully. A family in a village outside Nairobi had agreed to tell their story, and they had chosen the morning because the children would be at school. Joseph had the camera, the recorder and the consent forms. He arrived early, greeted the grandmother, explained once more what the photographs would be used for, and sat down to listen. Twenty minutes into the interview, the recorder stopped. The battery was empty. Joseph had charged it the night before, or he thought he had: in fact he had plugged the charger into a socket that did not work. There was no spare in his bag, because the spare was in the office in a different district. He had two choices. He could ask the grandmother to repeat everything, which would waste an hour of her morning and would make her tell the story a second time, less naturally. Or he could write the rest by hand and come back another day for the recording. He chose to write. Then he apologised, explained exactly what had happened, and asked whether he could return on Friday. She laughed and said that she had plenty of time, which was kind but not the point. Since that morning, Joseph has carried two spare batteries. There has not been a repeat.
