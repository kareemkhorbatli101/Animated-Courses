@TEXT
Ingrid: A campaign plan should fit on one page, and if it does not, the campaign is probably too complicated. @TASK Part 5: How to Plan a Campaign
@RUBRIC Read the text and choose the correct answer.
@TEXT
Most campaign plans fail in the same two places, and neither of them is the idea. The first is the objective. A great many plans begin with something like ‘raise awareness’, which cannot be measured, cannot be disproved and therefore cannot be managed. A usable objective says who will know or do what, and by when: four thousand families in three districts will know that the clinic has moved, before the first of March. That sentence can be checked afterwards, which is uncomfortable and is exactly the point. The second place plans fail is ownership. Every step on a timeline needs one name beside it. A task owned by ‘the team’ is owned by nobody; a task owned by three people is usually done twice or not at all, and the person who notices the gap is always the one with the least time to fix it. Beyond those two, the discipline is honesty. Put in the dates you will actually meet, not the ones that look impressive. Build a margin into the steps that depend on somebody outside the team, because those are the ones that slip. And write Plan B before you need it, when you are calm, rather than in the week of the launch, when nobody thinks clearly and the loudest voice usually wins. A plan is not a promise that nothing will change. It is an agreement about what everybody will do when it does.
@EX 0. Most campaign plans fail in two places. (example)
@MCQ 1 | What is wrong with ‘raise awareness’ as an objective? | It is too long | It cannot be measured | It is too expensive | It is not ambitious
@MCQ 2 | What happens to a task owned by three people? | It is done well | It is done twice or not at all | It is cancelled | It is done early
@MCQ 3 | When should Plan B be written? | In the week of the launch | Before you need it | After the campaign | Never

@TASK Part 5: Planning for Real Life in the Field
@RUBRIC Read about planning in difficult conditions. Then mark each sentence True (T) or False (F).
@TEXT
A plan made in a head office and a plan made in a field office are different documents, even when they share a page. In a capital city, a delay of two days is an inconvenience. In a district where the road is passable for part of the year, where the electricity comes on in the evening, and where the interpreter is also the only nurse, two days can move a launch by a month. Teams that plan well across both places do three things. They ask the field office for the dates rather than proposing them, because only the field office knows which week the market is held and which week nobody will be at home. They build margin into every step that depends on somebody outside the team, and they put that margin in the plan where everybody can see it, rather than hiding it in their own heads. And they agree in advance what will happen if a step slips: not ‘we will discuss it’, which means an argument in the worst week, but a decision taken calmly in advance. None of this makes a plan more likely to survive unchanged. It makes the team more likely to survive the change.
