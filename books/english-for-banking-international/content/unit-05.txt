@PART Part 2 · Grammar — Active and Passive Voice | Pre-class input

@TASK 2A | Notice the grammar
@INSTR Read the short dialogue. Underline the passive verbs. Who or what does the action?
@SCRIPT
Customer: I didn’t get my code.
Petra: Let me check. The code is sent to the number on your file.
Customer: That number is old.
Petra: Then it must be changed first. Your details are checked by the system, so the new number will be confirmed by a text tomorrow.
@NUM
is sent — active or passive? ____
must be changed — who changes it? ____
are checked by the system — why does the speaker say ‘by the system’? ____
@GF
@GRAMMAR
Form | Use | Example
am / is / are + past participle | the present passive, for systems and rules | A code is sent to your phone.
was / were + past participle | the past passive, for what happened | The card was blocked last night.
modal + be + past participle | the passive with can, must, will | The number must be changed first.
@REMEMBER use the passive when the doer is obvious, unknown or not important. Add by + the doer only when it matters.
@WATCH
✗ The code is send to your phone. → ✓ The code is sent to your phone.
✗ Your card was block. → ✓ Your card was blocked.

@TASK 5C | Should you trust the advert?
@INSTR Read the short note. What is the difference between a bank message and a scam message?
@READ Two Messages, One Difference | About 120 words
A customer received two messages on the same morning. The first said: “Your payment of 240 has been received. No action is needed.” The second said: “Your account has been blocked. Click here within one hour or it will be closed.” Both looked official. The first was from the bank. The second was not. A real message tells you what has happened. A false message tells you to act now, and gives you a link. The bank never asks for a password or a one-time code, and it never gives a deadline of one hour. If a message makes you feel in a hurry, that feeling is the warning.

@PART Part 8 · Global Case Study — The App-Only Bank | Practitioner track · read then discuss
@INSTR Before you read: could a bank work with no branches at all? Who would it not suit?
@READ A Bank With No Door | About 205 words · a made-up example based on real practice
An app-only bank was launched in four countries at once. It had no branches, no counters and no queues. Accounts were opened in eight minutes from a phone, and identity was confirmed by a short video instead of a visit. Costs were low, so the rates offered were better than those of older banks, and in the first year two million accounts were opened. Then the complaints began. They were not about the technology, which worked well. They were about the moments when something went wrong. A customer whose card was used by a thief wanted to speak to a person, and there was nobody to speak to. An older customer could not pass the video check, because the light in her flat was poor. A small business owner needed to pay in cash, and cash cannot be sent through an app. The bank made two changes. It opened a telephone line answered by people, not machines, twenty-four hours a day. And it agreed with a chain of shops that its customers could pay in cash there. It still has no branches. But it learned that a bank without a door still needs a way in.
@GLOSS
app-only bank — a bank with no branches
identity check — proving who you are
complaint — when a customer says something is wrong
cash deposit point — a place where customers can pay in cash

@PART Part 9 · Branch Case Study — Warsaw in Focus: Banking on a Weak Signal | Practitioner track · read then decide
@INSTR Before you read: what happens to a digital service when the connection is poor?
@READ When the Signal Drops | About 185 words
The Harbour Bank branch in Warsaw serves a wide area, and many of its customers live in villages where the mobile signal is weak. The bank has asked every branch to move routine payments to the app, and in the city this is working well. In the villages it is not. A customer told Marta Nowak last week that her payment had been started but never finished, because the one-time code arrived forty minutes late. Another said that the app had worked at her daughter’s house in town but not at home. Three customers have now gone back to paying at the counter, and two have said they will move to another bank. Marta understands the policy: payments made in the app cost the bank far less than payments made at the counter. But she also knows that a service that fails on a weak signal is not a service. She has one morning with the regional team on Thursday, and she wants to go with a clear proposal.
