@PART Part 2 · Grammar — Quantifiers (much, many, a few, a little) | Pre-class input

@TASK 2A | Notice the grammar
@INSTR Read the short dialogue. Underline much, many, a few and a little. Which words go with countable nouns?
@SCRIPT
Grace: Good morning. How much cash would you like today?
Customer: Three hundred, please. And how many notes can I have?
Grace: As many as you like. There are a few large notes left, and a little change.
Customer: Large notes, please. I don’t have much time.
@NUM
much — countable or uncountable? ____
many — countable or uncountable? ____
a few / a little — which one goes with notes? ____
@GF
@GRAMMAR
Quantifier | Use | Example |
much | uncountable nouns, mostly in questions and negatives | How much cash do you need? |
many | countable nouns | How many notes would you like? |
a few / a little | a small amount: a few (countable), a little (uncountable) | a few coins; a little change |
@REMEMBER cash, money, time and change are uncountable → much, a little. notes, coins, accounts and forms are countable → many, a few.
@WATCH
✗ How many money do you need? → ✓ How much money do you need?
✗ I have a little coins. → ✓ I have a few coins.

@TASK 5A | Teller guidelines
@INSTR Read the counter guidelines. Notice the quantifiers (much, many, a few, a little).
@READ Harbour Bank — Guidelines for the Counter | About 200 words
These guidelines are the same in every Harbour Bank branch, because every customer should get the same careful service. Before you pay out cash, always check two things: the customer’s ID and the balance on the account. If the amount is large, ask a colleague to check with you. It takes a little time, but it prevents mistakes. Count notes twice. Count them once in the drawer and once in front of the customer. Many customers like to see the money counted, and a few will count it again themselves. That is normal, and you should never hurry them. Ask how many large notes the customer would like. Some people want large notes because they are easier to carry; others want small notes because they are easier to spend. Give a receipt for every transaction, even a small one. Finally, remember that not much goes wrong at a quiet counter; most mistakes happen when there is a queue and too little time. If you feel rushed, slow down and say, “One moment, please.” A short pause is better than a wrong amount.

@TASK 5C | A customer’s bank statement
@INSTR Read the short statement note. What does each line show?
@READ Reading a Statement | About 130 words
A statement is a list of everything that has happened on an account. Each line shows the date, a short description, the amount, and the balance after the transaction. Money coming in is usually on the left, and money going out is on the right. A customer called the branch last week because she could not understand one line. It said “DD — CITY POWER — 85.00”. DD means direct debit: the customer gave the electricity company permission to collect the payment every month. Another line said “ATM — 14 MAR — 200.00”. That was a cash withdrawal from a machine. The last line showed the balance: the money left in the account. When you explain a statement, start with the date and read across the line slowly.

@PART Part 8 · Global Case Study — Cutting the Queue | Practitioner track · read then discuss
@INSTR Before you read: what makes a bank queue move faster — more staff, or something else?
@READ Twenty Minutes Saved | About 200 words · a made-up example based on real practice
A bank with branches in several countries had the same problem everywhere: at the end of the month, customers waited twenty minutes or more. The bank did not want to pay for many extra staff, so it looked at the queue itself. It found that a little over half of the people in the queue did not need a teller at all. They were checking a balance, printing a statement, or paying a routine bill — all things a machine or the app can do. So the bank made three changes. First, it put a member of staff at the door to ask each customer what they needed. This person is called a greeter. If the customer only needed a balance, the greeter showed them the machine. Second, it moved bill payments to a separate, faster desk. Third, it taught customers to use the app, one customer at a time, while they waited. After three months, the average wait fell from twenty minutes to eight. The bank had not added a single teller.
@GLOSS
greeter — a member of staff who meets customers at the door
queue — a line of people waiting
average wait — the usual waiting time
routine payment — a payment that happens again and again

@PART Part 9 · Branch Case Study — Istanbul in Focus: When the Rate Moves | Practitioner track · read then decide
@INSTR Before you read: what happens at the counter when an exchange rate changes quickly?
@READ A Morning of Questions | About 175 words
The Harbour Bank branch in Istanbul opens at nine. This morning the exchange rate has moved a long way overnight, and many customers want to change money before it moves again. By half past nine there is a queue of about twenty people. Most of them have only one question: “How much will I get today?” Zeynep Kaya is on the exchange desk. She is careful and polite, but each customer wants to talk about the rate, and a few are angry about the spread between the buy rate and the sell rate. Meanwhile, the ordinary counter has its own queue of deposits and withdrawals. The branch manager, Kerem Aydın, has four staff this morning and two desks. He knows the rate will probably move again at eleven, when the market opens. He must decide how to use his people in the next two hours, and what to tell the customers who are waiting.
