When an office is inspected, a process follows. The first phase involves a document review to confirm every required permit and statement exists. Inspectors then conduct an on-site check by comparing what the records room actually holds against what the register says. Discrepancies are raised and classified as serious or minor. The office submits corrections with evidence. @PANEL
Lead guides face difficult decisions when an office cannot meet every obligation at once. In any office, older arrangements rarely match current requirements. Lead guides must prioritize obligations based on risk to people and on the number of parties affected. Safety and licensing obligations receive priority over filing formalities. Matters affecting guests and agencies receive the strictest attention. Decisions with the highest legal exposure are examined first. Operational pressure is considered but never overrides the law. Lead guides maintain transparency by explaining their reasoning to the company. This transparency builds trust and understanding.

@INSTR Read about professional ethics and choose the best answer.
_80
@PASSAGE
Guiding practice is guided by core principles: legality, fairness, competence, and independence of judgement. Legality means acting only in ways the law allows, even where enforcement is weak. Fairness requires treating every guest, agency and supplier by the same rules. Competence means signing only what you are qualified to understand, and taking advice on the rest. Independence of judgement means a report rests on the facts, not on who is asking. These principles guide every professional decision and ensure ethical practice.

@PART 7 | Part 7: Culture
@EX Part 7: Law and Compliance in Mexican Contexts
@INSTR Read about law and compliance in Mexico and answer questions.
_80
@PANEL
Mexico has a long legal tradition and also a demanding compliance situation. A guide must be licensed by the Ministry of Tourism before working with a group, and the National Association of Tourist Guides and the National Institute of Anthropology and History set the rules on permits, group size and access. Foreign missions add their own requirements on top, so an office may need a certificate from the state government, a certified translation, and a mission's own consent forms at the same time. Compliance is complicated by records held in several registries at once, permits that expire at different times, and the cost of professional advice. Lawyers and accountants work to keep offices registered alongside the tax authority. Missing paperwork affects organisations across the country. Guides demonstrate extraordinary persistence and resourcefulness. Their work includes report drafting, record reconstruction, and the documentation a foreign mission will demand. International recognition of Mexican guides acknowledges their skill and dedication. Their resilience and procedural knowledge inspire support from partners abroad.

1. Why is compliance for an office serving foreign groups particularly challenging?
2. What is the scale of Mexico's missing-paperwork problem?
