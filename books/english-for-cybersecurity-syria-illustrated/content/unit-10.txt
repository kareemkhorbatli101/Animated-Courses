@BX A good IT department looks after three relationships. It listens to its users, because daily users see things no report shows. @EX Part 5: The User, the Provider and the Team
@IMG fig-10-4.png
@CAP Figure 10.4  ·  Three groups: the user, the provider and the team.
@IT Before you read: which three groups does an IT department stand between?
@GL satisfied = happy, because what was promised was given    ·    advisory = a note from the provider about a fault    ·    awareness = the staff knowing what to look out for
@BX An IT and security department stands between three groups, and it has to speak to all of them in different words. The users come first. They see things no report shows: the machine slow every afternoon, the message that looked wrong, the colleague using somebody else’s account. Most never reaches the department, because people do not know it matters, or fear looking foolish. So a good department asks, regularly and without blame, and thanks the person who reports nothing — because the one thanked for nothing comes back with something. Second, the provider. The provider sends advisories, and those advisories must be read on the day they arrive, not filed. An advisory says what is wrong, how serious, and what to do; a department that reads them late chooses to be surprised. Third, the team itself. The officer who learns something on Tuesday must tell the others by Friday, or the department has one knowledgeable person, not four. That is what the weekly meeting is for, and it is why the manager's job there is mostly to listen. A department that is good at all three is not the one with the newest machines. It is the one where the news travels.

@EX Part 5: Carrying a Famous Name and Our Own
@IT Before you read: what does it mean for a Syrian company to work to an international standard?
@GL responsibility = a duty; something you must do properly    ·    accredited = officially approved to work to a standard
@BX When an international provider accredited Cham Systems as its partner in Syria, Maher told the team two things on the same morning. The first was that the name on the certificate was not theirs: it belonged to a company in another country that had never met any of them, and it could be taken away in a week. The second was that the name on the door was theirs, and it could not. Being accredited brought real advantages — training, advisories that arrived first, a direct line to the provider's engineers when something was genuinely wrong. It also brought rules: every advisory read within a day, every engineer certified, every restore tested quarterly and recorded. The team followed them, and not only because they had to. A trainer came from abroad in the second year and said afterwards that he had not expected this record-keeping in Damascus. Maher told the team about it that week, and he said it carefully: he was proud, but he wanted them to understand that the trainer's surprise was the trainer's problem and not a compliment. The work was good because they had made it good, not because somebody from outside had been kind enough to notice.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — Launching Security for a New System
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: why must a department be trained before a new system arrives, not after?
@GL launch = when a new system is first used    ·    pilot = a small first group, used to find problems early    ·    rollout = giving the new system to everybody, group by group
@BX When a large organisation replaced the system nine thousand people used daily, the technical work was not the hard part. The hard part was that on the first morning nine thousand people would sign in to something they had never seen, and the help desk would take a year’s calls in a week. So the project did three things before the launch. It trained the help desk three months early on a working copy — not a presentation, but the system itself, with real tasks. It ran a pilot with one department of forty people, chosen for being the most difficult, not the most willing, and fixed what they found. And it wrote in advance the twenty answers the help desk would need, in plain words, on one page. The rollout then went department by department over six weeks, not all at once. The project manager said none of this was clever, and that the only unusual decision was training the help desk before the system was finished, when everybody could see reasons to wait.

@IT Before you read: what does a Syrian company gain, and what does it owe, when it carries an international name?
@BX Cham Systems became the accredited partner of an international provider three years ago. Maher has told the story to every new member of staff since, always the same way. The accreditation brought good things that are hard to get in Syria: proper training, advisories that arrive on the first day rather than the fourth, and a real engineer when something is genuinely beyond them. It also brought obligations costing money and time: every advisory read within a day, every engineer re-certified, every restore tested quarterly and recorded. Some rules make little sense in Damascus, where the power schedule decides what is possible, and the team has written exceptions for two, both approved. What Maher says to new staff is this: the certificate on the wall is borrowed and the work is ours. If the provider withdrew the accreditation tomorrow, every habit it taught us would still be worth keeping, and that is the test of whether a rule was ever really about the certificate.
