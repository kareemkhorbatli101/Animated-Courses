@EX Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAM 1700,2450,2200,2676
 | Form | Use | Example
Comparative | -er + than / more + than | comparing two things | This disk is fuller than that one.
Superlative | the -est / the most | comparing three or more | This is the fullest disk of all.
@END
@BR Remember: Short adjective + -er / -est (low → lower → the lowest).  Long adjective + more / the most (more careful).
@WO ✗ gooder / more good → ✓ better / the best.   ✗ lower as yesterday → ✓ lower than yesterday.

@EX Part 5: Why Numbers Matter in Diagnosis
@IMG fig-04-4.png
@CAP Figure 4.4  ·  Readings tell the story: in range, too low, too high.
@IT Before you read: is measuring better than guessing? Why?
@GL diagnosis = finding out what is really wrong    ·    tolerance = the range that is allowed    ·    baseline = the normal reading, measured when all is well
@BX Modern security work is about numbers. A careful officer does not guess that a machine is slow; she reads the free space and writes it down. She does not guess that the backup is working; she reads its age in hours. Each reading has a tolerance, and a reading inside the tolerance is not news. The important thing is the comparison. One reading on one day tells you very little. The same reading next to last week’s tells you whether the problem is getting better or worse, and the same reading next to the other machines tells you which one is the worst. That is why a good officer keeps a baseline: the normal number, measured when everything was well. Without a baseline, forty-one failed sign-ins looks frightening or ordinary, and nobody can say which. With one, it is plainly much higher than the three or four of a normal night, and it becomes the first thing to look at in the morning.

@EX Part 5: Checking Systems in Syria
@IT Before you read: why might a system in Syria need checking more often than the book says?
@GL rough = hard, with difficult conditions    ·    check interval = how often a check is done
@BX Systems in Syria often work in hard conditions. The power goes off and comes back several times a day, and every time it does, a machine that was writing a file may not finish. Dust gets into fans, so machines run hotter and their disks fail earlier than the maker expects. Connections are slow and shared, so a backup that should take one hour may take six, and may not finish before the office opens. Because of this, a careful officer in Damascus does not simply follow the interval in the book. She reads the free space weekly instead of monthly, and she checks the backup age every morning rather than every Friday. It is more work, but it is far better than finding out in June that the last good copy of the records is from March.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — Why Standard Figures Matter
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: why should offices in every country measure the same things in the same way?
@GL standard = an agreed correct way for everybody    ·    figure = a number in a report    ·    compare = to put two numbers side by side
@BX A company with offices in six countries once had a problem it could not see. Every office sent a monthly report, and every office wrote it differently. One counted failed sign-ins for the whole month; another counted only the ones after midnight. One measured free space in per cent; another in gigabytes, and a third only wrote “fine”. When the head office tried to compare them, it could not. The director asked for one page, the same for everybody: five numbers, the same units, the same days of the month, and the normal range printed next to each one. The first month was uncomfortable, because two offices that had always written “fine” turned out to be the worst in the group. But by the end of the year the reports took ten minutes to read instead of two days, and the head office could see a problem growing in one office before that office asked for help. The chief engineer said it afterwards: you cannot manage what you do not measure the same way twice.

@PART Part 9  ·  Local Case Study — Syria in Focus: Adjusting the Backup Interval
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class decide
@IT Before you read: should an officer in Syria follow the interval in the book exactly?
@BX Maher looked after a small company whose office was on a street with four power cuts a day. The maker of the backup program recommended one backup each night, which is normal everywhere. But in that office the night backup almost never finished: the power went off at some point between eleven and four, and the copy stopped halfway. For months the sheet said the backup had run, because it had started, and nobody checked whether it had ended. Maher changed two things. He moved the backup to the middle of the morning, when the generator was on and the office was working, and he set it to copy only the files that had changed, so that it took twenty minutes instead of six hours. He also changed the sheet: instead of “backup run”, it now asked for the age of the last finished backup in hours. The first week, that number was ninety-one hours. By the fourth week it was four.

@BX Answer frame: “I think the office should ___ because ___. The reading to watch is ___. This is better than ___ because ___.”
@LM Model — read this first:
@BX I think the office should move the backup into the hours with power, because a copy that stops halfway is not a copy at all. The reading to watch is the age of the last finished backup, not whether the backup started. This is better than backing up once a week, because a week of lost work is too much for a small company to rewrite.
