@PART Part 2  ·  Grammar — Present Simple vs Present Continuous
@TRK [OP] Operator track  ·  pre-class input
@EX Part 2: Notice
@IMG fig-01-4.png
@CAP Figure 1.4  ·  Present simple (every day) and present continuous (now).
@IN Read these lines from the help desk. Underline the verbs. Which are routines, and which are happening now?
@BX Nabil checks the backup. He usually starts at eight. Right now, he is resetting a password for a user. The help desk opens every morning. Today it is answering many calls, because the office is busy.
@EX Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAM 1700,2450,2200,2676
 | Form | Use | Example
Present simple | I/you check; he checks | routines, facts | The help desk opens every day.
Present continuous | am/is/are + -ing | now, this week | Nabil is resetting a password now.
@END
@BR Remember: every day, usually → present simple.  now, this week, at the moment → present continuous.
@WO ✗ He check the backup. → ✓ He checks the backup. (add -s for he/she/it).   ✗ I resetting now. → ✓ I am resetting now.

@EX Part 5: What Is an IT and Security Department?
@IT Before you read: what three areas do you think an IT and security department has?
@GL help desk = the place users call when something does not work    ·    ticket = a record of a problem and the work done on it    ·    backup = a copy of your files, kept in a safe place
@BX An IT and security department looks after the computers of a company. It has three main areas: the help desk, the workshop bench, and the store. A user calls the help desk, and the help-desk officer writes a ticket. A technician then checks the machine at the bench. The store keeps spare machines, disks and licences. The department also keeps the accounts: it opens an account for every new worker and closes it when a worker leaves. Every night it makes a backup of the files. A good department is tidy, careful and honest. It explains the problem in simple words, and it tells the user the truth about the time the work will take.

@EX Part 5: Computers in Syria
@IT Before you read: why do offices in Syria keep computers for many years?
@GL licence = the paper that gives you the right to use a program    ·    power cut = a time when the electricity stops
@BX In Syria, offices keep computers for many years. New machines are expensive and hard to import, so people look after the ones they have. Dust, heat and power cuts are hard on a computer, so the IT department cleans the machines and plans its work around the hours when the electricity is on. Many programs are old, because a new licence is difficult to buy from Damascus. An old machine can still do good work if somebody cares for it: a clean fan, a safe password and a backup every night are worth more than a new computer with none of them.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — A Small Office Gets a Help Desk
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: how can a small office with no IT staff start to look after its computers?
@GL record = to write something down and keep it    ·    honest = telling the truth and doing fair work    ·    reputation = what people say about your work
@BX In a small town, a company called Sadek Trading had eleven computers and nobody to look after them. When a machine stopped, the staff called a friend, and often nobody could say what had been done to it. Then the owner asked one worker, Sami, to spend two days a week on the computers. Sami did one simple thing first: he wrote down every call in a notebook — the date, the machine, the problem, and the work he did. After three months, the notebook showed him that the same two machines caused half the calls. He repaired them properly, and the calls went down. The staff started to trust him, because he always told them honestly how long a job would take. Today the company has a real help desk with three people, and it still keeps a record of every call.

@PART Part 9  ·  Local Case Study — Syria in Focus: The First Account List
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class decide
@IT Before you read: why is it a problem when several people in an office use the same computer account?
@BX Lina is a young officer in a company in Homs with twenty-two staff. When she started, there were four accounts for twenty-two people. Everybody knew all four passwords, and the passwords were written on a card under the keyboard. Nobody was doing anything wrong: the office had grown slowly, and sharing had always been the quick way to work. But when a file was deleted one Thursday, nobody could say who had deleted it, and the argument lasted a week. Lina asked her manager for two quiet afternoons. She wrote a list of every person and what each one needed to open, and then she made one account for each name. It took longer than she expected, because two people had left the company a year before and still had working accounts. Now every file shows who opened it, and the card under the keyboard is gone.
