@PART Part 2  ·  Grammar — Present Perfect vs Past Simple
@TRK [OP] Operator track  ·  pre-class input
@EX Part 2: Notice
@IMG fig-09-2.png
@CAP Figure 9.2  ·  Finished time, and time that is still open.
@IN Read these lines. Which have a finished time? Which are about now, or about experience?
@BX Past simple (finished time): I replaced the disk yesterday. She diagnosed the fault last Monday. We rebuilt that machine in March.  ·  Present perfect (no finished time; still true now): The same problem has come back twice. I have already checked the backup. Have you ever rebuilt a server?
@EX Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAM 1700,2450,2200,2676
 | Form | Use | Example
Past simple | verb + -ed / irregular | a finished time (yesterday, last week) | I replaced the disk yesterday.
Present perfect | have / has + past participle | no finished time; experience; still true | The problem has come back twice.
@END
@BR Remember: Past simple with a finished time.  Present perfect with ever, never, already, yet, and with things that are still true now.
@WO ✗ I have replaced it yesterday. → ✓ I replaced it yesterday.   ✗ Have you diagnose it? → ✓ Have you diagnosed it?

@EX Part 5: Diagnosis, Recovery and Repeat Problems
@IT Before you read: is finding the cause harder than repairing it?
@GL cause = the reason a problem happened    ·    rebuild = to set a machine up again from the beginning, clean    ·    evidence = what the log and the records actually show
@BX Finding a cause is like solving a puzzle, and the hardest part is not the repair. The repair is simple once you know what is wrong; the knowing takes the time. A careful officer starts with the symptoms — what the user saw, and exactly when — then reads the log, which remembers what everybody else has forgotten. Then she tests one thing at a time. Changing three things at once and finding that the problem has gone teaches nothing, because next month it will come back and nobody will know which of the three mattered. When the cause is found, there is a second decision: repair or rebuild. A machine that has had five different problems in a year is usually telling you something, and a clean rebuild often costs less than the next three repairs. Finally, every repeat problem must be taken seriously, and taken differently. If the same fault returns after a repair, the first repair almost certainly fixed a symptom and left the cause alone. The right answer then is not another new part. It is to go back to the beginning and ask what the evidence actually showed, including the possibility that the first diagnosis was wrong.

@EX Part 5: Keeping Old Machines Working in Syria
@IT Before you read: why is the skill to repair worth more in Syria than the money to replace?
@GL skilled = very good at a job learned over years    ·    spare part = a piece kept ready to replace a broken one
@BX In Syria, new machines are expensive and hard to import, so most offices are working with equipment that would have been replaced years ago somewhere else. This has produced a generation of technicians who are extremely good at something their colleagues abroad rarely learn: making an old machine work for another year. They repair power supplies that in other countries are simply thrown away. They keep a drawer of parts taken from machines that have finally died, carefully labelled, because the shop may not have that part for six months. They know which disk models fail, because they have seen forty of them. Lina was taught by a man in Aleppo who had kept the same four machines running for eleven years, and she says the most valuable thing he gave her was not a technique at all. It was the habit of asking why a thing had failed, every single time, even when the repair was obvious — because a part that fails twice in one machine is not a bad part, it is a message about something else.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — The Office That Recovered in a Day
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: is it always better to replace a system than to recover it?
@GL recover = to bring the work and the files back after a failure    ·    drill = a practice run of something you hope never to need    ·    offsite = kept in another building
@BX Two companies in the same city lost their main server in the same month, to the same kind of failure. The first had a backup and had never tested it. When the day came, the copy was of the wrong folder, and ten months of work was gone; the company took five months to rebuild what it could, and never fully recovered its records. The second company had the same equipment and the same budget, and one habit the first did not: twice a year, on an ordinary working morning, it pretended the server had died and recovered it onto a spare machine while everybody watched. The first drill took eleven hours and found four things that did not work. The second took five hours. By the fourth drill it took under three. When the real failure came, on a Tuesday, the office was working again by that afternoon. The manager said the drills had never once felt like a good use of a morning, and that he would not trade them for anything.

@PART Part 9  ·  Local Case Study — Syria in Focus: Rebuild or Replace?
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class decide
@IT Before you read: what can an office do when a machine fails and no replacement can be imported?
@BX An old server at a printing works in Homs failed mid-season, and its owner, Abu Firas, could not buy another. The model had not been sold in Syria for six years, and a new one would have come through Beirut at a price he could not meet. Lina looked at it for a day. The machine was sound; the power supply had burned out and taken a disk with it.
