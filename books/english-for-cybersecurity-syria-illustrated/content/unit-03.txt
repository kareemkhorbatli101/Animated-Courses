@EX Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAM 1700,2450,2200,2676
 | Form | Use | Example
Imperative | base verb (no subject) | an order, a rule, an instruction | Lock your screen.
Negative imperative | Don’t / Never + verb | a rule about what not to do | Never share your password.
@END
@BR Remember: Negative imperative = Don’t / Never + verb.  Use sequencers to show the order: First, Then, Next, Finally.
@WO ✗ You lock your screen. (as a rule) → ✓ Lock your screen.   ✗ Not share your password. → ✓ Don’t share your password.

@EX Part 5: A Family Business in Syria
@IT Before you read: how can a small office make its work safer without spending money?
@GL rule board = a board on the wall where the rules are written    ·    habit = something you do the same way every time
@BX In a small street in Damascus, the Khalil family has run a trading office for thirty years. For most of that time there were four machines and one password that everybody knew. Then the eldest son, Samir, came back from two years at a bank in Beirut, where he had learned how a careful office works. He did not change everything at once. In the first month he gave every person their own account and put the rules on a board by the door: five lines, in Arabic and English. In the second month he taught everybody the screen lock, and he locked his own screen in front of them every time he stood up, until it became a habit. In the third month he started the backup. None of it cost money. Samir says the hardest part was not the machines; it was asking his own uncle to stop using his son’s account.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — The Office with No Shared Passwords
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: how can an office stop a problem before it starts?
@GL incident = something that goes wrong and must be recorded    ·    rule = an instruction that everybody follows    ·    audit = a careful check of how things are really done
@BX A large company once had too many small incidents. A file was deleted and nobody knew who had deleted it. A payment was changed and three people had used the same account that morning. The new manager did not buy anything. She did two things. First, she put the rules on the wall in every room, in five short lines that a tired person could read. Second, she walked through one department every morning before the staff arrived and wrote down what she saw: an open screen, a password on a card, a drawer left unlocked. She did not name anybody; she wrote numbers, and she put the numbers on the same wall each week. In the first month there were nineteen open screens. By the sixth month there were none. She says good security is not a machine you buy; it is a habit you build, and the only way to build it is to count it.

@PART Part 9  ·  Local Case Study — Syria in Focus: Raising a Small Office to Bank Standard
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class decide
@IT Before you read: what does a small office need before a bank will work with it?
@BX Maher once visited a small company in Damascus that wanted to do work for a bank. The bank had sent a list of what it required, and the owner thought he needed to buy expensive equipment. Maher read the list and told him that most of it cost nothing. The bank wanted three things first: one account for each person, with no sharing; a written rule that staff follow, kept where anybody can see it; and a record of who may open which files, reviewed twice a year. The owner did all three in six weeks, with no new machines. The bank then asked for two more things that did cost money: a second backup kept in another building, and a proper lock on the server room door. After six months the bank signed the contract. The owner told Maher that the part he had expected to be hardest — the equipment — had been the easy part.
