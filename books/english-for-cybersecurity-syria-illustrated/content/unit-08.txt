@PART Part 2  ·  Grammar — Must / Have To / Mustn’t
@TRK [OP] Operator track  ·  pre-class input
@EX Part 2: Notice
@IMG fig-08-2.png
@CAP Figure 8.2  ·  Necessary, forbidden, and not necessary.
@IN Read these lines. Which say ‘it is necessary’? Which say ‘it is forbidden’? Which say ‘it is not necessary’?
@BX Must / have to (necessary): Every machine must pass the handover check. We have to write down every exception.  ·  Mustn’t (forbidden): You mustn’t give a machine back with the antivirus off. You mustn’t share an account, not even with your manager.  ·  Don’t have to (not necessary): If the scan ran this morning, you don’t have to run it again.
@EX Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAM 1700,2450,2200,2676
 | Form | Use | Example
must / have to | must + verb | it is necessary | Every machine must pass the check.
mustn’t / don’t have to | mustn’t = forbidden | don’t have to = not necessary | You mustn’t hide a problem.
@END
@BR Remember: must and have to are similar.  But mustn’t (forbidden) and don’t have to (not necessary) are completely different.
@WO ✗ mustn’t = don’t have to → ✓ You mustn’t hide a problem (forbidden).  You don’t have to run it twice (not necessary).

@EX Part 5: Why Standards Matter
@IT Before you read: what does ‘quality’ mean in IT work, where nobody can see the result?
@GL standard = an agreed correct level that everybody works to    ·    policy = the written rules of a company    ·    disclosure = telling somebody about a problem they would not otherwise see
@BX Quality in IT work is a promise about something the user cannot see. A user can tell whether a machine starts, but not whether its antivirus is running, whether its updates are three days or three years old, or whether the backup that was set up last year is still working. Everything a user believes about those things, they believe because somebody told them. That is why a written standard matters more here than in work you can look at. Every machine must pass the same checks before it goes back, and the officer must sign the sheet by name, so that in a year anybody can see who said it was true. Nobody may tick a line they have not checked, because a sheet with one invented line is worse than no sheet at all: it tells everybody afterwards that the sheets mean nothing. A department must also say what it has not done. If the backup could not be tested because the disk had not arrived, that goes on the sheet in writing, and the user is told. This is harder than it sounds. It is always easier to tick the line, and nobody would ever know. That is exactly why the rule has to be absolute: not "tick only what you checked, unless you are busy", but "tick only what you checked".

@EX Part 5: An Honest Guarantee in Syria
@IT Before you read: why does a written guarantee from a small company matter so much in Syria?
@GL guarantee = a promise to put work right, free, if it fails    ·    word of mouth = people telling each other about good work
@BX In Syria, a written guarantee from a small company is worth more than it looks, because so few are given and even fewer are honoured. At Cham Systems the rule is one line long: if the same fault comes back within thirty days, the work is put right free, with no argument and no discussion about whose fault it was. Last year a repair failed on the twelfth day. The disk Lina had fitted was faulty from the factory — nothing anybody could have seen — and the customer had lost two days of work. He expected an argument, because he had had one everywhere else. Maher did not argue. He replaced the disk the same afternoon, restored the two days from the backup, charged nothing, and wrote a short note explaining what had failed and why. The whole thing cost Cham Systems a disk and half a day. What it bought cannot be bought any other way: that customer has told the story to every business owner he knows, and he tells it better than any advertisement could.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — The Honest Disclosure
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: what should a company do when it finds a fault in work it has already delivered?
@GL fault = something wrong that needs fixing    ·    disclose = to tell people about a problem they could not see    ·    reputation = what people say about your work
@BX A software company once found a serious fault in a program that four hundred of its customers were already using. The fault was in how the program stored passwords, and it had been there for two years. Nobody outside the company knew, and almost certainly nobody would ever have known. The company had two choices. It could fix the fault quietly in the next ordinary update and say nothing, which is what its lawyers advised. Or it could tell all four hundred customers exactly what had been wrong, for how long, and what each of them should do. The directors argued for a week. In the end they wrote to every customer, published the whole story, and gave each one a tool to check their own records. Eleven customers left. The newspapers were unkind for about four days. But the company is still trading twenty years later, and the thing its customers say about it, over and over, is that it told them the time it did not have to.

@PART Part 9  ·  Local Case Study — Syria in Focus: The Guarantee That Kept Its Promise
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class decide
@IT Before you read: why might a user expect an argument when a repair fails?
@BX A customer, Mr Khalil, had the disk in his shop computer replaced at Cham Systems, with the usual thirty-day guarantee written on the completion note. Twelve days later the machine stopped in exactly the same way. He came back expecting a difficult conversation, because that is what had happened at the last two places he had used: first they tell you it is a different fault, then they tell you it is something you did. Lina opened the machine while he waited. The new disk had failed — a factory fault, nothing anybody could have predicted, and nothing Mr Khalil had done. She replaced it that afternoon from the store, restored his two lost days from the backup, and charged him nothing. Maher wrote four lines on the note explaining what had failed. Mr Khalil has sent six people to Cham Systems since, and every one of them has mentioned that story when they arrived.
@IN Answer the questions. (0. is done.)
@IT 0. What was replaced on Mr Khalil’s machine? — The disk.
@IT 1. What guarantee did Mr Khalil have?
@IT 2. What happened after twelve days?
@IT 3. What did Maher write on the note?
