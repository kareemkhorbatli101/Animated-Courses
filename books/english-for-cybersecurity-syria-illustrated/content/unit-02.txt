@EX Part 2: Grammar Focus Box
@GRAM 1700,2450,2200,2676
 | Form | Use | Example
There is / There isn’t | + singular or uncountable | one thing, or a quantity | There is a server in the room.
There are / There aren’t | + plural | more than one thing | There are two printers here.
@END
@BR Remember: some → + sentences.  any → questions and – sentences.  much → uncountable.  many → countable.
@WO ✗ There are much data. → ✓ There is much data (data is uncountable here).   ✗ There is three disks. → ✓ There are three disks.

@IMG fig-02-4.png
@CAP Figure 2.4  ·  What the store holds this month.
@IT Before you read: which part of the system do you think is the most important to protect?
@GL level = how much of something there is    ·    top up = to add more of something    ·    spare = an extra one, kept ready
@BX A system has many parts, and it needs several things to work well. There are the machines, and there is the data that lives on them. The disk keeps the files when the power is off, and the memory helps the machine work while it is on. There is a network that joins everything together, and there are licences that give the office the right to use its programs. The most important part, though, is the one you cannot see on a shelf: the backup. A machine can be replaced in a week, but ten years of records cannot. So a careful officer checks the levels every month — how much space there is on the disk, how many spare machines there are in the store, and how many licences are still good. If there isn’t much space left, the backup will stop, and nobody will notice until the day they need it.

@EX Part 5: Machines and Licences in Syria
@IT Before you read: are new machines and licences always easy to buy where you work?
@GL import = to bring from another country    ·    genuine licence = a licence bought from the company that made the program
@BX In Syria, machines and licences are not always easy to buy. New computers are expensive and hard to import, so many offices work with machines that are eight or ten years old. A genuine licence often cannot be paid for from Damascus at all, so an office buys it through a reseller in another country, and waits. Because of this, there are offices with twenty machines and only four licences, and the staff share what there is. A careful manager writes down how many licences there are and when each one ends, so that the office is never surprised. It costs nothing to keep that list, and it saves an argument later.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — The Office That Never Lost a Record
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: what is the problem if an office keeps its only copy of the records in one place?
@GL stock = the things a business keeps ready to use    ·    order = to ask a supplier to send more    ·    restore = to bring the files back from a backup
@BX A medium-sized company kept all of its records on one server in the basement. There was a backup, but nobody had ever opened it. One winter a pipe broke, and the water reached the machines. The company lost the server in one night. When the manager opened the backup, he found that it had stopped working eleven months before: the disk had been full, and the message had gone to an address nobody read. The company rebuilt what it could from paper and from the staff’s own copies, and it took five months. After that, the manager made two rules. First, there must be two backups, and one of them must be in another building. Second, somebody must restore one file from the backup every month and sign a sheet to say it worked. Ten years later the company has never lost a record again, and the sheet is still signed every month.

@PART Part 9  ·  Local Case Study — Syria in Focus: Genuine Licence or Cheaper Copy?
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class decide
@IT Before you read: when an office needs a program, what two choices does it often have?
@BX A trading company in Aleppo needed an accounts program for six machines. Rana, who looked after the computers, asked for prices. A genuine licence cost a great deal and had to be bought through a reseller in Beirut, with a wait of three weeks. A copy from the market cost almost nothing and could be installed the same afternoon. The owner wanted the cheap copy. Rana explained what he was really buying: the cheap copy came with no updates, so any fault in it would stay for ever; it could not be registered with the Ministry if the company was ever asked; and the shop that sold it would not answer the telephone in six months. In the end they bought two genuine licences for the two machines that held the accounts, and used the free reader on the other four. It was not the cheapest answer, and it was not the most expensive one.
