@BX Will (a decision now, a promise): I’ll call you as soon as it is ready. I’ll look at it myself this afternoon.  ·  Going to (a plan already made): We’re going to order the disk this week. @END

@EX Part 5: From Request to Handover
@IT Before you read: why is the help desk the most important desk in an IT department?
@GL approve = to agree to the work before it starts    ·    handover = giving the machine back and showing that it works    ·    window = the hours when a job will be done
@BX The help desk is the first place a user meets, and very often the only one. The bench work may be excellent, but a user judges the department by the desk. The process is simple and it is always the same. A request arrives and is given a slot in the week. Before anything begins, the user is told two things: how long it is going to take, and what it is going to cost. That is the estimate, and it exists so that nobody is ever surprised. Then the user approves it, and only then does the work start. While the machine is away, a loan machine is offered if the department has one. At handover the officer opens the machine in front of the user, shows the broken thing working again, and gives the completion note. If the work is going to be late, the user is told on the day it becomes late, not on the day it was promised. A good help desk depends on clear communication far more than on fast repairs, because a user who knows what is happening will wait patiently, and a user who is told nothing will not wait at all.

@EX Part 5: Late Parts and Power Cuts in Syria
@IT Before you read: what problems can a help desk in Syria have that one in another country does not?
@GL import = to bring from another country    ·    reseller = a company that buys from the maker and sells it on    ·    generator = a machine that makes electricity when the power is off
@BX Running a help desk in Syria is not the same as running one anywhere else. A disk that is in a warehouse in another country is not a disk you have; it is a disk you are hoping for. It may come in ten days, or it may come in two months, and the reseller in Beirut honestly does not know which. A licence often cannot be paid for from Damascus at all. And the power goes off on a schedule that everybody knows and nobody can change, so a job that needs four unbroken hours has to be planned around the generator, not around the working day. None of this is the user’s fault, and none of it is the officer’s fault either. What a user cannot forgive is being told that a machine will be ready on Thursday by somebody who already knew on Monday that the part had not left Beirut. The advisor who says “it may be two weeks, and I will tell you the day I know more” is giving a worse answer and a better service, and the users come back to that advisor.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — The Office You Can See Online
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: why do users call the help desk again and again about the same ticket?
@GL online = using the internet or a network    ·    status = where a piece of work has got to    ·    chase = to call again and again to ask about something
@BX A modern IT department had a problem it did not expect: its own telephone. The engineers were good and the work was quick, but the desk rang all day with the same question — where is my ticket? Each call took four or five minutes, and there were ninety of them a week. So the department built something very simple: one page that any user could open, showing their own tickets and where each one had got to. Logged. Investigating. Waiting for a part. Ready. That was all; no clever design, no messages, four words. The chasing calls fell by three quarters in a month. What surprised the manager was something else. Users stopped complaining about slow work even when the work was no faster. A ticket that said "waiting for a part since Tuesday" was accepted without a word, where the same wait in silence had produced three angry calls. He said afterwards that people do not really mind waiting. They mind not knowing.

It had to go through a reseller in Beirut, who said ten days and meant it, but who had been wrong twice before. At the same time the power schedule changed, and the four hours the team had counted on in the afternoon became two. Rana did not promise a date. She called the company on the Monday and told them exactly what she knew: the licence was ordered, the reseller had said ten days, she did not believe ten days, and she would call every Monday until it came, whether there was news or not. It came on the nineteenth day. She called four times with nothing to report. The company's director told Maher afterwards that those four empty calls were the reason he had renewed the contract — not the licence, which every company could eventually have supplied.
