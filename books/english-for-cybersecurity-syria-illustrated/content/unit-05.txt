@EX Part 5: From Report to Closure
@IT Before you read: why does a problem not go straight to the person who can repair it?
@GL ticket = the record that follows a problem through every step    ·    urgent = must be done before other things    ·    closure = the end of a ticket, after the work is checked
@BX When a problem arrives at an IT department, it does not go straight to the bench. It follows a process, and the process has a reason behind every step. First it is logged and given a number, so that it cannot be forgotten in a corridor conversation. Then it is read and given a priority: a whole office that cannot work is urgent; one slow printer is not. Next it is investigated, which is not the same as repaired. Investigating means finding out what really happened, because a problem that is repaired without being understood usually comes back. Then it is fixed, and the fix is tested by somebody who is willing to say that it failed. Finally the user is asked whether the problem is really gone, and only then is the ticket closed. Every step is written on the ticket as it happens, so that in six months anybody can see what was done and why. A department that works this way is slower on the first day and much faster by the end of the month, because the same problem is not solved three times by three people who never spoke to each other.

@EX Part 5: A Big Job in Syria
@IT Before you read: how can a small department handle a very large piece of work?
@GL branch = an office of the same company in another city    ·    stage = one part of a long job
@BX One week a company asked Cham Systems to move twenty-two accounts and all their files to a new server at its branch in Homs. The work could not be done in one day, and the branch could not stop working while it was done. So the job was divided into stages and written down as a plan before anything was touched. On the first day the accounts were copied, but nothing was switched over; the old server was left exactly as it was. On the second day four people were moved, and they worked all afternoon on the new system while the rest of the office carried on as before. Only when those four said that everything worked was the rest of the office moved, on the third day. The old server was kept, switched off but untouched, for two more weeks. Nothing was lost, and when one folder was found to be missing on the fourth day, it was simply copied again from the machine that was still sitting there.

@PART Part 8  ·  Global Case Study — The Team That Cut Waiting Times
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class discuss
@IT Before you read: why do users sometimes wait a long time for a small problem?
@GL process = a set of steps done in order    ·    queue = the line of work waiting to be done    ·    board = a wall display showing what is where
@BX A help desk in a large hospital had a problem that no amount of hard work could fix: users waited eleven days for small things. The staff were not lazy — they were doing eleven tickets at once and finishing none of them. The new manager changed one rule. Nobody was allowed to start a fourth ticket while three were open. At first this felt wrong, and two officers said so. But because nobody could start new work, the open tickets were actually finished, and the board on the wall showed it: each ticket was moved across four columns — logged, investigated, fixed, closed — and the whole department could see where the work was stuck. The stuck column was always the same one: tickets were being fixed but never tested, so they came back. Within two months the average wait fell from eleven days to two. The manager said afterwards that a good process is not about working faster; it is about finishing what you start before you start something else.

@PART Part 9  ·  Local Case Study — Syria in Focus: The Three-Day Job at the Branch
@TRK [PR] Practitioner track  ·  pre-read / in-class decide
@IT Before you read: how can a small department do a very large job without stopping the office?
@BX A ministry office in Damascus needed all of its machines moved to a new system, and it could not close for even one day. Nothing was lost, and the office never stopped.
