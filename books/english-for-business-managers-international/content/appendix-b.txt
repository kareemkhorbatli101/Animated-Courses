@ANSHEAD Exercise 31: Part 5: Discussing Business Management and Its Work
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
A business manager is a trained professional who sets objectives, arranges the people and money to reach them, keeps the work moving, and reports the result. A supervisor runs one part of the work and gets it done. Unlike the supervisor, who is judged on this week's output, the manager is judged on the year's objectives. The key difference is that the manager decides what will be done, while the supervisor gets it done.
_100
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
There are four functions of management. Planning decides in advance what the business will do, for whom, and at what cost. Organizing arranges the people, money and equipment so the plan can be carried out, and decides who may approve what. Leading explains a decision, settles a disagreement, and keeps a team working when the plan meets reality. Controlling compares what happened with what was planned and finds the cause of any difference. Reviewing looks back at a whole period rather than one job.
_100
@QHEAD Question 3 - Sample Answer:

If a manager improved the figures before the owners read them, the owners would invest in something that is not working. Reporting badly also removes the only chance to correct a problem while it is small. In Vietnam, where margins are thin, that early warning is the most valuable thing a manager produces.

@H2 Unit 3: Company Law, Governance and Compliance
@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: What Is Company Law?
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Company law is the body of rules a company must satisfy before it may trade and while it continues to trade. It covers registration, ownership, the powers of directors, accounts, employment and consumer protection. These rules are made by legislation and applied by the commercial registry, the tax authority and the courts. Examples include registering before trading, holding an annual general meeting, and filing accounts. Their goal is to protect everyone who deals with a company. Company law exists because failures in the past left creditors, staff and customers with no remedy.

@QHEAD Sample Answer:

Company law matters because thousands of people deal with companies they cannot inspect. Without it, many people would lose what they are owed.

@H2 Unit 8: Performance Measurement and Reporting
@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: Performance and Reporting Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Measurement and reporting serve several crucial purposes. First, accountability—the owners want to know what their money produced. Second, learning—managers need to know which decisions worked and which quietly failed. Third, correction—a problem found in a monthly report costs far less than one found at the year end. Fourth, evidence—figures persuade owners that training or a new machine was worth its cost. Fifth, responsibility—a manager who signs a report should know what the business actually did. Without reporting, a company repeats the same mistake for twenty years without ever learning that it is a mistake. Sixth, persuasion—a figure from a completed month convinces a sceptical owner far more effectively than any argument about principle. Seventh, fairness—reporting is how a company discovers that one department was never measured at all, which nobody notices from an office. The habit matters more than the method: a company that reports to itself improves, and one that does not simply becomes more confident about decisions that were never tested.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 3: Warm-Up: Types of Evidence Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Figures and written explanation serve different but equally important purposes. Figures show what happened across the whole month—what was sold, what was spent, what is owed and what is held in stock. The explanation shows why it happened: a customer delayed payment, a machine stopped for a week, an input doubled in price between order and delivery. @ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: What is Business Advocacy Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Business advocacy is working to change rules, systems and priorities so companies can trade, invest and employ people. Where a manager solves one delay, advocacy changes the procedure that governs thousands. An owner who argues one shipment through customs saves one delivery; an owner who gets the clearance rule changed saves a whole year's exports. None of these can be fixed by managing better. They can only be fixed by persuading the people who write them. Advocacy is slower than trading and far less certain, but its reach is much wider and its effects last long after a single shipment clears.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 3: Warm-Up: Systems Thinking Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS

A good charter also records how new members join once the association is running, and how it will be wound up when its purpose is achieved.

Strong campaign messages include: short memorable arguments that stay with an audience; one workshop's account showing what the problem means in a single week; evidence an owner can check without specialist help; a named decision-maker so the request has somewhere to go; and a specific action members can take. They avoid technical language, avoid blaming officials for conditions they did not create, and give a number alongside the account so the scale is clear. They repeat the same two or three arguments rather than offering a dozen, because a message only lands once it has been heard several times. And they always name a specific action a reader can take that day, because attention without an action produces nothing.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 38: Part 7: Kenyan Values - Consultation
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
A baraza means that leaders must consult widely before making decisions, valuing the knowledge of many people. Businesses affected by a rule must have a voice in making it. Associations practicing it make decisions collectively through discussion honouring different perspectives. Owners leading reform embody it—people directly affected deciding what changes matter most. Consultative advocacy rejects top-down approaches where a chamber decides what a sector should ask for. Instead members identify problems, shape requests, and guide strategy. Partners in associations practice it by creating spaces for real consultation rather than reporting. A baraza acknowledges that diverse perspectives improve decisions. Opposition deserves hearing and understanding. Officials and other parties should be consulted with genuine attention to their concerns and constraints. Consultative advocacy is slower than top-down decision-making but creates deeper ownership. People who helped decide a change work harder to carry it out. Decisions made through this process hold more legitimacy than imposed ones. Associations committed to it build reform that lasts because it is rooted in authentic member participation.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 39: Part 7: Kenyan Values - Justice
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Haki-centred advocacy goes beyond solving individual delays to addressing the systems that leave sectors unable to trade. It asks not just "How many shipments can we clear?" but "Why are so many shipments held? What systems produce this? How can we change them?" Haki-guided associations recognize that a failing business climate is not inevitable but results from systems—fees that reward delay, procedures written for a different economy, no administrative capacity, and small firms with no standing to object. Advocacy targets these systems. Pursuing haki means managers align with the smallest workshops and the employees, not only with those who hold licences. Advocacy following this principle gives voice to the unheard, supports the concerns of small firms against pressure from larger ones, and champions the interests of employees who cannot negotiate alone. It means associations accept some risk for the sake of fairness rather than seeking only comfortable agreement. Pursuing haki may create conflict—refusing a profitable arrangement means challenging those who gain from it. But without that willingness, nothing changes. Associations committed to haki view advocacy not as an optional extra but as a professional obligation. They understand that serving a sector honestly requires working for fairer rules rather than accepting unfair ones.
