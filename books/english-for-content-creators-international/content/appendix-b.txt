@ANSHEAD Exercise 31: Part 5: Discussing Content Creation and Its Work
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
A content manager is a trained professional who sets objectives, arranges the staff, space and equipment to reach them, keeps the work moving, and reports the result. An editor does one part of the work and finishes it. Unlike the editor, who is judged on today's cut, the manager is judged on the year's objectives. The key difference is that the manager decides what will be made, while the editor gets it made.
_100
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
There are five functions of content creation. Planning decides in advance what the channel will publish, for whom, and at what cost. Organising arranges the staff, space and equipment so the plan can be carried out, and decides who may approve what. Communicating circulates a decision, answers an enquiry, and keeps everyone informed when the plan meets reality. Controlling compares what was done with what was planned and finds the cause of any difference. Reviewing looks back at a quarter rather than one piece.
_100
@QHEAD Question 3 - Sample Answer:

Content staff must report a missed date honestly because the directors cannot see every edit. If an editor improved the publishing figures before the directors read them, the directors would plan around work that is not finished. Reporting badly also removes the only chance to correct a problem while the date can still be met. In Vietnam, where equipment is scarce, that early warning is the most valuable thing a studio produces.

@H2 Unit 3: Rights, Licensing and Compliance
@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: What Is Rights Compliance?
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Rights regulation is the body of rules an organisation must satisfy before it may publish work and while it continues to trade. It covers registration, licences, retention, access, release forms and consumer protection. These rules are made by legislation and applied by the Federal Telecommunications Institute, the Film workers' union and the courts. Examples include registering before trading, obtaining written releases, and keeping the masters. Their goal is to protect everyone who deals with the organisation. Rights rules exist because failures in the past left contributors, audiences and authorities with no remedy.

@QHEAD Sample Answer:

Rights rules show that a society takes responsibility for honest dealing. Without them, many people would lose what they are owed.

@H2 Unit 8: Performance Measurement and Reporting
@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: Performance and Reporting Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Measurement and reporting serve several crucial purposes. First, accountability—the management want to know what their content budget produced. Second, learning—managers need to know which pieces worked and which quietly failed. Third, correction—a problem found in a monthly report costs far less than one found at the year end. Fourth, evidence—figures persuade owners that training or new equipment was worth its cost. Fifth, responsibility—a manager who signs a report should know what the studio actually delivered. Without reporting, a studio repeats the same mistake for twenty years without ever learning that it is a mistake. Sixth, persuasion—a figure from a completed month convinces a sceptical director far more effectively than any argument about principle. Seventh, fairness—reporting is how a firm discovers that one channel was never measured at all, which nobody notices from a head office. The habit matters more than the method: a studio that reports to itself improves, and one that does not simply becomes more confident about decisions that were never tested.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 3: Warm-Up: Types of Evidence Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Figures and written explanation serve different but equally important purposes. Figures show what happened across the whole month—what was published, what was spent, what is disputed and what is held in store. Strong charter includes: an inspiring shared aim about a sector that can employ people properly; a clear statement of the association's purpose and its limits; who may join and on what terms; how decisions are taken and what counts as consensus; what each member contributes; how disagreements are settled; how the association speaks to the ministry and who may speak for it; and how a member may leave without damaging the others. A charter that omits the limits is the one that breaks first, because members discover too late that they are committed to positions they never agreed to. A good charter also records how new members join once the association is running, and how it will be wound up when its purpose is achieved.

Strong campaign messages include: short memorable arguments that stay with an audience; one producer's account showing what the problem means in a single week; evidence an owner can check without specialist help; a named decision-maker so the request has somewhere to go; and a specific action members can take. They avoid technical language, avoid blaming officials for conditions they did not create, and give a number alongside the account so the scale is clear. They repeat the same two or three arguments rather than offering a dozen, because a message only lands once it has been heard several times. And they always name a specific action a reader can take that day, because attention without an action produces nothing.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 38: Part 7: Kenyan Values - Consultation
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
A baraza means that leaders must consult widely before making decisions, valuing the knowledge of many people. Studios affected by a rule must have a voice in making it. Associations practicing it make decisions collectively through discussion honouring different perspectives. Small producers leading reform embody it—people directly affected deciding what changes matter most. Consultative advocacy rejects top-down approaches where a chamber decides what a sector should ask for. Instead members identify problems, shape requests, and guide strategy. Partners in associations practice it by creating spaces for real consultation rather than reporting. A baraza acknowledges that diverse perspectives improve decisions. Opposition deserves hearing and understanding. Officials and other parties should be consulted with genuine attention to their concerns and constraints. Consultative advocacy is slower than top-down decision-making but creates deeper ownership. People who helped decide a change work harder to carry it out. Decisions made through this process hold more legitimacy than imposed ones. Associations committed to it build reform that lasts because it is rooted in authentic member participation.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 39: Part 7: Kenyan Values - Justice
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Haki-centred advocacy goes beyond issuing individual permits to addressing the systems that leave sectors unable to publish work. It asks not just "How many files can we license?" but "Why are so many files held? What systems produce this? How can we change them?" Haki-guided associations recognize that a failing production climate is not inevitable but results from systems—fees that reward delay, procedures written for a different era, no administrative capacity, and small producers with no standing to object. Advocacy targets these systems. Pursuing haki means managers align with the smallest producers and the staff, not only with those who hold licences. Advocacy following this principle gives voice to the unheard, supports the concerns of small studios against pressure from larger ones, and champions the interests of staff who cannot negotiate alone. It means associations accept some risk for the sake of fairness rather than seeking only comfortable agreement. Pursuing haki may create conflict—refusing a profitable arrangement means challenging those who gain from it. But without that willingness, nothing changes. Associations committed to haki view advocacy not as an optional extra but as a professional obligation. They understand that serving a sector honestly requires working for fairer rules rather than accepting unfair ones.
