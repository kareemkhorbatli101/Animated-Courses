@ANSHEAD Exercise 31: Part 5: Discussing Logistics Management and Its Work
@QHEAD Question 1 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
A logistics manager is a trained professional who sets delivery objectives, arranges the carriage and space to reach them, keeps the goods moving, and reports arrivals. A driver carries one load and gets it there. Unlike the driver, who is judged on today's journey, the manager is judged on the season's service. The key difference is that the manager decides how goods will travel, while the driver moves them.
_100
@QHEAD Question 2 - Sample Answer:
@ANS
There are five functions of logistics. Planning decides in advance how goods will travel, by which route, and at what cost. Storing arranges the space, equipment and labelling so the plan can be carried out, and decides who may release a pallet. Moving books the carrier, loads the vehicle, and keeps the consignment travelling when a road closes. Controlling compares what arrived with what was promised and finds the cause of any difference. Reviewing looks back at a season rather than one load.

In Vietnam, where fuel is scarce, that early warning is the most valuable thing a manager produces.

@H2 Unit 3: Customs, Documentation and Compliance
@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: What Is Customs Compliance?
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Customs regulation is the body of rules a consignment must satisfy before it may cross a border and while it is in transit. It covers declaration, classification, valuation, licensing, marking and consumer protection. These rules are made by legislation and applied by the customs authority, the standards body and the courts. Examples include declaring before release, holding a valid import licence, and keeping the records. Their goal is to protect everyone who trades across a border. Customs rules exist because failures in the past left buyers, revenue and consumers with no remedy.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 4: Warm-Up: Discussion - Why Customs Rules Matter
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Customs rules matter because thousands of people rely on goods they cannot inspect before they arrive. They protect revenue by requiring a true declaration, protect safety by limiting what may enter, and protect buyers by setting what must be marked. In Mexico and other large trading economies, a clean declaration is often the only protection a shipper has before paying for carriage. Customs rules show that a society takes responsibility for honest trade. Without them, many people would lose what they paid for.

@ANSHEAD Exercise 18: Part 2: Grammar Summary - Compliance Communication
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Logistics staff should use clear, factual language to describe requirements. They must present accurate information about what the rules demand and what the documents show. Using passive voice makes reports more formal and professional. They should employ conditional statements like "If the declaration is late, a penalty will follow." Modal verbs help convey obligation: "We must file the declaration" or "The records should be kept for the period required." Reported speech allows them to pass on the inspector's position: "The inspector said the carton count had not been witnessed." Effective communication combines regulatory precision with plain explanation to persuade drivers while staying accurate.

@H2 Unit 8: Performance Measurement and Reporting
@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: Performance and Reporting Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Measurement and reporting serve several crucial purposes. First, accountability—the customers want to know what their freight money produced. Second, learning—managers need to know which routes worked and which quietly failed. Third, correction—a problem found in a monthly report costs far less than one found at the season end. Fourth, evidence—figures persuade owners that training or a new vehicle was worth its cost. Fifth, responsibility—a manager who signs a report should know what the operation actually delivered. The habit matters more than the method: an operation that reports to itself improves, and one that does not simply becomes more confident about decisions that were never tested.

@ANS

@ANSHEAD Exercise 1: Warm-Up: What is Logistics Advocacy Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Logistics advocacy is working to change rules, systems and priorities so goods can move and people can be employed. @ANSHEAD Exercise 3: Warm-Up: Systems Thinking Discussion
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS

Effective mobilization therefore says plainly what is uncertain, reports setbacks as openly as gains, and keeps asking for actions that are small enough that nobody has to risk more than they chose to.

A baraza means that leaders must consult widely before making decisions, valuing the knowledge of many people. Carriers affected by a rule must have a voice in making it. @ANSHEAD Exercise 39: Part 7: Kenyan Values - Justice
@QHEAD Sample Answer:
@ANS
Haki-centred advocacy goes beyond clearing individual containers to addressing the systems that leave sectors unable to move goods. It asks not just "How many containers can we clear?" but "Why are so many containers held? What systems produce this? How can we change them?" Haki-guided associations recognize that a failing transport climate is not inevitable but results from systems—fees that reward delay, procedures written for a different fleet, no administrative capacity, and small hauliers with no standing to object. Advocacy targets these systems. Pursuing haki means managers align with the smallest hauliers and the drivers, not only with those who hold permits. Advocacy following this principle gives voice to the unheard, supports the concerns of small carriers against pressure from larger ones, and champions the interests of drivers who cannot negotiate alone. It means associations accept some risk for the sake of fairness rather than seeking only comfortable agreement. Pursuing haki may create conflict—refusing a profitable arrangement means challenging those who gain from it. But without that willingness, nothing changes. Associations committed to haki view advocacy not as an optional extra but as a professional obligation. They understand that serving a sector honestly requires working for fairer rules rather than accepting unfair ones.
