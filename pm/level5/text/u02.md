A paper from 1936 about building airplanes observed that each time the number of units built
doubles, the labor per unit falls by a roughly constant percentage. It is one of the oldest
quantitative findings in industrial cost, it is three-quarters of a century older than our
estimating software, and it was not in our bid.

> **A:** We lost the kiosk tender by nearly half. Explain how that is possible.
> **B:** **We priced forty-six identical units at the rate of the first one.** **One hundred and
> eighty hours each, times forty-six: eight thousand two hundred and eighty hours.**
> **A:** They are identical units. What else would you price them at?
> **B:** **At a falling rate.** **The first unit takes one hundred and eighty hours. The forty-sixth
> takes about seventy-three.**
> **A:** On what basis?
> **B:** **On a 1936 paper about building airplanes.** The observation is simple: **each time
> cumulative output doubles, the labor per unit falls by a roughly constant percentage.** **At
> eighty-five per cent, every doubling takes fifteen per cent off.**
> **A:** So what should the bid have been?
> **B:** **About four thousand four hundred hours**, as a cumulative total. **A cumulative average
> of about ninety-six hours per unit, not one hundred and eighty.**
> **A:** And the competitor knew this.
> **B:** **The competitor applied a curve and we applied a multiplication.** **Our labor was almost
> twice theirs on exactly the same scope.**
> **A:** Does the curve always work?
> **B:** **No, and this is the half of it nobody quotes.** **It applies to repeated, identical,
> continuous work.** **Material does not follow it at all**: forty-six kiosks need forty-six kiosks'
> worth of steel.
> **A:** And if production stops?
> **B:** **You lose part of the learning.** **Our own job was interrupted for eleven weeks, we
> changed four of the six fitters, and we used about five thousand one hundred and fifty hours
> instead of four thousand four hundred.**
> **A:** So the curve was wrong.
> **B:** **The curve was right about the direction and optimistic about the amount, because we broke
> the continuity it assumes.** **Five thousand one hundred and fifty is still a long way below eight

> ## Lindhurst · cost engineering note 2
> *Cost engineering · forty-six identical kiosks · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The paper.** **T. P. Wright**, *Factors Affecting the Cost of Airplanes*, **Journal of the
> Aeronautical Sciences**, **February 1936**. Wright examined airframe manufacture and reported that
> the **labor required per unit falls by a roughly constant percentage each time cumulative output
> doubles** — in his data, about **twenty per cent**, which is called an **eighty per cent curve**.
>
> **The arithmetic on our own job**, at an **eighty-five per cent** curve, first unit **180 hours**:
>
> | Cumulative units | Hours for that unit | Cumulative average |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1** | **180** | 180 |
> | **2** | **153** | 167 |
> | **4** | **130** | 148 |
> | **8** | **111** | 131 |
> | **16** | **94** | 116 |
> | **32** | **80** | 103 |
> | **46** | **73** | **96** |
>
> **The forty-sixth unit takes about forty per cent of the first one's hours**, and the **cumulative
> average over all forty-six is about 96 hours, not 180.**
>
> **What our bid said.** **180 hours × 46 = 8,280 hours.** One rate, repeated forty-six times. **The
> curve-based figure is about 4,400 hours.** We lost the tender, and the gap was mostly this.
>
> **What actually happened on the job we did win**, a similar scope of thirty units:
>
> | | Hours |
> |---|---|
> | Priced at the first-unit rate | **8,280** |
> | Predicted by an 85 % curve | **4,400** |
> | **Actually used** | **5,150** |
>
> **The 750-hour difference between the curve and the outcome is explained**, and it is the honest
> part of this note. **Production was interrupted for eleven weeks** when the client's access was
> withdrawn. **Four of the six fitters were different afterwards.** The curve assumes **continuity**,
> and we broke it. **About 700 of those 750 hours were re-learning.**
>
> **What the finding does not cover, in its own terms and ours.** It concerns **direct labor on
> repeated, identical units**. **Material does not follow it**: forty-six kiosks require forty-six
> kiosks' worth of steel, cable and valves, and no amount of repetition changes that. The paper is
> about **airframe manufacture in the 1930s**, and the **eighty per cent figure is an empirical
> observation from that work**, not a law — improvement rates differ by industry, by process and by
> how much of the work is manual. It says nothing about **one-off work**, which is most of what this
> company does. And it **assumes continuity of production and of the crew**, which is precisely the
> assumption our own job violated.
>
> **One recommendation.** **Price anything we build more than eight times on a curve, and state the
> rate and the assumed continuity in the bid.** **Eight is where the cumulative average has already
> fallen by more than a quarter.**
>
> *Source: T. P. Wright, Factors Affecting the Cost of Airplanes, Journal of the Aeronautical
> Sciences, February 1936. The paper reports an empirical relationship observed in airframe

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Bids multiply because multiplication is easy to check and a curve is not. A reviewer can verify
forty-six times one hundred and eighty in two seconds; verifying a cumulative average takes a
spreadsheet and a conversation about an assumption, and the person who proposed the curve is the one
who gets asked why the number is lower. So the safest bid in most organizations is the one that is
quietly too expensive, and it loses tenders without anybody being able to say why. The second habit
worth carrying is to treat the curve's conditions as part of the price rather than as a disclaimer.
Continuity is not controlled by the people doing the work: access, sequencing and interruptions
belong to the client, and if you price improvement you have bought from continuity you must say in
writing what happens when continuity goes. The people on the bench always know the curve exists.
They have been saying *the first one was a nightmare* for years, and nobody writes it down.

**Discuss:** Who at your organization would have to defend a number that was lower?

a. Price anything built more than eight times on a stated curve
b. Get the curve rate from your own timesheets, not from a textbook

### 1 · Read a model

**1A  Look at the two build-ups. Answer with a partner.**

%%FIG pe02-f12 | Two labor build-ups, one above the other. The first has one rate repeated and a single total. The second has unit hours at each doubling, a cumulative average, a stated curve rate, and a condition about continuous production.%%

1. Which build-up states a curve rate?
2. Which one gives a cumulative average?
3. Which one names a condition?

**1B  Listen. What does the writer say about the client's own records?** %%AUD TL5-02-14%%

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Labor build-up · forty-six kiosks
> *From O. Bondar · 9 April · for L. Eriksen and the bid file · one page*
>
> **Priced on an 85 per cent curve: the hours per unit fall to 85 per cent of the previous figure for
> every doubling of cumulative output.** **First unit 180 hours. Forty-sixth unit about 73.
> Cumulative average about 96. Labor total about 4,400 hours.**
>
> | Unit | Hours | Cumulative average |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | **180** | 180 |
> | 2 | 153 | 167 |
> | 4 | 130 | 148 |
> | 8 | 111 | 131 |
> | 16 | 94 | 116 |
> | 32 | 80 | 103 |
> | **46** | **73** | **96** |
>
> **Where the rate comes from.** **Not from a textbook: from our own timesheets** on the thirty-unit
> job, where the first unit took 180 hours and the last took under 80. **That is an 85 per cent curve
> measured in our own records**, and I would rather defend our figures than an industry average.
>
> **What this price does not include, and will not follow.** **Material does not learn.** Forty-six
> kiosks require forty-six kiosks' worth of steel, cable and valves, and those lines are flat. **The
> curve applies to direct labor only.**
>
> **The condition, which is part of the price.** **This figure assumes continuous production by a
> stable crew.** On the thirty-unit job **production was interrupted for eleven weeks** and **four of
> six fitters changed**; the outcome was **5,150 hours against a curve prediction of 4,400**, and
> **about 700 of that difference was re-learning.** **I therefore propose a continuity clause: if
> production is interrupted for more than two weeks, the remaining units revert to a rate two
> doublings back.**
>
> **What I will not claim.** The 1936 paper reports **an empirical relationship in airframe
> manufacture**, not a law, and improvement rates vary by process and by how manual the work is. **It
> says nothing about one-off work**, which is most of what we do. **Eight units is the threshold I
> propose for using a curve at all**, because by the eighth the cumulative average has already fallen
> by more than a quarter.
>
> *Source: T. P. Wright, Factors Affecting the Cost of Airplanes, Journal of the Aeronautical
> Sciences, February 1936, reporting an empirical relationship observed in airframe manufacture
> between cumulative output and labor per unit. The improvement percentage is an observation from
> that industry and period rather than a constant; the relationship concerns direct labor on repeated
> identical units and not material; and it presumes continuity of production.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Say where your curve rate came from. A rate from your own timesheets survives a
> commercial review; a rate from a textbook invites a conversation about whether your work is like
> airframes.
