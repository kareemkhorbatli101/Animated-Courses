# Unit 4 · The Rate Does the Arguing
%%SUB A project that loses money and is worth building, and the one borrowed number holding it up%%
%%GOALS
Use appraisal conditionals
Distinguish a financial from an economic result
Find the switching value of an assumption
%%

A guide published in December 2014 sets out how to appraise an investment: once as a business, once
as a benefit to everybody. Our water scheme fails the first test and passes the second, which is
exactly what the grant exists for. The second result rests on a number from a study in another
country, and it would reach zero if that number fell by less than a quarter.

> **A:** You have given me a project with a negative net present value and a recommendation to build
> it.
> **B:** **Two results, and both are correct.** **Financially it loses 6.2 million in present-value
> terms over thirty years. Economically it gains 14.8 million.**
> **A:** How can the same project do both?
> **B:** **Because the two analyses count different things.** The financial one counts **what comes
> into our account**: tariffs, grants, operating costs. **The economic one counts what happens to
> everybody**: the time households spend fetching water, the illness that does not occur, the
> abstraction that stops.
> **A:** And the guide says to do both.
> **B:** **It requires both.** **A project with a negative financial result and a positive economic
> one is the normal case for public infrastructure**, and it is the reason a grant exists at all. **If
> the financial result were positive, somebody would build it without us.**
> **A:** What rates did you use?
> **B:** **Four per cent real for the financial analysis and five per cent for the economic one**,
> which are the benchmarks the guide gives. **The reference period is thirty years, which is its
> indicative figure for water and sanitation.**
> **A:** And if the rates were different?
> **B:** **At a social rate of three per cent the economic gain is larger. At eight it is close to
> zero.** **The guide gives benchmarks and allows a departure with justification, so the rate is
> ours and it should be stated as ours.**
> **A:** Now tell me the part you are uncomfortable with.
> **B:** **One number carries most of the economic case: a willingness to pay for improved water
> quality, taken from a study in another country and adjusted for income.** **Were that figure to
> fall by twenty-three per cent, the economic net present value would reach zero.**
> **A:** Twenty-three per cent.
> **B:** **Twenty-three. That is the switching value, and it is in the first paragraph of my report
> rather than an annex.** **The project may well be worth building. But it is being held up by a
> single transferred number, and whoever signs it should know that.**

> *Appraisal · the Calder valley water scheme · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The guide.** The **Guide to Cost-Benefit Analysis of Investment Projects**, published by the
> **European Commission**, **December 2014**, as the appraisal tool for major projects under its
> cohesion policy for **2014–2020**.
>
> **It requires two appraisals of the same project.**
>
> | | What it counts | Our result |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Financial analysis** | money into and out of the project's own account: tariffs, operating costs, capital | **FNPV −6.2** |
> | **Economic analysis** | effects on everybody, with market prices replaced by **shadow prices** and unpriced effects monetized | **ENPV +14.8** |
>
> **Both are correct and they are not in conflict.** **A negative financial result with a positive
> economic one is the ordinary case for public infrastructure**, and it is the condition under which
> a grant makes sense: **if the financial result were positive, a commercial operator would build it
> unaided.**
>
> **The numbers we chose, and where they came from.**
>
> | | Used | Source |
> |---|---|---|
> | Financial discount rate | **4 % real** | the guide's benchmark |
> | Social discount rate | **5 % real** | the guide's benchmark for cohesion countries |
> | Reference period | **30 years** | the guide's indicative period for water and sanitation |
> | Conversion factors | national | our own statistics office |
>
> **The guide gives these as benchmarks and permits a departure where it is justified**, so they are
> our choices and are stated as such.
>
> **Sensitivity, and the number that matters.** We tested every input one at a time and recorded the
> **switching value** — the change that would take the economic result to zero.
>
> | Input | Switching value |
> |---|---|
> | Capital cost | **+61 %** |
> | Operating cost | **+94 %** |
> | Social discount rate | **7.8 %** |
> | Time savings per household | **−38 %** |
> | **Willingness to pay for improved water quality** | **−23 %** |
>
> > **The willingness-to-pay figure is the weak point and it carries most of the case.** It is
> > **taken from a study carried out in another country**, adjusted for income and household size as
> > the guide's own method allows. **Were it to fall by twenty-three per cent, the economic net
> > present value would be zero.** No survey has been carried out here. **One would cost about
> > 40,000 and take eleven weeks**, which is roughly a quarter of one per cent of the capital cost.
>
> **The counterfactual.** Without the scheme, **the existing abstraction continues and two boreholes
> are expected to fail within eight to twelve years**, at which point tankering begins. **That is
> not a neutral baseline, and most of the economic benefit is the avoidance of it rather than
> anything new.**
>
> **What the guide does not do.** It is the appraisal tool for **one policy framework and one
> programming period**, and it says so on its cover. Its **rates are benchmarks, not requirements**.
> Its **shadow prices and conversion factors depend on national data** which it does not supply. It
> notes that a full analysis may be **disproportionate for small projects**. And it is explicit that
> **cost-benefit analysis is one input to a decision and not the decision**: distributional
> questions, strategic fit and political choices sit **outside the monetized result**, and no net
> present value settles them.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

An appraisal has two audiences and only one of them reads the annexes. The summary is read by people
deciding, the annexes by people checking, and almost every weak assumption in the world lives in an
annex — not because anybody hid it, but because a sensitivity table is long and a recommendation is
short. The result is predictable: the decision is taken on a single figure, and the figure's weakest
input is discovered later by somebody with a grievance. There is a second pattern worth naming. The
cost of removing an uncertainty is usually tiny compared with the decision it affects — a survey at a
quarter of one per cent of capital cost — and it is refused anyway, because it costs weeks rather
than money and the deadline is real while the uncertainty is abstract. Two habits follow. Put the
switching value in the summary, where it will be read by the person signing. And when a check is
refused for timetable reasons, ask for the refusal in writing: it converts a deadline into a
decision, which is what it actually is.

**Discuss:** What check at your organization has been refused for timetable reasons?
