# Unit 9 · Should, Not Shall
%%SUB Six clauses nobody can breach, four of them carrying marks, and a requirement only two firms could meet%%
%%GOALS
Tell a requirement from a recommendation
Write a requirement with a threshold and a consequence
Say when a requirement nobody can meet is not a requirement
%%

A document published in 2017 gives guidance on buying sustainably. It says *should* on every page, it
is not a management system standard, and it cannot be certified against — and it says all three
things about itself plainly. We copied its language into our contracts, where *should* obliges
nobody, and then scored bidders on it.

### 5 · Word study — guidance and requirement

**5A  Complete the chart.**

%%FIG pe09-f6 | A three-column chart headed the word, what it creates and an example, with rows for shall, should, may, is encouraged to, must be able to demonstrate and failing which, four filled in and two empty.%%

| Word | What it creates | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **shall** | an obligation that can be breached | The supplier **shall** provide a mill certificate. |
| **should** | a recommendation that cannot | The supplier **should** minimize waste. |
| **may** | a permission | The engineer **may** inspect the records. |
| **is encouraged to** | nothing enforceable at all | The supplier **is encouraged to** report annually. |
| **must be able to demonstrate** | | |
| **failing which** | | |

> **Grammar Note** — In standards, *shall* marks a requirement and *should* a recommendation, and
> the distinction is formal rather than stylistic. Copying a *should* into a contract does not
> promote it: the sentence arrives with its own weakness attached, and no amount of surrounding
> formality repairs it.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Guidance documents are written in recommendations because they are guidance, and they say so about
themselves on their first pages. The failure is almost never the document's: it is the habit of
lifting its sentences into a contract, where *should* arrives with its weakness intact, and then
awarding marks for the answers. Thirty points for prose is the most expensive kind of pointlessness,
because four companies pay people to write it and the organization pays people to read it. The second
habit is less obvious and more damaging: setting a requirement nobody can meet. It feels like
ambition and it functions as surrender, because the first supplier who refuses gets a waiver and
every supplier afterwards knows what this organization does under pressure. The way out of both is
the same and it is unglamorous. Four parts to every requirement — obligation, threshold, proof,
consequence — and a telephone call to the market before you choose the threshold, so that the number
you write is one that nine suppliers in ten can actually meet.

**Discuss:** What requirement at your organization has been waived, and what happened next?

### 🗣 Group ranking

*In fours.* Six clauses, thirty marks, two suppliers of eleven. Rank these, best to worst. You must
agree.

a. Rewrite every *should* in the contract as *shall* with four parts, or delete it
b. Stop awarding marks for undertakings that cannot be breached
c. Telephone the market before choosing a threshold
d. Put a date on a rising threshold so the market can plan
e. Publish every waiver granted, with the clause and the reason

Report your order, and say which one your organization would resist.

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Requirement schedule S · replacing clauses S1 to S6
> *From H. Sato · 12 March · for L. Eriksen and the contract file · one page*
>
> **Clauses S1 to S6 are withdrawn. Every one of them begins *the supplier should*, none contains a
> threshold, a means of proof or a consequence, and none has been enforced in four years. Four of
> them carried 30 of the 100 quality marks in tender evaluation.**
>
> | # | Obligation | Threshold | Proof | Consequence |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **S1** | reinforcement **shall** contain recycled content | **not less than 90 %** | **mill certificate with each delivery** | **delivery rejected; no payment certified** |
> | **S2** | plant hours **shall** be worked by plant meeting the named emissions stage | **60 %**, rising to **90 % from month 18** | **hire records with each monthly application** | **those hours not certified for payment** |
> | **S3** | the supplier **shall** appoint a named individual accountable for subcontract labor conditions | **one named person, in writing** | **quarterly signed declaration** | **no further application certified until received** |
>
> **Achievability, which is why the thresholds are these and not higher.** **I telephoned eleven
> plant hire firms in this region.** **Two could meet an all-plant requirement at the named stage.
> Nine can meet 60 percent of hours now**, and **seven say they can reach 90 percent within
> eighteen months if a contract requires it.** **The 90 percent figure has a date on it for that
> reason: a dated threshold tells a supplier it is worth replacing a machine.**
>
> **My own error, recorded.** **My first draft required all plant to meet the named stage.** That is
> a requirement with four correct parts and **no market**: it would have doubled our plant cost or,
> far more likely, **been waived at the first refusal in week three**. **A requirement waived once is
> gone for three years**, because every supplier then knows what this company does when it is pushed.
>
> **On the source of the old wording.** **Clauses S1 to S6 were taken from a guidance document which
> states, on its own early pages, that it is not a management system standard and is not intended for
> certification**, and which is written in recommendations throughout **because that is what guidance
> is**. **The document is not at fault. We pasted recommendations into a contract and then awarded
> marks for the answers.**
>
> **Evaluation.** **No marks are awarded for any of S1 to S3.** They are requirements; a bidder
> either prices them or does not bid. **The 30 marks are reassigned to the method statement for the
> one difficult interface**, which does discriminate between bidders.
>
> *Source: ISO 20400:2017, Sustainable procurement — Guidance. It provides guidance on integrating
> sustainability within procurement; states that it is not a management system standard and is not
> intended for certification; is written as recommendations rather than requirements; and does not
> establish thresholds, metrics, targets or definitions of sustainable goods and services.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the number of suppliers who can comply next to every threshold. It is the
> only line that protects a requirement from being waived, and it takes an afternoon of telephone
> calls to produce.

### 3 · Write

Write **130 to 150 words**: a requirement schedule.

**Write three requirements, each with an obligation using *shall*, a threshold, a means of proof and a

happens to any marks it carried.**
