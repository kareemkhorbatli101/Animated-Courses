**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **it is claimed that** | **It is claimed that** most projects fail. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Cannot be verified* and *is wrong* are completely different claims, and
> mixing them up costs credibility in both directions. If you say *wrong*, somebody will ask for
> your evidence; if you say *unverifiable*, the burden stays where it belongs.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A striking figure with no date on it travels further than a careful one with a source, in every
country and every industry. It gets into a white paper, then a textbook, then a tender, and by the
third step the date and the definition have fallen off and only the number survives. Two habits
cost almost nothing and stop it. The first is to follow a figure back until the trail stops, which
usually takes twenty minutes and often ends at a document nobody in the building has read. The
second is harder, because it refuses both comfortable positions: *it cannot be verified* is not *it
is wrong*, and saying the stronger thing repeats the original error in the opposite direction. And
whenever a success rate appears, the definition is doing most of the work — ask which rule produced
it before you argue with the percentage.

> ### Bid library note · the project failure statistic
> *From C. Nkemelu · 24 June · for A. Holm and the bid team · one page*
>
> **The paragraph is coming out of the library.** It has appeared in **fourteen** tender responses
> since 2019. **I wrote four of them.**
>
> **The chain.** Our text cites a **consultancy white paper**; that cites a **textbook**; that cites
> the **CHAOS report, first published 1994** by a private research firm. **Three steps. Nobody here
> has read the primary source.**
>
> **What can and cannot be said.** Reports can be **bought**. The **sampling method, the
> questionnaire and the definitions have never been published**, so the finding **cannot be
> independently reproduced**. It has been **questioned in two peer-reviewed papers** — one in
> **2006** on the overrun figures, one in **2010** on the measurement approach — **and neither
> offers a replacement figure.**
>
> **So the honest sentence is: *the figure cannot be verified and has been questioned in
> peer-reviewed work.* It is not *the figure is wrong*.** Writing *disproved* would repeat exactly
> what the paragraph did: **say something stronger than the evidence supports because it reads
> better.**
>
> **What goes in instead: our own record, with the definitions printed.**
>
> | Of **44** completed projects | |
> |---|---|
> | **accepted by the client** | **41** |
> | **met their original time, cost and scope** | **9** |
> | **canceled** | **3** |
>
> **Both figures or neither.** The gap is **32 projects** that were replanned, descoped or extended
> **with the client's agreement** and delivered to their satisfaction. **One definition calls those
> failures and another calls them successes, and nothing about the projects changes.**
>
> **One uncomfortable note for the bid team.** **Nine of forty-four is twenty per cent**, which is
> **worse than the famous figure we are removing**. **It is ours, it is checkable, and I would rather
> be asked about it than quote a number I cannot source.**
>
> *Sources: the CHAOS report, first published 1994 by the Standish Group, a private research firm
> whose sampling method, questionnaire and definitions have not been published, so that its results
> cannot be independently reproduced; M. Jørgensen and K. Moløkken-Østvold, Information and
> Software Technology, 2006; and J. L. Eveleens and C. Verhoef, IEEE Software, 2010. The two
> peer-reviewed papers question the figures and the measurement approach and neither establishes a
> replacement figure; the original definition of success requires delivery on original time, cost
> and scope.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put your own worse number in. A note that removes somebody else's statistic and
> replaces it with a flattering one of your own will be read as a maneuver; the same note with
> twenty per cent in it is read as a standard.

>

> replacement figure.
>
> Under the original definition, a project succeeds only if it delivers on its original time, cost
> and scope. Applied to the company's own forty-four completed projects, nine qualify. Forty-one
> were accepted by the client and three were canceled.
>

> questioned it. Our own honest number is twenty per cent, which is worse than the one I am
