# Unit 7 · Round Again, With the Risks First
%%SUB Four cycles, two of them not cycles at all, and a protocol that failed cheaply in week three%%
%%GOALS
Talk about iteration
Say what makes a cycle a cycle
Say why an author wrote a second paper twelve years later
%%

A paper from 1988 proposed going round the same four activities repeatedly, and ordering each pass
by risk: do the frightening thing first, cheaply. It works. Twelve years later its author had to
publish a second paper, because so many things were being called by his model's name that were not.

> | | |

> >

> > good*, but: **do the thing that could kill the project while being wrong is still cheap.**
>

> | 1 protocol | yes | **yes — failed, cheaply** | yes | **yes** |

> decision at the end is a lap**, and two of our four were laps with a cycle's name on them.
>

> the model's **invariants** — the features it must have — and naming several **hazardous

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Every organization does the easy work first, and the reason is social rather than technical: easy
work shows progress, and progress is what a monthly report rewards. The frightening question — the
one whose answer could end the project — gets scheduled late, where it is expensive to be wrong, and
nobody plans it that way deliberately. Reversing the order costs almost nothing and feels wrong,
because week three then contains a failure instead of a demonstration, and somebody has to stand in
front of a sponsor with an invoice for a test that did not work. The second habit is naming things
accurately. A turn with no decision at the end is a lap, and calling it a cycle costs nothing today
and costs the word's meaning in three years, which is precisely why the model's author had to
publish a second paper twelve years later.

**1A  Look at the two records. Answer with a partner.**

%%FIG pd07-f12 | Two records, one above the other. The first lists four cycles with their deliverables. The second lists the same four with the risk resolved, the review and the commitment, two of them marked as laps.%%

1. Which record names the risk resolved on each turn?
2. Which one marks two turns as laps?
3. Which one could be audited?

**1B  Listen. Which two turns were laps, and why?** %%AUD TL4-07-14%%

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Cycle record · control system
> *From C. Nkemelu · 11 July · for E. Okonjo and W. Zhao · one page*
>
> **Four turns. Two were cycles and two were laps, and the difference is the fourth column.**
>
> | Turn | Objective | Risk resolved | Verified | Review and commitment |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **1 protocol** | can the new controller read the plant's **2004** protocol? | **yes — tested week 3, failed, 900** | yes | **yes, cycle 2 funded** |
> | **2 loop logic** | can the existing field wiring carry the new loops? | **yes — 14 loops bench-tested** | yes | **yes, cycle 3 funded** |
> | 3 graphics | build the operator screens | **no risk identified** | yes | **no** |
> | 4 reports | build the reporting set | **none set** | yes | **no** |
>
> **What turn one bought.** The protocol question would have been answered in **month nine**, inside
> a built panel, with the plant shut down. **Cost of discovering it then, as costed afterwards:
> about 60,000 and a second shutdown.** **Cost of discovering it in week three: 900.** **The
> prototype failed, and that is why it was worth doing.**
>
> **Why turns three and four were laps.** **Not indiscipline.** The contract bought the whole scope
> at signature, so **there was nothing left to commit** at the end of a turn and therefore nothing
> to decide. **The model's own author states that it sits awkwardly with fixed-price acquisition**,
> and he published a second paper in **2000** naming processes that use the model's name without its
> required features. **Turns three and four belong on that list, and I would rather write that than
> be told it.**
>
> **What we keep, and what we cannot.** **The ordering is free and we keep it**: on each pass, the
> most dangerous unknown first, answered as cheaply as possible, **until the frightening questions
> are gone.** **The commitment decision needs a contract that pays by turn**, which we do not have
> and did not ask for.
>
> **One recommendation for the next bid.** Price **two exploratory turns** separately, before the
> main scope. **Nine hundred against sixty thousand is an argument a client can follow**, and it is
> the only version of this method our commercial arrangements can actually support.
>
> *Source: B. W. Boehm, A Spiral Model of Software Development and Enhancement, IEEE Computer, May
> 1988; and B. W. Boehm, Spiral Development: Experience, Principles and Refinements, 2000, which
> sets out the model's invariants and identifies processes using its name without them. The model is
> written for software development and enhancement; it depends on risk assessment expertise; and its
> author noted that its steps required further elaboration and that it fits awkwardly with
> fixed-price contractual acquisition.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the failed prototype in the first row with its price. A record that opens
> with a success reads as a report; one that opens with a cheap failure reads as a method.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

### 3 · Write

Write **120 to 140 words**: a cycle record.

**Describe at least three turns, each with an objective, a risk resolved, verification, and whether a
review and commitment took place. Mark any turn with no commitment as a lap. Give two costs for the
same question. Use *on each pass* and *until*. End with one sentence on what the model's author said
about fixed-price contracts.**

**Read your record aloud to your partner first.** Then check your own against the list, and swap.

| | ✓ |
|---|---|
| Does every turn name a risk, or say none was identified? | |
| Are the laps marked as laps? | |
| Are two costs given for the same question? | |

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's record as the sponsor. Would you fund the next turn?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* What would your cheapest frightening test cost?

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD TL4-07-16%%

%%FIG pd07-f16 | A desk in July. A four-turn spiral lies drawn on a sheet with two turns marked, beside a failed prototype board, an invoice for nine hundred, a costing sheet for sixty thousand, a 1988 journal, a 2000 paper, and a short note of three sentences. A clock shows five.%%

> A control system was delivered in four turns rather than one design-and-build sequence. On the
> first turn, the question of whether a new controller could read the plant's 2004 protocol was
> tested in week three with two days of one engineer's time, at a cost of about 900. The test
> failed. Answering the same question in month nine, inside a built panel with the plant shut down,
> was afterwards costed at about 60,000 and a second shutdown.
>
> Of the four turns, two ended with a review at which the sponsor funded the next one. Two did not.
> The contract had bought the entire scope at signature, so there was nothing left to commit.
>
> The model's author published a second paper in 2000 setting out the features the model must have
> and naming processes that use its name without them.
>
> Chidi's note on the file is three sentences. *The ordering is the whole of the method and it is

> thousand in week three and a failed prototype I had to defend to a sponsor. Two of our four turns
> were laps, not because anybody lacked discipline but because a fixed-price contract leaves nothing
> to decide at the end of a turn, which the author said himself in 1988. If we want the rest of the
> model we have to price two exploratory turns separately in the next bid, and nine hundred against
> sixty thousand is an argument a client can follow.*

2. Why were two of the four turns laps?
3. Do you agree that the ordering is the whole of the method?
