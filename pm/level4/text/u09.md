# Unit 9 · Must-Meet and Should-Meet
%%SUB Thirty-one projects through gate three in four years, none stopped, and the four questions that changed it%%
%%GOALS
Write must-meet and should-meet criteria
Say what a gate is for
Say why a gate that never stops anything is a meeting

A 1990 article proposed a set of decision points with criteria written down in advance, dividing
them into the ones you must meet and the ones you should. The second kind are a score. The first
kind are the only ones that can stop anything, and most organizations have none.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Most organizations believe they have gates, and most have review meetings with a gate's name. The
test is not the number of criteria or the thickness of the pack; it is a single number in the
outcomes column, and that number is usually zero. Nobody decides this. A scoring sheet is written
because scoring feels fairer than a checklist, and a score cannot refuse — so after four years the
gate has heard thirty-one projects and approved thirty-one, and everyone involved will describe the
process as rigorous. Two habits change it, and both are uncomfortable. The first is writing criteria
that are answered yes or no, with any single no sufficient to stop. The second is that whoever writes
them writes at least one that would have caught their own project, because criteria that can only
catch other people's work are read as politics within a year, and they are usually right.

**Discuss:** How many projects has your organization stopped at a gate?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Gate three record · project 42
> *From C. Nkemelu · 8 May · for E. Okonjo and the gate file · one page*
>
> **Decision: hold.** The project **fails must-meet criterion 3**.
>
> **Must-meet. The project shall not pass gate three unless all of the following are true, any one of
> which is sufficient to stop it.**
>
> | | Criterion | Answer | Evidence |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | named sponsor, budget confirmed in writing | **yes** | memo, 3 Feb |
> | 2 | specification less than two years old | **yes** | issued Nov last year |
> | 3 | **operating owner has agreed in writing to accept the result** | **no** | **no document exists** |
> | 4 | one person named for the benefits | **yes** | named in the case |
>
> **Should-meet: total 71, threshold 60.** **This is a good project by the scoring sheet and the
> score is not what stopped it.** I record it here so that nobody later reports the project as
> having been weak. **It is strong and it is not ready.**
>
> **Criterion 3 in detail.** The department that will operate this system **has never been asked
> whether it will accept it.** It has not refused. **It has not been approached in nine months, and I
> am the person who did not approach it.**
>
> **Condition for go.** **One document: a written acceptance from the operating owner.** Estimated
> at **three weeks**. **The same discovery at commissioning would arrive as a refusal, roughly
> fourteen months from now, with the system built.**
>
> **Why the four criteria exist.** In four years this gate reviewed **31 projects and passed 31** —
> no hold, no recycle, no kill. **Not through indulgence: through arithmetic.** All fourteen criteria
> were scored and weighted, and **a score cannot refuse.** The **1990 article** that proposed this
> kind of gate separates **must-meet** from **should-meet** for exactly this reason, and **we had
> implemented only the second kind.** **Of the eleven projects since the change, nine passed and two
> were stopped — this one, and one on criterion 2.**
>
> **One note on the author.** He has written since about gates becoming **tollgates**, where a
> project presents its paperwork and passes through. **We built one over four years, and nobody ever
> decided to.**
>
> *Source: R. G. Cooper, Stage-Gate Systems: A New Tool for Managing New Products, Business
> Horizons, May–June 1990. The article is a practitioner account addressed to new product
> development; it argues for the system it describes rather than reporting a controlled trial, and
> its gates depend on decision-makers willing and able to stop projects — a condition the system
> itself cannot supply.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Record the should-meet total even when you are stopping the project. Otherwise
> the file reads as though the project was weak, and in two years somebody will say so.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. The decision was (hold / kill).
2. The criterion that failed was number (2 / 3).
3. The should-meet total was (60 / 71).
4. Projects stopped in the previous four years: (0 / 2).

### 3 · Write

Write **120 to 140 words**: a gate record.

**Give four must-meet criteria with the evidence for each, using *all of the following* and *any one
of which*. Give a should-meet total and threshold, and say plainly that the score is not what
stopped it, if it is stopped. Name the criterion that failed. Give a condition for go with an
estimate. End with one sentence comparing the cost of discovering it later.**

**Read your record aloud to your partner first.** Then check your own against the list, and swap.

| | ✓ |
|---|---|
| Does each must-meet criterion name its evidence? | |
| Is the should-meet total recorded even so? | |
| Is there a condition for go? | |

**Peer feedback.** Read your partner's record as the sponsor. Would you accept the hold?

**Then talk it over.** *In pairs, out loud.* Which criterion would catch your own project?

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD TL4-09-16%%

%%FIG pd09-f16 | A desk in May. A gate record with four yes-or-no criteria lies beside a scoring sheet totalling seventy-one, a wall chart showing thirty-one and zero, a specification dated 2021, a journal from 1990, and a short note of three sentences. A clock shows seven.%%

> For four years one decision point reviewed thirty-one projects and approved all thirty-one. It had
> fourteen criteria, each scored one to ten and weighted to a total against a threshold, and no
> criterion that could be answered no.
>
> Four yes-or-no criteria were added, any one of which is sufficient to stop a project: a sponsor who
> has confirmed the budget in writing, a specification less than two years old, a written acceptance
> from the department that will operate the result, and one named person for the benefits.
>
> Of the next eleven projects, nine passed and two were stopped. One failed on the specification. One
> failed because the operating department had never been asked, and the project manager who had not
> asked it was the person who had written the criterion.
>
> Chidi's note on the file is three sentences. *A gate that cannot produce the word no is a review
> meeting with a stronger name, and ours was one for four years without anybody deciding that it
> should be. The scoring sheet was not the problem: scoring ranks projects well and refuses nothing,
> and we had simply built half of what the 1990 article describes. I wrote the criterion that stopped
> my own project, which cost me an unpleasant week and is the only reason anybody accepted the other
> three.*

1. Why was the old gate incapable of stopping a project?
2. What did the scoring sheet do well?
3. Why do you think Chidi's own criterion made the others acceptable?
