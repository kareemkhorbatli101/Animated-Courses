competent project manager can do, written to be assessed against evidence. We used it on eleven of
our own, and the result was not the one anybody expected.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Self-assessment measures fluency about oneself, which is distributed very unevenly between people
and almost unrelated to competence at the work. In some cultures and some families, describing your
own achievements is close to rude; in others it is expected at every interview. Any process that
asks people to score themselves therefore ranks them partly by upbringing, and the person who writes
*fine* in nine boxes may be the one you would send to the hardest job in the company. The framework
in this unit never asked for self-scores: it asks an assessor to look for things a person has done
that somebody else can confirm. That costs two afternoons instead of two hours. And when nine of
eleven managers cannot state their organization's strategy, a framework for assessing individuals
has quietly produced a finding about the organization, which is the only kind of finding the person
running the exercise cannot delegate.

**Discuss:** Who at your company would score themselves lowest, and what are they best at?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Competence record · M. Bah
> *Compiled by A. Holm from interviews with A. Saleh, W. Zhao and E. Caruso · 19 May · one page*
>
> **This record replaces a self-assessment.** The framework asks for **evidence** — things a person
> has done that somebody else can confirm — and we had asked eleven people to **score themselves**,
> which is cheaper and measures something else. **M. Bah scored tenth of eleven and wrote *fine* in
> nine boxes.**
>
> | Element | What she did | Month | Outcome | Witness |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | conflict and crisis | mediated a two-hour dispute with the racking supplier | **Mar** | second quote at **4,000**; no letters | A. Saleh |
> | quality | rewrote the test regime after a failed witness test | **Jan** | 41 of 44 tests passed first time since | W. Zhao |
> | negotiation | renegotiated a variation without a claim | **Feb** | **11,000** avoided | E. Caruso |
> | teamwork | trained two detailers rather than refusing help | **Apr** | panel schedules handed over in half a day | A. Saleh |
> | risk | insisted on a bow tie for the top five | **Mar** | racking risk reranked first; 4,000 spent | W. Zhao |
> | results orientation | closed four lessons by changing templates | **Nov** | four templates now carry the field | A. Holm |
>
> **Eleven pieces of evidence came from three interviews of forty minutes each.** **Not one of them
> appeared on her own form**, and she says she would not have thought of them. **That is the normal
> result, not an unusual one.**
>
> **Assessed position.** On evidence, **M. Bah moves from tenth to second of eleven**. **The
> framework did not change; the method of collecting evidence did.**
>
> **One finding that is not about her, or about any of the eleven.** **Nine of the eleven could not
> state this company's strategy in two sentences.** Strategy is the first element of the first area.
> **I could not state it either when I was asked, and I run the project office.** The action is
> mine: **two sentences, written, published, by 31 May.** **My own two scores — the self-assessment
> and the colleague evidence — are in the appendix with everybody else's**, because an exercise the
> assessor is exempt from is an exercise that happens to other people.
>
> *Source: Individual Competence Baseline for Project, Programme and Portfolio Management, version
> 4, International Project Management Association, 2015. It describes twenty-nine competence
> elements in three areas with key competence indicators, as a basis for assessing individuals; it
> is not a method or process, does not prescribe how to manage a project, does not state how much
> competence a project requires, and its certification concerns an individual at a point in time
> rather than an organization.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the witness in the same row as the claim. A list of achievements with the
> names collected at the end reads as a reference letter; a name on every row reads as a record.

### 3 · Write

Write **120 to 140 words**: a competence record.

**Say whose evidence it is and who was interviewed. Give at least five elements, each with an action,
a month, an outcome in numbers and a witness. Say what the self-assessment produced and why it
differed. End with one finding that is about the organization rather than the person, and whose
action it is.**

**Read your record aloud to your partner first.** Then check your own against the list, and swap.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD TL4-05-16%%

%%FIG pd05-f16 | A desk in May. Eleven self-assessment forms lie in a pile with one showing the word fine nine times, beside a sheet of interview notes from three colleagues, a spider chart with one short arm, a list of twenty-nine elements, and a short note of three sentences. A clock shows five.%%

> A company assessed eleven project managers against twenty-nine competence elements in three areas.
> It ran the exercise as a self-assessment: two hours each, scored one to five. Practice averaged
> 3.9, people 3.2 and perspective 2.1.
>
> Nine of the eleven could not state the company's strategy in two sentences. Strategy is the first
> element of the first area. The head of the project office could not state it either.
>
> The two managers the company would send to its hardest project scored lowest overall. One of them
> wrote *fine* in nine boxes. When three colleagues were asked instead what they had seen her
> actually do, they produced eleven separate pieces of evidence with dates and outcomes, none of
> which she would have written down herself, and she moved from tenth to second.
>
> The framework had never asked for self-scores. It asks an assessor to collect evidence somebody
> else can confirm.
>
> Anders's note on the file is three sentences. *I chose a two-hour form over two afternoons of
> interviews and then ranked eleven people on how comfortable they are describing themselves, which
> is a real skill and not one I need on a pump station. The perspective finding is mine and not
> theirs: if nine project managers cannot state the strategy then nobody has stated it, and I could
> not do it either when I was asked. My own two scores are in the appendix with everybody else's,
> because an exercise the assessor is exempt from is just something that happens to other people.*

1. Why did the two best managers score lowest?
2. Whose finding is the perspective score, and why?
3. Do you agree that the assessor should publish their own scores?
