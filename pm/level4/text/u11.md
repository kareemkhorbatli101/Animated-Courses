A four-word citation in a quality procedure — *PDCA (Deming, 1950)* — contains three errors. The man
credited did not call it that, did not publish it in that year, and objected publicly to one of the
four letters. And the step he objected to is the step the procedure does not actually perform.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Citations in internal procedures are almost never read, and they survive audits for the same reason:
an auditor checks that a reference exists, not that it says what the document claims. A compliment
can therefore become a name, a name becomes a citation, and a citation acquires a date that nobody
can find. None of this matters until the mistake reaches the forms, and it usually does. The word in
the third box decides what people write there: *check* produces a tick and *study* produces three
sentences, and a cycle whose third step is a tick cannot feed its fourth step with anything. So the
habit worth carrying has two halves. Attribute at the strength of your evidence — *set out in* when
you have the page, *widely known as* when you are reporting usage, *through secondary accounts* when
you only have somebody's report. And then check whether the word in your own form asks for a
confirmation or a conclusion, because that is where a wrong attribution finally costs money.
