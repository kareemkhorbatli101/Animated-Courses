A 1996 book set out eight steps for leading change, in order, and warned that skipping one produces
the appearance of change without the thing itself. Most organizations do the steps that involve
talking and skip the one that involves removing an obstacle. And the percentage everybody quotes from

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Change work divides into steps that cost time and steps that cost somebody's standing, and almost
every organization completes the first kind. Presentations, visions, coalitions and launch events are
genuinely useful and they are also safe: nobody loses anything by attending one. Removing an approval
means one person signs fewer documents, which in most places is the visible measure of how much they
matter, and so the rule survives eighteen months of enthusiasm without anybody ever speaking in its
favor. The second habit worth carrying is about numbers. A striking percentage attached to a famous
book travels for years, and tracing it is a twenty-minute job that almost nobody does. When the trace
ends in a deck rather than a page, the honest move is to delete the slide and replace it with the
smaller true sentence about your own organization — which is less impressive and is the only version
you can defend when somebody asks where it came from.

> *From C. Nkemelu · 2 September · for E. Okonjo and A. Holm · one page*
>
> **Six steps of eight. Eighteen months. One rule unchanged since 2017.**
>
> | Step | What we did | Days | **What we removed** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1 urgency | created **by showing** three quarters of declining margin to 380 people | 12 | — |
> | 2 coalition | built **by seating** six decision-makers together, nine times | 9 | — |
> | 3 vision | developed **by cutting** four pages to one paragraph | 4 | — |
> | 4 communicate | **by presenting** it 14 times | **41** | — |
> | **5 empower** | **not attempted** | **0** | **nothing** |
> | 6 short-term wins | two, both real | 7 | — |
> | 7 consolidate | partly | 3 | — |
> | **8 anchor** | **not attempted** | **0** | **nothing** |
>
> **What we did well involved talking, and what we skipped involved subtraction.** **Forty-one days
> presenting; zero days removing.** The 1996 book warns that skipping a step **creates the illusion
> of speed and never produces a satisfying result**, which is a fair description of our eighteen
> months.
>
> **The obstacle.** The **2017** approval rule requires **three signatures** above a threshold and
> adds a **median of eleven days** to a purchase. **It was never defended in public and it was never
> removed**, because an obstacle is removed **by taking something away from somebody**, and the three
> signatories were present at every one of the fourteen presentations. **Silence costs nothing and
> worked perfectly.**
>
> **A correction to our own deck.** Our opening slide read **"70 % of change programmes fail"**,
> attributed to this book. **It is not in the book.** I traced the attribution through **three decks**
> and it ended with no page. **What the book says is that more than half the companies its author
> observed failed in the first phase** — a different fraction, of a different event, in a population
> that has never been published. **The slide is withdrawn.** **We had opened a programme about
> behavior by quoting an unsourced number.**
>
> **What the book is.** A practitioner's account based on observing **more than a hundred
> organizations** in the course of consulting. **It does not report a controlled study and does not
> claim to.** The order of the eight steps is an argument, not a finding — **and it is still the most
> useful argument I have read about why our programme produced nothing.**
>
> **One request.** **Reduce three signatures to one above the threshold.** Then the next report can
> contain a sentence with *by* in it.
>
> *Source: J. P. Kotter, Leading Change, Harvard Business School Press, 1996, developing Leading
> Change: Why Transformation Efforts Fail, Harvard Business Review, March–April 1995. The book is a
> practitioner account based on the author's observation of organizations during his consulting work;
> it does not report a controlled study and publishes neither sample nor measures. The widely quoted
> figure of 70 % does not appear in it.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put a *by* clause in every claim of improvement. A sentence about change with no
> *by* clause is almost always a sentence about a presentation.
