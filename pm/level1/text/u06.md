# Unit 6 · Must, Should, Could, Won't
%%SUB Four words for priority, and the one that means "not this time" rather than "never"%%
%%GOALS
Use *must*, *should*, *could* and *will not* for priority
Put a list of requirements in four groups
Say why a list of all *musts* is not a priority list
%%

Four words, invented in 1994 for a software method, now used on every kind of project. They work
only if two things are true: there is a fixed amount of time, and the last group really means *not
this time*. Where either is missing, the four words become decoration.

> **The four groups.**
>
> | Group | What it means |
> |---|---|
> | **Must have** | the delivery fails without it |

>
> > **"Won't have" means *not this time*. It does not mean *never*.** A *won't* with a date beside it
> > — *next release, March* — is a decision everybody has agreed to. **A *won't* with no date is a
> > refusal that somebody will discover in six months.**
> >

> | Won't have *(with a date)* | 0 | **7** |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Ask a group of users which requirements are essential and almost all of them will say all of them —
not because people are unreasonable, but because *is this important?* is a question about their own
job, and everything in your own job is important. The question that works is about the delivery
date: *if we go live on this day without it, does the work still function?* Thirty-two times out of
forty-one the answer is *it would be worse, but yes*, and that sentence is the definition of a
*should*. The second half of the technique is the one that gets dropped: the fourth group means *not
this time*, and a *won't* with no date beside it is read, six months later, as a refusal that was
hidden. The date and the name of the person who agreed it are what make the argument happen in May
instead of November.

**Discuss:** What is on your *won't have* list, and does it have a date?

> **If you want a tenth must.** Two things can happen and you choose: **the date moves**, or
> **something leaves the first column**. **We cannot add a tenth must and keep 30 November.**
>
> *Source: the MoSCoW technique, devised by Dai Clegg in 1994 for a software development method. It
> is a technique, not a standard: the suggested proportions are guidance, and the four groups mean
> nothing unless the delivery date is fixed.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put a date and a name on every *won't have*. Those two items are the difference
> between a decision and a surprise.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. There are (nine / forty-one) must haves.

3. The list depends on the (team / date).
4. A tenth must means the date (moves / stays).
