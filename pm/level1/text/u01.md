# Unit 1 · Three Words That Are Not the Same
%%SUB A project, a programme and a portfolio — and a standard that only gives you the words%%
%%GOALS
Use *be* and *have* to describe a piece of work
Say the difference between a project, a programme and a portfolio
Use singular and plural forms correctly
%%

Three words are used every day in every project office in the world, and most people use at least
one of them to mean something else. There is an International Standard that defines them. It gives
you the words and nothing more: no method, no rules, and no help in deciding which one you are
running.

%%FIG pa01-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the standard and its year, a three-row table of the three terms with a short meaning each, a boxed paragraph on what the standard does not do, a short list of four other terms, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 1
> *Project office · the three words · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The standard. ISO 21506**, *Project, programme and portfolio management — Vocabulary*, dated
> **2018**. It is a **vocabulary** standard. It defines terms. It has no rules in it.
>
> **The three terms.**
>
> | Term | What it means |
> |---|---|
> | **a project** | temporary work, with one or more defined objectives |
> | **a programme** | a group of **related** projects and other work, managed together for one aim |
> | **a portfolio** | a **collection** of projects, programmes and other work, managed together so the organization can choose |
>
> **Four more terms we use every week.** A **deliverable** is a thing the work hands over. A
> **stakeholder** is a person or organization with an interest in the work. A **benefit** is the good
> result that the work makes possible. **Governance** is the set of rules about who decides.
>
> **Why the words matter here.**
>
> > We have five water network projects. Last year we called them "the water programme" in our
> > reports. **They had no shared aim written down anywhere.** Five managers, five plans, five end
> > dates, and one word on the front of a report.
> >
> > **This year they have one aim: less water lost from the network.** It is written on one line, the
> > programme manager owns that line, and the five projects still have their own managers. **Now the
> > word is correct.**
>
> **What the standard does not do.** It is **not a method**. It gives **no rules** and **no process**.
> It does not say when a group of projects should become a programme. It does not say how to run any
> of the three. And **other organizations define these words differently**, so a document from
> somewhere else may use *programme* for something this standard calls a project.
>
> *Source: ISO 21506:2018, Project, programme and portfolio management &mdash; Vocabulary. It defines
> terms used in project, programme and portfolio management. It is a vocabulary standard only: it
> specifies no requirements, prescribes no method or process, and does not determine how any work
> should be organized. Other bodies define some of the same words differently.*

### 1 · *Be* and *have*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD TL1-01-06%%

> **Chidi:** I have to write one line about my work for the portfolio report. Is it a project?
> **Nadia:** Ask three questions. **Has** it got an end date?
> **Chidi:** Yes. The 30th of November.
> **Nadia:** **Is** there one objective, or several?
> **Chidi:** One. A new booking screen for the depot.
> **Nadia:** And **is** the team temporary?
> **Chidi:** Four people, until November. Then they go back to their teams.
> **Nadia:** Then it **is** a project. End date, objective, temporary team. **Write those three
> things and nothing else.**
> **Chidi:** My old manager called everything a project.
> **Nadia:** Most people do, and it costs us something real. **Our report last year had
> twenty-nine "projects" in it. Eleven of them had no end date.** The board asked why so many
> projects were late, and the answer was that eleven of them could not be late, because they never
> finish.
> **Chidi:** So they were not projects at all.
> **Nadia:** They **are** ordinary work, and ordinary work is not a failure. **It is just a
> different thing, and it needs a different report.**

**1B  Underline four forms of *be* and two of *have* in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **is** / **are** | The pump station **is** a project. The five jobs **are** a programme. |
| **is not** / **are not** | Running the network **is not** a project. |
| **Is** … ? / **Are** … ? | **Is** there one objective? **Are** the dates agreed? |
| **has** / **have** | The project **has** nine deliverables. The projects **have** five managers. |
| **has not** / **have not** | It **has not** got a sponsor yet. |
| **there is** / **there are** | **There is** one aim. **There are** four risks. |

> **Grammar Note** — Use *there is* and *there are* to say that something exists. Use *has* and
> *have* to say what something contains or owns.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Almost every organization calls more things "projects" than it has. The reason is not vanity. A
project gets a budget, a manager and a place in a report; ordinary work gets neither, and the person
doing it would like to be visible. So the word spreads, and the cost arrives later, in a meeting
where somebody asks why so many projects are late. Half of them are not late. They are not projects,
and the dates in their rows were typed in because the form had a column and an empty cell looks
wrong. The fix is dull and it works: two tables instead of one, dates only where dates exist, and an
owner's name on every piece of work that will never finish.

**Discuss:** What work at your company is called a project and has no end date?

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD TL1-01-16%%

%%FIG pa01-f16 | A desk in March. A printed report front page lies with the number twenty-nine circled, beside a second front page with two tables, a sheet showing one written aim for five projects, and a short note of three sentences. A clock shows five.%%

> Lindhurst's portfolio report last year listed twenty-nine projects. Fourteen were late, and the
> board spent two hours on the question.
>
> Eleven of the twenty-nine were not projects. They were ordinary work — running the network,
> answering service calls, keeping the data clean — and ordinary work cannot be late, because it does
> not finish. Somebody had typed dates into the empty column, because the form had a column and an
> empty cell looks wrong.
>
> The report now has two tables: eighteen projects with end dates, and eleven pieces of ordinary work
> with owners. The next board meeting took twenty minutes.
>
> Hanna's note on the file is three sentences. *A column on a form makes people write something, even
> when the true answer is "this has no end date". Nobody meant to mislead anybody. The meeting got
> shorter because every row could be answered, not because the numbers got smaller.*

1. Why could eleven pieces of work not be late?
2. Why were dates typed into the empty column?
3. Do you agree with Hanna's first sentence? Which column at your work does the same thing?
