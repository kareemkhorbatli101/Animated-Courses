%%FIG pa05-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the engineer and the year, a drawing of a triangle with three labelled corners, a boxed paragraph on what happens when one corner moves, a two-column comparison of two plans with numbers, a short section on what the triangle leaves out, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 5
> *Project office · the triangle · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** The triangle of **time, cost and quality** is attributed to **Martin
> Barnes**, a British engineer, who used it in teaching from about **1969**. Later versions put
> **scope** at the third corner instead of quality. **It is a teaching picture. There is no equation
> and no units.**
>
> **What it says.** The three corners are connected. **Move one and at least one other must move.**
>
> | If somebody asks for | Then one of these must happen |
> |---|---|
> | the same work, **sooner** | cost goes **up**, or scope goes **down** |
> | the same work, for **less money** | time goes **up**, or scope goes **down** |
> | **more** work, same date, same money | nothing. This is the request that produces silence. |
>
> **A real choice, with numbers.**
>
> > Northgate Water asked for the pump station **four weeks earlier**. We had two answers.
> >
> > | | Second crew | Remove the training |
> > |---|---|---|
> > | Time | 4 weeks earlier | 4 weeks earlier |
> > | Cost | **18,000 more** | nothing now |
> > | Scope | unchanged | **two training days removed** |
> > | Cost later | none | **unknown** |
> >
> > **The second crew is more expensive and easier to measure. The training is cheaper now and
> > nobody can tell you what it costs later.** We chose the second crew, and the reason we chose it
> > was not that it was better. **It was that we could put a number on it.**
>
> **What the triangle leaves out.** It has **no corner for value** — whether the project was worth
> doing at all. It has no corner for **risk**. It does not say **which** corner should move, or who
> decides. And **it is not a formula**: two weeks of time are not worth a fixed amount of money, and
> nothing in the picture tells you the rate.
>
> **Why it is still on our wall.** Because the request *more work, same date, same money* arrives
> about once a month, and **a picture is the shortest way to show that it is not a request but three
> requests**.
>
> *Source: the time&ndash;cost&ndash;quality triangle, attributed to Martin Barnes and used by him in
> teaching from about 1969; later versions substitute scope for quality. It is a teaching device, not
> a model: it contains no equation, no units and no exchange rate between the corners, and it has no
> term for value, benefit or risk. It does not indicate which corner should move or who should
> decide.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The commonest request in project management is impossible, and the commonest answer to it is worse
than a refusal. *More work, the same date and the same money* is three requests, and when a manager
says *we will absorb it* a corner moves anyway — usually into unpaid hours, dropped testing or
training quietly removed, none of which has a column on the report. On paper the project then
delivers early at the same cost, which looks like excellence and arrives six months later as a
resignation or a defect. The repair is not to refuse. It is to write two options with four lines
each, name the corner that moves in both, and put a number on every corner you can. The option with
a number will usually be chosen, and that is worth knowing about yourself: managers pick what they
can defend in a meeting, not what is best.

**Discuss:** When your team last "absorbed" something, where did it actually go?
