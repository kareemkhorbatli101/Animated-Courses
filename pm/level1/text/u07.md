# Unit 7 · A Date Somebody Will Ask About
%%SUB Stages, gates and milestones — and a standard number that means two different documents%%
%%GOALS
Use *will* and *by* to talk about future dates
Say what a stage and a milestone are
Check which edition of a standard somebody means
%%

A project is usually cut into stages, with a decision at the end of each one. The dates at those
decisions are the dates people remember. There is an International Standard about the concepts, and
it carries a useful warning: the same number was used for a different document before 2021.

### 1 · The future with *will* and *by*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD TL1-07-06%%

> **Sofia:** When will the design be approved?
> **Chidi:** It **will** be approved **by** the 15th of July.
> **Sofia:** And the gate meeting?
> **Chidi:** The gate meeting is **on** the 17th. **Those are two different dates and I used to give
> people one of them.**
> **Sofia:** Which one did you give?
> **Chidi:** The 17th, because that is the date in my diary. **But the approval has to exist before
> the meeting, or there is nothing to approve.** The design must be finished and circulated **by**
> the 15th.
> **Sofia:** So *by* and *on* are doing different work.
> **Chidi:** **_By_ is a deadline — not later than. _On_ is the day itself.** My first report said
> *design approved on 15 July*, and Oskar asked me whether I meant the design would be finished that
> day or approved that day.
> **Sofia:** What did you mean?
> **Chidi:** I had not thought about it. **Which is how a three-day problem becomes a three-week
> problem: the design arrives on the 15th, the board reads it on the 17th, and nobody has had time to
> read it.**
> **Sofia:** And now?
> **Chidi:** Three dates in the plan. **Finished by the 8th. Circulated by the 10th. Approved on the
> 17th.** Seven days for five people to read forty pages.
> **Sofia:** That is better. Will it hold?
> **Chidi:** **It will hold if the structural numbers come back from Mariama by the 5th. If they do

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Most plans contain the date the work finishes and no date for anybody to read it, and the result is
a gate meeting that is not a decision but a room agreeing with a document nobody has opened. The
people who approve things are usually the busiest in the organization and the least likely to admit
in public that they have read eleven pages of forty, because fourteen people are waiting and the
alternative is to look unprepared. That is not dishonesty; it is what a two-day reading window
produces. Three dates instead of one fixes it — finished by, circulated by, decided on — together
with one rule agreed in advance: if the work is late, the gate moves, and the reading time does not
shrink. The rule is easy to write and hard to keep, because moving a gate is visible and shortening
reading time is not.

**Discuss:** When did you last approve something you had not finished reading?

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD TL1-07-16%%

%%FIG pa07-f16 | A desk in June. A forty-page design document lies with a cover sheet dated the 15th, beside a one-page plan with three dates on it, a note recording eleven pages read, and a short note of three sentences. A clock shows six.%%

> A forty-page design reached the sponsor on the 15th of a month. The gate meeting was on the 17th.
> She was traveling on the 16th.
>
> She came to the meeting having read eleven of the forty pages, and she approved the design.
> Fourteen people were in the room, and the alternative was to say that she was unprepared.
>
> The plan had said *design approved on 15 July* — one date doing two jobs. The project manager had
> meant the date the design would be finished. He had not thought about when anybody would read it.
>
> The plan now carries three dates: finished by the 8th, circulated by the 10th, decided on the 17th
> — seven days for five people to read forty pages. And one rule: if the design is late, the gate
> moves and the reading time does not.
>
> Ebele's note on the file is three sentences. *I approved a design I had not read, and I would like
> that in the minutes rather than in anybody's memory. Almost every plan I see has the date the work
> finishes and no date for the reading, which turns a gate into a room agreeing with a document
> nobody has opened. Two projects have stopped at a gate in six years, and if I had read eleven pages
> of forty on either of them, neither would have stopped.*

1. Why did Ebele approve a design she had not read?
2. What was wrong with the sentence *design approved on 15 July*?
3. Do you agree with her last sentence? How much reading time do your decisions get?
