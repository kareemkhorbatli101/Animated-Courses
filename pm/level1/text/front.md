# Throughline Foundation
%%SUB English for Project Managers · International Edition · CEFR A2%%

## How to use this book

This is the first book of six. It teaches the English of **the parts of a project**: the words for
the work, the people, the dates, the money and the things that might go wrong. Every unit is built
on one real published document, named with its edition and its date.

**Twelve units**, sixteen pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | One real standard, paper or published method, with its date and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `TL1-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Every unit has one real source.** Not a summary of good practice: one document, named, dated, with
the sentence in which its own authors say what it does not cover. Eleven of this book's twelve units
carry a published standard, paper or method; the twelfth is a review.

**The sources are older than you expect.** A bar chart drawn in 1896 by a Polish engineer, seven
years before the American engineer it is named after. A triangle drawn on a course in 1969. A paper
about small groups from 1965. A priority list invented for a software method in 1994. Project
management did not begin with the software you use.

**The English is simple and the honesty is not.** At this level the sentences are short. What they
say is exact: which document, which edition, what it covers, and what it leaves to you.

**Money has no symbol in this series.** Every figure is written without a currency sign — *a budget
of 240,000*, *an overspend of 18,000* — because this book is used in many countries and the unit is
whatever currency your project uses. Where a figure matters, the text says what it is a figure of.

## The people

One company and one project team, in every unit of all six books.

**Lindhurst** is an engineering and services company of about nine hundred people. It runs projects
for other organizations. **Nadia Rahimi** is the project manager and the voice you will hear most.
**Oskar Lindqvist** builds the schedule. **Linh Tran** controls the cost. **Ahmad Saleh** runs the
work on site. **Wei Zhao** checks the quality. **Ida Berg** keeps the risk register. **Omar Nasser**
buys things. **Hanna Virtanen** works in the project office and makes the reports. **Mariama Bah** is
the engineer. **Elena Caruso** manages the contracts. **Tunde Oyelaran** asks what the project is
actually for. **Ebele Okonjo** is the sponsor, and signs.

**Chidi Nkemelu** joined four weeks ago. He is the newest project manager in the company, he has
never run a project before, and he asks the questions this book is an answer to.

**Sofia Marchetti** works for the client. She is not in the team, she reads everything the team
sends, and she is the reason the writing in Lesson 5 has to be clear.

The clients — **Northgate Water**, **Calder Transit**, **Aurora Health Trust**, **Pennine Energy**
and **Sorrel Foods** — are invented, on purpose.
