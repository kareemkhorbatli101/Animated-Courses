A bar for every task, time along the top, and the length of the bar is the length of the work.
Everybody draws one. It is named after an American engineer who used it around 1910, and a Polish
engineer drew the same thing in 1896. In its original form it shows two things and hides two others.

%%FIG pa02-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, two lines giving the two engineers and their dates, a small drawing of a five-bar chart, a boxed paragraph on the two things the chart does not show, a short list of what to add in pen, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 2
> *Project office · the bar chart · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** **Henry Gantt**, an American engineer, used charts of this kind in his
> work from about **1910**, and they appear in his books of **1913** and **1919**. The form is named
> after him. **Karol Adamiecki**, a Polish engineer, drew the same kind of chart — he called it a
> *harmonogram* — in **1896**, and published it in Polish and Russian. **Very few people outside
> Poland and Russia read it for many years.**
>
> **What the chart shows.**
>
> | On the chart | What it tells you |
> |---|---|
> | the **left end** of a bar | the start date |
> | the **right end** of a bar | the end date |
> | the **length** of the bar | the duration |
> | a **diamond** | a milestone: an event, not work |
> | the **vertical line** | today |
>
> **What the chart does not show.**
>
> > **It does not show why a bar is where it is.** Two bars, one after the other, look like two bars
> > one after the other. **Nothing on the paper says that the second cannot start until the first has
> > finished.** That rule is called a **dependency**, and in the original chart it is not drawn at
> > all.
> >
> > **It does not show which bars decide the end date.** Some tasks can be late and the project still
> > finishes on time. Some cannot. **The chart draws both kinds of bar in exactly the same way.**
> >
> > So the paper is **honest about dates and silent about reasons**, and a reader cannot tell the
> > difference between a date somebody agreed and a date the form needed.
>
> **What we write on ours in pen.** Three things, every week: **the today line**, **the name of the
> person who agreed each date**, and **a question mark on any bar that nobody has agreed**. **Last
> month eleven of our thirty-one bars had a question mark.**
>
> *Source: bar charts of this kind are associated with Henry Gantt, who used them from about 1910 and
> whose books of 1913 and 1919 show them, and with Karol Adamiecki, who drew an equivalent chart &mdash;
> the harmonogram &mdash; in 1896 and published it in Polish and Russian. In its original form the
> chart shows start dates, end dates and durations. It does not show dependencies between tasks, it
> does not show which tasks determine the end date, and it does not show spare time.*

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD TL1-02-06%%

> **Chidi:** Sofia asked me when the testing is. I said "in June". She asked again.
> **Oskar:** Because *in June* is thirty days, and she has to book two of her own people.
> **Chidi:** The chart says the bar starts on the 1st of June.
> **Oskar:** The chart says that because I had to draw the bar somewhere. **Nobody agreed the 1st of
> June. I needed a left end for the bar, and the 1st was the first working day of the month.**
> **Chidi:** So the chart is wrong?
> **Oskar:** The chart is not wrong. It is **more certain than the information in it**. That is what a
> bar does: two ends, both of them looking like a decision.
> **Chidi:** Then what do I tell Sofia?
> **Oskar:** The truth, with the right words in it. ***In* June, not yet *on* a day.** And then one
> more sentence: **the testing can start three days after the pipe work ends, and the pipe work ends
> on the 23rd of May if the pumps arrive in week 12.**
> **Chidi:** That is a long answer.
> **Oskar:** It is four facts, and she can act on all four. **_In June_ is one word she cannot act
> on.** She will ask again next week, and the week after, until somebody gives her a day.
> **Chidi:** And when do we give her a day?
> **Oskar:** **When Omar confirms the pumps.** Until then I write *in June* on the chart in pen and a
> question mark beside it. **A question mark is a date I do not have. An empty space looks like a
> date I forgot.**

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A bar chart is the most trusted document in most projects and the least honest, and it is not the
chart's fault. A bar needs two ends. If nobody has agreed a date, the person drawing it still has to
put the end somewhere, so they use the first working day of the month, or last year's plan, or the
date the client once mentioned. The drawing that comes out is identical to a drawing of agreed dates.
Everybody then reads the wall as a set of promises, and the planner is the only person who knows
which bars are real. The cheapest repair in this book is a pen: a question mark on every bar nobody
has agreed, and a name beside every bar somebody has. It makes the chart uglier and it moves the
argument to where it belongs, which is the eleven bars nobody has discussed.

**Discuss:** How many bars on your chart would have a question mark?
