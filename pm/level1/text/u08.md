# Unit 8 · What Might Go Wrong
%%SUB A one-line definition that includes good news, and a standard with no scoring scheme in it%%
%%GOALS

Everybody keeps a list of things that might go wrong. The International Standard on risk defines the
word in one line, and the definition surprises most people twice: it is about **objectives**, and it
includes things that might go **right**.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Most risk registers grow until nobody reads them, and they grow because a one-word row is free to
write and impossible to argue with. *Weather. Suppliers. Delays.* Everybody nods, the row goes on,
and five years later there are sixty-one of them and the board cannot be given a top five, because
you cannot rank rows that have no stated effect. The honest way to think about a one-word row is not
that it is a small risk but that it is an unknown one, and some of them will turn out to be the
largest on the list. Two other habits are worth the trouble. Check where your scoring matrix came
from — ours came off a slide in 2019 and uses the same bands for a forty-thousand project and a
nine-million one. And write down one thing that might go **right**: the definition covers effects in
both directions, and a team that finishes two weeks early with no plan for them spends them doing
the next thing slowly.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

**Listen and read.** %%AUD TL1-08-16%%

%%FIG pa08-f16 | A desk in May. A printed register of sixty-one rows lies with twenty-two marked, beside a five-by-five matrix with a handwritten note saying 2019 course, one row written out in full on a card, a card headed good surprise, and a short note of three sentences. A clock shows five.%%

> Lindhurst's risk register had sixty-one rows. Twenty-two of them were a single word: *weather*,
> *suppliers*, *delays*.
>
> In March the board asked for the top five risks. The project office could not produce them. Thirty-
> nine rows had no stated effect, and rows with no effect cannot be ranked against each other.
>
> The five-by-five scoring matrix on the office wall came from a training course one member of staff
> attended in 2019. The standard contains no matrix and says the criteria are the organization's own.
> The same bands were used for a forty-thousand project and a nine-million one.
>
> Not one of the sixty-one rows described something that might go right.
>
> Ida's note on the file is three sentences. *A one-word row is not a small risk; it is an unknown
> one, and some of them will turn out to be the largest on the list. We could not give the board five
> risks out of sixty-one, which is the clearest measure of a register that has stopped working. And I
> have written the first good surprise in six years, because the definition covers effects in both
> directions and a team that finishes two weeks early with no plan for them will spend those weeks
> doing the next thing slowly.*

1. Why could the board not be given a top five?
2. Where did the matrix come from, and what is wrong with it?
3. Do you agree with Ida's last sentence? What might go right on your project?
