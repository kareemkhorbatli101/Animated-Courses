### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Two organizations on one project will keep two registers almost everywhere, and the reason is
administrative rather than cultural: each one has its own system, its own owners and its own
reporting cycle, and reading the other side's list is nobody's job. That is how two competent
companies can manage the same risk in opposite directions for four months with nothing hidden and
nobody at fault. The harder clause is the exit. Writing down how a relationship ends while it is
working reads, in most boardrooms, as planning for failure, and the first draft is usually so
diplomatic that both legal teams approve it and neither side could ever use it. The argument for
writing it is simple and unanswerable: everybody in the room is well disposed this morning, and the
document is for a morning when they are not.

> | **Joint register** | **one** register, **66 rows**, **one owner per row**. **Neither party may** change an owner without written notice to the other. |
> | **Governance** | monthly joint review; anything above 20,000 or two weeks **jointly** escalated to both executives within 5 working days |
> | **Exit** | see below |
>
> **Why there is one register and not two.** Their register had **19** rows and ours **61**. **Eleven
> appeared in both**, with different owners. **Three were real conflicts.** The worst: **we were
> holding four weeks of float and paying a supplier to hold stock, while they were deferring the
> same order into the next financial year.** **Four months. Both sides competent. Nothing hidden —
> it had simply never been written down in the same place.**
>
> **The exit strategy, in five lines.** **Who owns the operating data** (the client, from handover).
> **Who holds the spares** (the client's depot, from month 3). **How long support continues** (24
> months). **How the as-built records transfer** (electronically, within 20 working days). **Which
> disputes survive the end** (defects and intellectual property). **Two of those five are arguments
> we have already had on a previous contract.**
>
> **This took three months and four drafts.** The first draft said the parties would **cooperate in
> good faith at the appropriate time**. Both legal teams approved it, **which is how we knew it was
> worthless**.
>
> **What our certificate does not say.** The standard is a **requirements standard** and we are
> certified to it. **That proves a system exists and is operated.** It does **not** prove that the
> two organizations trust each other, it says nothing about the contract, and it has **no answer to
> the structural problem**: **the client is paid to spend less and we are paid for work done.** A
> joint register does not remove that. **It makes it visible, which is all a document can do.**
>
> *Source: ISO 44001:2017, Collaborative business relationship management systems &mdash;
> Requirements and framework, replacing BS 11000-1:2010. It specifies requirements for a management
> system covering collaborative business relationships and may be certified against; certification
> concerns the existence and operation of that system rather than the quality of any relationship;
> and it does not address contract terms, commercial dispute resolution or the alignment of the
> parties' commercial incentives.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Write *persons, not posts*, and add what happens if one of them leaves. A name
> with no replacement rule becomes a post again within eighteen months.
