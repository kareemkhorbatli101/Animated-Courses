# Unit 6 · If, Then, and the Thing in Between
%%SUB Five boxes, four arrows, and the arrow where sixty-one per cent of the evidence pointed the other way%%
%%GOALS
Build an *if … then* chain
Say what a logic model shows and what it does not test
Say why a justified project can rest on a false sentence
%%

A logic model is five boxes and four arrows: what we put in, what we do, what comes out, what
changes, and what difference it makes in the end. Each arrow is an *if … then*. The model displays
the chain beautifully and has no opinion whatever about whether it is true.

**1B  Underline four conditional structures in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **if … then** | **If** the station exists, **then** pressure is held. |
| **only if** | Pressure is held **only if** the mains are replaced. |
| **provided that** | **Provided that** the mains work is funded. |
| **unless** | Interruptions will not fall **unless** the mains are renewed. |
| **which means that** | The bursts dominate, **which means that** the claim fails. |
| **in which case** | The mains may not be funded, **in which case** arrow three fails. |

> **Grammar Note** — *If* states a condition; *only if* states that it is the **only** condition
> that will do. Writing *if* where you mean *only if* is how a chain comes to look stronger than it
> is: *pressure is held if the station is built* is a different and weaker claim from *only if the
> mains are also replaced*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A diagram that looks finished is the most expensive kind of document, in any country. Five boxes and
four arrows, every box filled in, every arrow drawn: it reads as a completed piece of thinking, and
nobody asks for the log that would test the last arrow. The guide this one comes from says the
arrows and not the boxes carry the argument, and that is exactly the part that gets skimmed. There
is a second effect worth knowing about: a benefit that sounds impressive is often the one several
projects claim at once. *Fewer interruptions* was claimed by three projects in the same programme
and would have been counted three times. *Firefighting pressure at that estate, for the first time
since 2019* can be claimed by one project only, which is what makes it worth less in a brochure and
far more in a decision.

**Discuss:** Which benefit at your company is claimed by more than one project?

> *From C. Nkemelu, with P. Rao · 26 March · for E. Okonjo · one page*
>
> | | The arrow, as a sentence | Tested against |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1** | **If** 2.70 m and 11 people, **then** the station is built and commissioned | the schedule and the cost report |
> | **2** | **If** it is commissioned, **then** the station and controls exist and are accepted | the witness test and handover note |
> | **3** | **If** the station exists, **then** pressure is held at the estate — **only if** the client's mains work (**90,000**) is done | the network engineer's confirmation, 9 Mar |
> | **4** | ~~**If** pressure is held, **then** interruptions fall~~ **FALSE** | **the client's own three-year interruption log** |
>
> **Why arrow four is false.** Of three years of logged interruptions: **61 per cent were bursts on
> the old mains**, **22 per cent planned shutdowns**, **9 per cent failures at the old pumping
> station**. **Bursts are a function of pipe age and pressure cycling**, and higher pressure makes
> them **marginally more likely**, not less. **So this project addresses the 9 per cent and may add
> slightly to the 61.**
>
> **The arrow that replaces it.** **If** pressure is held at the estate, **then** the estate has
> **firefighting pressure for the first time since 2019**. The fire service wrote to the client in
> **2021** and **2023**. **Nothing else in the client's programme restores it**, so the benefit is
> **attributable to this project alone** — unlike *fewer interruptions*, which **two other projects
> in the same programme have also claimed.**
>
> **How this was missed for two years.** The diagram was complete. Five boxes, four arrows, every
> box filled in. **A model that looks finished stops people looking**, and the log that disproves
> arrow four takes about **twenty minutes** to read.
>
> **What the guide is and is not.** It is a **foundation's practical guide** for non-profit
> programme planning, **not a project standard**. It helps you **display** a theory of change and
> **provides no test of it**; it notes that outcomes are often influenced by factors outside the
> programme's control; and it warns against models that are **a list of activities with arrows
> added**. **Ours was a list of activities with arrows added, and writing arrow four as a full
> sentence is what killed it.**
>
> *Source: W. K. Kellogg Foundation, Logic Model Development Guide, 2004. It is a practical guide
> for non-profit programme planning and evaluation rather than a project management standard; the
> model displays a theory of change and provides no test of it; the guide notes that outcomes are
> frequently affected by factors outside the programme's control; and it warns against models that
> amount to a list of activities.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Write each arrow as a full sentence with a subject and a verb. An arrow drawn
> between two boxes cannot be false; the same arrow written out as *if … then* can be, and that is
> the entire value of the exercise.
