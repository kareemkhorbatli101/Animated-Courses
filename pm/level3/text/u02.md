The money index says we are at 0.79 and it will say 1.00 on the last day, however late that day is.
There is a way of turning the same data into weeks. It was published in a practitioners' newsletter
in 2003, it is simple, and it is honest about what it still cannot see.

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 2
> *Project office · lateness in weeks · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** **Walt Lipke** published *Schedule is Different* in **The Measurable
> News** in **2003**. It is a **practitioners' newsletter**, not a standard, and the technique is
> known as **earned schedule**. It uses the numbers an earned value system already has and returns
> an answer in **time**.
>
> **The method, in one sentence.** Take today's **earned value**, find the point on the **planned
> curve** where the plan reached that figure, and read the **date**.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | earned value today | **1.11 m** |
> | the plan reached 1.11 m at | **week 22.5** &nbsp;&larr; the **earned schedule** |
> | time actually elapsed | **week 30** &nbsp;&larr; the **actual time** |
> | **schedule variance, in time** | **&minus;7.5 weeks** |
> | **time index** | 22.5 ÷ 30 = **0.75** |
> | **forecast duration** | 39 ÷ 0.75 = **about 52 weeks** |
>
> **Why this exists at all.**
>
> > The money-based schedule index on this project is **0.79**. On the **last day** of a project —
> > any project, early, on time or eleven weeks late — **earned value equals planned value**, so
> > that index equals **1.00**.
> >
> > **A project that finishes thirteen weeks late reports a perfect schedule index on the day it
> > finishes.** It drifts upwards all the way through, and it is at its least useful exactly when
> > the decisions are hardest.
> >
> > **The time index does not do this.** It stays at **0.75** until something actually changes, and
> > at completion it reports the ratio of planned to actual duration, which is the truth.
>
> **What it still cannot see.**
>
> > **Which activities are late.** Not one word. It is a **whole-project measure**: it will tell you
> > the project is seven and a half weeks behind and has no opinion about the valves, the drawings
> > or the inspector.
> >
> > **It rests on the same two things as the money figures**: a baseline worth comparing with, and
> > an honest measurement of physical progress. **If the 1.11 is twelve points high, so is the week
> > 22.5.**
> >
> > **And it assumes today's rate continues**, which is a forecast, not a measurement. On this
> > project the valves arrive in week 17 and the chain afterwards is faster, so **our own view is
> > 47 weeks, not 52** — and that view comes from the schedule, not from this arithmetic.
>
> **What the technique is not.** It is a **published technique in a professional newsletter**, with
> a body of case reports behind it rather than a standard. There is **no requirement anywhere** to
> use it. It makes the lateness of a whole project visible in weeks, **and it does that one job**.
>
> *Source: W. Lipke, Schedule is Different, The Measurable News, 2003. It is a technique published
> in a practitioners' newsletter rather than a standard; it derives time measures from earned value
> data and therefore inherits the quality of the performance measurement baseline and of the
> physical progress measurement; it is a whole-project measure and identifies no individual late
> activity; and its duration forecast assumes that present performance continues.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A measure that gets better as a project gets worse will survive for decades, in any country, because
nobody has to decide to protect it. The money-based schedule index climbs steadily towards a perfect
1.00 as a project runs late, and on the final day of the latest project in the company's history it
reports success. Replacing it costs three lines of arithmetic and a difficult sentence: *we are
behind by seven and a half weeks, and because our earned value may be optimistic, that is probably
an understatement.* The other half of the honesty is just as important and gets forgotten: a
whole-project number is an alarm, not an address. Used as an address, it arrives in the room as an
accusation against whichever team is nearest, and the actual answer was in the chain all along.

**Discuss:** Which measure at your company would survive because nobody has to defend it?

> ### Schedule performance note · month 7
> *From O. Lindqvist · 6 October · for E. Okonjo and S. Marchetti · one page*
>
> **As of 30 September, this project is behind by seven and a half weeks.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | earned value **to date** | **1.11 m** |
> | the plan reached 1.11 m at | **week 22.5** — the **earned schedule** |
> | weeks **elapsed** | **30** |
> | **schedule variance in time** | **&minus;7.5 weeks** |
> | **time index** | **0.75** |
>
> **How it was derived, in one sentence.** Read across from today's earned value to the baseline
> curve and drop a line to the time axis. **Three lines of arithmetic on figures the cost report
> already contained.**
>
> **Two forecasts, in this order.** **52 weeks** at today's rate (39 ÷ 0.75). **47 weeks** on the
> schedule, because the **valves arrive in week 17** and the chain behind them runs faster. **The
> difference between the two numbers is one delivery.** I believe 47, and **if the valves move
> again I will report 52 in that week**, not in March.
>
> **Why we have not had this figure before.** The money-based schedule index was **0.91** in month
> three, **0.86** in month five and **0.79** now, **and at completion it would have reported 1.00
> whatever happened**. On the last day of a project thirteen weeks late it shows perfect schedule
> performance. **A measure that improves as a project gets later takes no effort to defend.**
>
> **Two things this number cannot do.** It names **no activity**: it is a whole-project measure, and
> the address for this project is the **valve chain, weeks 9 to 17**. And it inherits the quality of
> the earned value, where **14 of 38 packages are self-scored and ran 12 points high** — so **seven
> and a half weeks is more likely an understatement than an exaggeration.**
>
> *Source: W. Lipke, Schedule is Different, The Measurable News, 2003. It is a technique published
> in a practitioners' newsletter rather than a standard; it inherits the quality of the baseline
> and of the physical progress measurement; it is a whole-project measure and identifies no
> individual late activity; and its duration forecast assumes present performance continues.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Name the weakness of your own number in the same note. *Seven and a half weeks
> is probably an understatement* cannot be used against you later, and it is the sentence that makes
> the other four believable.
