%%FIG pc11-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, two lines giving two different authors with two different years, the two sentences set out one under the other, a table of three measures and their side effects, a spread of order durations with a median and a tail marked, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 11
> *Project office · what a measure does to people · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The sentence everybody quotes.**
>
> > **When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.**
>
> **It is almost always called Goodhart's law. It was not written by Goodhart.** That phrasing comes
> from the anthropologist **Marilyn Strathern**, in an article about **audit in British
> universities** published in the **European Review** in **1997**, where she was summarizing
> Goodhart's idea.
>
> **What Goodhart actually wrote.** **Charles Goodhart**, an economist, in a paper on monetary
> management given in **1975**, put it much more narrowly:
>
> > **Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for
> > control purposes.**
>
> **He was writing about monetary aggregates**, not about projects, universities or depots. **A
> third version**, closer to the popular one, was published by **Donald Campbell** in **1979**,
> about social indicators.
>
> **Why the difference matters.** Goodhart's version is **narrow and testable**: a relationship used
> for control stops holding. The popular version is **broad and almost unfalsifiable** — every
> measure becomes a target, every target distorts, and no observation could contradict it. **It is
> the Book 2 Unit 12 problem again**, and it is the reason this note gives the dates and the authors
> rather than the slogan.
>
> **Three measures in four years, at our own depot.**
>
> | Year | The measure | What improved | What it led people to do |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **2021** | orders dispatched **per day** | count **+18 percent** in two months | **split orders in two**; two dispatches instead of one |
> | **2023** | **on-time** dispatch percentage | **81 → 96 percent** | **refuse late orders**; **14 percent** moved to the next day on purpose |
> | **2025** | **median** days from order to dispatch | **11 → 6 days** | **take the easy orders first** |
>
> **The 2025 measure is ours.**
>
> > *Days from order to dispatch, counted weekly* is the measure this course recommended in Book 2,
> > as an example of a benefit written properly, with a number and a frequency.
> >
> > **It did exactly what it was designed to do.** The median fell from **eleven days to six**.
> >
> > **And the ninetieth percentile rose from fourteen days to twenty-three.** The nine hardest orders
> > each week now wait almost three weeks, because the number on the wall is the middle one and the
> > middle is improved fastest by doing the easy work first.
> >
> > **Nobody at the depot was instructed to do this, and nobody did anything dishonest.** A measure
> > that somebody is judged on gets managed. **That is not a failure of character; it is what
> > measurement is.**
>
> **What we are doing about it.** Not abandoning the measure. **Reporting the median and the
> ninetieth percentile together, every week, on the same line.** One number can be gamed quietly;
> **two numbers in opposite directions cannot.**
>
> **What neither source will do for you.** Goodhart's paper is about **monetary policy** and offers
> no remedy for a depot. Strathern's article is about **university audit** and is a critique, not a
> method. **Neither says which measure to use, and the popular sentence, taken alone, is an excuse
> to measure nothing** — which is the one conclusion this note does not support.
>
> *Sources: C. A. E. Goodhart, Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience, 1975, in which
> the claim is that an observed statistical regularity tends to collapse when pressure is placed on
> it for control purposes; and M. Strathern, "Improving ratings": audit in the British University
> system, European Review, 1997, which is the origin of the popular phrasing usually attributed to
> Goodhart. A related formulation was published by D. T. Campbell in 1979. Goodhart's claim concerns
> monetary aggregates and Strathern's article is a critique of audit practice; neither addresses
> project measurement, neither offers a remedy, and the popular phrasing is broad enough that no
> observation would contradict it.*

> **Jonas:** Fourth. In 2021 it was orders per day and we **ended up splitting** orders in two. In

> was never in a pack.

*Why does Jonas say the 2023 measure was worse than the 2021 one?*

**1B  Underline five indirect-effect structures in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **leads people to** | The target **leads people to** take the easy orders first. |
| **end up** + *-ing* | We **ended up splitting** orders in two. |
| **has the effect of** + *-ing* | It **had the effect of** making us refuse late orders. |
| **so that** | Moved to the next day **so that** they could not be late. |
| **turn into** | One number **turns into** a target. |
| **is treated as** | A median **is treated as** the whole distribution. |

> **Grammar Note** — *End up* and *has the effect of* are followed by the **-ing** form: *we ended
> up splitting*, *it had the effect of making*. These structures describe what happened **without
> accusing anybody**, which is why they are the right grammar for this subject.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The most quoted sentence about measurement is attributed to the wrong person almost every time it is
used, and the mistake matters. The popular version — any measure that becomes a target stops being a
good measure — is broad enough that nothing could disprove it, and in a meeting it functions as
permission to measure nothing at all. The narrower original claim, about a statistical relationship
collapsing when pressure is applied to it for control, actually tells you what to do: watch the
relationship, not just the number. Everywhere, the practical repair is the same and it is cheap. Put
a second number beside the first, chosen so that the two move in opposite directions when the work
is being resequenced rather than improved — a median and a ninetieth percentile, an on-time
percentage and a refusal rate. And describe what people did without the word *gaming*, because the
supervisor with forty orders and one number on the wall was not cheating. They were reading the
wall.

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Measure review · order to dispatch
> *From C. Nkemelu, with J. Weber · 4 December · for E. Okonjo · one page*
>
> **The measure worked. That is the first and most important sentence in this note.** Median days
> from order to dispatch: **11 → 6** in nine months. **Nobody worked longer hours and no process was
> changed.**
>
> **What it cost.** **Ninetieth percentile: 14 → 23 days.** The nine hardest orders each week now
> wait almost three weeks.
>
> **The mechanism, with nobody at fault.** A supervisor with forty orders and **one number on the
> wall** works on the forty that move the number. **The measure led people to resequence the work**;
> it did not lead them to do less of it. **Nobody instructed this and nobody concealed it** — Jonas
> said it out loud in two meetings, and it was never in a number, so it was never in a pack.
>
> **This is the fourth measure in four years, and all four were met.**
>
> | Year | Measure | Result | What it led people to do |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 2021 | orders per day | **+18 percent** | **split orders in two** |
> | 2023 | on-time percentage | **81 → 96** | **refuse late orders** — 14 percent moved to the next day |
> | 2025 | median days to dispatch | **11 → 6** | **take the easy orders first** |
>
> **The 2025 measure is the one this course recommended**, as an example of a benefit written
> properly with a number and a frequency. **It is the best of the four and it still did this.**
>
> **What changes on Monday.** **The median and the ninetieth percentile are reported on the same
> line, every week.** **One number turns into a target. Two numbers that move in opposite directions
> cannot be improved by resequencing.** The on-time percentage, where it is still used, will carry
> **the number of orders refused at the cut-off** beside it.
>
> **On the famous sentence.** *When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure* is
> **Marilyn Strathern's wording, from a 1997 article on university audit**, not Goodhart's.
> **Goodhart, in 1975, wrote something narrower and about monetary aggregates.** The difference is
> practical: **the popular version is an argument for measuring nothing; the narrow one tells you to
> watch the relationship.** Neither offers a remedy for a depot.
>
> *Sources: C. A. E. Goodhart, Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience, 1975, whose
> claim is that an observed statistical regularity tends to collapse when pressure is placed on it
> for control purposes; and M. Strathern, "Improving ratings": audit in the British University
> system, European Review, 1997, the origin of the popular phrasing usually attributed to Goodhart;
> a related formulation was published by D. T. Campbell in 1979. Goodhart's claim concerns monetary
> aggregates and Strathern's article is a critique of audit practice; neither addresses project
> measurement or offers a remedy.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Open with *the measure worked*. Everything after it is then a finding rather
> than a complaint, and the person who set the measure can stay in the room.
