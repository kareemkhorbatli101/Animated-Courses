# Throughline · sources

**English for Project Managers · International Edition.** Six books, seventy-two units,
seventy-two separate source bodies. Nothing in this series reuses a figure or a source from
Wayfinder English, from the Clearline business series, from Foreign Service English or from
Keystone.

## The rule this series is built on

Every reading text in every book quotes something real: a published standard, a guidance document,
a journal paper, a named method, a government publication. Three rules apply without exception.

1. **The text names its document, its edition or its date, and its publisher or author.** Not "the
   standard says" but "ISO 21500:2021, which is a different document from the 2012 edition of the
   same number". An edition is part of a citation, and a project manager who cannot name one has
   not finished the sentence.
2. **The text prints what the document does not cover.** In this series that is the point. A
   vocabulary standard gives no method. A guidance document gives no form, no threshold and no
   approval limit. A sixty-year-old triangle has no corner for the people who absorb the
   difference. A 1965 review of small groups was mostly not about project teams, and its author
   never said it was. Every reading text ends with that line, and every unit's honesty note
   repeats it.
3. **Every person, team, company, project, number and incident is invented.** Lindhurst, the
   engineering and infrastructure company in these books, does not exist. Neither do its staff,
   its clients, its projects — Calder, the pump station, the depot move, Dispatch 6 — nor any of
   its delays, arguments, resignations or handover disputes. Where a unit needs a failure, that
   failure is written from the shape of the document being taught, not from any real project at
   any real organization.

**The series is method-neutral.** The sources are the published work of standards bodies, named
authors and public institutions — ISO and IEC, peer-reviewed journals, government guidance, and
openly published methods such as MoSCoW and the time–cost–quality triangle. Where a commercial
body of knowledge or a proprietary method is taught, it is named and dated as the publication it
is, and no certification, training provider or software product is recommended anywhere in the six
books.

**Money has no symbol anywhere in this series.** A cost is written as a number — 20,000, 140,000 —
and the unit is whatever currency the reader's own project uses. A number without a symbol is
readable in every country; a number with the wrong symbol is readable in none.

**This is an independent teaching draft.** It is not published or endorsed by any standards body,
institute or professional association, and it carries no official status. Nothing in it is legal,
financial or contractual advice. Where a document is quoted, it is quoted as published; where a
figure or a count is given, it carries the edition or the date of the page it came from. Every
internal rule printed in these books — *two months of amber is red*, *20,000 and two weeks*, *four
lines, not a form* — is one invented company's practice, printed because a concrete number teaches
better than an abstraction.

### Honesty notes, Book 1

- **Unit 1.** A vocabulary tells you what a word means inside one document. Eleven of twenty-nine
  entries on the invented register have no end date; they are not late projects, they are ordinary
  work with a project name on them, and no standard can stop a company doing that.
- **Unit 2.** The chart is about 130 years old and still cannot draw a condition. Every bar has the
  same confident edge whether its date is contracted or hoped for.
- **Unit 3.** The guidance will not say how small a package should be. The working answer in the
  book — small enough that somebody could be wrong about it in under a month — is the invented
  company's, not the standard's.
- **Unit 4.** Roles are named in the standard and filled in a building. Six of eighteen invented
  projects name a department as sponsor, and a department cannot be asked a question.
- **Unit 5.** Three corners held, and the thing that actually moved was in none of them: eleven
  unpaid Saturdays and an engineer who resigned in May.
- **Unit 6.** A prioritization method cannot stop anybody writing forty-one must-haves. Only a
  person can do that, out loud, in a room, by asking a different question.
- **Unit 7.** One document number, two different documents, nine years apart. The number alone
  names nothing.
- **Unit 8.** The most widely used risk tool in the world is not in ISO 31000. The unit says so,
  and says that the invented company's copy came from a 2019 training course nobody can trace.
- **Unit 9.** Four stages everybody can name, from a 1965 review of groups that were mostly not
  project teams. The paper is useful and it is not a law.
- **Unit 10.** The rule was always right. The record was made so expensive that people chose
  between helping the project and following the process, and everybody chose the project.
- **Unit 11.** Nobody lied. Four true amber reports in a row told a board nothing, because a status
  cannot report that nothing has moved.
- **Unit 12.** A definition is right inside its own covers and nowhere else. Four documents define
  *project* and none of them identically, and none of them provides the one page that actually
  governs the work.

### Honesty notes, Book 2

- **Unit 1.** The arithmetic has been exact since 1959 and has never once asked whether the
  dependencies it was handed are real. Nine activities with no predecessor hid five weeks.
- **Unit 2.** The weights in the most widely taught estimating formula in the world were chosen
  because the sums had to be done by hand in 1958.
- **Unit 3.** A satirical column about naval office staff is quoted at the people doing the work, by
  organizations whose own reporting pack has grown from four pages to thirty-one.
- **Unit 4.** The participants' own worst case was beaten on average. The paper measures the error
  and declines to supply a replacement number.
- **Unit 5.** Everything after step one is arithmetic, and step one — which projects count — is also
  the easiest place to produce the answer you wanted. The author says so himself.
- **Unit 6.** Fifty projects, one country, two decades ago, and still the most useful figure in a
  board paper, because it is the one that has to be argued away from in public.
- **Unit 7.** A book of essays about 1960s software explains both halves: why four extra detailers
  made a drawing package later, and why three crews made a fence three times faster.
- **Unit 8.** A novel. The arithmetic took an afternoon; what made it work was one sentence signed
  before anybody was asked for a number, and two welders who refused until it existed.
- **Unit 9.** A plan can contain all fourteen required sections, conform completely, and be wrong in
  every figure it holds. The only requirement a standard can test is whether a subject is
  addressed.
- **Unit 10.** The guidance asks for a benefit with a number, a date and an owner — and that owner
  is usually in another organization, will be there years after the team has dispersed, and has to
  agree to be named.
- **Unit 11.** Three projects stopped in six years, none at a review. The guidance says a portfolio
  should be balanced against capacity and says nothing whatever about the meeting.
- **Unit 12.** The best-written source in the whole book cannot be disproved by anything, which is
  exactly why it appears in the back matter and not in the plan.

### Honesty notes, Book 3

- **Unit 1.** The arithmetic of earned value is flawless, and everything uncertain has already
  happened by the time the division is done. Fourteen of thirty-eight packages scored themselves,
  twelve points high, and not one of those fourteen people was being dishonest.
- **Unit 2.** The money-based schedule index improves as a project gets later and reads 1.00 on the
  last day of the latest project in a company's history. The repair is three lines of arithmetic on
  figures the cost report already holds.
- **Unit 3.** Book 1 said the five-by-five matrix is not in ISO 31000, which is true; Unit 3 corrects
  the implication, because the matrix is in IEC 31010 as one technique among many, with its
  limitations printed beside it.
- **Unit 4.** Three hundred and eleven records about the concrete and two about how the company runs
  projects, because one of those two things can be certified and the other cannot.
- **Unit 5.** *Stakeholders remain supportive* cannot be false, which is worse than being wrong.
  Rewriting the fourth column found ninety thousand of somebody else's work in four days.
- **Unit 6.** The funding case rested on the single arrow the client's own log contradicted, and the
  project was still worth building — for a benefit nobody had written down.
- **Unit 7.** Two competent companies managed one risk in opposite directions for four months with
  nothing hidden, because the two registers had never been in the same room.
- **Unit 8.** Two hundred and twelve lessons, nine openings, no document changed. The destination
  was wrong, not the intention.
- **Unit 9.** Two numbers on a board report traced to nothing at all, and at least one reader had
  been treating one of them as a fact about resignations.
- **Unit 10.** A continuity plan read perfectly for six years with a seized valve in the middle of
  it, and two correct documents in two organizations differed by a factor of twenty-four.
- **Unit 11.** All four measures were met. The best measure in this course distorted the work within
  nine months, and the famous sentence about it is by the wrong author in its famous form.
- **Unit 12.** A clause two pages long, in a standard the company is proud of, went unread for
  eleven years, with every audit correct throughout.

## Book 4 · Throughline 3 (B1+)

This is the book of named methods, and the only one in the series whose sources were mostly written
by organizations that sell training and credentials against them. Every unit therefore asks the same
four questions of its document — who publishes it, which edition is in front of you, what does it
claim to be, and what does it say it does not cover — and prints the answers. The book is not
affiliated with, endorsed by or derived from any of the bodies whose documents it discusses, and it
is not a study guide for any examination.

### The honesty notes, Book 4

- **Unit 1.** Nothing in the 112 pages became untrue when the edition changed. Seventy-one pages
  lost their parent, and nobody noticed for four years because nobody reads a procedure forwards.
- **Unit 2.** Fourteen people certified in a method the company applies five sevenths of, and the
  two principles skipped are the two that would have reported something upwards.
- **Unit 3.** The sentence that protects documentation is **in** the sixty-eight words, which makes
  dropping four handover manuals a decision the company made and then attributed to a manifesto.
- **Unit 4.** Every method in this book expects to be tailored except the shortest one, which says in
  plain words that its parts are indivisible — and four of the eleven elements missing on site are
  the four that let somebody outside the team see what is true.
- **Unit 5.** A self-assessment measures willingness to claim. The man who wrote *fine* in nine
  boxes was second of eleven on colleague evidence, and nine of eleven could not state the strategy
  whose area they had scored.
- **Unit 6.** Fourteen tenders quoted a number nobody in the office had read, three citation steps
  from a document whose method is unpublished. *Cannot be verified* is not the same as *not true*.
- **Unit 7.** Nine hundred in week three against sixty thousand in month nine, for the same
  question — and two of the four "cycles" were laps, because a fixed-price contract left nothing to
  commit at the end of a turn.
- **Unit 8.** Four days, fourteen of sixty-one people, forty-one documents the project chose itself,
  and the uncomfortable file left in a drawer. The silence in that report is largely the project's.
- **Unit 9.** Thirty-one projects through one gate in four years with no stops, because every
  criterion was a score and a score cannot refuse. The author of the four new questions wrote the
  one that caught his own project.
- **Unit 10.** Forty-one days presenting, zero days removing. Nobody spoke in favor of the
  three-signature rule and nobody needed to, because silence is free and effective.
- **Unit 11.** Forty-eight ticked boxes, every one of them honest, and no written conclusion in three
  years — because the word in the third box asks whether we did it, not whether it worked.
- **Unit 12.** Thirty-four elements of ninety-five, with a name and a date against every omission.
  The two rows carrying a warning are the rows that make the other nine believable.

## Book 5 · Throughline 4 (B2)

Twelve units, under the title *Money, Contracts, Benefits*. The Starting Point carries no source
body. This is the book in which the series' method turns into a single habit, stated in Unit 12: in

## Book 6 · Throughline 5 (B2+ to C1)

Twelve units, under the title *Governance, Assurance, Closure*. The Starting Point carries no source
body. This is the last book of the series and the only one whose sources are mostly **arguments
rather than instructions**: six books and papers, one official commission report, one independent
review panel report, and four international standards. Two of its units — 5 and 6 — are about the
same 1986 accident: the commission that investigated it, and the study published ten years later
which argues that the commission reached for the wrong kind of explanation of the launch decision.
**Neither unit summarizes the accident, reconstructs any conversation or invents any detail**, and
neither suggests that anybody involved was careless. They are in the book because holding two serious
accounts of one event is the most advanced thing the series asks of a reader.

## The series, closed

**Six books. Seventy-two units. Seventy-two source bodies, none of them reused anywhere in
Throughline or in its four sister series.** Every reading text names its document, its edition or
date and its publisher or author, and then prints what that document says it does **not** cover.
Every person, team, company, project, number and incident across all six books is invented;
Lindhurst does not exist.

The series arrives, in six different vocabularies, at one sentence: **a document is only safe to use
once you know what it does not claim.** A class 5 estimate is not a budget. A learning curve does not
cover steel. A life-cycle cost is not a whole-life cost. A notice is not an argument. A delivery term
is silent on title. A balance sheet is not a risk register. A sample is not a population. A
certificate is not compliance. An injury rate is not process safety. And a policy with the word
*anonymous* in it is not anonymity on a site of six.
