# Throughline · sources

**English for Project Managers · International Edition.** Six books, seventy-two units,
seventy-two separate source bodies. Nothing in this series reuses a figure or a source from
Wayfinder English, from the Clearline business series, from Foreign Service English or from
Keystone.

## The rule this series is built on

Every reading text in every book quotes something real: a published standard, a guidance document,
a journal paper, a named method, a government publication. Three rules apply without exception.

1. **The text names its document, its edition or its date, and its publisher or author.** Not "the
   standard says" but "ISO 21500:2021, which is a different document from the 2012 edition of the
   same number". An edition is part of a citation, and a project manager who cannot name one has
   not finished the sentence.
2. **The text prints what the document does not cover.** In this series that is the point. A
   vocabulary standard gives no method. A guidance document gives no form, no threshold and no
   approval limit. A sixty-year-old triangle has no corner for the people who absorb the
   difference. A 1965 review of small groups was mostly not about project teams, and its author
   never said it was. Every reading text ends with that line, and every unit's honesty note
   repeats it.
3. **Every person, team, company, project, number and incident is invented.** Lindhurst, the
   engineering and infrastructure company in these books, does not exist. Neither do its staff,
   its clients, its projects — Calder, the pump station, the depot move, Dispatch 6 — nor any of
   its delays, arguments, resignations or handover disputes. Where a unit needs a failure, that
   failure is written from the shape of the document being taught, not from any real project at
   any real organization.

**The series is method-neutral.** The sources are the published work of standards bodies, named
authors and public institutions — ISO and IEC, peer-reviewed journals, government guidance, and
openly published methods such as MoSCoW and the time–cost–quality triangle. Where a commercial
body of knowledge or a proprietary method is taught, it is named and dated as the publication it
is, and no certification, training provider or software product is recommended anywhere in the six
books.

**Money has no symbol anywhere in this series.** A cost is written as a number — 20,000, 140,000 —
and the unit is whatever currency the reader's own project uses. A number without a symbol is
readable in every country; a number with the wrong symbol is readable in none.

**This is an independent teaching draft.** It is not published or endorsed by any standards body,
institute or professional association, and it carries no official status. Nothing in it is legal,
financial or contractual advice. Where a document is quoted, it is quoted as published; where a
figure or a count is given, it carries the edition or the date of the page it came from. Every
internal rule printed in these books — *two months of amber is red*, *20,000 and two weeks*, *four
lines, not a form* — is one invented company's practice, printed because a concrete number teaches
better than an abstraction.

## Book 1 · Throughline Foundation (A2)

Twelve units. The Starting Point carries no source body.

| Unit | Title | Source, with its edition or date | What the reading prints as the document's own limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Three Words That Are Not the Same | **ISO 21506:2018**, *Project, programme and portfolio management — Vocabulary* | It defines terms for use in that family of standards; it contains no requirements, no process and no method, and it does not say what a company should call its own work |
| 2 | Bars on a Wall | **Karol Adamiecki's harmonogram, 1896**; **Henry Gantt's charts, published c. 1910–1919** | Neither inventor gave the chart the meaning now hung on it; a bar shows a start and an end and has no way of showing a condition or a dependency |
| 3 | Cutting the Work Up | **ISO 21511:2018**, *Work breakdown structures for project and programme management* | Guidance, not requirements: it fixes no number of levels, no package size and no numbering scheme |
| 4 | Who Answers for It | **ISO 21502:2020**, *Project, programme and portfolio management — Guidance on project management* | Guidance, not requirements: it describes roles and practices without prescribing an organizational structure, and it names nobody in your organization |
| 5 | Three Corners and a Choice | **Martin Barnes's time–cost–quality triangle, 1969** | Its author later said the triangle was never meant to describe the whole of the job; it has no corner for the people who absorb the difference |
| 6 | Must, Should, Could, Won't | **MoSCoW prioritization, Dai Clegg, 1994**, later carried by the **DSDM** framework | It orders requirements and does not say how many must-haves a release can hold, nor what to do when a stakeholder refuses to move one |
| 7 | Stages and Gates | **ISO 21500:2021**, *Project, programme and portfolio management — Context and concepts* | The same number named a different document in 2012, so the edition is part of the citation; it is guidance on concepts and contains no stages and no gate criteria |
| 8 | What Might Happen | **ISO 31000:2018**, *Risk management — Guidelines* | Guidelines, not for certification: it contains no risk matrix, no scoring scheme and no thresholds, and the five-by-five grid most companies use is not in it at all |
| 9 | Four Stages of a New Team | **Bruce W. Tuckman**, *Developmental sequence in small groups*, **Psychological Bulletin, 1965**; fifth stage added in **Tuckman and Jensen, 1977** | A review of about fifty studies, most of them therapy and laboratory groups rather than project teams; it does not say how long a stage lasts or how to move a team through one |
| 10 | Nothing Changes Without a Request | **ISO 10007:2017**, *Quality management — Guidelines for configuration management* | Guidelines, not requirements: no form, no software, no approval limits, no definition of a significant change, and not intended for certification |
| 11 | Who Is Told, and When | **ISO 21505:2017**, *Project, programme and portfolio management — Guidance on governance* | Guidance, not requirements: no colors, no thresholds, no meeting frequency and no membership of a governing body |
| 12 | One Page for the Whole Project | **ISO 9000:2015**, *Quality management systems — Fundamentals and vocabulary* | A vocabulary and concepts document: no requirements, no templates, no project method, and not itself a certification standard |

### Honesty notes, Book 1

- **Unit 1.** A vocabulary tells you what a word means inside one document. Eleven of twenty-nine
  entries on the invented register have no end date; they are not late projects, they are ordinary
  work with a project name on them, and no standard can stop a company doing that.
- **Unit 2.** The chart is about 130 years old and still cannot draw a condition. Every bar has the
  same confident edge whether its date is contracted or hoped for.
- **Unit 3.** The guidance will not say how small a package should be. The working answer in the
  book — small enough that somebody could be wrong about it in under a month — is the invented
  company's, not the standard's.
- **Unit 4.** Roles are named in the standard and filled in a building. Six of eighteen invented
  projects name a department as sponsor, and a department cannot be asked a question.
- **Unit 5.** Three corners held, and the thing that actually moved was in none of them: eleven
  unpaid Saturdays and an engineer who resigned in May.
- **Unit 6.** A prioritization method cannot stop anybody writing forty-one must-haves. Only a
  person can do that, out loud, in a room, by asking a different question.
- **Unit 7.** One document number, two different documents, nine years apart. The number alone
  names nothing.
- **Unit 8.** The most widely used risk tool in the world is not in ISO 31000. The unit says so,
  and says that the invented company's copy came from a 2019 training course nobody can trace.
- **Unit 9.** Four stages everybody can name, from a 1965 review of groups that were mostly not
  project teams. The paper is useful and it is not a law.
- **Unit 10.** The rule was always right. The record was made so expensive that people chose
  between helping the project and following the process, and everybody chose the project.
- **Unit 11.** Nobody lied. Four true amber reports in a row told a board nothing, because a status
  cannot report that nothing has moved.
- **Unit 12.** A definition is right inside its own covers and nowhere else. Four documents define
  *project* and none of them identically, and none of them provides the one page that actually
  governs the work.
