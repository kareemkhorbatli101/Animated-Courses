# Unit 5 · Ask What Happened Last Time
%%SUB Two hundred and fifty-eight projects, seventy years, and the question of which nineteen count%%
%%GOALS
Compare numbers with data
Explain reference class forecasting in three steps
Say what a reference class cannot fix

There is a way of forecasting that does not ask you anything about your project. It asks what
happened to projects like it. It is uncomfortable, it is better than judgment, and the hardest part
is deciding which projects count as *like it*.

> **Reader's Tip** — Find the distinction the author makes between an error and a lie. The method

%%FIG pb05-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the author, the journal and the year, a three-step list, a small table of overrun figures by project type, a boxed paragraph about nineteen pump stations and two excluded rows, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 5
> *Project office · forecasting from the past · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** **Bent Flyvbjerg** published *From Nobel Prize to Project Management:
> Getting Risks Right* in the **Project Management Journal** in **August 2006**. It sets out
> **reference class forecasting** for projects, building on work in economics and psychology that
> won the **Nobel Prize in economics in 2002**.
>
> **The three steps.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **1** | identify a **reference class** of past, similar projects |
> | **2** | establish how their outcomes were **distributed** |
> | **3** | place **your** project on that distribution |
>
> **Why it was thought necessary.** An earlier study by the same author with Holm and Buhl
> examined **258** transport projects in **20** countries over about **70 years**. Average cost
> overruns, measured against the figure at the decision to build:
>
> | Type | Average overrun |
> |---|---|
> | rail | **44.7 per cent** |
> | bridges and tunnels | **33.8 per cent** |
> | roads | **20.4 per cent** |
>
> **The inaccuracy had not improved over seventy years.** Demand forecasts were no better: for rail
> projects, actual passenger numbers were on average about **half** of what had been forecast.
>
> **Error, or lie.** The author separates two causes. One is **optimism** — a genuine mistake, which
> this method corrects. The other is **strategic misrepresentation**: a figure made deliberately
> attractive so that a decision is taken. **Reference class forecasting does nothing about the
> second**, because a person willing to misrepresent a cost is also willing to choose a flattering
> reference class.
>
> **Our nineteen.**
>
> > Lindhurst has finished **19** pump stations in about **12** years and knows what each one
> > finally cost.
> >
> > Median overrun against the approved figure: **22 per cent**.
> >
> > **Two** of the nineteen hit unexpected rock and overran by more than **90 per cent**. Exclude
> > them and the median falls to **12 per cent**. Treat them as ordinary members of the class and
> > the figure a prudent sponsor should carry is nearer **38 per cent**.
> >
> > **The method is arithmetic after step one. Step one is an argument**, and the argument is about
> > whether rock is bad luck or part of building pump stations.
>
> **What the method needs, and what it cannot do.** It needs **enough finished projects** with
> **real outturns recorded** — which most organizations do not have. It gives a **distribution, not
> a cause**: it will tell you that you are likely to overrun and never tell you why. And the
> **choice of class** changes the answer, so the method moves the judgment rather than removing it.
>
> *Source: B. Flyvbjerg, From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right, Project
> Management Journal, August 2006; the overrun figures are from B. Flyvbjerg, M. S. Holm and S.
> Buhl, Underestimating Costs in Public Works Projects: Error or Lie?, Journal of the American
> Planning Association, 2002. The method requires a reference class of comparable completed
> projects with recorded outturns; it produces a distribution rather than an explanation; the
> selection of the class remains a judgment; and its author states explicitly that it does not
> address deliberate misrepresentation.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Everywhere, the most political sentence in a forecast is step one. Choosing the comparable projects
decides the answer, and everybody in the room knows which choice produces the number they want. The
2006 paper is unusually blunt about this: it separates honest optimism, which the method fixes, from
deliberate misrepresentation, which it cannot touch, because somebody willing to shade a cost is
also willing to pick a flattering class. There is only one defense, and it is not a technique. It is
writing the choice down in a sentence somebody else can attack — *we excluded two projects that had
one borehole each; we have four* — and writing beside it what it costs you if the sentence turns out
to be wrong. A forecast with a named risk is manageable. A tidier number with a silent judgment
inside it is not.

**Discuss:** Who at your company would notice if a reference class had been chosen to flatter?

> ### Cost forecast · Harbour pump station
> *From C. Nkemelu, with P. Rao · 30 May · for E. Okonjo · one page*
>
> **The approved figure is 2.4 million. I am asking the board to carry 2.7.**
>
> **The class.** **Nineteen** pump stations finished by Lindhurst in about **twelve** years, with
> recorded outturns. Median outturn: **22 per cent above** the figure approved at the gate.
>
> **The exclusion, and the argument for it.** Two of the nineteen overran by more than **90 per
> cent** after hitting unexpected rock. **Both had one borehole.** This project has **four**, across
> the footprint, and the deepest goes two meters below the chamber base. **On that basis I exclude
> the two, and the median of the remaining seventeen is 12 per cent**, which gives **2.7 million**.
>
> **What the exclusion costs us if it is wrong.** **If the four boreholes have missed a rock shelf,
> this is a 90-per-cent project and we will have argued ourselves out of the only warning the data
> gave us.** That is the single largest exposure on this job and it is not covered by the
> contingency.
>
> **What I am not claiming.** The method gives a **distribution, not a cause**. It does not say this
> station will cost 2.7; it says that stations like it have landed there. **And the paper is explicit
> that it corrects optimism and not misrepresentation** — the honesty of this note depends on the
> exclusion above, which is mine, and which I have written down so it can be argued with.
>
> **One recommendation beyond this project.** **Four of the nineteen outturns took a week to find.**
> I would like every finished project to record its final cost on the day it completes. Without
> that, this method stops working here within three years.
>

> recorded outturns, produces a distribution rather than an explanation, leaves the choice of
