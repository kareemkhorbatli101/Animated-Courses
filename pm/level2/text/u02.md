# Unit 2 · Three Numbers, Not One
%%SUB A formula from 1958, a worst case that was not one, and nineteen packages out of forty-four%%
%%GOALS
Give an estimate as a range
Use the three-point formula and say what it assumes
Say why the worst case is the least reliable number
%%

Asked for one number, most people give the one they hope for. Asked for three, they give the one
they hope for, one slightly better, and one slightly worse. The formula is sixty-seven years old and
it is only as good as the third number.

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 2
> *Project office · three-point estimates · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** PERT was built in **1958** by the **Special Projects Office** of the
> **US Navy**, with Booz Allen Hamilton and Lockheed, for the **Polaris** missile program. The
> *PERT Summary Report, Phase 1* is dated **July 1958**. The name began as *Program Evaluation and
> Research Task* and became *Program Evaluation and Review Technique*.
>
> **The formula.** Give three numbers: optimistic **a**, most likely **m**, pessimistic **b**.
>
> > **expected time = (a + 4m + b) ÷ 6**
>
> **Where the weights came from.** The report assumed a particular shape of probability curve and
> chose weights that could be worked out **by hand**, in 1958, by a room of people with slide
> rules. **It was a convenience that fitted the mathematics of the day, not a measurement of how
> projects behave.** The arithmetic is exact. The assumption under it has never been tested on a
> single Lindhurst job.
>
> **What happened on the pipework.**
>
> | | Weeks |
> |---|---|
> | optimistic | **10** |
> | most likely | **12** |
> | pessimistic | **16** |
> | expected, by the formula | **12.3** |
> | **what it actually took** | **19** |
>
> The formula also returns a spread: with 10 and 16 as the ends, the standard deviation is **one
> week**. **The overrun was seven times that.** The arithmetic was not wrong. The **16** was.
>
> **Karin's spreadsheet.**
>
> > The estimator has **thirty years** of finished packages in one file. For **44** of them, a
> > three-point estimate had been recorded at the start.
> >
> > The actual duration came in **above the stated pessimistic number** in **19** of the 44 — **more
> > than two in five.** A number that is supposed to be the worst case was beaten by reality
> > almost half the time.
> >
> > **Not one of those 19 packages was run by a careless person.** In every case, the pessimistic
> > number had been produced the same way: take the most likely, and add a bit.
>
> **One more thing the formula does not do.** Where **three** chains must all arrive before a
> milestone, and each has a **90 percent** chance of arriving on time, the milestone's chance is
> about **73 percent**, not 90. PERT computes the spread along one path. **Reality merges paths**,
> and every merge makes the date less likely while every individual estimate stays honest.
>
> *Source: PERT Summary Report, Phase 1, Special Projects Office, Bureau of Naval Weapons,
> Department of the Navy, July 1958, developed for the Polaris program with Booz Allen Hamilton and
> Lockheed. The three-point weights follow from an assumed distribution chosen for ease of hand
> computation, not from measurement; the method models the spread along a single path and does not
> account for the effect of merging paths; and the claim that PERT itself saved time on Polaris was
> questioned in H. M. Sapolsky, The Polaris System Development, Harvard University Press, 1972,
> which argued that it functioned substantially as a management and political instrument.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Three numbers are harder to give in some rooms than others. Where a manager is expected to sound
certain, a range reads as weakness, and the person who says *between five and eleven* is heard as
somebody who has not done the work. So a single number is offered, it is the one that can be said
without flinching, and everybody in the room knows it is the hopeful one. The repair is not courage.
It is the third number with a story attached, because a story cannot be dismissed as pessimism: *the
seals take three weeks to replace and then we retest* is a fact about seals, not a mood. And it is a
file of what actually happened, because the one thing that reliably stops a person adding a bit to
twelve is forty-four rows in front of them saying what twelve turned into last time.

> **For the record.** Our last pipework package was estimated **10 / 12 / 16** and took **19**. The
> formula's standard deviation on those three numbers was **one week**, so the actual was seven
> deviations out. **The arithmetic was fine. The 16 was not.** Of **44** packages in the
> estimator's file with a recorded three-point estimate, **19** came in above their own pessimistic
> number.
>
> *Source: PERT Summary Report, Phase 1, Special Projects Office, Bureau of Naval Weapons,
> Department of the Navy, July 1958. The three-point weights follow from a distribution assumed for
> ease of hand computation, not from measurement; the method models the spread along one path and
> not the effect of merging paths; and the claim that the method itself saved time on Polaris was
> questioned in H. M. Sapolsky, The Polaris System Development, Harvard University Press, 1972.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put a condition beside each of the three numbers. A range with conditions is a
> plan; a range without them is a shrug with arithmetic on it.
