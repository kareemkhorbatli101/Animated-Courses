# Throughline 1
%%SUB English for Project Managers · International Edition · CEFR A2+ to B1%%

## How to use this book

This is the second book of six. Book 1 taught the **parts** of a project. This one teaches the
hardest sentence in the job: **when will it be finished, and how do you know?** Every unit is built
on one real published document, named with its edition and its date.

**Twelve units**, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | One real standard, paper or published method, with its date and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `TL2-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**It is a book about estimates, and estimates are mostly about people.** Seven of the twelve
sources are not methods at all. They are a 1955 magazine column, a 1975 book about software, three
papers of psychology and economics, a 1997 business novel and a government guidance note. They are
in this book because the arithmetic of a schedule is easy and the reason schedules are wrong is
not.

**Three of the sources tell you that you are optimistic, and one of them measured it.** In 1994
three psychologists asked students when they would finish their theses. The students' own
best-guess dates were wrong by a wide margin, and their *worst-case* dates were wrong too. There is
no unit in this book that lets you keep believing your first number.

**Every unit still prints what its document does not cover.** A critical path tells you which dates
cannot move. It does not tell you whether anybody agreed them. A three-point estimate gives you a
range. It does not make the range honest. A standard that lists what a plan must contain says
nothing about whether the plan is true.

**Money has no symbol in this series.** Every figure is written without a currency sign — *a budget
of 240,000*, *an uplift of 41 per cent* — because the unit is whatever currency your project uses.

## The people

The same company and the same team as Book 1, one project further on.

**Lindhurst** is an engineering and services company of about nine hundred people. **Nadia Rahimi**
is the project manager. **Oskar Lindqvist** builds the schedule, and in this book he is the one who
has to defend it. **Linh Tran** controls the cost. **Ahmad Saleh** runs the work on site. **Wei
Zhao** checks the quality. **Ida Berg** keeps the risk register. **Omar Nasser** buys things.
**Hanna Virtanen** works in the project office and makes the reports. **Mariama Bah** is the
engineer. **Elena Caruso** manages the contracts. **Tunde Oyelaran** asks what the project is for.
**Ebele Okonjo** is the sponsor, and signs.

**Chidi Nkemelu** is now four months in. He has produced his first schedule, and most of this book
is what happened to it.

**Sofia Marchetti** works for the client and reads everything the team sends.

Two new voices arrive in this book. **Karin Dahl** is the company's estimator; she has thirty years
of finished projects in a spreadsheet and is the only person who ever looks at them. **Priya Rao**
is an economist the client brought in, and she asks, politely and repeatedly, what the last ten
projects like this one actually cost.
