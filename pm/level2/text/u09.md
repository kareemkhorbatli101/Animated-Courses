# Unit 9 · What a Plan Has to Contain
%%SUB Fourteen required sections, all fourteen present, and three of them one sentence long%%
%%GOALS
Use modals for documents
Say what a standard requires a plan to contain
Explain why a complete plan can still be wrong
%%

There is a standard that says what a project management plan must contain. It is useful, it is
precise, and it will never tell you whether your plan is true. Those are two different jobs, and
confusing them is how a sixty-one-page plan loses five weeks.

### 1 · Modals for documents

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD TL2-09-06%%

> **Wei:** The audit says we conform. Fourteen of fourteen.
> **Chidi:** And four of the fourteen were blank for two years.
> **Wei:** **Blank is not missing.** The heading is there, so the requirement is met. The standard
> says the plan **shall** address external dependencies; it does not say **how much** it shall say.
> **Chidi:** That seems like a hole.
> **Wei:** **It is a boundary, not a hole.** A document standard can require a subject to be
> addressed. It cannot require the content to be any good, because nobody can write that
> requirement in a testable way.
> **Chidi:** Somebody could require a minimum length.
> **Wei:** **And they would get a page of nothing**, which is worse than a blank, because it looks
> answered.
> **Chidi:** The blank section was the one with the valves in it.
> **Wei:** It was. **The standard had put the question on the page in 2019 and we did not answer it
> until April.** That is not the standard failing. **That is us failing a question we had been handed
> in writing.**
> **Chidi:** So what do I change?
> **Wei:** Two things, and neither is in the standard. **A section that is blank is reported as
> blank at every gate**, in a list, by name. And **each section gets an owner**, because fourteen
> sections with one author is how three of them end up one sentence long at midnight.
> **Chidi:** Is *shall* really that strict?
> **Wei:** **In a standard, yes.** *Shall* is a requirement, *should* is a recommendation, *may* is
> permission. **If you translate *shall* as *should*, you have changed the document**, and I have
> seen that happen twice in tender responses.
> **Chidi:** And *must*?
> **Wei:** **Standards prefer *shall*.** In our own plan, write *must* if you like, but write it only
> where you mean it, because somebody will hold you to the word and not to your intention.

*What two changes does Wei want, and why are they not in the standard?*

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **shall** *(requirement)* | The plan **shall** identify external dependencies. |
| **should** *(recommendation)* | The plan **should** list its assumptions. |

> **Grammar Note** — *Need not* and *shall not* are opposites, not variants. *Need not* means it is

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A conformance statement is one of the most comfortable sentences in professional life, in any
language: *the plan conforms*. It is true, it is checkable, and it ends a conversation. The trouble
is that it answers a question nobody actually asked — whether the required subjects appear — while
the question in the room was whether the plan is any good. Standards bodies know this perfectly
well; they write requirements that can be tested, because a requirement that cannot be tested is
worse than none. So the honest habit has to be added by the people using the document, and it is
one sentence long: never say *we conform* without saying what is thin. The audit that found four
blank sections took one afternoon, and the five weeks it would have saved had been sitting under a
heading somebody had already been told to write.

**Discuss:** At your company, who would be allowed to say a conforming plan is not finished?

> | 10 Decision management | **one sentence** | N. Rahimi | **14 July** |
> | 12 Configuration management | **blank since 2023** | H. Virtanen | **28 July** |
>
> **What section 5 should have contained.** The valve order **cannot be placed until drawing
> 1.2-04 is approved** (week 9); the lead time is **four weeks**; therefore the pressure test
> **cannot begin before week 17**. **That is the five weeks found in April.** The standard put that
> question on the page in 2019. **We had the heading and not the content, and a heading is not a
> warning.**
>
> **What conformance does and does not say.** It says the required **subjects are addressed**. It
> says **nothing** about whether the dates, costs or assumptions in the plan are **true**, and it
> sets **no minimum length**. **A plan can conform in full and be wrong in every number it
> contains.**
>
> **Two changes, neither of them required by the standard.** **Every section now has a named
> owner.** And **every gate paper will list the blank or one-sentence sections by name**, so that
> *the plan conforms* is never again said on its own.
>
> *Source: ISO/IEC/IEEE 16326:2019, Systems and software engineering &mdash; Life cycle processes
> &mdash; Project management, second edition, replacing the 2009 edition. It specifies the content
> of a project management plan; it prescribes no length, format, tool or level of detail; it is
> written for systems and software projects and requires tailoring for others; and conformance
> concerns the document's content, not the accuracy of the plan it describes.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put *it is not finished* on its own line, directly under the conformance
> statement. Anywhere else in the document, it will be read after the reader has already relaxed.
