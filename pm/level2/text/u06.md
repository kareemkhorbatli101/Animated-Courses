# Unit 6 · The Number You Add on Purpose
%%SUB An upper bound of forty-four percent, one word that moves it to sixty-six, and a number nobody may spend%%
%%GOALS
Talk about percentages and increases
Explain an optimism bias uplift
Say why an uplift is not a contingency

There is a kind of number you add to a forecast because you know, before you start, that the
forecast is too low. It is not a guess and it is not a secret. It is published, it has an upper
bound, and the hardest word in it is *standard*.

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **increase by** *(the change)* | It increases **by** 1.06 million. |
| **increase to** *(the level)* | It increases **to** 3.46 million. |
| **reduce from … to** | Reduced **from** 44 **to** 12 percent. |

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A published uplift is a strange, useful thing: a number from outside the room that nobody present
can be blamed for. That is its real value in most organizations. Your own data is better evidence
and carries less authority, because nobody outside the company has any reason to believe it, and the
person presenting it is the person who wants the project approved. So the published figure becomes
the thing you argue away from, in writing, and the argument is the part a board can check. The two
ways this goes wrong are the same everywhere. A project gets quietly relabeled as *standard*,
because one word is cheaper than an argument. And an uplift gets spent, because it is sitting in the
paper looking exactly like money, and nobody wrote the word beside it.

**Discuss:** Who at your company would be allowed to call a project non-standard?

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Cost paper · Harbour pump station · optimism bias
> *From C. Nkemelu · 6 June · for the board, 24 June · one page*
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **1 The base** | Engineer's estimate: **2.40 million**. |
> | **2 The upper bound** | Published guidance, **standard civil engineering**: **44 percent**, taking it **to 3.46 million**. |
> | **3 The reduction** | **To 12 percent — 2.70 million** — on two pieces of evidence: **19** finished Lindhurst stations at a **median of 22 percent**, and **four boreholes** on this site where the two worst of the nineteen had **one** each. |
> | **4 The warning** | **The 300,000 above the estimate is not contingency. It may not be spent.** |
>
> **The word *standard*.** The upper bound for **non-standard** civil engineering is **66 percent**,
> a difference of about **530,000** on this estimate. This station has a chamber design Lindhurst
> has not built before, so **an honest person could call it either**. **I have chosen standard. I am
> recording that I chose it**, and I will answer the chamber question at the gate.
>
> **What this costs us if I am wrong.** **If the boreholes have missed a rock shelf, this is a
> 90-per-cent project**, like two of the nineteen, and the reduction above will look like the only
> warning we were given and ignored.
>
> **Why we do not simply use 22 percent.** It is our own figure and nobody outside the company has
> a reason to believe it. **The published 44 is the number I have to argue away from, in writing,
> and that argument is the part the board can check.**
>
> **One precedent.** In **2021** an uplift of **180,000** was spent — **174,000** of it — on a
> switchgear change that was properly approved. **The paper that year said *allowance*, not
> *uplift*, and five people read past the gap.**
>
> *Source: supplementary guidance on optimism bias issued with a government treasury's appraisal
> green book, first published 2003, based on a 2002 review of fifty large public procurement
> projects. The percentages are upper bounds intended as a starting point, to be reduced only with
> evidence of mitigation; they derive from one country's public projects of that period; the
> guidance addresses appraisal rather than delivery, does not replace risk analysis, and offers no
> test of whether a project is standard.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Put the warning in the same table as the number. A caution three paragraphs
> later is read by nobody, and the 2021 paper proves it.

### 3 · Write

Write **90 to 110 words**: an uplift paper.

**Give the base figure. Give the published upper bound and the level it takes you to, using *by* and
*to*. Give the reduction and the evidence for it. Say what it costs you if the evidence is wrong. End
with the warning that the uplift may not be spent, and one sentence on where the percentages came
from.**

**Read your paper aloud to your partner first.** Then check your own against the list, and swap.

accident?
