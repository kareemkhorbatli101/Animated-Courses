# Starting Point · A Date, and What Is Behind It
%%SUB Four things every date rests on, and what happens when one of them is missing%%
%%GOALS
Review the English of Book 1
Say what a date depends on
Separate an estimate from a promise
%%

Anybody can say a date. The useful question is what the date rests on, and there are only four
things it can rest on. This book takes them one at a time.

### 1 · Six things you brought from Book 1

%%FIG pb00-f1 | A single review sheet with six boxes. Each box has a heading — part and whole, accountable for, comparatives, modals of priority, will and by, and the passive — and one short example sentence from a planning meeting.%%

**1A  Read the six examples aloud with a partner. Which two did you actually use at work?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Part and whole** | The branch **is divided into** four packages. |
| **Accountable for** | Ebele **is accountable for** the result. |
| **Comparatives** | Faster is **more expensive**. |
| **Modals of priority** | It **must** be in the first handover. |
| **Will and by** | It **will be** finished **by** the 8th. |
| **The passive** | The money **has been spent**. |

### 2 · The four things a date can rest on

%%FIG pb00-f2 | Four stacked boxes, each with a heading and one line of smaller type, under a single date written at the top; an arrow at the foot points to a line reading take one box away and the date is a wish.%%

**2A  Read the four. Then say which one your last date rested on.**

| What the date rests on | What that means |
|---|---|
| **1 Somebody did it before** | you have finished this work, measured it, and kept the number |
| **2 Somebody who will do it agreed** | the person holding the tool said the date out loud |
| **3 Arithmetic** | the work was broken down, added up, and the sum is the date |
| **4 Somebody needs it** | the contract, the season or the client needs it on that day |

The fourth is not an estimate. **It is a requirement wearing an estimate's clothes**, and more than
half the dates in most plans are that one.

**2B  Listen.** %%AUD TL2-00-02%%

### 3 · An estimate and a promise are different sentences

%%FIG pb00-f3 | Two cards side by side. The left card is headed an estimate and carries a range and a condition; the right card is headed a promise and carries one date, a name and a signature line.%%

**3A  Read the two cards. Which one has your name on it at work?**

| An estimate | A promise |
|---|---|
| "Between 10 and 16 weeks, most likely 12." | "The 14th of June." |
| It can be wrong without anybody breaking a word. | It cannot. |
| It gets better with data. | It gets kept, or it does not. |
| It belongs to the person doing the work. | It belongs to whoever signed. |

Both sentences are needed. **The trouble starts when an estimate is written down as a promise by a
third person who was not in the room.**

### 4 · One number is almost never the answer

%%FIG pb00-f4 | Two speech bubbles, one above the other. The first gives a single date. The second gives a best case, a most likely case and a worst case, with one line beneath saying which of the three the speaker would sign.%%

**4A  Read the two answers. Then discuss: which one would you rather receive?**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Answer 1** | "Twelve weeks." |
| **Answer 2** | "Ten weeks if the valves are early, twelve if they come as ordered, sixteen if they slip. I would sign thirteen." |

Answer 2 is four times as long and about ten times as useful. **It says what the speaker is worried
about**, which is the only part of an estimate anybody can act on.
