> ## Lindhurst · project office note 3
> *Project office · work and the time available · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** *Parkinson's Law* appeared in **The Economist** on **19 November 1955**.
> The author, **C. Northcote Parkinson**, was a **naval historian**. It was later the first chapter
> of his book, published by John Murray in **1958**.
>
> > **Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion.**
>
> **What the column was actually about.** Not tasks. **Office staff.** Parkinson's evidence was the
> British Admiralty between **1914** and **1928**.
>
> | | 1914 | 1928 |
> |---|---|---|
> | capital ships | **62** | **20** |
> | officers and men | **146,000** | **100,000** |
> | Admiralty officials | **about 2,000** | **about 3,569** |
>
> **The fleet got smaller and the office got bigger**, and he put the growth in officials at
> **78 per cent**. He offered two reasons, which he called almost axiomatic: **an official wants to
> multiply subordinates, not rivals**, and **officials make work for each other**.
>
> **What it is, and what it is not.** It is **satire** in a weekly magazine, written by a historian,
> with real statistics in it. **It is not a measured law of how long work takes.** The famous
> picture of an elderly lady spending a whole day on one postcard is an illustration, not data.
> Parkinson never studied a project and never claimed to.
>
> **How it gets misused.** It is quoted to justify **cutting a deadline in half**. The column does
> not say that a task can be done in any time you choose. It says that **an organization will find
> work to fill whatever time and staff it has** — which is an argument about administration, not
> about welding.
>
> **Our own version of it.**
>
> > Our monthly reporting pack was **4 pages** in **2022**. It is **31 pages** now. Over the same
> > three years the project team went from **12 people to 8**.
> >
> > Nobody decided this. Each page was added by somebody sensible, for a reason, usually after a
> > question nobody could answer at a meeting. **Not one page has ever been removed.**
> >
> > **Pages read by the board, measured in February: six.**
>
> **And the honest other half.**
>
> > We cut three packages to shorter timeboxes last year to test the idea. **Two finished on time
> > and well.** The third was the harbour survey, which came back thin: the surveyor did what the
> > time allowed and we paid for a second visit in October. **Squeezing a timebox did not make that
> > work faster. It made it worse, more expensive, and twice.**
>
> *Source: C. Northcote Parkinson, Parkinson's Law, The Economist, 19 November 1955; later the
> opening chapter of Parkinson's Law: The Pursuit of Progress, John Murray, 1958. It is a satirical
> essay by a naval historian whose evidence concerns the growth of administrative staff, not the
> duration of tasks; it offers no measurement of project work, and it does not claim that work can
> be completed in any period chosen for it.*

### 1 · The zero conditional

**1A  Listen and read. Then answer the question below.** %%AUD TL2-03-06%%

> **Hanna:** The pack is thirty-one pages. Can I cut it?
> **Nadia:** **Which pages do you want to remove?**
> **Hanna:** Pages eleven to nineteen. The supplier tables.
> **Nadia:** Omar asked for those in 2023 after a meeting where nobody could say which orders were
> late.
> **Hanna:** I know. **And if I remove them, somebody will ask that question again in eighteen
> months and we will add eleven pages instead of nine.**
> **Nadia:** That is the law in one sentence. **If you take out a page, the question comes back; if
> the question comes back, somebody adds two.**
> **Hanna:** So we are stuck.
> **Nadia:** We are not stuck. **We are just not allowed to do it quietly.** Removing a page needs
> the same thing as adding one: a name and a reason, written down.
> **Hanna:** Nobody has ever written a reason for adding one.
> **Nadia:** **No. And that is why the pack grows in only one direction.** Adding is free and
> removing needs courage.
> **Hanna:** Six pages are read.
> **Nadia:** Six are read by the board. **Pages twenty to twenty-four are read by the client's
> engineer and nobody here knows that**, because she reads them quietly and never asks us anything.
> **Hanna:** So she would notice.
> **Nadia:** She would notice in November and say nothing until February. **Tends to be how it
> goes.**
> **Hanna:** Then what do I cut?
> **Nadia:** **Ask the eleven people who receive it which page they would miss.** Whatever nobody
> names, we remove, with both our names on the note.

*Why does Nadia say the pack grows in only one direction?*

**1B  Underline three zero conditionals and one *tends to* in 1A.**

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **if** + present, present | **If** you give it four weeks, it **takes** four weeks. |
| **if** + present, present *(negative)* | **If** nobody **names** a page, we **remove** it. |
| **whenever** | **Whenever** a question is asked, a page is added. |
| **tends to** | Work **tends to** fill the time available. |
| **as a rule** | **As a rule**, a pack grows in one direction. |
| **generally / usually** | The board **generally** reads six pages. |

> **Grammar Note** — The zero conditional uses the **present** in both halves: *if you give it four
> weeks, it takes four weeks*. Using *will* in the second half turns a general truth into a
> prediction about one case, which is a weaker claim and usually not what you mean.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Everywhere, adding a page is free and removing one takes nerve. The page was asked for by somebody
real, after a meeting where a question could not be answered, and taking it out means saying that
the question no longer matters — which nobody can prove and everybody can be blamed for. So packs
grow in one direction, in every country, under every method, and the famous line from 1955 gets
quoted at the wrong target: at the welder's four weeks rather than at the thirty-one pages. The
honest version is harder in both directions. A round number in a plan deserves to be challenged,
and so does a halved deadline, because the three experiments most companies never run would show
what this one did: squeezing time sometimes produces speed and sometimes produces a thinner job you
pay for twice.

**Discuss:** Who at your company is allowed to remove a page?

> could not answer, and **we paid for a second visit**. The work did not go faster. **It went
> thinner, and then it went twice.**
>
> **The honest other half.** Of **three** timeboxes we shortened last year, **two** finished on time
> and well. **Shortening is not always wrong.** It is wrong when nobody names what will be thinner,
> and in October nobody did.
>
> **What I am asking for.** Keep **four weeks**, and let me move the **inspector booking** to
> **today** rather than week 30. **That removes four of the twenty days** without removing any of
> the work, and it is the only compression on this task that costs nothing.
>
> *Source: C. Northcote Parkinson, Parkinson's Law, The Economist, 19 November 1955; later the
> opening chapter of Parkinson's Law: The Pursuit of Progress, John Murray, 1958. It is a satirical
> essay whose statistics concern the growth of administrative staff rather than the duration of
> tasks, and it does not claim that work can be completed in any period chosen for it.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Split a duration into **work** and **named waiting**. A four-week task nobody
> can defend becomes twelve days of work plus eight days with somebody else's name on them, and
> most of the compression is in the waiting.

**1D  Circle the correct word.**

1. The survey is (twelve / twenty) days of actual work.
2. Shortening it means (one / two) dives.
3. Of three shortened timeboxes, (two / three) worked.
4. Booking the inspector today removes (four / twelve) days.
