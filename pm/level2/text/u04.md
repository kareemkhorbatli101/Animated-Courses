> ## Lindhurst · project office note 4
> *Project office · why the first number is always short · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** **Roger Buehler**, **Dale Griffin** and **Michael Ross** published
> *Exploring the "planning fallacy"* in the **Journal of Personality and Social Psychology** in
> **1994**. The **name** is older: **Kahneman and Tversky** used it in **1979**.
>
> **What they did.** They asked students to predict when they would finish a real piece of work —
> their thesis — and also to give a best case and a worst case. Then they recorded what happened.
>
> | | Days, on average |
> |---|---|
> | if everything went **as well as possible** | **about 27** |
> | their **realistic** prediction | **about 34** |
> | if everything went **as badly as possible** | **about 49** |
> | **what it actually took** | **about 55** |
>
> **Read the last two rows again.** The average actual was longer than the average **worst case**.
> About **three in ten** finished by the date they themselves had predicted.
>
> **What the paper suggests is happening.** People predict by building a story about **this** task:
> the steps, the plan, the week ahead. They do not predict by asking what happened the last four
> times they did something like it. The first way is the **inside view**; the second is the
> **outside view**, and it is almost never used because it feels like it ignores everything you know
> about your own job.
>
> **Our own thirty.**
>
> > The estimator's file holds **30** dates that somebody at Lindhurst stated **in writing**, at the
> > time, for a package that later finished.
> >
> > **Nine** were met. **Twenty-one** were not. The average overrun on the twenty-one was
> > **38 percent**.
> >
> > In **17** of the 30, a stated worst case exists. **In 11 of those 17 the work took longer than
> > the worst case.** The same shape as the paper, in a different country and a different industry.
>
> **The one thing that changed anybody's answer.**
>
> > We tried asking the question two ways on the same afternoon with the same eight people.
> >
> > *How long will the control panel take?* — **average answer: five weeks.**
> >
> > *How long did the last four control panels take?* — **average answer: eight weeks.**
> >
> > **Nobody changed their mind. They answered a different question.**
>
> **What the paper does not do.** The participants were **students**, mostly on **academic work**,
> predicting **their own single task**. It is not a study of project teams, of contractors or of
> construction. It measures the error and **does not tell you what number to use instead**: it
> reports how wrong people are, not how to be right.
>
> *Source: R. Buehler, D. Griffin and M. Ross, Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people
> underestimate their task completion times, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1994;
> the term was introduced by D. Kahneman and A. Tversky in 1979. The study measured individuals
> predicting their own academic tasks, not project teams; its explanation in terms of inside and
> outside views is an interpretation; and it offers no corrected figure to use in place of a
> prediction.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The outside question is uncomfortable in every language, because it answers *how long will your work
take* with *what happened to other people*, and that sounds like an insult to craft. In workplaces
where a manager is expected to know their own job, asking for the last four durations can read as a
check on competence rather than on arithmetic. The way round it is to ask for the file rather than
for an opinion, and to ask it of yourself first and out loud: *my plan says five and my plan has
said five three times*. That sentence costs a little pride and buys a great deal of credibility,
and it leaves judgment exactly one job, which is the job a file cannot do — saying which of the four
finished panels was genuinely unusual, and why this one is like it.

**Discuss:** How would you ask a colleague for the last four durations without it sounding like a
check on them?

> **eleven**. In April I said it was nearly done and I believed it; it was 80 percent drawn and the
> last 20 percent was the part containing her comments. **I would have written six again in March**,
> which is the whole reason I am now reading the file instead of my own plan.
>
> **The question back.** If eight is too long, **tell me which of those four panels was unusual**. I
> have looked and I cannot find it. **Of thirty dates recorded in Karin's file, nine were met**, and
> of the seventeen with a stated worst case, **eleven took longer than that worst case**.
>
> *Source: R. Buehler, D. Griffin and M. Ross, Exploring the "planning fallacy", Journal of
> Personality and Social Psychology, 1994; the term was introduced by Kahneman and Tversky in 1979.
> The study measured individuals predicting their own academic tasks, not project teams, and it
> offers no corrected figure to use in place of a prediction.*

> **Writer's Tip** — End with the question back. *Tell me which of those four was unusual* is
> polite, specific, and hands the work of disagreeing to the person who wants a shorter number.
