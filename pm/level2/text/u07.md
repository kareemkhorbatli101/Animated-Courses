# Unit 7 · Adding People to a Late Project
%%SUB Six people became ten, fifteen conversations became forty-five, and the fence went twice as fast%%
%%GOALS
Use *the more … the more*
Explain why adding people can make work later
Say which work this does not apply to
%%

When a job is late, the first instinct everywhere is to put more people on it. Sometimes that works
perfectly. The difference between the two cases is the most useful thing in this unit, and a book
from 1975 explains it better than anything written since.

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 7
> *Project office · adding people · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** **Frederick P. Brooks Jr.** published *The Mythical Man-Month* with
> Addison-Wesley in **1975**. He had managed the development of a large operating system, and the
> book is a set of **essays from that experience**. An anniversary edition in **1995** added further
> chapters. **The book is about software, and it says so on every page.**
>
> > **Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later.**
>
> **Why.** Three reasons, and the third is the one people forget.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **1 the work must be divided** | and some of it cannot be |
> | **2 the new people must be trained** | by the people who are already behind |
> | **3 everybody must talk to everybody** | and that grows much faster than the head count |
>
> **The third reason, in numbers.** Every pair of people is a link.
>
> | People | Links between them |
> |---|---|
> | 3 | **3** |
> | 6 | **15** |
> | 10 | **45** |
> | 15 | **105** |
>
> **Add four people to a team of six and the conversations triple.**
>
> **What happened on the drawings.**
>
> > The drawing package was **three weeks late** with **six** people on it. **Four** were added.
> >
> > The six who were already there spent most of the first week explaining which revision was
> > current and why the chamber had been redesigned in March.
> >
> > The package slipped a further **two weeks** before it recovered. **Nobody added was weak.** Two
> > of them were the best detailers in the company.
>
> **And the fence.**
>
> > The same month, the yard fencing was given **three crews** instead of **one**. It finished in
> > **a third** of the time, almost exactly as expected.
> >
> > **Nobody building a fence needs to know what another crew decided.** The work divides into
> > pieces that do not talk to each other, and no training is needed because every crew has built a
> > fence.
> >
> > **This is the distinction the book is actually about.** Not *people never help*, but: **work
> > that partitions without communication behaves completely differently from work that does
> > not.**
>
> **The unit itself is the problem.** A **man-month** treats people and months as interchangeable —
> *twelve man-months* sounds like twelve people for one month or one person for twelve. For a fence
> it is nearly true. For a drawing package it is not true at all, and the author's point is that the
> **unit hides the difference**.
>
> **What the book does not do.** It is **essays from experience**, not a measured study, and it
> concerns **software development** in the 1960s and 1970s. It gives **no formula** for how many
> people a task should have, **no measurement** of the training cost, and it does not say that
> adding people is always wrong — only that for connected work it is not the obvious remedy it
> appears to be.
>
> *Source: F. P. Brooks Jr., The Mythical Man-Month: Essays on Software Engineering,
> Addison-Wesley, 1975; anniversary edition 1995. It is a set of essays drawn from the author's
> experience of large software development, not a controlled study; its claims are stated for

> one person who owns them.

**1C  Study the chart.**

| | Example |
|---|---|
| **the more … the more** | **The more** people, **the more** explaining. |
| **the more … the less** | **The more** we add, **the less** each produces. |
| **the fewer … the sooner** | **The fewer** you give me, **the sooner** you get something. |

> **Grammar Note** — Both halves of *the more … the more* take **the** + comparative, and neither
> half is a question: *the more people, the more links*. English does not put *will* in the first
> half: not *the more people we will add*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Refusing help is socially difficult everywhere, and in many workplaces it is read as pride: a
manager offers four people, the team says no, and what the room hears is *we would rather be late
than admit we need anybody*. The way through is arithmetic and an offer. Fifteen pairs becoming
forty-five is not an opinion, and two days of a detailer per new joiner is a cost anybody can check.
Then comes the part that makes the refusal acceptable: name something you would accept. A whole task
cut off with one owner is help; four extra people inside one connected argument is company. And it
is worth saying the other half out loud too, because a team that always refuses people loses the
right to be believed: the fence took three crews and finished in a third of the time, and the same
book explains both.

**Discuss:** How would you refuse offered help without sounding proud?

> weeks behind, **per person**, and it cannot be delegated to the other new people.

>
