# Unit 1 · What Cannot Start Until
%%SUB Sixty-one activities, a critical path of fourteen, and nine things that started on day one%%
%%GOALS
Say what depends on what
Explain float, and why nobody watches it
Say what the critical path does not tell you
%%

A schedule is not a list of dates. It is a list of things that cannot start until other things have
finished, and the dates are what comes out of that. Get the order wrong and every date is wrong
together, politely, in the same direction.

%%FIG pb01-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the two authors, the conference and the year, a small network diagram of nine boxes with one chain drawn heavily, a boxed paragraph about nine activities with no predecessor, a short table of float, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Lindhurst · project office note 1
> *Project office · the critical path · page 1 of 1*
>
> **Where it comes from.** The critical path method was developed at **DuPont** from about **1957**
> by **Morgan R. Walker**, with **James E. Kelley Jr.** of **Remington Rand**. Their paper,
> *Critical-Path Planning and Scheduling*, was presented at the **Eastern Joint Computer
> Conference** in **December 1959**. It is older than almost every planning tool sold today, and
> every one of them is still doing the arithmetic in that paper.
>
> **What it does.** You give it a list of activities, a duration for each, and what each one cannot
> start until. It returns the longest route through the network. That route is the **critical
> path**, and its length is the **shortest** time the project can take. Everything not on it has
> **float**: spare time before it delays anything.
>
> **What came out of Chidi's first schedule.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | activities | **61** |
> | on the critical path | **14** |
> | with no predecessor at all | **9** |
> | project duration returned | **34 weeks** |
>
> **The nine.**
>
> > Nine activities in that file had **no predecessor**. In the software, an activity with no
> > predecessor starts on **day one**. So the file showed site clearance, the valve order, the
> > training plan and **the as-built drawings** all starting in week one.
> >
> > **Nobody believed the as-built drawings would be written in week one.** The file was not lying
> > and Chidi was not careless. **The file said what it had been told, and it had been told nothing
> > about those nine.**
> >
> > When each of the nine was given a predecessor, **the duration moved from 34 weeks to 39**, and
> > the critical path left the pipework chain entirely.
>
> **Float, and who watches it.**
>
> | Activity | Float | Watched weekly? |
> |---|---|---|
> | yard fencing | **41 days** | **yes, every week** |
> | valve delivery | **0 days** | no |
> | panel wiring | **2 days** | no |
>
> **We spent a year watching the fence.** It was visible, it was outside the window, and it could
> have been late by two months without moving the handover by a day. Meanwhile the valve order,
> with no float at all, was nobody's agenda item.
>
> **What the method does not do.** It takes **one number** per activity and asks nothing about where
> that number came from. It has **no view on uncertainty** — that came later, from a different
> project. In its original form it says nothing about **who is available** to do the work. And
> **critical** means *has no float*, not *risky*: a two-day activity on the path can be more
> dangerous than a ten-week one with a month of float.
>
> *Source: J. E. Kelley Jr. and M. R. Walker, Critical-Path Planning and Scheduling, Proceedings of
> the Eastern Joint Computer Conference, December 1959, from work begun at DuPont around 1957. The
> method computes the longest route from the durations and dependencies it is given; it treats each
> duration as a single fixed number, models no uncertainty, took no account of resource
> availability in its original form, and makes no judgment about whether the dependencies it is
> given are real.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The arithmetic of a critical path is the same in every country, and the thing that defeats it is the
same everywhere too: a dependency is a statement about another person, and saying it out loud can
sound like an accusation. *The drawing cannot be approved until Ebele is back* is a neutral
sentence about a calendar, and in many offices it is heard as a complaint about Ebele. So planners
learn to leave those links out, the file fills with activities that start on day one, and the
arithmetic returns a number that is too short by exactly the amount nobody wanted to say. The 1959
method cannot help with this. It does the sum it is given, perfectly, in a second. The skill the
method needs from you is social: being able to say *this waits for you* without it sounding like
*this is your fault*.

**Discuss:** How do you say *this waits for you* politely in your language?

> which is **week nine**. The lead time is **four weeks**, so they arrive in **week seventeen**. The
> pressure test **depends on** the valves, and commissioning **cannot start until** the test
> **passes** — not until it is finished, which on this job is **eleven days** apart.
>
> **What I am asking for.** **Either** approve **38,000** for the expedited valve order, which
> returns about **three weeks**, **or** approve **week 39** as the handover date. **I recommend the
> second**, because the client's own commissioning engineer is unavailable until week 37 in any
> case.
>
> **One thing for the file.** The fence has **41 days of float** and has been on the weekly agenda
> since October. The valves have **zero** and have never been on it. **I would like to swap them.**
>
> *Source: J. E. Kelley Jr. and M. R. Walker, Critical-Path Planning and Scheduling, Eastern Joint
> Computer Conference, December 1959, from work begun at DuPont around 1957. The method computes the
> longest route from the durations and dependencies it is given; it treats each duration as a single
> fixed number, models no uncertainty, and makes no judgment about whether the dependencies are
> real.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Write *found*, not *added*. The weeks were in the work before they were in the
> file, and the verb decides whether the reader hears a discovery or a mistake.
