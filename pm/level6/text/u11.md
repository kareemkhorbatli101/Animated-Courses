# Unit 11 · Who Can Say It
%%SUB Forty-one reports, then nine, then four — and the person who was right eleven months early%%
%%GOALS
Ask restorative questions as well as retributive ones
Say who was in a position to know
Say where the argument does not reach
%%

A book first published in 2007 argues that an organization faced with a failure can ask two quite
different sets of questions, and that which set it asks determines whether anybody tells it anything
next year. Our own reporting line received forty-one reports in its first year, nine in its second
and four in its third. One person was disciplined after a report in between.

> ## Lindhurst · quality note 11
> *Quality · forty-one, nine, four · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The book.** **S. Dekker**, *Just Culture*, first published **2007**, with later editions. It is
> **an argument**, not a standard: nothing in it can be audited, and it is **contested** by people who
> hold that accountability requires consequences.
>
> **Its central distinction: two sets of questions.**
>
> | **Retributive** | **Restorative** |
> |---|---|
> | Which rule was broken? | **Who is hurt?** |
> | Who broke it? | **What do they need?** |
> | How serious was the breach? | **Whose obligation is it to meet that need?** |
> | What sanction follows? | |
>
> **The second set is not the soft version.** **An obligation is a demand, usually on the
> organization, and usually more expensive than a reprimand.** The argument is also **not** that
> nobody should ever face a consequence; it is that **which set of questions you ask determines what
> you are told next year.**
>
> **It also makes a point about the line between acceptable and unacceptable behavior:** that the
> difficulty is less **where the line is** than **who draws it**, since different parties — a
> regulator, a manager, a colleague, a court — will draw it in different places and all of them will
> be sure.
>
> **Our evidence: three years of a reporting line.**
>
> | Year | Reports received |
> |---|---|
> | **1** | **41** |
> | **2** | **9** |
> | **3** | **4** |
>
> **Between year one and year two, one person was disciplined following a report.** Not for
> reporting: **for something the report revealed about his own work.** **The decision was procedurally
> correct and I can find no fault in how it was taken.** **Every person on that site drew the obvious
> conclusion, and they were not wrong to.**
>
> **The report that went nowhere, which I consider the more serious of the two findings.**
>
> > **Eleven months before 14 June, N. Haddad reported that the standby interlock's test record had a
> > gap: one annual test with no signature.**
> >
> > **She was told it was outside her remit.** It was.
> >
> > **The interlock is the device that stopped the event on 14 June.**
> >
> > **Nothing whatever happened to her.** No sanction, no acknowledgment, no reply. **Her report
> > entered a system and stopped.** **That is worse than a sanction**, because a sanction at least
> > tells you that somebody read it.
>
> **The two rule-breaches earlier in this book, through both sets of questions.**
>
> | | Retributive | Restorative |
> |---|---|---|
> | the operator who **tripped without authority** | breached the emergency procedure | **nobody hurt**; he needed **written authority**; the obligation was ours and is now discharged |
> | the fitter with a **drawer of paper waivers** | departed from the records procedure | **nobody hurt**; he needed **a system that does not log him out in four minutes**; the obligation is ours and is not discharged |
>
> **And the case where I am not going to pretend the argument reaches.**
>
> > **In 2019 a test record was deliberately falsified and the person responsible was dismissed.**
> > **I supported that decision then and I support it now.**
> >
> > **Neither this book nor I can tell you exactly where the line sits**, and the book is candid that
> > the line is drawn by somebody rather than discovered. **What I can tell you is what our report
> > numbers did after the only other disciplinary case in six years.**
>
> **What the argument does not do.** It is **an argument and not a procedure**: it offers **no test
> for culpability**, no threshold for recklessness, and **no method for the genuinely hard case.** It
> is **contested**, and the opposing position — that accountability without consequence is not
> accountability — is held by serious people. **Restorative practice requires structures and time that
> we do not currently have**: somebody has to convene the people affected and follow through on
> obligations, and that is a job nobody here does. **And it cannot make a reporting line work. Only a
> reply can do that**, and in Haddad's case there was none.
>
> *Source: S. Dekker, Just Culture, first published 2007, with subsequent editions. It distinguishes a
> retributive response to failure — which rule was broken, by whom, how seriously, and what sanction
> follows — from a restorative response, which asks who is hurt, what they need, and whose obligation
> it is to meet that need, and argues that the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable behavior
> is drawn by particular parties rather than discovered. It is an argument rather than a standard,
> offers no test of culpability, and is contested.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

A reporting line is destroyed by one case and a thousand silences. The case gets noticed — one
correct, procedurally sound disciplinary decision following a report, and every person on the site
learns something true about the cost of writing things down. The silences do not get noticed at all,
and they do more damage: a report that receives no reply teaches its author, and everybody she talks
to, that the system files things rather than reading them. Both effects are measurable in one number
that almost nobody tracks, which is how many reports arrive this year compared with last. The second
thing worth holding is that the restorative questions are not the soft option. *Who is hurt, what do
they need, whose obligation is it* places a demand on the organization, with a name and a date on it,
and a reprimand places a demand on nobody but the person reprimanded. That is why the first set is so
much more popular, and it has nothing to do with rigor.

**Discuss:** How many reports did your system receive last year, and the year before?

> ### Reply to report R-417, eleven months late
> *From W. Zhao · 19 December · to N. Haddad, copied to E. Okonjo and the quality file*
>
> **Your report of 12 July last year was correct. The standby interlock's test record contains one
> annual test with no signature. That interlock is the device that prevented the event of 14 June, and
> nobody replied to you for seventeen months.**
>
> **On remit.** **You were told the interlock was outside your remit, and that was accurate.** **What
> it means is that our remit boundaries are wrong, not that your judgment was.** **There is at present
> no route by which a person who notices something outside their area can be sure of a reply, and your
> report is the evidence.**
>
> **The three questions, answered.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Who is hurt?** | **Nobody.** The event of 14 June injured no one and the interlock worked. |
> | **What is needed?** | **A signed annual test record for the interlock**, and **a route for reports that cross remits.** |
> | **Whose obligation is it?** | **Mine**, for both, **by 1 September**. I will write to you again when each is done. |
>
> **What I am not going to write.** **That this would have prevented the near-miss.** **It would not:
> the interlock worked.** **What a reply would have done is let you argue with somebody in August of
> last year instead of telling two colleagues not to bother** — which you did, and you were right to
> on the evidence available to you.
>
> **The number that concerns me most.** **Reports to this line: 41 in year one, 9 in year two, 4 in
> year three.** **One disciplinary case occurred between year one and year two, following a report; it
> was procedurally correct and I would have reached the same conclusion.** **Nobody announced a policy
> change. Everybody drew a conclusion from one case and the absence of replies to everybody else.**
>
> **One change to the procedure, which is my whole proposal.** **Every report receives a written reply
> within ten working days, from a named person, even where the reply is that nothing will be done and
> why.**
>
> **And where I will not pretend to have a rule.** **In 2019 a test record was deliberately falsified
> and the person responsible was dismissed. I supported that then and I support it now.** The argument
> I am drawing on here **does not draw that line for me, and says itself that such lines are drawn by
> particular people rather than discovered.** **What I have is a judgment, and I would rather say so
> than dress it as a policy.**
>
> *Source: S. Dekker, Just Culture, first published 2007, with subsequent editions, which distinguishes
> a retributive response to failure — which rule, by whom, how seriously, what sanction — from a
> restorative one asking who is hurt, what they need and whose obligation it is to meet that need, and
> argues that the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable behavior is drawn by particular parties
> rather than discovered. It is an argument rather than a standard, offers no test of culpability, and
> is contested.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Answer a report even when the answer is no. A refusal with a reason keeps the
