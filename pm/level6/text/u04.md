# Unit 4 · The Holes Line Up
%%SUB Seven defenses, four holes, one that worked — and nobody had touched it in four years%%
%%GOALS
Describe layered causation
Separate an active failure from a latent condition
Say why the number of layers is a construction

A book published in 1997 distinguishes the unsafe act of the person at the controls from the
conditions that were put in place long before by people doing their jobs properly. It draws defenses
as layers with holes in them, and an accident as the moment the holes line up. On 14 June four of our
seven layers had holes open, and the one that stopped the event was the one nobody had thought about
since 2021.

### 5 · Word study — layered causation

**5A  Complete the chart.**

%%FIG pf04-f6 | A three-column chart headed the structure, what it does and an example, with rows for the immediate cause, a contributing condition, lay dormant until, was itself a consequence of, combined with and which in turn, four filled in and two empty.%%

| Structure | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **the immediate cause** | names what happened last | **The immediate cause** was a pump started against a closed valve. |
| **a contributing condition** | names something that was already there | **A contributing condition** was an alarm suppressed in February. |
| **lay dormant until** | shows the waiting | The waiver **lay dormant until** June. |
| **combined with** | shows the conjunction | The suppression, **combined with** the open waiver… |
| **was itself a consequence of** | | |
| **which in turn** | | |

> **Grammar Note** — *The immediate cause* and *the cause* are not the same phrase. The immediate
> cause is the last event in the chain and is almost always an act by the person nearest the
> equipment, which is why reviews that name *the cause* end up naming that person. Writing *the
> immediate cause was X; four contributing conditions were already present* costs eight words and
> changes who the report is about.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Two habits of organizations are visible in this unit and both are nearly universal. The first is that
a review names the last event in a chain, because the last event has a time, a place and a person,
and the conditions behind it have none of those. So the report says *operator error*, the
recommendation says *more care*, and the four conditions that made the morning possible remain exactly
as they were. Writing *the immediate cause was X; four contributing conditions were already present*
costs eight words and changes who the document is about. The second habit is stranger and more
expensive: organizations review what failed and never review what saved them. A defense that works
produces no incident, appears in no report, enters no improvement plan, and is therefore the first
thing removed when somebody needs a saving or a shortcut. On 14 June the thing that stopped an
incident was a device installed in 2019 and not mentioned in a meeting since 2021. Nobody could have
told you it was there. That is what a working defense looks like from the inside, and it is why one
sentence in every review should ask what prevented this from being worse.

**Discuss:** What is protecting you right now that nobody has mentioned this year?

> ### Near-miss review, part 2 · defenses on 14 June
> *From W. Zhao · 3 September · for C. Nkemelu and the quality file · one page*
>
> **The immediate cause was a pump started against a closed valve at 05:20 — a lapse at the end of a
> night shift, and the only unsafe act in the sequence. Four contributing conditions were already
> present. Three were created by reasonable decisions; the fourth was never decided at all.**
>
> | | Defense | State | Explanation | Remedy owner |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | sensor redundancy | **degraded** | **two sensors, same model, same batch**: one failure mode bought twice | M. Bah |
> | 2 | telemetry alarm | **holed** | **suppressed Feb**, logged with a review date that fell in an absence week | control engineer |
> | 3 | weekly plant log | **holed** | **no escalation threshold ever proposed**; log owner had no stopping authority | **W. Zhao** |
> | 4 | commissioning sign-off | **holed** | **waiver of 2022 unclosed**; closing it meant five weeks of handover delay | project manager |
> | 5 | drawing register | **holed** | **two modifications behind**, drawing resource losing to live work | technician |
> | 6 | operator training | **degraded** | **handover pack omitted the bypass** | operations |
> | 7 | **standby interlock** | **intact** | **installed 2019, tested annually, never modified** | — |
>
> **Row 3 is the finding.** The other holes were **opened by somebody for a reason, and there is a
> record of each**. Row 3 is an **absence**: never proposed, never refused, never reviewed. **An
> absence has no author and therefore no advocate or opponent, which is why four earlier reviews of
> this plant did not mention it.** **They were not poor reviews. There was nobody to interview.**
>
> **Row 7 is the reason this is a near-miss and not an incident.** It appears in **no improvement
> plan, no meeting minute since 2021 and no risk report**. **It has never been claimed as a success by
> anybody.** **Recommendation: it enters the risk report, so that its removal for convenience becomes
> visible to somebody.**
>
> **What this table is not.** **The seven layers are my choice.** Telemetry could be one layer or
> three; training could sit inside operations. **Redraw the lines and the number of holes changes**,
> and the model's own author has cautioned against treating the picture as a measurement. **I have
> drawn it anyway because drawing it found row 3.** **The deliverable is the table with its owners,
> not the diagram.**
>
> **What I am not recommending.** **Nothing about care or attentiveness.** The lapse at 05:20 is the
> least changeable thing in this file; the four conditions are all changeable and three of them are
> cheap.
>
> *Source: J. Reason, Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents, 1997, developing Human Error,
> 1990. It distinguishes active failures by those in direct contact with a system from latent
> conditions created earlier in design, construction, maintenance or management, and represents
> defenses as successive layers whose holes must align for an accident to occur. The representation is
> an organizing metaphor rather than a quantitative model: it specifies no number of layers, no method
> of counting holes, no probabilities and no order of priority among remedies.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Give every hole an owner and a record, and mark the one that has neither. The
> hole with no author is the one that will still be open at the next review.
