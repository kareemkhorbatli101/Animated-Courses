# Unit 1 · The Hiding Hand
%%SUB One genuine case in our files, nine of the opposite, and a sponsor who quoted it as a policy%%
%%GOALS

An essay published in 1967 observed that people underestimate the difficulties of a project and also
underestimate their own ingenuity in dealing with them, and that the two errors sometimes cancel. It
is one of the most generous ideas in the literature of project failure. It is also, in our own files,
outnumbered three to one by its opposite — and it has been quoted at us as a reason not to finish a
ground investigation.

> *Project office · twenty-three projects, six years · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The essay.** **A. O. Hirschman**, *The Principle of the Hiding Hand*, **The Public Interest,
> Winter 1967**, and the book of the same year in which he set out his observations of **eleven
> development projects** financed by one institution.
>
> **The observation, in two parts.** People **underestimate the difficulties** a project will
> encounter. People also **underestimate their own ingenuity** in dealing with difficulties once
> encountered. **Where the two errors are of similar size, they cancel**, and a project that would
> never have been authorized had its difficulties been known is nevertheless completed — sometimes
> better than planned.
>
> | Difficulties | Ingenuity | Outcome |
> |---|---|---|
> | underestimated | **underestimated by about as much** | **the benevolent case: completed, often with a better solution** |
> | underestimated | **not underestimated at all** | **the malevolent case: an overrun, a descope or an abandonment** |
> | about right | about right | **no surprise in either direction** |
>
> **One genuine case in our own files.**
>
> > **The depot, 2019.** A **buried culvert** appeared in an excavation. It was **on no drawing** and
> > **in no record** held by us, the client or the utility.
> >
> > The team invented a **cantilevered slab detail** to bridge it rather than divert it. **The detail
> > has since been used on four later jobs** and has saved, on our own estimate, **about 1.2 million
> > across them.**
> >
> > **Had the culvert been known at tender, we would have priced a diversion and the detail would
> > not exist.** **That is exactly the mechanism the essay describes, and I can name the week it
> > happened.**
>
> **And the count.** Twenty-three completed projects, six years, sorted by the two questions above.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **Benevolent** — difficulties and ingenuity both underestimated | **3** |
> | **Malevolent** — difficulties underestimated, no offsetting idea | **9** |
> | **Neither** — no surprise in either direction | **11** |
>
> **Three to one against, in our own files.** A larger argument to the same effect was published in
> **2016** by two scholars who tested the principle against a much bigger sample and concluded that
> the benevolent version is **rare** while the pattern of **underestimated difficulty without
> offsetting creativity** is the normal case.
>
> **The misuse, which is why I have written this note.** A paper submitted in **2023** cited the
> hiding hand as a reason to **begin construction before the ground investigation was complete**.
>
> > **The essay does not support that and says so.** **A difficulty cannot be hidden from oneself on
> > purpose.** The mechanism works, where it works, **because nobody knew** — not because somebody
> > decided not to find out. **The moment you choose ignorance, you are relying on luck and giving it
> > a distinguished name.**
>
> **What the essay does not claim.** It rests on **eleven projects**, selected and observed by **one
> author**, in **one institution's portfolio**, in the **1960s**. It is an **essay**, not a measured
> regularity: it offers no frequency, no sample frame and no test. Its author presents the principle
> as **providential and unplannable**, and the later literature has argued that it is also
> **uncommon**. **It explains three of our twenty-three projects very well, and it would be a
> catastrophic basis for authorizing the twenty-fourth.**

> *Source: A. O. Hirschman, The Principle of the Hiding Hand, The Public Interest, Winter 1967, and

| | Example |

> **Grammar Note** — A concession is only worth making if it is a real one. *Granted that the
> mechanism is real* concedes the other side's strongest point; *granted that you have a point*
> concedes nothing and signals that you are about to ignore it. Concede the strongest version of the
> claim you are arguing against, and your counter-claim becomes much harder to dismiss.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Every organization keeps two records of its own history and only one of them is written down. The
written one is a spreadsheet of outcomes; the spoken one is three or four stories about brilliant
recoveries, and the stories are true. What makes them misleading is not falsehood but selection: a
clever fix has a hero, a date and a drawing, and nine quiet overruns have none of those, so the three
cases become the company's self-description and the nine become background. The second habit is
subtler and it is why this unit opens the book. A description of something that sometimes happens is
one of the easiest things in the world to convert into a permission, especially when the description
comes from a distinguished source: *this has been known to work* becomes *we are relying on it*, and
the conversion happens in a single sentence of a board paper written by somebody acting in perfectly
good faith. The defense against it is dull and it works. Count both sides. Say who is deciding. And
when you borrow an idea, read its last pages, where authors put the limits of what they are
claiming.

**Discuss:** What three stories does your organization tell about itself?

>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | Completed projects reviewed | **23** |
