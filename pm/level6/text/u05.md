# Unit 5 · What the Commission Found
%%SUB How a public report separates a cause from a contributing cause, and what it says it could not establish%%
%%GOALS
Distinguish a finding from a recommendation
Separate a cause from a contributing cause
Say what an investigation could not establish
%%

A commission reported in June 1986 on the loss of a space shuttle and seven lives the previous
January. This unit is about how such a report is built — what it calls a cause, what it calls a
contributing cause, how it words a recommendation, and where it states the limits of its own
inquiry. That structure is the model for every serious review you will ever write.

> **B:** **Because it is the clearest public example of the structure we need, and because our own
> reports mix up three things that it keeps apart.**
> **A:** Which three?
> **B:** **A cause, a contributing cause and a recommendation.** **The report names a technical cause
> — the failure of a pressure seal in a joint, from a design unacceptably sensitive to a number of
> factors, temperature among them.** **Then, separately, it addresses the decision-making that
> preceded the launch.**
> **A:** And that is the contributing part.
> **B:** **The report treats the flawed decision process as a contributing cause and sets out what
> those who took the decision did not have in front of them: the recent history of problems with the
> joint, the contractor's written recommendation against launching below a stated temperature, and a
> clear account of the engineers' concerns.**
> **A:** So the finding is about information, not courage.
> **B:** **The finding is about what reached the decision and what did not**, and that is a
> reviewable thing. **Courage is not reviewable and recommendations about it do nothing.**
> **A:** How many recommendations?
> **B:** **Nine, each addressed to a named recipient and each separate from the findings.** **Our
> reports blend the two, so a finding arrives sounding like a demand and a recommendation arrives
> sounding like an accusation.**
> **A:** And the appendices?
> **B:** **One of them is worth the whole exercise for our purposes.** **A member of the commission
> published his own observations in his own name, including a comparison of failure probabilities
> estimated at very different orders of magnitude by different parts of one organization.**

> **B:** **Orders of magnitude, and that is the point rather than the figures.** **Two parts of one
> organization held numbers that could not both be true, and nothing in the system required them to
> meet.**

> **B:** **It is exactly our Unit 2 problem, and the report says the useful thing plainly: it states

%%FIG pf05-f5 | A one-page internal note: a heading, a line giving the report, its date of submission and the body that produced it, a four-row table showing the report's structural divisions, a short block on the separation of a technical cause from a decision process, a block on an appendix written in one member's own name, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

> ## Lindhurst · quality note 5
> *Quality · how a public report is built · page 1 of 1*
>
> **The document.** The *Report of the Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger
> Accident*, **submitted in June 1986**, on the loss of the vehicle and its crew of seven on **28
> January 1986**. It runs to **several volumes**. **This note is about its structure. It is not a
> summary of the accident, which is not ours to summarize.**
>
> **Why we are reading it.** Our own incident reports put findings, opinions, recommendations and
> complaints in one list. **This report keeps four things apart, and the separation is the reason it
> is still read.**
>
> | | What it is | Why separate |
> |---|---|---|
> | **terms of reference** | what the commission was asked to do | everything outside them is **beyond its mandate**, and it says so |
> | **findings** | what it concluded happened | a finding can be **right or wrong**; it is not a demand |
> | **recommendations** | what should be done, and **by whom** | a recommendation can be **accepted or refused**; it is not an accusation |
> | **appendices** | material, and views held by **individual members in their own names** | a member may go **further than the collective text** without binding it |
>
> **The technical cause and the decision process are treated separately.** The report identifies the
> **failure of a pressure seal in a joint of a solid rocket motor**, attributing it to a **design
> unacceptably sensitive to a number of factors**, including **temperature**. **Then, as a distinct
> matter, it addresses the decision to launch.**
>
> **What the report records about that decision is a statement about information.** Those who took it
> **did not have before them** the recent history of problems with the joint, the contractor's
> **written recommendation against launching below a stated temperature**, or a clear account of the
> **engineers' concerns.**
>
> > **That is a reviewable finding, and it is the kind we almost never write.** It does not say that
> > anybody was careless or lacked courage. **It says what reached the decision and what did not**,
> > which is a property of an organization and can be changed by one.
>
> **Nine recommendations, each with a recipient.** They cover, among other things, **redesign of the
> joint**, **review of the management structure**, **independent oversight**, **a safety
> organization**, **improved communications** and **maintenance safeguards**. **Each is addressed to
> somebody.** **A recommendation with no recipient is a wish, and most of ours have no recipient.**
>
> **An appendix in one member's own name.** One commissioner published **personal observations** on
> the reliability of the vehicle, in which he set out **estimates of failure probability made within
> one organization that differed by orders of magnitude** between engineering and management levels.
>
> > **The figures are not the lesson. The gap is.** **Two parts of one organization held numbers that
> > could not both be true, and nothing required them to be compared.** **That is the problem of our
> > own Unit 2, in a different industry, thirty-odd years earlier.**
>
> **And the sentence I most want us to learn.** The report, like every careful inquiry, **states
> where it could not reach a conclusion.** **An investigation that concludes everything has concluded
> too much.** **Our last four incident reports contain no such sentence, and all four of them had
> questions nobody could answer.**
>
> **What the report does not do, and does not claim to.** It was asked to determine the **probable
> cause** and to **make recommendations**: it is **not a determination of legal liability**, and it
> says what is **beyond its mandate**. It worked in **a few months**, under public attention, with
> the evidence available in that time. **It is one account, produced under its own terms of
> reference** — and the next unit of this book is about a study, published ten years later, which
> argued that its central explanation of the launch decision was mistaken. **Holding both is the work
> of Units 5 and 6 together, and it is the most advanced thing this series asks of you.**
>
> *Source: Report of the Presidential Commission on the Space Shuttle Challenger Accident, submitted
> June 1986, concerning the loss of the vehicle and its crew on 28 January 1986. The report sets out
> terms of reference, findings, nine recommendations addressed to named recipients, and appendices
> including observations published by individual members in their own names. It was charged with
> determining the probable cause and recommending corrective action; it is not a determination of
> legal liability, it states where it was unable to reach conclusions, and it was produced within a
> few months of the event on the evidence then available.*

> **Grammar Note** — *It was found that* and *in our view* must never be mixed in one sentence. The
> first claims a fact that somebody can disprove; the second claims a judgment that somebody can
> disagree with. A sentence that does both — *it was found that the threshold should be two
> readings* — cannot be answered at all, which is why it survives in so many reports.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

The structure of a report decides what happens in the meeting about it. Put findings, judgments,
recommendations and grievances in one list and the recipient will argue about the findings, because a
finding printed beside a demand reads as an accusation; separate them and the same recipient argues
about the recommendation, which is the argument you wanted. The second habit is rarer and harder: a
sentence saying what the inquiry could not establish. It feels like weakness and it is the opposite,
because the alternative is to write a plausible name — and the moment a report contains a name it
cannot support, the meeting is about that person and the one finding that would prevent a recurrence
is never discussed. A third thing is worth noticing in every serious public inquiry: where the
investigators cannot agree, or where one of them wants to go further, that view appears in an
appendix in their own name rather than being smoothed into the collective text. Organizations that
have no mechanism for a signed minority view get consensus documents that nobody believes.

**Discuss:** Where would a dissenting view go in your organization?

### 🗣 Group ranking

*In fours.* Four blocks against one list. Rank these, best to worst. You must agree.

a. Separate findings from recommendations in every review
b. Give every recommendation a recipient and a date
c. Include a section headed *matters we were unable to determine*
d. Word findings about decisions as statements about information
e. Allow a signed minority view in an appendix

Report your order, and say which one would be resisted most.
