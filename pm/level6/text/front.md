# Throughline 5
%%SUB English for Project Managers · International Edition · CEFR B2+ to C1%%

## How to use this book

This is the sixth and last book of six. Books 1 to 5 were about the parts of a project, estimating
it, measuring it, the methods organizations buy, and money. This one is about the question none of
those answer: **what happens when everything has been done correctly and the thing still fails, and
who is accountable then.**

**Twelve units**, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | One real report, book or standard, with its date and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · At Work** | Clear English, a dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `TL6-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Half of its sources are not standards, and two of them disagree with each other in print.** The
other five books were built mostly on documents that tell you what to do. This one is built on
documents that explain why organizations fail while behaving reasonably: an economist's essay from
1967, four books about accidents, an official commission report, and a sociologist who spent ten
years arguing that the official report got the explanation wrong.

That changes how you have to read, and the language changes with it.

1. **You will hold two accounts of the same event.** Units 5 and 6 are both about one accident in
   1986: the commission that investigated it, and the book that says the commission's central
   explanation was mistaken. Neither is dishonest. You will need the English for *disputing an
   account without calling anybody a liar*.
2. **You will separate accountability from responsibility.** They are not synonyms, three of the
   standards in this book define them differently, and the difference decides who answers a
   question in public.
3. **You will learn to say that something was reasonable and wrong.** This is the hardest sentence
   in the series, and almost every unit here needs it: *every decision in that chain was defensible
   on the day it was taken.*
4. **You will write the sentence that ends a project.** The last four units are about closure,
   audit, and the person who can still say the uncomfortable thing when nothing is left to decide.

**Money has no currency symbol anywhere in this series.** Every figure is in whatever unit your own
project uses.

## A warning, and it is a different one this time

**Nothing in this book is legal, regulatory, safety or professional advice, and none of it is a
substitute for the investigation report, standard or law that applies to your own work.**

Two of this book's sources are accounts of accidents in which people died. They are discussed here
because they are the clearest published explanations of how competent organizations fail, and
because the English used to discuss them — *latent condition*, *finding*, *contributing cause*,
*normalized*, *accountable* — is the English of every serious review you will ever take part in.
They are discussed **without reconstructed dialogue, without invented detail, and without any
suggestion that the people in them were careless.** The invented company in this book has its own
invented near-miss, in Unit 4, and every scene, person, figure and consequence in that story belongs
to the invented company and to no real event.

**Where this book describes what a real report concluded, it says which report and in which year, and
it prints what the report itself said it could not establish.** That last part is the whole method of
all six books, and it matters most here.
