# Unit 2 · The Incubation Period
%%SUB Nine signals over seventeen months, nobody holding more than three, and two signals that led nowhere%%
%%GOALS

A book first published in 1978 examined official inquiry reports and found that serious failures are
not sudden: they have a long preparatory phase in which small discrepancies accumulate while nobody
assembles them. Our own near-miss had nine such signals over seventeen months, held by seven
different people — and two more that were just as discrepant and meant nothing at all.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

Reviews of near-misses are written after the event and read as though they were written before it,
and that single fact distorts almost everything about them. A sequence of nine signals printed in
date order looks like a line anybody could have drawn, so the natural conclusion is that somebody was
not paying attention, and the natural recommendation is more attention — which has never improved any
organization, because nobody can be instructed to notice the unobvious in advance. The defense is
mechanical and slightly tedious: print the signals that led nowhere alongside the ones that mattered,
name the system and the person for each, and state what the view was on the day. The second habit is
about naming. Pieces of a failure fail to meet because the same thing has three different names in
three different registers, and nobody has ever compared them, which is nobody's fault and everybody's
problem. An organization that can query four systems on one identifier has bought more safety than
one that has run twelve awareness campaigns.

**Discuss:** How many names does your most important asset have?

### 🗣 Group ranking

*In fours.* Nine signals, two that led nowhere, seven people, four systems. Rank these, best to
worst. You must agree.

a. A monthly query across all systems on one asset identifier
b. Printing the signals that led nowhere alongside the ones that mattered
c. One name per asset, enforced in every register
d. A review date on every suppressed alarm, with an owner who is never away
e. Reporting *assemblable* rather than *obvious* in every finding

Report your order, and say which one your organization would resist.

**1C  Read the model text.**

> ### Near-miss review · Calder control failure, 14 June
> *From W. Zhao · 29 July · for C. Nkemelu and the quality file · one page*
>
> **Eleven discrepant events occurred over seventeen months. Nine of them form the chain that ended on
> 14 June. Two of them, equally discrepant, are connected to nothing, and they are in this report on
> purpose.**
>
> | # | Signal | System | Holder | Chain? |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 1 | sensor replaced **3×** in 14 months | maintenance | fitter | **yes** |
> | 2 | plant log item, twice | weekly log | log owner | **yes** |
> | 3 | *recurring fault, cause unknown* | maintenance | supervisor | **yes** |
> | 4 | **telemetry alarm suppressed**, Feb | telemetry | control engineer | **yes** |
> | 5 | **commissioning waiver**, 2022, not closed | project file | project manager | **yes** |
> | 6 | drawing not updated after a 2021 modification | drawing register | technician | **yes** |
> | 7 | verbal instruction about a bypass | a notebook | shift leader | **yes** |
> | 8 | contractor's query unanswered | an email thread of 41 | **nobody** | **yes** |
> | 9 | training gap in the handover pack | handover file | operations | **yes** |
> | 10 | valve stuck twice, Mar–Apr, then stopped | maintenance | fitter | **no** |
> | 11 | unexplained pressure reading, Sep | telemetry | control engineer | **no** |
>
> **The view at the time.** **Seven people held at least one signal; no one of them held more than
> three; and the three that one person did hold were the three least alarming.** Items 4, 5 and 8 lived
> in three different places and **the circuit they concern has three names**: CV-12 in maintenance, *the
> bypass line* in the project file, and *the new valve* in a shift notebook. **No mechanism existed that
> would have compared them.** **Assembling them afterwards took me six weeks, knowing what I was
> looking for.**
>
> **Two reasonable acts that look bad written down.** The **alarm suppression** was logged correctly,
> with a review date, because the alarm was waking people and the readings appeared spurious; **the
> review date fell in a week when two people were away.** The **maintenance note** did exactly what a
> maintenance note should do.
>
> **Why rows 10 and 11 are here.** **The incubation period is identified by its ending.** A report
> printing only the nine would show a straight line and invite the conclusion that somebody was
> inattentive. **At the time there were eleven things that did not fit and no means of knowing which
> nine were the chain.** **The finding therefore says *assemblable*, not *obvious*.**
>
> **One recommendation, and only one.** **A monthly query across the four systems on a single asset
> identifier, run by a named person.** **Four days to build; about two hours a month to run.** **All
> eleven signals would have appeared in one place.** **I am making no recommendation about
> attentiveness, because none of these eleven documents required anybody to be more alert than people
> are.**
>
> *Source: B. A. Turner, Man-Made Disasters, first published 1978, second edition 1997 with N.
> Pidgeon. Derived from official accident inquiry reports; describes a staged sequence in which
> discrepant events accumulate during an incubation period before a precipitating event; characterizes
> such failures as failures of foresight arising from information distributed among parties none of whom
> holds enough of it. It is a sociological account rather than a predictive method, inherits the framing
> of the reports it draws on, and offers no means of identifying an incubation period from within one.*

> **Writer's Tip** — Print the signals that led nowhere. They cost you a paragraph and they are the
> only thing standing between your report and a recommendation that somebody try harder.
