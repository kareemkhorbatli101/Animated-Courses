There is a triangle on a wall at almost every industrial site on earth. A small number at the top, a
bigger one in the middle, a very big one at the bottom. It comes from a book published in 1931, and
the numbers in it have never been independently checked, because the data behind them was never
published.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG re01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its publisher and its year, a triangular diagram with three bands, a row of five dominoes, a boxed paragraph headed what cannot be checked, a second boxed paragraph headed what the ratio does not measure, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the triangle on the wall, and where it came from**
> **Source: H. W. Heinrich, *Industrial Accident Prevention: A Scientific Approach*, McGraw-Hill,
> first edition **1931**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What the book is.** It is the first attempt to study industrial accidents as a body of data
> rather than as a series of misfortunes. It is enormously influential. Most of what the world does
> about workplace safety can be traced back to it, directly or at a distance, and that is true
> whether or not the book is right.
>
> **The three claims.** Three things in it have survived.
>
> **One, the ratio.** For a given group of accidents, the book reports a relationship between
> injuries of different severity: a small number of major injuries, a larger number of minor ones,
> and a much larger number of events that caused no injury at all. Drawn as a triangle, this is the
> diagram on the wall.
>
> **Two, the sequence.** An accident is presented as one event in a chain of five, each one knocking
> over the next like a line of dominoes. Remove any domino and the chain stops. The practical
> conclusion is that you do not have to prevent the injury; you can intervene earlier.
>
> **Three, the proportion.** The book reports that the great majority of industrial accidents were
> attributable to the **unsafe acts of persons**, with a much smaller share attributable to unsafe
> mechanical or physical conditions, and a very small remainder treated as unpreventable.
>
> **WHAT CANNOT BE CHECKED**
> The ratio and the proportion came from records held by insurers and from accident reports made by
> employers in the nineteen-twenties. **The underlying data was never published in a form that
> anybody has been able to re-examine.** There is no dataset. There is no list of plants. There is no
> coding manual saying how a cause was decided. Later researchers have looked for it and have not
> found it.
>
> That does not make the claims false. It makes them **unverifiable**, which is a different thing, and
> it means a figure repeated for ninety years has been repeated rather than reproduced.
>
> There is a second problem with the proportion. The cause of each accident was taken from a **box on
> a form**, completed by a supervisor or an inspector, in an era in which the expected answer was the
> worker. A classification that records what the classifier expected is not a measurement.
>
> **WHAT THE RATIO DOES NOT MEASURE**
> The ratio is about injuries: cuts, falls, crush injuries, the ordinary harm of industrial work.
> **It is not about explosions, fires, releases or structural failures**, which are rare, have
> different causes, and are not the top of a pyramid of minor injuries. A plant can drive its minor
> injury rate down for years and simultaneously allow the conditions for a catastrophe, because the
> two are produced by different things. Unit 2 of this book is an investigation into a case where
> exactly that happened.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its author, publisher and year. Read the book, and
> then go and look at the triangle on your own wall and ask who can show you the data.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> The triangle is the most successful diagram in industrial history. It is simple, it is memorable,
> it fits on a wall, and it tells an organization that small, cheap, visible actions prevent large,
> expensive, invisible disasters. That is a very attractive message and it is partly true. The part
> that is true is about injuries: watching for hazards and tidying up does reduce the ordinary harm
> of industrial work, and that matters. The part that is not true is the extension — that the same
> activity protects you from a catastrophe. The counting is easy, the counting is popular, and the
> counting creates the belief. Nobody on any site has ever been promoted for reporting that an
> inspection backlog grew.
> **Ask your group: what gets counted at your site, and what gets rewarded for being counted?**
