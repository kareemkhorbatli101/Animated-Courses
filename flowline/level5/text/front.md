## How to use this book

This is the fifth book of six, and it is the only one that adds no new plant. Books 1 to 4 built the
chain: the wellhead, the reservoir, the pipeline, the refinery. This book goes back over all of it
and asks a different question. Not *how does it work* and not *what is the number*, but **how do
organizations that know all of this still have accidents**.

Twelve units, and every one of them is a document written after somebody was killed or in order to
stop it. A book from 1931 whose pyramid is on a wall at your site. An investigation into a refinery
explosion whose injury statistics were excellent that year. A method for imagining failure before it
happens. A standard for choosing between methods. An inquiry into a night in the North Sea that
changed how every permit in the world is written. A paper that explains why plants drift. Two
documents about the two jobs that kill most often: entering a vessel and isolating a line. A
preparedness framework for the day it is in the sea. An assessment of what sixty years of leaks does
to a region. A book about why organizations do not learn. And at the end, the argument that counting
what goes wrong is the wrong way round.

**Twelve units**, twenty-two pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | One real book, paper, inquiry report, standard or regulation |

## What is different about this book

**The English is harder, and it has to be.** Everything in this book is an argument rather than a
procedure. You will be reading sentences that say *this is probably true, for these reasons, within
these limits*, and writing them yourself. That is the real language of risk, and it is the hardest
register in technical English, because it has to be exact about uncertainty.

**Four units are about one thing: a signature.** A permit, an isolation certificate, an entry
authorization, a handover. In every one, the paper is not the protection. The conversation the paper
forces two people to have is the protection, and the paper is the evidence that it happened. Where
the conversation stops happening and the paper keeps being signed, the system is at its most
dangerous, because from the outside it looks identical.

**One unit is somebody else's regulation**, as Book 4's fuel law was. It is in the book because its
structure — the entry permit, the attendant, the atmospheric test, the rescue arrangement — is the
structure of nearly every confined space system in the world, and because the part of it that is most
often missing in practice is the part that matters most.

**Three of the twelve sources disagree with each other**, and they are printed as they are. A 1931
book says most accidents are caused by the unsafe acts of workers. A 1997 paper says organizations
drift towards their boundaries because of ordinary pressures. A 2014 book says counting accidents
tells you almost nothing about safety. All three are real, all three have been enormously
influential, and a reader who finishes this book should be able to say what each one is good for and
where each one misleads.

**And one unit has no happy ending.** Unit 10 is a scientific assessment of contamination across a
region. It is in the book because what happens to land and water is part of this industry's record,
because the document is public and careful, and because the English of an environmental assessment is
a skill. It is written without exaggeration and without comfort.

**A language course is not a safety course.** Nothing here authorizes anybody to issue a permit,
isolate a line, enter a confined space, assess a risk or sign anything. **Safety information in a
language course is not a substitute for a competent person, a procedure and a permit.**
