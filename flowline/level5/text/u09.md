already moving, and already heading somewhere that belongs to other people. The first hour is yours,
whatever your plan says, because nobody else can get there.

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: three tiers, and the hour that is always yours**
> **Sources: *Tiered preparedness and response*, an IPIECA–IOGP Good Practice Guide, **2015**; and the
> **International Convention on Oil Pollution Preparedness, Response and Co-operation, 1990**,
> adopted under the International Maritime Organization. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What tiered preparedness means.** Response capability is organized in three levels, and the
> levels are defined by **the resources available and how far away they are** — not by the size of the
> spill.
>
> **Tier one** is what you have on your own site, under your own control, available immediately.
> **Tier two** is regional: your other sites, a mutual aid partner, a shared regional store. **Tier
> three** is national or international: large stockpiles, specialist teams, aircraft, equipment that
> arrives in containers.
>
> **The common misunderstanding, and the guide is explicit about it.** Tiers are **not** categories of
> spill size. A small release in a sensitive, inaccessible place, in bad weather, with nothing nearby,
> may require tier three resources. A large release inside a bund with a vacuum truck standing beside
> it is a tier one job. **The tier describes the response, not the spill.**
>
> **What a plan has to contain.** Credible **scenarios**, assessed rather than assumed. The
> **resources** for each tier, listed by what they are and where they are. **Activation triggers**:
> what, exactly, causes somebody to make the call, and who may make it. **Escalation**: at what point
> and on whose authority the next tier is mobilized. And **sensitivity information**: what is
> downstream, downwind or down-current, and which of it matters most.
>
> **Escalation is a decision made early or not at all.** Mobilizing tier two takes hours and tier
> three can take days. If you wait until you are certain you need them, they arrive after the window
> in which they could have helped. So the guidance is to escalate on the **credible worst case** and
> stand resources down if they are not needed, which is operationally and politically much harder
> than it sounds, because a stand-down looks like an overreaction and nobody is thanked for one.
>
> **What the convention does.** It is an agreement between **states**. Parties undertake to establish
> national systems for preparedness and response, including a national contingency plan; to require
> ships and offshore units to carry oil pollution emergency plans; to report incidents; and to
> **co-operate** and provide assistance to each other on request. It is the reason a national system
> exists for a company to escalate into.
>
> **WHAT NEITHER DOCUMENT CAN DO**
> **The guide is good practice, not law.** It is written by industry associations to describe what
> works. It binds nobody.
>
> **The convention binds states, not companies.** It obliges parties to put systems in place. What
> exists in any particular country, and how quickly it can move, is a national matter, and the
> convention does not promise you a response.
>
> **Neither can make a plan real.** A plan is a document until somebody has **deployed the
> equipment**, driven the route, found the key, started the pump and discovered that the boom needs
> two more people than the plan allocates. **An unexercised plan is not a plan. It is a hypothesis.**
>
> **And neither can change the arithmetic of the first hour.** Whatever tier a response becomes, it
> starts as tier one, with the people on site and the equipment in the locker. That hour is the one
> that decides where the material goes, and it is the hour nobody is coming to help with.
>
> *Our summary of a published good practice guide and a published international convention, named with
> their publishers and their years. Read them, and then go and time the drive from your regional store
> to your site.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Spill response is the only part of this book where the consequences are mostly somebody else's. A
> release that reaches water leaves the site and becomes the problem of farmers, fishermen, a
> municipality and a government, and the organization that caused it experiences it mainly as a
> reputational and legal event. That asymmetry is why preparedness decays so reliably: the cost of an
> exercise is immediate, visible and internal, and the benefit is deferred, invisible and external.
> Every honest plan in this industry has the same weakest point, and it is not the equipment. It is
> that the people who would have to find the key at three in the morning have never opened the locker
> in daylight.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG re09-f16 | A desk in the evening. A spill response plan lies open at a page showing an assumed four hour response time with seven hours twenty written beside it in pen, next to an exercise log with one entry from 2016, a photograph of perished boom joints, a skimmer pump with a tag reading will not start, a mutual aid agreement with a signature and an unobtainable telephone number, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**The plan is well written and it was revised last year.** Scenarios are assessed, tiers are defined,
triggers are stated, and there is a signed mutual aid agreement. An auditor reading the document
would find very little to criticize.

Sixty meters of boom is perished. The skimmer will not start. One person holds the only key. The
regional store is seven hours twenty away, not four. The mutual aid partner's number rings out. The
plan has not been exercised since 2016.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is this site prepared?
2 Every one of those findings took an afternoon. Why had nobody made them?
3 The plan was revised and nothing was opened. What should a revision include?
4 What would you do with the first hour at your own site tomorrow morning?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 10 is what sixty years of releases
smaller than this one do to a place where people live.
