%%SUB Three roles, one atmosphere, and the arrangement that is usually a sentence%%

More people are killed in this industry by the inside of empty vessels than by almost anything else,
and a large proportion of them went in to help somebody who had gone in first. The regulation in this
unit is detailed, well written and widely copied. The part of it that is most often a fiction is the
part about getting somebody out.

%%FIG re07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the regulation, its number, the agency that makes it and the country, a diagram of a manway with an attendant outside, a tripod and a monitor, a four-row table of reasons a space needs a permit, a boxed paragraph headed the part that is usually a fiction, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: three roles, one atmosphere, and the way out**
> **Source: **29 CFR 1910.146**, *Permit-Required Confined Spaces*, a general industry standard made
> by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration of the United States. Printed here in our own
> words. **It does not apply here.** It is in this book because its structure is the structure of
> nearly every confined space system in the world, including ours.**
>
> **What a confined space is.** Three things together: it is **large enough to enter and work in**, it
> has **limited or restricted means of entry or exit**, and it is **not designed for continuous
> occupancy**. A tank, a vessel, a column, a sewer, a pit, a hopper. An open-topped pit with a ladder
> is one. A plant room with a normal door is not.
>
> **What makes it need a permit.** Any one of four things: an actual or potential **hazardous
> atmosphere**; the potential for **engulfment**; an internal configuration that could **trap or
> asphyxiate** somebody — inwardly converging walls, or a floor that slopes to a smaller
> cross-section; or **any other recognized serious safety or health hazard**.
>
> **The three roles.** The **entry supervisor** authorizes the entry, confirms the conditions are met
> and ends it. The **authorized entrant** goes in, knows the hazards and the signs of exposure, and
> leaves when told to or when something changes. The **attendant** stays outside, knows who is inside,
> watches for anything changing, orders an exit, and summons rescue. **The attendant does not enter.
> Not to look, not to help, not for a moment.**
>
> **The atmosphere.** It is tested **before entry**, in a stated order: **oxygen** first, then
> **flammable** gases and vapors, then **toxic** contaminants. The order matters because the
> instruments depend on each other — several flammable gas detectors do not read correctly in a low
> oxygen atmosphere. Testing is then **repeated or continuous** as conditions require, because an
> atmosphere is a state, not a property: it changes with temperature, with movement, with sludge being
> disturbed, with a blower stopping.
>
> **The permit.** A written entry permit records the space, the purpose, the date and duration, the
> entrants, the attendant, the supervisor, the hazards, the measures taken to isolate and ventilate,
> the test results **with times**, the communication arrangements, and the **rescue** arrangements.
> The supervisor signs it. It is available at the entrance while the entry is in progress, and it is
> ended and retained.
>
> **THE PART THAT IS USUALLY A FICTION**
> **Rescue.** The regulation requires that arrangements be made for rescue, that **non-entry
> retrieval** be used where it is feasible — a harness and a line to a tripod and winch, so that
> somebody can be lifted out without anybody going in — and that where a rescue service is to be
> relied on, the employer **evaluates** it: can it do this kind of rescue, can it reach this space, how
> long would it take, has it been offered the chance to practice here.
>
> In practice, in a very large number of organizations, the rescue arrangement is a **telephone
> number**. Nobody has asked the service whether it performs confined space rescue. Nobody has timed
> the response. Nobody has tried to get a rescue dummy out of that particular manway, which is
> nineteen inches across and has a platform in front of it.
>
> **And this matters more than anything else in the regulation, because of the pattern of the
> fatalities.** A very high proportion of deaths in confined spaces include people who **went in to
> help**. The first person collapses, and somebody who can see them goes in. The atmosphere that
> stopped the first person stops the second within the same minute.
>
> **What the regulation does not cover.** It is **one country's law and does not apply here**. It sets
> **minimums**, not good practice. It has a **separate** standard for construction work. It cannot
> make a rescue arrangement real, and it cannot stop a person going in to help somebody they can
> see.
>
> *Our summary of a published regulation, named with its number, its agency and its country. Read your
> own national requirements, and then go and ask who would come, how long they would take, and
> whether they have ever seen the manway.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write the time next to every gas reading, and write the time of entry.** Two numbers, one line
> apart. If the gap is more than a few minutes the permit has answered its own question. **And once —
> once in your career — find out who would actually come.** Ring them. Ask whether they perform

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Confined space entry is the most procedurally controlled job in this industry and the one that kills
> the most predictably. The reason is not ignorance. Everybody involved knows the rules; the permit is
> usually filled in; the gas test is usually done. The failures are in the gaps: the time between the
> test and the entry, the attendant who is also doing something else, the rescue arrangement nobody
> has ever tried. All three gaps exist because closing them costs somebody time on the morning of the
> job, and the job is always late. And the second-body pattern is not a failure of discipline. It is
> the most human thing there is: you can see them, they are four meters away, and you are not going to
> stand at the hole and wait. The only defense against that is an arrangement that exists before
> anybody is lying down.
> **Ask your group: if somebody collapsed in a vessel at your site this morning, what would actually
> happen in the first ten minutes?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG re07-f16 | A desk in the evening. An entry permit lies open with three gas readings all timed 06:40 and an entry time of 09:15, beside a blank continuous monitoring line, an attendant log showing two spaces sixty meters apart, a note of a telephone call confirming the emergency service does not perform confined space rescue, a photograph of a nineteen inch manway with a platform in front of it, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**Every box on the permit is filled in.** Four competent people signed it. The isolation was done and
proved, the blower ran all morning, the gas test was taken by a trained person with a calibrated
instrument, and the results were good.

The test was at 06:40 and the entry was at 09:15. The attendant was logged for two openings sixty
meters apart. The rescue line says *call the emergency service*, and that service does not perform
confined space rescue.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Was this entry safe?
2 Which of the three gaps would you close first, and why?
3 Nobody falsified anything. Which part of the system was supposed to catch this?
4 What would you put on the permit form itself to make the first gap impossible?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 8 is the other job in this pair: proving
that the line you are about to open is actually dead.
