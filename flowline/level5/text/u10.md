Every other unit in this book is about one event. This one is about sixty years of events too small to
investigate, in a place where people live, and about a published scientific assessment that went and
measured what was left. It is the plainest and the hardest reading in the series.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG re10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the assessment, the organization that published it and its year, a cross-section showing surface staining, clean-looking soil and product floating on groundwater, a table of the sampling program figures, a boxed paragraph headed what the assessment says it did not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: what sixty years leaves behind, measured**
> **Source: *Environmental Assessment of Ogoniland*, published by the United Nations Environment
> Programme, **2011**. Printed here in our own words. Ogoniland is a region of the Niger Delta in
> Nigeria, and everything in this note refers to that assessment and to that region.**
>
> **What the document is.** A scientific environmental assessment of a region with a long history of
> oil production, carried out by a United Nations body and published in 2011. It is not an inquiry
> into an accident and not a legal judgment. It is a measurement exercise, and the reason it is in
> this book is that it is the clearest published example of **the English of findings**.
>
> **The scale of the work, as the assessment describes it.** Field work at **more than two hundred
> locations**. Examination of pipeline **rights of way**. Thousands of **soil and water samples** and
> dozens of **groundwater monitoring wells** drilled specifically for the study. Review of existing
> **medical records**. Community meetings in a large number of settlements. **The sampling program is
> described in the report**, which is what allows anybody to argue with it.
>
> **The findings, as published.** Contamination was found to be **more widespread and in places deeper
> than had been assumed**, including below surfaces that looked clean. **Hydrocarbons were detected in
> groundwater** at a number of locations. At one named community the assessment reported a layer of
> **refined product floating on the groundwater** and benzene in drinking water **above the World
> Health Organization guideline value**, and recommended emergency action there.
>
> **The finding about remediation is the one that matters most for this course.** Sites that had been
> **remediated and signed off** under a method relied on at the time were examined and, in a number of
> cases, found **still to be contaminated**. The method, and the way completion was certified, were
> judged **not fit for purpose** for the conditions. The certificates were issued in good faith by
> people doing what the procedure said. **The procedure did not include digging deep enough to find
> out.**
>
> **The causes.** The assessment discusses **both** operational failures — old pipelines, failed
> equipment, poor maintenance — **and** interference by third parties, including deliberate damage and
> artisanal refining. Both contribute. **The proportions are contested**, and this book does not
> pretend to settle a dispute that the document itself treats carefully.
>
> **What it recommended.** A long-term, funded restoration program, with a stated initial
> capitalization, under a dedicated authority, and the observation that full environmental recovery
> in the worst-affected areas would take **decades** rather than years.
>
> **WHAT THE ASSESSMENT SAYS IT DID NOT DO**
> **It did not assess the whole of the Niger Delta.** Its scope is one region. Its findings describe
> what its samples found, where they were taken.
>
> **The health observations are not an epidemiological study.** They are drawn from existing records
> and from the sampling of exposure routes such as drinking water. **No conclusion is offered that a
> proper study would be needed to support.**
>
> **It is not an attribution of legal liability.** It states what was measured. Who is responsible
> for what, and in what proportion, is a legal question that the assessment does not decide.
>
> **And it could not recover a baseline.** Nobody measured that ground before production started in
> the 1950s. Where there is no baseline, an assessment can describe **the condition now** and cannot
> fully separate **what each contributor added**. That is not a flaw in the assessment. It is a
> permanent consequence of not having measured, and it is the single most transferable lesson in this
> unit.
>
> *Our summary of a published assessment, named with its publisher and its year. Read the assessment,
> and then go and find out whether any baseline soil or groundwater data exists for your own site.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write down what you did not sample.** It is the one line that turns a reassuring page into a

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> This is the one subject in this book where the organization's interest and the public's interest can
> genuinely point in opposite directions, and pretending otherwise is dishonest. Sampling can only
> produce two outcomes: a reassuring result that nobody will thank you for, or a finding that creates
> an obligation, a cost and a conversation with people who live there. The incentive not to look is
> real, and it does not require anybody to be dishonest — it only requires the question to stay off
> the form. That is why the assessment in this unit matters so much: it is a published, careful,
> externally produced measurement, and it found that certificates issued in good faith had not been
> testing the thing everybody assumed they tested. The transferable lesson is not about one region. It
> is that *signed off* and *measured* are different words.
> **Ask your group: has anybody ever sampled the ground where you work, and would you want to know?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG re10-f16 | A desk in the evening. A site history lies open with eleven releases and seven of them marked minor with no volume, beside a 2014 completion certificate, a sampling summary covering eleven kilometers of sixty, a map with three village wells inside four hundred meters, a measuring tube with a thin dark layer floating on water, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A corridor has eleven recorded releases over twenty-two years.** Four have a volume. The other seven
are recorded as *minor*, by people who were doing what the form asked, at a time when nobody required
a figure.

Three of the eleven sites have completion certificates. Those certificates record that the surface was
cleaned and inspected, which is what the procedure of the time required. Nobody drilled. There are
three village wells within four hundred meters that have never been sampled, and there is no baseline
data for the site, because production began in 1974.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 What is known about this ground?
2 What would you have to do to be able to answer question 1 properly?
3 Nobody falsified a certificate. What was the procedure not asking?
4 There will never be a baseline for 1974. What can you still create, and for whom?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 11 is about why an organization that has
been told all of this, in writing, more than once, still does not act.
