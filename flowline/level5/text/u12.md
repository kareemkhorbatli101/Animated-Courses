Eleven units, eleven documents, and a pattern you have probably noticed. Almost everything in this
book counts what went wrong, and almost everything in this book has a passage explaining why that is
not enough. The last document says it directly, and then says something harder: that the things that
go right and the things that go wrong have the **same** cause.

%%FIG re12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its publisher and its year, two columns comparing two definitions of safety, a four-box diagram of respond, monitor, learn and anticipate, a boxed paragraph headed what the argument does not claim, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the last document in the course**
> **Source: Erik Hollnagel, *Safety-I and Safety-II: The Past and Future of Safety Management*,
> Ashgate, **2014**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **The first definition.** For about a century, safety has been defined as **the absence of
> accidents and incidents**. The work that follows from that definition is to find out what goes
> wrong, establish its causes, and eliminate them. Every document in this book belongs to that
> tradition, and the tradition has been enormously successful: this industry is far safer than it
> was.
>
> **The problem with the definition.** If safety is an absence, then **safety is measured by counting
> things that are not there**. A good year produces a low number and tells you very little, because
> the same low number is produced by a plant with large margins and by a plant with none — which is
> precisely the mechanism in Unit 6. And as plants get safer, the number gets smaller, so the measure
> gets **less informative** exactly as performance improves.
>
> **The second definition.** Safety can instead be defined as **the ability to succeed under varying
> conditions**: the capacity of a system to keep doing what it is supposed to do when things are not
> as assumed. On that definition, safety is a **presence**, not an absence, and it can be examined on
> an ordinary day when nothing has happened.
>
> **The central argument, and it is the uncomfortable one.** Things do not go right because people
> follow procedures and go wrong because they do not. Procedures are written for conditions that are
> never exactly the conditions on the day. So people **adjust**: they work round a seized valve, they
> read a gauge they know runs high, they start a job in a different order because a crane is late.
> These adjustments are **the reason almost everything works**. Occasionally, in a particular
> combination, an adjustment of exactly the same kind contributes to an accident.
>
> **It follows that success and failure have the same source.** You cannot eliminate the adjustments
> without eliminating the performance that depends on them. An organization that responds to every
> event by adding a constraint eventually removes the flexibility it has been relying on, and it does
> not notice, because flexibility is invisible until it is needed.
>
> **The four abilities.** If safety is a capability, it can be described: the ability to **respond**
> to what happens, to **monitor** what is changing, to **learn** from what has happened, and to
> **anticipate** what has not happened yet. Three of the four can be examined on a quiet day. Only
> *learn* looks backwards, and it is the only one most organizations have a process for.
>
> **Work as imagined and work as done.** The procedure describes work as imagined. What people
> actually do is work as done. The gap is not misconduct; it is the price of operating a real plant.
> An organization that does not know what the gap is at its own site is managing a plant that does not
> exist.
>
> **WHAT THE ARGUMENT DOES NOT CLAIM**
> **It does not say stop investigating accidents.** The author is explicit that the second view
> **complements** the first. Investigating failures remains necessary, and everything in Units 1 to 11
> remains worth doing.
>
> **It does not say procedures are unnecessary.** It says they are approximations, which is what their
> authors also say.
>
> **It does not tell you what to measure.** This is the honest weakness of the argument and the main
> criticism of it. Counting accidents is easy and nearly useless; assessing capability is useful and
> extremely hard, and this book does not hand you a metric.
>
> **And it is an argument, not a finding.** It is one author's framework, widely discussed and widely
> contested, and it has not displaced the older view in practice.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its author, publisher and year. Read the book, and then
> go and ask three people at your site what they do differently from the procedure, and why.*

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG re12-f16 | A desk at the end of the course, evening. The book lies open at the contents page with the twelfth row of the review table filled in by hand. Beside it: a proposed two-column front page, a barrier condition sheet with four names on it, a list of three things three crews said they do differently, a 612 days without injury certificate, and a glass of tea going cold. A clock shows seven.%%

**A site has had a good year, and it is a real good year.** No lost-time injuries in 612 days, an
engaged workforce, a competent HSE officer, and every document in this book now read by fourteen
people who did not have to read it.

The barrier condition sheet has four hazards on it. Two of the four are carried by a single
protection. The isolation certificates still have no proving box. The spill plan has still never been
exercised. Three crews have told somebody what they do differently from the procedure, and two of the
three workarounds are better than the document.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is this site safer than it was a year ago?
2 Nothing has been fixed yet, and fourteen people now know. Is that worth anything?
3 Of everything in this book, what will still be happening at your site in three years?
4 What will you put in the blank row, and where will you write it?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book, and there is no Unit 13.** Book 6 leaves the
plant entirely: contracts, revenue, local content, transparency, and what happens to a country that
has this industry in it.
