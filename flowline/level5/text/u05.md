# Unit 5 · The Permit and the Handover
%%SUB Two pieces of paper for one pump, in two different trays%%
%%GOALS
Describe a permit to work system
Say what a handover must transfer
Report what an inquiry found
Use responsibility language
Say what a system on paper does not prove
%%

On the night of 6 July 1988 an offshore installation in the North Sea was destroyed. A hundred and
sixty-seven people died. A public inquiry sat for thirteen months and published its report in 1990,
and among its findings was an account of a permit to work system that existed, was in use, was
documented, and did not transfer one piece of information across one shift handover.

Every permit form in this industry was rewritten because of it.

%%FIG re05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the inquiry report, its chairman, its publisher and its year, a diagram of two permits on one pump filed in two trays, a four-row handover table, a boxed paragraph headed what the inquiry report is and is not, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the night that rewrote every permit form in the industry**
> **Source: *The Public Inquiry into the Piper Alpha Disaster*, report by the Hon. Lord Cullen,
> presented to Parliament and published by HMSO, **1990**, in two volumes. Printed here in our own
> words, and nothing is attributed to it that it does not say.**
>
> **What the document is.** A public inquiry into the destruction of an offshore production platform
> in the North Sea on **6 July 1988**, in which **167 people died**. The inquiry took evidence over
> many months and published its findings and **106 recommendations**. It led, in that country, to a
> completely different regulatory approach, in which an operator must demonstrate in writing that its
> risks are understood and controlled.
>
> **The part of it this unit is about.** Among the inquiry's findings on the initiating event is an
> account of a **permit to work system**. The published account includes the following elements.
>
> Work was in progress on **one of two condensate injection pumps**. Separately, a **pressure safety
> valve** on that pump had been **removed** for recertification, leaving the pump incapable of safe
> operation, and that work was covered by a **different permit**.
>
> The work on the valve was not completed during the day. At the **shift handover**, the state of that
> work was **not effectively communicated** to the oncoming shift. Later that evening, with the other
> pump unavailable, the pump on which work had been done was **started**.
>
> **The inquiry's findings on the system include**: that permits were **not consistently
> cross-referenced** where more than one permit affected the same equipment; that permits were held
> in a way that did not bring related permits together; that the handover arrangements did not
> reliably transfer the status of **incomplete** work; and that the system as operated depended on
> **informal communication** between individuals rather than on the documentation itself.
>
> **The rest of the report is about escalation, and it is the reason the number is so large.**
> Findings as published include firewalls designed to resist fire rather than explosion; the
> continued supply of hydrocarbon from **pipeline risers** connected to other installations; the
> position of the **accommodation** in relation to the hazard; and the arrangements for emergency
> response and evacuation. The initiating event killed very few people. The escalation killed almost
> everybody.
>
> **WHAT THE REPORT IS AND IS NOT**
> **It is an inquiry into one event, on one installation, under the regulatory regime of that time.**
> The permit arrangements described are the arrangements described. Nothing in the report says that
> any other operator's system was the same.
>
> **It is not a criminal proceeding and does not establish individual liability.** A public inquiry of
> this kind is held to establish what happened and what should change. Different proceedings, with
> different standards of proof, address blame.
>
> **Its recommendations are addressed to a particular industry in a particular country.** What travels
> is not the regulation. What travels is the **mechanism**: two permits, two places, one handover,
> one conclusion that existed in nobody's hands.
>
> **And it cannot make a handover happen.** The report produced better forms everywhere in the world.
> A form with a cross-reference box is only as good as the thirty seconds somebody spends filling it
> in, and the conversation it is supposed to prompt.
>
> *Our summary of a published public inquiry report, named with its chairman, its publisher and its
> year. Read the report, and then go and find out whether any piece of equipment at your own site has
> two live permits on it tonight.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **A handover must end with a sentence beginning *do not*.** Facts are easy and conclusions are hard,
> and every form ever designed collects facts. *P-212 has two permits, one suspended, relief valve
> removed* is three facts that a tired person at the end of a twelve-hour shift has to combine
> correctly in their head. **Do not start P-212 this shift** is the combination already done, in six
> words, by the person who knew. **And fill in the cross-reference box.** It takes thirty seconds and
> it is the only part of the form that knows about the other form.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Permit systems are the most audited thing in this industry. They are also, in almost every
> organization, the thing most obviously treated as paperwork — a queue at a window in the morning, a
> signature to be collected, a form to be got through so the work can start. The inquiry this unit is
> about changed the forms everywhere in the world within a few years, and it could not change the
> queue. The honest test of a permit system is not whether the forms are complete. It is whether a
> stranger, at two in the morning, alone, with only the paperwork in front of them, would reach the
> right conclusion. In most places the answer is no, and the gap is filled by people who happen to
> know, which works until the people who happen to know are on their days off.
> **Ask your group: at two in the morning, with only the paperwork, would a stranger get it right at

**Two permits exist. Both are correctly completed.** The mechanical permit is properly suspended. The
valve permit properly records the removal and the blank flange. Both were issued by an authorized
person, signed by the work party, and filed where the system says to file them.

The cross-reference box on each form is empty. The two forms are in two rooms. The handover passed on
eleven open permits and the words *nothing significant*. Everyone who filled in a form did what their
form required.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Where does the conclusion live?
2 Who in this system was supposed to combine the two facts?
3 Nobody broke a rule. What rule is missing?
4 What would you change tonight, and what would you change this year?
