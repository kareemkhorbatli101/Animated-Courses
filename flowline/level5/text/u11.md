# Unit 11 · Why Nobody Learned
%%SUB Three audits, the same finding, and a sentence that got softer on the way up%%
%%GOALS
Explain why warnings are not acted on
Distinguish a cause from an inference
Use inference language
Say what blaming culture explains

By now you have read an investigation, an inquiry, a method, a standard, two regulations, a framework
and an assessment. Every one of them tells an organization what to do. The uncomfortable question in
this unit is the one that comes next: the information usually exists already, inside the
organization, in writing, more than once. So why does nothing happen?

%%FIG re11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its publisher and its year, a diagram of a finding rising through four levels and losing its number, a three-row table of the structural, informational and attentional reasons, a boxed paragraph headed what this book is and is not, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: why an organization that was told does not act**
> **Source: Andrew Hopkins, *Failure to Learn: The BP Texas City Refinery Disaster*, CCH Australia,
> **2008**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What the book is.** An analysis, by one author, of the refinery explosion you read about in Unit
> 2, written for people who work in industry rather than for academics. It draws on the published
> investigation material and asks a narrower question than the investigations did: **the information
> was there; why did it not produce action?**
>
> **The first argument: two kinds of safety, and only one of them was being managed.** You have met
> this already. The book's contribution is to show how completely a **personal injury focus** can
> occupy the available attention. Injury rates were reported monthly, discussed, compared between
> sites and attached to incentives. Process safety condition was not reported in a comparable way, so
> it was not discussed in a comparable way, and attention is **finite**.
>
> **The second argument: the warnings existed.** Internal audits, reviews, engineering reports and
> staff surveys had raised concerns. People had written them down. The book's point is not that nobody
> knew. It is that **knowing, in a document, somewhere in a large organization, is not the same as the
> organization knowing**.
>
> **The third argument: why information loses its force going up.** A report written at a site
> contains numbers, dates and equipment tags. The reader two levels up has forty sites to consider,
> so somebody summarizes. Summarizing removes detail — that is what it is for — and the detail is
> frequently the finding. *Thirty-eight inspections overdue on relief devices* becomes *backlog being
> addressed* becomes *within normal range*. **Nobody has to lie for this to happen.** And once the
> number is gone, there is nothing left that anybody could act on.
>
> **The fourth argument: structure.** Where responsibility is decentralized, a finding that crosses
> boundaries belongs to nobody. Site management owns the plant, a central function owns the standard,
> a project owns the modification, and the finding sits between them. Each party can act correctly
> within its own boundary while the finding is never owned by anyone.
>
> **The fifth argument: incentives.** Where cost and output are measured precisely and rewarded, and
> process safety condition is measured loosely or not at all, the organization will optimize what it
> measures. This is not a claim that anybody chose production over safety. It is a claim about what
> happens when one thing is on a scorecard and the other is a judgment.
>
> **What the book says would help.** Reporting process safety indicators to the top with the **same
> seriousness** as injury rates. Making somebody **accountable by name** for each major hazard.
> Cultivating what the author, drawing on research into organizations that operate hazardous
> technology reliably, calls **collective mindfulness**: a habit of attending to small signs of
> failure instead of being reassured by their absence. And being very careful with the words **safety
> culture**, which can be used to explain anything and therefore explains nothing.
>
> **WHAT THIS BOOK IS AND IS NOT**
> **It is one author's analysis, not an official finding.** The investigations are the findings. This
> is an argument about them, by a writer with a point of view, and he says so.
>
> **Other analysts emphasize different factors.** Some put more weight on engineering and
> instrumentation, some on regulation, some on the specific decisions of named individuals. The
> disagreement is real and is part of the literature.
>
> **It is about one company and one refinery.** The mechanisms travel. The conclusions about that
> organization do not.
>
> **And it cannot be implemented.** There is no procedure at the back. Three of its five arguments
> are about how attention and information behave in large organizations, which is not something a
> management system can be configured to fix.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its author, publisher and year. Read the book, and then
> go and find a finding at your own site that has been raised more than once.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Keep the number in the sentence all the way up.** One figure, in brackets, survives summary better
> than any argument: *relief valve inspections overdue (51, was 38 in 2019)*. Eleven words. It cannot
> be softened into *being addressed*, because the number is doing the talking. **And require evidence
> on every closure.** Not a signature. A document, a photograph, a test record, a dated list. Closure
> without evidence means somebody said so, and the finding will come back with a larger number, which
> is exactly how you can tell.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> The phrase *safety culture* is the most popular explanation in this industry and the least useful.
> It is popular because it fits any outcome — a good year is a strong culture, a bad year is a weak
> one — and because it locates the problem in something diffuse that nobody has to be accountable for.
> The author of the book in this unit is careful with the phrase for exactly that reason. The
> alternative is less comfortable: the three mechanisms in this unit are all **design** features of
> large organizations, which means they can be changed by design, which means somebody would have to
> change them. A number in a summary line, a document on a closure, a name against a hazard. None of
> those is inspiring and all three are auditable, and that is the point.
> **Ask your group: when something goes wrong at your site, how long is it before somebody says the
> word culture?**
