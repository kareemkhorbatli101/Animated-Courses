# Unit 6 · Why Plants Drift
%%SUB Twelve small decisions, none of them a decision, and a boundary nobody can see%%
%%GOALS
Describe the three boundaries

Nobody at your site has decided to run it closer to the edge. And yet almost every plant, left alone,
moves that way: a little faster, a little leaner, a few fewer checks, a slightly wider tolerance. Each
step is reasonable. Each step is approved. Nobody ever decides.

There is a paper from 1997 about exactly this, and it is the most useful piece of writing in this
book.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG re06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its author, the journal and its year, a diagram of a space with three boundaries and a drifting dot with two arrows, a six-level stack from government down to the work itself, a boxed paragraph headed what the paper does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: why a plant moves when nobody moves it**
> **Source: Jens Rasmussen, "Risk management in a dynamic society: a modeling problem", *Safety
> Science*, **1997**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **The central picture.** Think of the way a plant actually operates as a point inside a space. The
> space has **three boundaries**.
>
> **The boundary of economic failure.** Go too far this way and the operation is not viable.
> **The boundary of unacceptable workload.** Go too far this way and the people cannot do it: they
> leave, they make errors, they stop.
> **The boundary of functionally acceptable performance** — in plainer words, the **safety**
> boundary. Go past this one and you lose control.
>
> **The two gradients.** Inside that space, the way work is actually done is pushed by two steady
> pressures. There is a **management gradient towards efficiency**: do it cheaper, do it faster, do it
> with fewer people. And there is an **individual gradient towards least effort**: do it the way that
> is easiest, which is usually also quickest. **Neither of these is misconduct.** The first is how an
> organization survives. The second is how a person survives thirty years of shift work.
>
> **Both gradients point the same way:** towards the safety boundary. And crucially, **there is no
> equivalent gradient pointing back**, because nothing rewards you for keeping a margin you never
> needed.
>
> **Why the movement is invisible.** The first two boundaries give **feedback**. Cross towards
> economic failure and a monthly report tells you. Push past acceptable workload and people resign or
> make visible mistakes. **The safety boundary gives no feedback at all until it is crossed**, and the
> reason is **defense in depth**: a well-designed plant has several independent protections, so losing
> one produces **no observable consequence whatsoever**. The plant keeps running. The work keeps
> succeeding. The margin is gone and the evidence that it is gone is **nothing happening**.
>
> This is the mechanism by which an experienced, careful, well-intentioned organization ends up with
> no margin and no idea.
>
> **Why it has to be studied across levels.** The paper argues that risk cannot be managed by looking
> at any single level — not the operator, not the plant, not the company. Decisions at the level of
> **government**, **regulators**, **company management**, **plant management**, **staff** and **the
> work itself** all interact, and a change at one level alters what is reasonable at another. Studying
> errors by individuals, in isolation, cannot see any of this.
>
> **What follows practically.** If the boundary gives no feedback, you must **construct** the
> feedback. Mark the boundary. Decide in advance what the margin is, write down where it is, measure
> whether you still have it, and make the loss of a protection a **reportable event in itself**, even
> though nothing happened. Nothing happening is exactly the condition under which drift is invisible.
>
> **WHAT THE PAPER DOES NOT DO**
> **It is not a method.** It is a framework and an argument about how risk should be modeled. It gives
> you no procedure and no forms.
>
> **It does not tell you where the boundary is.** It tells you that the boundary is not marked, and
> that you must mark it. Where to put the mark is your engineering judgment, and the paper does not
> pretend otherwise.
>
> **It does not predict anything.** It explains a direction of movement. It cannot tell you how far
> along it any particular plant has got, which is frustrating and honest.
>
> **And it does not excuse anybody.** Saying that drift is a systemic phenomenon is not the same as
> saying that nobody is responsible for noticing it.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its author, journal and year. Read the paper, and then
> go and list every small change at your own site over the last five years on one sheet.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Drift is the hardest thing in this book to raise, because there is no villain and no event. An
> investigation into an accident has a date, a photograph and a report. Drift has a change register
> and an afternoon of somebody's time, and the finding is that a plant that has had no incidents for
> five years is in worse condition than it was. That is an extremely unwelcome sentence in any
> organization, and the people most likely to be able to say it are the people with the least
> standing: the ones who have been there long enough to remember four rounds a night, and are not
> senior enough to be asked. Which is why the one-page list matters. A list with dates on it does not
> need standing.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG re06-f16 | A desk in the evening. A single sheet lies open with twelve numbered changes, nine approver names and five years of dates, beside a protection status list showing one of three remaining, a rounds sheet with four crossed to two, an empty pallet photograph from a store, a five-year incident record showing none, and a glass of tea. **A plant has had no incidents in five years.** That is true, it is recorded, and it is the figure in
every report. In the same five years, twelve changes have been approved by nine different competent
people, each one below the threshold requiring assessment, each one for a reason any of us would have
accepted.

The system that had three independent protections in 2019 has one. Nobody removed two protections.
Two protections were, at different times, for different sensible reasons, allowed to become
unavailable and not restored.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is this plant safer than it was in 2019?
2 The incident record says yes. What does the incident record actually measure?
3 Nobody decided this. So who could have noticed it, and when?
4 What one report would you create, and what would trigger it?
