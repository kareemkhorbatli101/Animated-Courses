# Starting Point · How Things Actually Go Wrong
%%SUB Four books of plant, and the question none of them answered%%

You have spent four books learning what the equipment does. A well, a reservoir, a pipeline, a
refinery, and about two hundred numbers with their definitions attached. None of it has gone wrong
yet, except on paper.

This book is about the going wrong. Not the physics of it — you have that — but the part that keeps
surprising people: **the organization**.

### 1 · What you already have

**1A  Look at the six rows with a partner. Say which one you trust least, and say why.**

**1B  Read the six examples aloud, taking turns. Then discuss: why is a signature with a date the
easiest of the six to produce and the hardest to trust?**

**1C  Listen to the six rows read aloud.** %%AUD FL5-00-01%%

| What you have | An example |
|---|---|
| A hazard | pressure · gas · a hot surface · a confined space |
| A barrier | a relief valve · a shutdown system · a bund · a permit |
| A number with its definition | 354 degrees, by a named method, reproducible to four |
| A document with a scope page | the standard says what it does not cover |
| A tolerance with a confidence | plus or minus ten percent, at eighty percent confidence |
| A signature with a date | two names on a checklist, and a dash where a tick would lie |

Every one of those six appears in an accident report, and usually all six at once.

### 2 · The four shapes of a bad day

%%FIG re00-f2 | Four panels: a single violation of a clear rule, a drift over years with nobody deciding anything, a protection that was tested but never sized again, and two failures arriving together that nobody had put on one list.%%

**2A  Read the four shapes aloud. Say which one you have seen.**

| The shape | What it looks like afterwards |
|---|---|
| **A violation** | somebody did a thing they knew was wrong |
| **A drift** | nothing was decided; everything moved, slowly, for good reasons |
| **A stale assumption** | the hardware was fine; the paper it was sized from was old |
| **Two at once** | each failure was on somebody's list, and never on one list |

**Nearly every investigation in this book found the second or the third, and almost none found the
first on its own.** That is the single most useful fact in this book, and it is also the one most
organizations do not act on, because the first shape has somebody to blame and the others do not.

**2B  Discuss in threes.** Why do you think the first shape is the one most often reported? Say what
would have to change for the second to be reported instead.

### 3 · Twelve documents, four years apart from each other

%%FIG re00-f3 | A timeline from 1931 to 2014 with twelve marks on it, each labeled with a kind of document: a book, an inquiry report, a method, a standard, a regulation, a paper, a guidance note, a framework, an assessment, and a book again.%%

**3A  Look at the timeline. Say how many years it covers.**

The oldest document in this book was published in **1931**. The most recent in **2014**. In between
are an inquiry into a night in 1988, a paper from 1997, an investigation into an explosion in 2005,
and an assessment published in 2011.

**Three of them disagree with each other**, and they are printed as they are:

> **1931:** most accidents are caused by the unsafe acts of individual workers.
> **1997:** organizations move towards their boundaries because of ordinary pressures, and nobody
> decides to.
> **2014:** counting the things that go wrong tells you very little about safety.

**3B  Ask and answer with a partner.** Which of those three statements does your own workplace behave
as if it believes? Say why you think so.

**3C  Listen to the three statements read aloud.** %%AUD FL5-00-02%%

### 4 · The thread

%%FIG re00-f4 | A diagram of a signature line with four callouts: who signed, what they were signing, what they had been told, and whether the conversation the signature records actually happened.%%

**4A  Look at the four callouts. Say which one a filing system can check.**

Four of the twelve units in this book are about a **signature**: a permit, an isolation certificate,
an entry authorization, a handover. In all four, the same thing is true, and it is the thread of the
whole book:

> **The paper is not the protection. The conversation it forces is the protection. The paper is the
> evidence that the conversation happened.**

A system where the conversation has stopped and the signing continues looks, from the outside,
exactly like a system that is working. That is why this book exists, and it is why Unit 12 argues
that the whole industry counts the wrong things.

**4B  Discuss in your group.** Think of one form you sign regularly. **Say what conversation it is
supposed to record, and say honestly whether that conversation happens.** Then say who would ever
find out if it stopped.

**4C  Listen to two people describe the same signature.** %%AUD FL5-00-03%% Which one says what the
conversation was?

### 5 · What this book will not do

**5A  Read the four lines aloud with a partner. Then say which one surprises you.**

> · It will **not** make you competent to issue a permit, isolate a line or authorize an entry.
> · It will **not** give you a safety culture, because nobody can give anybody one.
> · It will **not** tell you that any of these documents is the right one. Three of them contradict
> each other on purpose.
> · It will give you the **English** to say *I am not claiming that; I am asking who can*, which is
> the most useful sentence in this subject.

**5B  Say the sentence in the last line aloud twice.** You will write it, in one form or another, in
nine of the twelve units.

**5C  Ask and answer with a partner.** Why do you think that sentence is more useful at work than an
opinion about whether something is safe?

**Unit 1 is a book from 1931** whose triangular diagram is probably on a wall somewhere at your site,
and whose data nobody has ever been able to check.
