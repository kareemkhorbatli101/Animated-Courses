There is a method for finding out how a plant could fail before it has failed. It works. It has
prevented a very large number of accidents, and most of them will never be counted, because a
prevented accident leaves no record. It is also entirely dependent on who sits in the room and on
whether the drawing on the table is the plant outside the window.

%%FIG re03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author and publisher, and the international standard with its number, a drawing of a node between two marks on a pipe, a seven-row guide word table, a five-column worksheet extract, a boxed paragraph headed what the method cannot find, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: a structured way of imagining failure**
> **Sources: Trevor Kletz, *HAZOP and HAZAN: Identifying and Assessing Process Industry Hazards*,
> Institution of Chemical Engineers, first published **1983** and revised in later editions; and
> **IEC 61882**, *Hazard and operability studies (HAZOP studies) — Application guide*. Printed here
> in our own words.**
>
> **The two things in the title are different.** A hazard and operability study — a HAZOP — is a way
> of **finding** what could go wrong. A hazard analysis — a HAZAN — is a way of **estimating how
> likely it is and how bad**. Kletz is insistent that they are separate activities, done by different
> methods, often by different people, and that confusing them is a mistake. **Finding is not
> quantifying.**
>
> **How a study works.** A drawing of the plant is divided into **nodes**: sections small enough to
> have a single **design intention**. For each node the team states that intention in plain words —
> *eighty cubic meters an hour of treated water at one hundred and forty bar, from the pump discharge
> to the manifold*.
>
> Then the team applies **guide words** to each **parameter** of that intention: flow, pressure,
> temperature, level, composition. Seven guide words, used in order: **no, more, less, as well as,
> part of, reverse, other than**.
>
> Each combination gives a **deviation**: *no flow*, *more pressure*, *reverse flow*. For each
> deviation the team asks four questions, and the answers go in four columns: **what could cause it**,
> **what would happen**, **what already protects us**, and **what action is required**.
>
> **Why guide words at all?** Because a team asked *what could go wrong here* will say what it
> remembers. A team asked *what if there were reverse flow* has to think about reverse flow whether it
> remembers an example or not. The guide word is a device for making a group of experienced people
> **search systematically instead of recalling**.
>
> **WHAT THE METHOD CANNOT FIND**
> **A hazard nobody in the room knows about.** This is the fundamental limit, and no amount of rigor
> removes it. The study finds what the team can imagine between them. Six people who have all worked
> on the same kind of plant for twenty years will imagine the same things and miss the same things.
> **The composition of the team is the single biggest determinant of the quality of the study**, and
> it is usually decided by who was available.
>
> **Anything the drawing gets wrong.** The study is performed on a drawing. If the plant has a spool
> piece where the drawing shows a valve, or a line that was blanked in 2014 and never marked up, the
> study has examined something that does not exist. Checking the drawing against the plant is tedious,
> unpopular and frequently skipped.
>
> **Anything that changed afterwards.** A study describes a plant on a day. Plants are modified. A
> study is a **photograph**, and it needs revalidating when the plant stops matching it.
>
> **And the study does not protect anybody at all until the actions are done.** A completed study
> with an open action list has found the hazard and not removed it. Kletz is blunt about this: the
> most common failure of the method is in **follow-up**, not in the study room. An action closed as
> *no action required* with no reason recorded is an action that has been deleted, not answered.
>
> *Our summary of a published book and a published international standard, named with their authors
> and numbers. Read them, and then go and find the action list from the last study at your own site
> and count the closures.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **When a safeguard is recorded, write where you saw it.** *Recirculation line, per drawing* and
> *recirculation line, confirmed on plant 14 May* are two completely different statements and they
> look the same on a worksheet. **And never close an action as *no action required* without the
> reason on the same line.** The reason takes eleven words. Without it, the next team to look at that
> node has no idea whether the question was answered or abandoned, and will usually assume it was
> answered.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> A hazard study is one of the few genuinely successful inventions in industrial safety. It is cheap,
> it needs no instruments, it works on a drawing and a table, and the accidents it prevents are
> invisible, which is its curse. Nobody is thanked for a study. The study room is also one of the few
> places in this industry where a junior person is expected to speak, because the method needs
> somebody who has not spent twenty years learning what is normal. That is written into the method
> and it almost never happens, because the senior people in the room answer first and the room moves
> on. The chairperson's real job is not the guide words. It is noticing who has not spoken.
> **Ask your group: in the last study you attended, who spoke least, and what did they know?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG re03-f16 | A desk in the evening. A close-out summary lies open showing sixty-one actions and forty-four closed, beside a worksheet row reading safeguard: recirculation line, per drawing, a photograph of a blanked flange, a drawing revision box dated 2011, a list of three modifications since, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A hazard study was done properly.** A competent chairperson, a full team, the right method, a
scribe, seven guide words applied node by node. Sixty-one actions were raised, which is the sign of a
study that was taken seriously. Forty-four are closed.

The study was done on a drawing from 2011. One safeguard it relied on had been physically removed
before the study took place. Nine actions were closed as *no action required* with no reason written
down, and nobody now remembers the discussions.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Was this a good study?
2 The method says to verify safeguards and mark up drawings. Why does that not happen?
3 Nine discussions happened and none was recorded. What is lost, exactly?
4 What two changes would you make to the close-out form?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 4 is the standard that tells you how to
choose between methods like this one, and why choosing is itself a judgment.
