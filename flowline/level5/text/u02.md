On 23 March 2005 a refinery unit in the United States was being started up. A tower was overfilled,
liquid went out of a vent, a vapor cloud formed, and it found an ignition source. Fifteen people
died. Nearly all of them were in portable office buildings parked near the unit.

The company's personal injury statistics that year were among the best it had.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG re02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the two documents, the board and the panel that published them and their years, a diagram of a tower overfilling into a vent stack with a cloud at ground level, a two-column table of personal and process metrics, a boxed paragraph headed what the two documents do not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the year the injury figures were good**
> **Sources: the investigation report of the **US Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board**
> into the **BP Texas City refinery explosion and fire of 23 March 2005**, published **2007**; and
> *The Report of the BP U.S. Refineries Independent Safety Review Panel*, chaired by James Baker III,
> published **January 2007**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What happened, as the investigation describes it.** A unit was being started up. A tower was
> filled far beyond the level intended. Liquid went up and out of a vent stack that discharged to
> atmosphere. A heavier-than-air vapor cloud formed at ground level, found an ignition source, and
> exploded. **Fifteen people were killed and many more injured.** Most of those killed were in or
> near **portable office buildings** that had been placed close to the unit.
>
> **What the investigation found about the mechanisms.** The findings as published include: level
> **indication** that did not show the operators what was actually in the tower; a **high-level alarm**
> that did not function as intended; no **automatic** action to stop the filling; a vent arrangement
> that discharged flammable liquid and vapor **to atmosphere** rather than to a safer system; and
> **occupied portable buildings sited near a unit** being started up, without a hazard review that
> asked whether they should be there.
>
> **What the investigation found about the organization.** This is the part this unit is about. The
> findings as published include **cost and staffing reductions affecting maintenance and
> infrastructure**, a **backlog** of overdue work, warnings raised in internal audits and surveys and
> not acted on, and — repeatedly — **the use of personal injury rates as the measure of safety
> performance**, in an organization whose process safety condition was deteriorating.
>
> **What the review panel added.** The panel was asked to look at corporate safety culture, process
> safety management systems and oversight at the company's refineries. Its conclusions as published
> include that **personal injury rates are not an adequate measure of process safety performance**,
> that **process safety requires its own leadership attention, its own metrics and its own
> expertise**, and that a good personal injury record can **coexist** with serious process safety
> deficiencies.
>
> **WHAT THESE TWO DOCUMENTS DO NOT DO**
> **They are not law.** An investigation board makes findings and recommendations; it does not write
> regulations, and recommendations are addressed to named parties who may or may not act on them.
>
> **They are about one event at one site, and one company's refineries.** Nothing in either document
> entitles anybody to say that the same conditions exist anywhere else. What travels is the
> **mechanism**, not the conclusion.
>
> **The panel's remit was explicitly not the cause of the accident.** It examined management systems
> and culture. Reading it as an account of why the explosion happened is reading it as something it
> says it is not.
>
> **And neither document is a judgment of individual blame.** Investigations of this kind are
> conducted to establish what happened and what should change. Other proceedings, with different
> rules and different purposes, deal with liability.
>
> *Our summary of two published documents, named with their publishers and their years. Read them,
> and then go and look at where the portable buildings are parked at your own site.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every organization measures what it can measure. Injuries to people are countable, comparable
> between sites, reportable to regulators and visible within weeks. Process safety condition is a
> judgment assembled from backlogs, instrument reliability, audit findings and the quality of hazard
> reviews, none of which produces a single number anybody can put in an annual report. So the
> countable thing becomes the published thing, and then it becomes the managed thing, and eventually
> people inside the organization believe it is the subject rather than one part of it. The investigation
> this unit is about is the clearest published case of that happening, which is why it is read all over
> the world by people who have never been to that refinery.
> **Ask your group: which safety figure does your senior management see monthly, and which one do
> they see once a year?**

**Every figure on both sheets is correct.** The personal safety figures are the best the site has ever
produced, and they were produced by real work by real people. The process safety figures moved the
other way in the same twelve months. Nobody has hidden either sheet; they are in two different
documents going to two different meetings.

Four occupied cabins sit within fifty meters of a unit. They have been there for eleven years. Each
one was installed by a properly approved project.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is this site getting safer?
2 Which document would an investigator ask for, and why?
3 Eleven years and four approved projects produced the cabin situation. What question was each
project not asked?
4 What would you put on the front page of next month's report?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 3 is the method for imagining the
failure before it happens, and the reason it so often finds nothing.
