# Unit 4 · Choosing the Method
%%SUB A standard about choosing, a hierarchy about designing, and a number that changes nothing%%
%%GOALS
Name the levels of the hierarchy
Describe a barrier diagram
Select a method and justify it
Say what a risk figure can and cannot decide
Say when a decision is cheapest
%%

Unit 3 was a method. This unit is about the document that tells you **which** method, and when, and
on what grounds. That sounds like paperwork about paperwork. It is actually the most expensive
decision in the whole subject, because it is made at the beginning, when changing the design still
costs almost nothing.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG re04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its number, its year and the organization that publishes it, a staircase of five levels, a bow-tie diagram with threats, barriers, a top event and consequences, a boxed paragraph headed what the standard does not give you, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the document that tells you which method to use**
> **Source: **ISO 17776:2016**, *Petroleum and natural gas industries — Offshore production
> installations — Major accident hazard management during the design of new installations*,
> International Organization for Standardization. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What kind of document this is.** It is not a method. It is a standard about **choosing and using
> methods**, for the management of major accident hazards during **design**. That makes it unusual and
> easy to misread. It will not tell you how to run a study. It will tell you what to think about when
> deciding **which** study, **when**, and **how much effort** it deserves.
>
> **The first idea: the hierarchy.** Hazards should be dealt with in order of preference.
> **Eliminate** the hazard if you can. If you cannot, **prevent** the event. If you cannot prevent it,
> **detect** it early. Then **control** it. Then **mitigate** the harm. The order matters because the
> higher levels depend on equipment working, on people responding and on assumptions holding, and the
> lowest level does not depend on anything, because the hazard is not there.
>
> **An eliminated hazard needs no maintenance, no testing, no procedure, no competent person and no
> permit.** That sentence is the whole argument for inherently safer design, and it is almost always
> made too late to act on.
>
> **The second idea: barriers, drawn as a diagram.** Put the loss of control — the **top event** — in
> the middle. On the left, the **threats** that could cause it. On the right, the **consequences** that
> could follow. Between the threats and the event, and between the event and the consequences, draw
> the **barriers** as vertical bars. The shape is called a bow-tie, and the useful questions it forces
> are: how many barriers are there really, are any two of them the same thing wearing two hats, who
> maintains each one, **what tests it**, and **what would tell you it had failed**.
>
> **The third idea: proportionate effort.** Not every hazard deserves the same analysis. The standard
> expects **screening** first — a quick pass that sorts hazards by how much attention they need — and
> then the choice of technique for each group. A qualitative study may be entirely sufficient. A
> quantified assessment may be necessary where a decision genuinely depends on a number, where
> several options must be compared, or where the hazard is dominated by rare events.
>
> **The fourth idea: as low as reasonably practicable.** Risk reduction continues until the effort of
> further reduction is grossly disproportionate to the benefit. That is a judgment, not an
> arithmetic.
>
> **WHAT THE STANDARD DOES NOT GIVE YOU**
> **A threshold.** It does not tell you what level of risk is tolerable. Those figures, where they
> exist at all, come from national regulators and differ between countries.
>
> **A method.** It names and characterizes techniques. It does not teach any of them.
>
> **Existing plant.** Its scope is the **design of new installations**, and offshore production
> installations at that. The reasoning travels anywhere. The scope does not, and a scope page is a
> scope page.
>
> **And it cannot solve the central problem, which it states honestly:** the decisions that matter
> most are available only at the beginning, when the least is known. By the time you have the
> information to be certain, the vessel is bought, the structure is built, and *eliminate* is no
> longer on the list.
>
> *Our summary of a published international standard, named with its number and its year. Read the
> standard, and then go and look at a barrier diagram for something at your own site and ask what
> tests each bar.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write down what you rejected.** A method selection record that says *we did a qualitative study*
> is worth very little. One that says *we did a qualitative study, a quantified assessment was
> considered and rejected because the two options do not differ materially, and here is the screening

> barriers by their instruments, not by the bars on the drawing.** Two bars from one transmitter is
> one barrier, and it takes thirty seconds to find out.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> A number is extraordinarily comfortable. It can be put on a slide, compared with last year, carried
> into a review and defended by somebody who does not understand it. A list of findings cannot do any
> of those things, and a sentence saying *two of these four barriers are the same transmitter* is
> actively unwelcome, because somebody has to do something about it. So organizations buy numbers,
> and the numbers are usually correct, and they frequently decide nothing at all. The question *what
> decision does this change* is the cheapest question in engineering management and the least often
> asked, because asking it in front of a consultant's proposal is socially expensive.
> **Ask your group: what was the last study at your site, and what did it change?**
