# Unit 8 · Proving the Line Is Dead
%%SUB A closed valve, a drain that ran clear, and the difference between those two facts%%
%%GOALS
Name the isolation standards
Say what proportionate means here
Describe how an isolation is proved
Use proving language
Say what a certificate does not establish
%%

Somebody is going to break a flange. Before they do, somebody has to establish that there is nothing
behind it. That sounds like the simplest task in this book. It is the job that produces the largest
number of releases, and almost always for the same reason: the valve was closed, and closed is not
the same as proved.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG re08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the guidance, its number, the body that published it and its year, a column of four isolation arrangements with increasing hazard beside them, a diagram of a drain, a vent and a gauge, a boxed paragraph headed what guidance cannot do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: four standards, one proving step**
> **Source: **HSG253**, *The safe isolation of plant and equipment*, published by the Health and
> Safety Executive of Great Britain, second edition **2006**. Printed here in our own words. **It is
> guidance from another country and does not apply here.** It is in this book because it is the
> clearest published account of the subject.**
>
> **The first principle: the isolation must match the hazard.** The guidance does not say *always use
> a spade* or *a closed valve is enough*. It sets out a range of arrangements and says the choice
> should be **proportionate** to what is in the line, how much of it there is, what pressure it is at,
> what it would do to somebody, and **how long the work will last**. Duration matters as much as
> hazard, because every hour behind an isolation is another hour for something to be opened in error.
>
> **The arrangements, weakest to strongest.** A **single valve**, closed and secured. **Double block
> and bleed**: two valves in series with a bleed point between them, so that opening the bleed shows
> whether either valve is passing. Double block and bleed **plus a positive isolation** — a spade, a
> slip plate or a spectacle plate — beyond it. And **physical disconnection**: the line broken, swung
> away and blanked at both ends.
>
> A **positive isolation** is a solid barrier. Nothing can pass through a plate. A valve is a
> mechanical device with a seat, and a seat wears.
>
> **The second principle: an isolation is not complete until it is proved.** This is the part most
> often missing from certificates. Closing a valve is a **position**. Proving is a **measurement**:
> depressurize, drain, vent, and watch a gauge at a point you can see, **after** the isolation is in
> place, for long enough to be sure it is not creeping. If a bleed is fitted, open it. If the line has
> a drain, drain it, and **know what you expect to come out**.
>
> **The third principle: stored energy does not care about the supply.** A depressurized line can hold
> a trapped liquid head in a vertical leg, pressure trapped between two closed valves, a nitrogen or
> instrument air connection still live, a spring, an electrical supply from a second source, or a
> blocked **dead leg** with sludge in it that will come out when the flange moves. Each one of those
> has released on somebody who had correctly isolated the supply.
>
> **The fourth principle: write it down and mark it up.** An isolation has a certificate: what is
> isolated, by what arrangement, at which points, who fitted it, who proved it and **how**, who may
> remove it, and when. The points should be identified on a marked-up drawing, because a point
> described in words is a point somebody else will find a similar one of.
>
> **WHAT GUIDANCE CANNOT DO**
> **It is guidance from one country and does not apply here**, and in that country it supports legal
> duties rather than replacing them. Your own national requirements govern.
>
> **It cannot tell you what is in the line.** Nothing can. The line contains what forty years of
> operation put in it, and a dead leg is exactly the place nobody has looked.
>
> **It cannot detect a passing valve for you.** It can tell you to fit a bleed and watch it, which is
> the only reason anybody ever finds out.
>
> **And it cannot make the proving step happen on a Friday afternoon**, with a crew waiting and a
> gauge that would need somebody to stand and watch it for twenty minutes. That is where this subject
> actually lives.
>
> *Our summary of published guidance, named with its number, its publisher and its year. Read your own
> national requirements, and then go and look at the last ten isolation certificates at your site and
> count the ones with a proving step recorded.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write two things on every isolation certificate: how it was proved, and what you expected to come
> out.** The first stops a position being mistaken for a measurement. The second is the one almost
> nobody writes, and it is the one that catches a blocked drain, because *clear* is only good news if
> clear is what you were expecting. Four words — **expected: black sludge** — turn a reassuring
> result into a question. **And when the duration of a job changes, the isolation standard changes
> with it.** A half-day job that becomes two days is a different job.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Isolation is where the whole of this book becomes physical. A permit is paper, a hazard study is a
> table, drift is a list — and an isolation is a man with a wrench on a flange who is about to find
> out whether the paperwork was true. Everybody in this industry knows somebody who has been sprayed.
> And yet the proving step, which is the only part that establishes anything, is the step most often
> compressed, because it is the only part that requires standing still. A gauge watched for twenty
> minutes looks, to a passing supervisor, exactly like four people doing nothing. That is the honest
> reason it gets skipped, and any serious attempt to fix it has to start by making standing and
> watching a visible, respectable, recordable activity.
> **Ask your group: who at your site is allowed to stand and watch a gauge for twenty minutes without
> being asked what they are doing?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG re08-f16 | A desk in the evening. An isolation certificate lies open reading single valve, closed and locked, with the drain box ticked and ran clear written beside it, two blank boxes, a rod with black sludge on the end of it, a photograph of a spade not yet fitted, a job card showing a half day changed to two days, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**The crew did more than most crews do.** They locked the valve, they tagged it, they opened the drain,
and they watched it. Nothing came out. Every box on the certificate that exists was filled in
correctly, and the certificate has no box for what they expected.

The drain was blocked with two inches of iron sulfide sludge. The job had grown from half a day to
two days, and nobody re-chose the isolation standard, because there is no step in the process that
asks.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Would you have broken that flange?
2 The crew did everything the form asked. What was the form missing?
3 A half-day job became a two-day job. Who should have noticed, and how?
4 Which two boxes would you add, and which one would you put first?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 9 is the day it is already out, already
moving, and already somebody else's coastline.
