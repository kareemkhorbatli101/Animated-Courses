### 2 · Read

%%FIG rc01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the code, its number, its committee and its publisher, a diagram of a pipeline system with a tank farm at one end and a delivery point at the other with the covered section marked, a four-row table of what a code gives and does not give, a boxed paragraph headed where the scope ends, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: what the code on the shelf covers**
> **Source: **ASME B31.4**, *Pipeline Transportation Systems for Liquids and Slurries*, one of the
> codes in the ASME **B31 Code for Pressure Piping**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> A design code is a document that says, for a stated pressure and a stated steel, how thick the pipe
> has to be, how it must be welded and inspected, how it must be tested before it is used, and what
> has to be done to it while it is in service.
>
> **What this one covers.** Liquid pipeline systems: the line itself, and the pump stations, tank
> farms, terminals and delivery points along it. Broadly, the pipe between where the liquid is
> produced or stored and where it is handed over.
>
> **How the thickness comes out.** Steel is bought to a specification which states a **specified
> minimum yield strength** — the stress below which the steel is not expected to deform permanently.
> The code does not let you design to that figure. It applies a **design factor**, a number below one,
> and the resulting allowable stress is what the wall thickness is calculated from. In this code the
> factor used for most of the pipeline length is **0.72**, which means the design sits at about
> seventy-two percent of the specified minimum yield, with the rest as margin.
>
> That margin is not spare capacity. It is the room the line will need for everything nobody has
> calculated: a dent, a scratch from a digger, thirty years of internal corrosion, a weld that was
> acceptable rather than perfect.
>
> **The test.** Before a new line is used it is filled with water and pressurized **above** its
> intended operating pressure, and held. A hydrostatic test does two things: it finds defects large
> enough to fail at that pressure, and it proves nothing at all about defects that are smaller.
>
> **WHERE THE SCOPE ENDS**
> Every code has a **scope** section, and it is the most important page in the book. This one says
> what it applies to and then says what it does **not**.
>
> It does **not** cover piping inside refineries, process plants and similar facilities. That is a
> different code in the same family, with a **lower** design factor, because inside a plant there are
> people standing next to the pipe all day. Same steel, same pressure, different rules, because the
> consequence of a failure is different.
>
> It does **not** cover gas pipelines. Those are another code again.
>
> And so **somebody has to draw a line** — on a drawing, usually at a flange, usually at a fence —
> and say: this side is pipeline, that side is plant. That line is a judgment, made once, by a person,
> often decades ago. Where it was drawn wrongly, or where the plant has since grown out past it, you
> have pipe designed as cross-country line with people working beside it. Nobody will notice, because
> the pipe does not know which code it was designed to.
>
> **And a code is a minimum.** It states requirements, in the present tense, for a new system. It
> does not say your forty-year-old line is fit for service today, it does not predict corrosion, and
> it does not make anybody competent. The editions change, the factors occasionally change, and a
> company's own standard may be stricter — which is allowed, and is the opposite of a problem.
>
> *Our summary of a published code, named with its number and its publisher. Read the published code,
> and write down the edition you read. Start at the scope.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Codes get a bad reputation in every industry, and the reason is almost always that somebody has met
> one as an obstacle rather than as a document. Read as an obstacle, a code is a list of things
> stopping you. Read as a document, it is a record of what an industry learned, usually from failures
> and usually at a price, written down so that nobody has to learn it again the same way. The design
> factor in this unit is a number somebody chose after a long argument about consequences. It is not
> bureaucracy; it is the shape of a disagreement that was settled before you were born.
> **Ask your group: which rule at your site do people find most annoying, and does anybody know why it
