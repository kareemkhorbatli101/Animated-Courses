# Unit 5 · The Pump Curve
%%SUB The machine that pays for the friction, and what *just enough* actually means%%
%%GOALS

Say what the operating point is
Say what NPSH is and why the margin matters

%%

Everything in the last three units costs pressure, and something has to put the pressure back. A
centrifugal pump has a curve, the system has a curve, and the pump runs where the two cross. Most
pumps on most sites are not running where anybody intended, and the datasheet in the file does not
know that.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rc05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its number, its publisher and the identical international standard, a curve sheet with four lines on it and a preferred region shaded, a three-row table of suction head, a boxed paragraph headed how the required suction head is defined, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the curve, the crossing point, and the thing at the inlet**
> **Source: **API Standard 610**, *Centrifugal Pumps for Petroleum, Petrochemical and Natural Gas
> Industries*, published as an identical standard by ISO under its own number. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> This standard tells a pump manufacturer what to supply for this industry: how the pump is built,
> what it must achieve on test, what the bearings and the seal must be capable of, how much vibration
> is acceptable, and **where on its curve it is intended to run**.
>
> **The crossing point.** A centrifugal pump gives less head as the flow rises: its curve falls. The
> system it is pumping into needs more head as the flow rises, because friction rises: that curve
> climbs. **A pump runs where the two curves cross**, and nowhere else. If you want a different flow
> you must change one of the two curves, by trimming the impeller, changing the speed, or opening and
> closing valves — and the last of those is paying for pressure and then throwing it away.
>
> **The preferred region.** Every pump has a flow at which it is most efficient. The standard sets out
> a **preferred operating region** around that flow and expects the rated point to sit within a stated
> band of it. The reason is not efficiency; it is **force**. Away from that region the pressure around
> the impeller becomes uneven, the shaft is pushed sideways, and the bearings and the mechanical seal
> take a load they were not sized for. A pump running far off its best point does not stop working. It
> fails repeatedly, in the same two components, for years.
>
> **Below a minimum flow it is worse.** The liquid recirculates inside the casing, heats up and
> vibrates. The standard requires the manufacturer to state a minimum continuous stable flow, and that
> number belongs on the operator's wall, not in the file.
>
> **The thing at the inlet.** A pump cannot pull. It can only accept liquid that arrives with enough
> pressure already in it to stay liquid as it is accelerated into the impeller. The pressure available
> at the inlet, above the liquid's own boiling pressure, is the **net positive suction head
> available**. What the pump needs is the **net positive suction head required**. Available must
> exceed required, with a margin.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **available** | what your system gives: the vessel pressure, the liquid level, minus the suction line losses |
> | **required** | what the pump needs, from the manufacturer's test |
> | **the margin** | available minus required, and it must be positive with room to spare |
>
> If available falls below required, vapor forms at the impeller inlet and collapses a few
> millimeters later. That is **cavitation**: it sounds like gravel, it removes metal from the vane, and
> it will destroy an impeller in months.
>
> **HOW THE REQUIRED SUCTION HEAD IS DEFINED**
> This is the part of the subject that catches people, and it is worth reading twice.
>
> The required figure is established by a **test**, and the test's criterion is the suction condition
> at which the pump's head has already fallen by a defined small amount — conventionally **three per
> cent**.
>
> So *required* does not mean *the point at which cavitation begins*. It means **the point at which
> cavitation has already begun and is costing three per cent of the head**. A pump operating with
> available suction head exactly equal to required is not comfortable. It is cavitating slightly, all
> day, every day.
>
> That is why every competent specification asks for a **margin** above the required figure rather than
> equality, and why *we meet the NPSH requirement* is one of the most misleading true sentences in
> this industry.
>
> **And what this standard does not do.** It is a **purchase specification**. It tells a manufacturer
> what to deliver. It says nothing about the pump you already have: not what its curve looks like
> after fifteen years of impeller wear, not whether the flow it was bought for still exists, and not
> whether the figures on the datasheet in your file were ever achieved on site. The curve supplied was
> measured on a test fluid under test conditions, and your fluid is not that fluid.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its number and its publisher. Read the published
> standard, and then go and read the curve sheet for a pump you operate.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write the three flows together: bought for, best efficiency, running at.** Those three numbers on
> one line explain most repeat pump failures on most sites in about four seconds, and almost no
> maintenance record contains them. And never write *NPSH requirements are met* on its own. Write the
> two figures and the margin, because *met* can mean a margin of three meters or of three centimeters,
> and at zero margin the pump is cavitating while fully compliant with its datasheet.

Eleven mechanical seal faces lie in a row, all worn identically, beside a pump curve sheet with the best efficiency point and the actual flow far apart, eleven closed work orders in a stack, an invoice for an upgraded seal trial, a control valve throttled to twenty per cent open, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A pump has had eleven mechanical seals in eleven years.** Every replacement was done properly, by a
competent mechanic, and every work order was closed on time. The pump has never once failed to move
oil when it was needed. On the reliability figures it appears as a well-maintained machine with a seal
problem.

It runs at half the flow it was bought for, because the field makes less oil than it did in 1998. The
difference is taken up by a control valve that has been eighty per cent shut for a decade.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Whose job was it to notice, and why was it nobody's?
2 Eleven work orders closed on time. What did the measure of success hide?
3 The control valve has been eighty per cent shut for ten years. What has that cost?
4 What one page, read once a year, would have caught this?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 6 is the machine that goes inside the
pipe, and what a reported wall thickness actually means.
