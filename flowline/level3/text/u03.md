# Unit 3 · The Chart Everybody Still Uses
%%SUB An accurate chart, and the one number going into it that nobody has measured%%
%%GOALS
Say what a friction factor is

In 1944 an engineer published a single chart that let anybody calculate the pressure lost in a pipe.
Eighty years later it is in every handbook, on every engineer's wall, and inside every piece of
pipeline software. It is accurate. One of the two numbers you have to put into it is a guess.

%%FIG rc03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its author, the transactions it appeared in and its year, a reproduction of the chart's three regions in simple form, a three-row table of what the factor depends on in each region, a boxed paragraph headed the input nobody measures, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the chart on the wall**
> **Source: Lewis F. Moody, "Friction Factors for Pipe Flow", *Transactions of the ASME*, **1944**.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> To work out how much pressure a pipe takes out of a fluid, you need one number: the **friction
> factor**. Moody's contribution was to put that number on a single chart that an engineer could read
> in ten seconds.
>
> **Two inputs.** The first is the flow regime, from Unit 2. The second is the **relative roughness**:
> the height of the bumps on the inside of the pipe, divided by the pipe's diameter. The same bumps
> matter more in a small pipe than in a large one, which is why the figure is a ratio.
>
> **Three regions.**
>
> | The region | What the friction factor depends on |
> |---|---|
> | **laminar** | only the flow regime. The roughness has no effect at all |
> | **transition** | both the flow regime and the roughness |
> | **fully turbulent** | only the **roughness**. The curves go flat; the flow regime stops mattering |
>
> That last row is the important one for us. **Almost every line on a production site runs in the
> fully turbulent region**, which means the friction factor is set entirely by how rough the inside of
> the pipe is.
>
> **Moody named his sources.** The chart is a **compilation and correlation of other people's
> measurements** — experimental work by several researchers in the 1930s on smooth and artificially
> roughened pipes, and the equations fitted to it. Moody said whose work it was and what each piece
> contributed. He presented a chart, not a law of nature, and he discussed how accurate he thought it
> was.
>
> **THE INPUT NOBODY MEASURES**
> The published roughness value for commercial steel pipe — a few hundredths of a millimeter — is for
> pipe that is **new and clean**. That is stated in every table that prints it.
>
> **Nobody measures the roughness of a pipe in service.** You cannot get at the inside. So the value
> used in the calculation for a line that has carried crude, water and sand for twenty-seven years is
> the value for a pipe that came off a mill.
>
> What has actually happened inside it, in some combination, is: general corrosion has made the
> surface rougher; pitting has made it rougher still in places; wax, scale or solids have built up in
> layers that are in some places smoother than steel and in others very much rougher; and the internal
> diameter has changed.
>
> So the honest position is this. **The chart is accurate and the number you put into it is an
> assumption**, and in the region where most lines operate, that assumption is the only thing the
> answer depends on. A pressure drop calculation for an old line is not a measurement; it is a
> prediction with one soft input, and the way to deal with it is not to pretend otherwise but to
> compare the calculation with the **actual** pressures at each end of the line and see how far apart
> they are.
>
> That comparison is free. It is also the thing that tells you the inside of your pipe is changing,
> years before anything else does.
>
> **And the chart has limits of shape.** It is for **circular** pipe, **running full**, with **one**
> fluid in it. Two fluids at once is a different and much harder problem, and that is the next unit.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its author, its transactions and its year. Read the
> published paper, and look at how carefully its author credited the work he had compiled.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Compare the calculation with the gauges, every month, and write both numbers down.** It costs
> nothing, it uses instruments you already read, and it is the only routine measurement anybody makes
> of the inside of a pipeline. A calculated drop on its own is a prediction. A calculated drop **beside
> a measured one** is a trend, and a trend is the first thing that will tell you something is growing
> in there. And when they disagree, say what the difference implies in a number — *a roughness about
> four times the new-pipe value* — rather than writing *losses appear elevated*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every organization has one or two numbers that are treated as facts because they came from a
> published table. A published table is an excellent thing and it is usually honest about its own
> conditions — *new*, *clean*, *commercial*, *at twenty degrees* — in small type under the numbers.
> What travels is the number. What stays behind is the small type. The habit this unit asks for is not
> skepticism about tables; it is reading the line underneath them, once, and then writing that line
> into your own calculation so that the next person has to read it too.

**For six years a line's measured pressure drop has been higher than its calculation, and the gap has
grown every year.** Both numbers were recorded every month, on the same sheet, by people doing their
jobs properly. The implied roughness has risen from 0.07 to 0.18 millimeters.

Nobody worked the roughness backwards, because the sheet asked for the two drops and did not ask for
anything to be done with them. The booster pump is now drawing eighteen percent more power than it
did in 2019 at the same flow, and that has been put down to pump wear.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 The data existed for six years. Why did nobody see it?
2 The extra pump power was explained as pump wear. Was that unreasonable?
3 What single column added to the sheet would have changed everything?
4 Is this a problem about pipes, or about forms?
