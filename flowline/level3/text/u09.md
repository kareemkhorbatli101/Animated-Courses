# Unit 9 · One Standard, Many Countries
%%SUB A design factor that is really a statement about who lives nearby%%
%%GOALS

Say what a fluid category is
Say how the two combine
Use generalization and exception
Say what an international standard does not override

The same pipe, carrying the same fluid at the same pressure, is allowed to be thinner in the desert
than it is where a town has grown up beside it. That is not a technical fact about steel. It is a
decision about people, written into a number, and an international standard is where the two sides of
a border find the same words for it.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rc09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its number, its year and its publisher, a grid of location classes against fluid categories with design factors in the cells, a two-row table of the two questions, a boxed paragraph headed what an international standard cannot do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the same pipe in two countries**
> **Source: **ISO 13623:2017**, *Petroleum and natural gas industries — Pipeline transportation
> systems*. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Unit 1 was a national code. This is the international standard that covers the same ground, and it
> is written to be used **alongside** national rules rather than instead of them.
>
> **It covers the whole life.** Design, materials, construction, testing, operation, maintenance — and
> **abandonment**, which is unusual and worth noticing. A pipeline has an end, and the standard
> expects somebody to have thought about it.
>
> **The two questions.**
>
> | The question | What it gives you |
> |---|---|
> | **where** is the pipe: how many people and what activity are near it? | the **location class** |
> | **what** is in the pipe: how dangerous is it if it gets out? | the **fluid category** |
>
> Locations are classified by how built-up and busy they are, from essentially unoccupied land up to
> densely populated areas and places where people gather. Fluids are categorized by hazard: at one end
> non-flammable water-based fluids, at the other toxic or highly volatile gases, with ordinary stable
> liquid hydrocarbons in between.
>
> **The two together give a design factor**, and the design factor sets how much of the steel's
> strength the design is allowed to use. More people nearby, or a more dangerous fluid, means a
> **lower** factor, which means a **thicker** wall or a lower pressure.
>
> **That is a decision about consequences, not about steel.** The pipe does not behave differently
> near a school. What differs is what happens if it fails, and the standard encodes that in a number
> so that it can be used in an equation. It is one of the clearest places in engineering where a
> social judgment is written down as a decimal and then treated as a fact — which is fine, as long as
> everybody remembers which it is.
>
> **WHAT AN INTERNATIONAL STANDARD CANNOT DO**
> It does **not override national law**. The standard says so. Where a country's own regulations are
> more stringent, those apply, and where they conflict, the law wins. An international standard is a
> common technical language, not a legal authority.
>
> So on a line that crosses a border, you may have: two sets of national regulations, two location
> class assessments made by two authorities using two sets of criteria, two inspection regimes, two
> languages of record, and one piece of steel. The standard's value is that both sides can describe
> that steel in the same words. Everything else still has to be agreed in writing, and that agreement
> is a document somebody has to maintain for decades.
>
> **And location class is assessed at a moment.** Somebody walked or flew the route, counted buildings
> and classified it. If the route was classified as essentially unoccupied in 1998 and there is now a
> village beside kilometer nine, the design factor that was correct then describes a pipeline that no
> longer exists in the place it was designed for. The standard expects reassessment when land use
> changes. Reassessment requires somebody to look, and looking requires somebody to be asked.
>
> **What else it leaves out.** It is a functional standard: it says what has to be achieved rather than
> exactly how. Detailed material specifications, welding procedures, inspection methods and operating
> procedures all come from elsewhere — including from the documents in the other eleven units of this
> book.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its number, its year and its publisher. Read the
> published standard, and find the location class table.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> The design factor in this unit is one of the few places where an engineering number openly admits to
> being about people. Everybody accepts that a pipe near a school should be stronger, and almost
> nobody notices that this means the acceptable risk to a human being is written into a decimal in a
> table. That is not cynicism; it is how societies actually make these decisions, and writing them
> down is better than leaving them to be made case by case in a meeting. What goes wrong is not the
> number. It is that the number is set once and the people move afterwards, and nobody is paid to
> notice people moving.
> **Ask your group: who at your site would notice if a village grew beside a line, and what would they
