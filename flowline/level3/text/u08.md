### 2 · Read

%%FIG rc08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its two numbers, its three parts and its publishers, a region map with pH on one axis and hydrogen sulfide partial pressure on the other divided into numbered zones, a four-row table of the environmental variables, a boxed paragraph headed what moves after you have chosen, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: cracking, not corrosion**
> **Source: **NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156**, *Petroleum, petrochemical and natural gas industries —
> Materials for use in H2S-containing environments in oil and gas production*, published in three
> parts. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **This standard is about cracking.** It is not about general corrosion, and the distinction is the
> whole unit. A material can comply fully with this standard and still corrode away. A material can
> have lost no measurable wall at all and crack through in a day.
>
> **What causes it.** In the presence of water and hydrogen sulfide, atomic hydrogen is produced at the
> steel surface and some of it enters the metal. Steel that is **hard** has little ability to deform,
> and hydrogen inside it makes it **brittle**. Under stress it cracks. The headline mechanism is
> **sulfide stress cracking**; there are related mechanisms in which hydrogen collects inside the
> plate and produces internal cracks and blisters, and in which those internal cracks link up in the
> direction of stress.
>
> For corrosion-resistant alloys the mechanism is different again — **stress corrosion cracking** —
> and chlorides and temperature matter much more.
>
> **What defines the environment.**
>
> | The variable | Which way makes cracking more likely |
> |---|---|
> | the **partial pressure of hydrogen sulfide** | higher |
> | the **pH** of the water | lower |
> | the **chloride** concentration | higher, particularly for alloys |
> | the **temperature** | depending on the mechanism |
>
> The standard divides the combinations of pH and hydrogen sulfide partial pressure into **regions**,
> and what a material is permitted to be depends on which region the service falls in. That is why a
> single sentence like *this is sour service* is not enough to select a material: you need the
> numbers.
>
> **The hardness limit.** For carbon and low alloy steels the central control is a **maximum
> hardness**, with the long-established figure for many applications being **22 on the Rockwell C
> scale**. Softer steel tolerates hydrogen; harder steel does not.
>
> **And so the weld is the problem.** Welding heats a narrow band of the parent steel and then lets it
> cool quickly. That band — the **heat-affected zone** — comes out **harder than the parent metal or
> the weld metal**, unless the welding procedure is controlled to prevent it: controlled heat input,
> controlled cooling, sometimes heat treatment afterwards, and hardness testing to prove it.
>
> A pipe with a compliant certificate, welded by a qualified procedure, is compliant. **The same pipe
> with one unrecorded repair in it has one short length that is not**, and no certificate anywhere
> will mention it.
>
> **WHAT MOVES AFTER YOU HAVE CHOSEN**
> Every one of the four variables changes over the life of a field, and a material selection is made
> once, at the beginning.
>
> Hydrogen sulfide content **rises** in many fields as they age, and souring can also be introduced by
> water injection. Water cut rises, so there is more water and it is in contact with more of the
> metal. Chlorides **concentrate** as water is produced and evaporated. Temperatures change as rates
> fall and as fluids are blended differently.
>
> So a material that was correctly selected, certified and installed in 1998 for a stated environment
> may be sitting in 2026 in a **different environment** — and nothing has been done wrong by anybody
> at any point. **Compliance is a statement about a moment**, and the moment has passed.
>
> **What the standard does not do.** It does not tell you whether your service is sour: that is a
> measurement, and it needs repeating. It does not cover general corrosion, erosion or fatigue. It
> does not make a welding procedure: it sets limits that a procedure must achieve. And it is a
> materials standard, not an inspection standard — it will not tell you whether your line has already
> cracked.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with both its numbers and its publishers. Read the
> published standard, and find the region map.*

> **Date every compliance statement.** *Compliant* is a verb's worth of work short: *certified

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every site has repairs nobody has a record of, and almost all of them were made by people doing
> their best in difficult conditions at an inconvenient hour. The temptation when this is discovered
> is to find out who, and that is both unkind and useless: the welder was competent, the supervisor
> was right to fix a leak, and the gap is in the paperwork system rather than in anybody's judgment.
> The useful question is never *who did this* but *what is the hardness now*, because one of those
> questions has an answer and the other one only has an argument.
> **Ask your group: what repair at your site has no record, and would anybody be blamed for saying

service of 400 parts per million hydrogen sulfide.** Everything was right.

In 2009 a leak was repaired at two in the morning by a competent contract welder, who did a good job
and whose weld has not leaked since. No procedure reference and no hardness record exist for it.

This year the gas measured 2,100 parts per million. Nobody did anything wrong at any point, and there
are twenty centimeters of unmeasured steel in a service that nobody selected it for.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Who is responsible for this situation?
2 Three correct decisions produced an unknown. How?
3 What would have caught it: a different form, a different rule, or a different habit?
4 The weld has held for sixteen years. Is that evidence?
