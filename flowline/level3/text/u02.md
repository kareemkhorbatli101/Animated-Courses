In 1883 a professor in Manchester let water run through a glass tube and put a thread of dye into it.
At low speeds the dye stayed in a line. At higher speeds it broke up and filled the tube. Every
pressure drop calculation in this industry begins with that observation.

### 2 · Six more words, and the four quantities

%%FIG rc02-f3 | Six panels: a velocity arrow, a diameter arrow, a density symbol, a viscosity symbol, the four combined into one ratio with the units canceling, and a boxed line headed four quantities, one number.%%

**2A  Read the four quantities aloud with a partner. Then cover them and say all four.**

| The quantity | Which way it pushes the number |
|---|---|
| **velocity** — how fast the fluid is moving | up |
| **diameter** — how wide the pipe is | up |
| **density** — how heavy the fluid is | up |
| **viscosity** — how thick the fluid is | **down** |

number that tells you which kind of flow you have, whatever the fluid and whatever the pipe.

%%FIG rc02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its author, the journal and its year, a drawing of the 1883 apparatus with a tank, a glass tube and a dye needle, a four-row table of the quantities, a boxed paragraph headed what the experiment could not settle, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the experiment behind every pressure drop calculation**
> **Source: Osborne Reynolds, "An experimental investigation of the circumstances which determine
> whether the motion of water shall be direct or sinuous, and of the law of resistance in parallel
> channels", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, **1883**. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> Reynolds took a tank of still water, led a glass tube out of it, and let water run through the tube.
> Into the mouth of the tube he introduced a fine thread of colored dye.
>
> At low flow rates the thread of dye ran the whole length of the tube **as a straight line**. The
> water was moving in smooth parallel layers, and nothing crossed between them. He called this
> **direct** motion; we call it **laminar**.
>
> As he increased the flow, at some point the thread suddenly **broke up** and mixed through the whole
> tube. He called this **sinuous**; we call it **turbulent**.
>
> **The great result is not the observation. It is what decides which one you get.**
>
> | The quantity | Which way it pushes |
> |---|---|
> | the **velocity** of the fluid | up |
> | the **diameter** of the pipe | up |
> | the **density** of the fluid | up |
> | the **viscosity** of the fluid | **down** |
>
> Combined in the right way, those four give a quantity with **no units at all**: the units cancel.
> That single number — now called the Reynolds number — decides the regime.
>
> Why does that matter so much? Because a number with no units can be **compared across completely
> different situations**. A glass tube of water in Manchester in 1883 and a twelve inch line of crude
> in Deir ez-Zor today, at the same value of that number, are in the same kind of flow. Nothing else
> in fluid mechanics gives you that, and almost everything in this book depends on it.
>
> **The approximate values.** Below about **2,000** the flow is laminar. Above about **4,000** it is
> turbulent. Between the two is a **transitional** band where it may be either, and where no careful
> engineer will promise you a figure.
>
> **In practice, almost every line on a production site is turbulent**, and comfortably so. Laminar
> flow in an oil pipeline is rare: it needs a very viscous fluid, a small pipe, or a very low rate.
>
> **WHAT THE EXPERIMENT COULD NOT SETTLE**
> Reynolds was careful about something that people who cite him often are not. The transition was
> **not a sharp line**.
>
> He found it depended on **how disturbed the water was as it entered the tube**. With a very smooth
> entry and still water in the tank, laminar flow could be **sustained to much higher values** than
> 2,000. With a disturbed entry it broke down earlier. The figure is not a property of the fluid
> alone; it is a property of the fluid, the pipe **and the conditions at the inlet**.
>
> So the two numbers in every textbook are **conventions built on a real observation**, not
> constants of nature. They are good enough for design, which is what they are for.
>
> He was also working with **water**, in **one tube**, at ordinary temperatures, in a laboratory. The
> extension to other fluids, other geometries, heat transfer, and two fluids at once came later, from
> other people, and some of it is still argued about. Unit 4 is about the part that is most argued
> about.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its author, its journal and its year. Read the
> published paper. It is a beautiful piece of writing, and the apparatus is described well enough to
> rebuild.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> The most-copied cell in any engineering organization is a fluid property. It is entered once, by
> somebody who knew where it came from, and then it travels through spreadsheets for fifteen years,
> losing its origin at every step, until it is simply *the viscosity*. This is not carelessness; it is
> what happens when a number is useful and its provenance is not stored beside it. The habit that
> fixes it is small and almost nobody does it: put the source in the cell next to the value, every
> time, so that the two cannot be separated by a copy and paste.
> **Ask your group: what is the oldest number in your most-used spreadsheet, and does anybody know
> where it came from?**
