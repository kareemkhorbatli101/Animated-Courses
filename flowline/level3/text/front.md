## How to use this book

This is the third book of six. Book 1 was the plant and the people. Book 2 went down the hole and
came back up to the tank. This one is **the pipe**: what it costs to move a fluid, the two
experiments that explain why it costs anything at all, the pumps that pay the bill, the tool that
runs inside the line looking for trouble, the chemistry of a pipe that is slowly being eaten, the
tank at the end, the ship alongside it, and the management system that one published inquiry decided
was the thing that had actually failed.

**Twelve units**, twenty pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | One real code, standard, paper or guide, with its date and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · On Site** | Clear English, a dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `FL3-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Two of the twelve sources are experiments, not documents.** Osborne Reynolds in 1883 put dye into
water in a glass tube and watched. Lewis Moody in 1944 drew a chart from other people's measurements
and said whose. Everything else in this book — every pump curve, every pressure drop calculation,
every pipeline design code — rests on those two pieces of work, and both authors wrote plainly about
what they had and had not shown.

**Two of them are about materials rather than equipment.** Units 7 and 8 are about corrosion: the
thing that is happening to every pipe on every site, slowly, whether or not anybody is looking. They
are the two units in this series that are least like a language lesson and most like a warning.

**Three of them are documents that two parties have to agree on.** A pipeline crosses somebody's
land, a tank is filled by one company and emptied by another, a ship ties up to a shore. Where two
organizations share a hazard, the English stops being technical and becomes contractual, and this
book says so.

**And the last unit is about the word *system*.** A published inquiry into a pipeline failure found
that the weld was not the problem. This book ends there on purpose, and Book 5 begins there.

**A language course is not a safety course.** This book quotes design codes and corrosion standards.
It does not authorize you to design, operate, inspect or repair anything.

**Money has no symbol in this series.**

## The people

The same company and the same fourteen people, in the same jobs.

**Jazira Petroleum Operations** runs the **Tell Sahra** field, the **Mureij** gathering station and
the **Sahra** central processing facility, and moves its crude out by a line to a tank farm. All of
it is **invented**.

**Nour Al-Masri**, process engineer, runs the **Mureij Training Center**. **Samir Haddad** is the
field operations superintendent. **Yusuf Khalaf** is the HSE officer. **Samira Qassab** is an
instrument technician. **Ahmad Sweid** is a mechanic. **Nadia Shahin** works on metering and
allocation. **Yara Barakat** is a laboratory chemist. **Mariama Bah** is the reservoir engineer.
**Lars Eriksen**, **Ida Lindqvist** and **Wei Zhao** work for contractors. **Omar Dabbagh** is now
nine months in.

Two people carry this book. **Adeel Rahman** works on pipeline integrity, and from Unit 6 onward he
is the one asking what a reported wall thickness actually means. **Tomas Varga** is a corrosion
specialist, and his line, repeated in four units, is *it is already happening; the question is how
fast*.
