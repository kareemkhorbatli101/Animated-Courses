# Unit 11 · Ship and Shore
%%SUB Two organizations, one hose, and a checklist that is signed twice%%
%%GOALS

A terminal and a tanker belong to two companies, operate under two command structures, work to two
sets of procedures, and are joined for a few hours by a hose. The guide that governs that few hours
is not mainly about equipment. It is about getting two organizations to write the same thing down and
both sign it.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rc11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the guide, its edition, its year and the three bodies that publish it, a drawing of a ship alongside a jetty with the manifold connection marked, a four-row table of what must be agreed, a boxed paragraph headed the item everybody ticks, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: two organizations and one hose**
> **Source: the ***International Safety Guide for Oil Tankers and Terminals***, **sixth edition,
> 2020**, published jointly by the International Chamber of Shipping, the Oil Companies International
> Marine Forum and the International Association of Ports and Harbors. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> A tanker alongside a terminal is the only place in this industry where **two separate organizations
> hold opposite ends of the same hazard** and neither one is in charge of the other. The ship's master
> is responsible for the ship. The terminal representative is responsible for the terminal. There is
> no shared manager, no shared procedure and, usually, no shared first language.
>
> **The ship/shore safety checklist.** The central document in the guide is a list of items, each one
> marked according to whether it is the **ship's** responsibility, the **terminal's**, or **both**,
> and completed and **signed by a representative of each side** before cargo transfer begins. Some
> items are marked as needing a joint **agreement**, some as needing re-checking at intervals during
> the transfer, and some as needing **permission**.
>
> Almost nothing in that list is information either party did not already have. Mooring arrangements,
> hoses in good order, deck watch set, fire-fighting equipment ready, scuppers plugged, the right
> communications — a competent ship and a competent terminal know all of it. **The purpose of the
> document is to make the agreement explicit, written and signed**, item by item, by named people,
> before anything is connected. That is a different thing from either party being careful.
>
> **Four things that must be agreed, not assumed.**
>
> | What | Why it is on the list |
> |---|---|
> | the **cargo plan** and the **maximum rate** | so that neither side is surprised by how fast the oil arrives |
> | the **topping-off** procedure, including the reduced rate | because the last part of a tank is where overfills happen, and this is where Unit 10 and this unit meet |
> | the **emergency stop** procedure, and **who may call it** | so that a person who sees something wrong does not have to work out who to ask |
> | **communications**: the means, the language and the backup | because all three items above depend on it |
>
> **Either side may suspend the transfer.** The guide is clear that both the ship and the terminal have
> the right to stop operations if they are not satisfied, and that is the only real authority either
> party has over the other. It costs both of them money to use it, and that cost is a permanent
> pressure in the opposite direction.
>
> **THE ITEM EVERYBODY TICKS**
> The emergency stop. Everybody knows where the button is. Very few sites know **how long it takes to
> stop the flow after it is pressed**, because finding out means stopping the transfer, and stopping
> the transfer costs money that somebody will ask about.
>
> That is the same arithmetic as the last unit, in a different place: a protection whose response time
> nobody has measured is a protection nobody has.
>
> **What else the guide covers**, and this note does not. Hydrocarbon gas and oxygen measurement, and
> why the gap between a flammable mixture and a safe one is not where intuition puts it; inert gas
> systems; static electricity, bonding and the hazards of loading into a tank that is not inert;
> enclosed space entry; mooring and emergency release. The sixth edition gave substantially more
> attention to **human factors** — fatigue, workload, and communication between people who do not
> share a language — which is the part of the guide most directly about what actually goes wrong.
>
> **And what it is not.** It is a **guide**, published by industry bodies. It is not law, though much
> of its content appears in terminal regulations and in charter terms, which is a different kind of
> force. It cannot make two organizations trust each other, and it cannot make a representative who
> has been awake for nineteen hours read forty items carefully.
>
> *Our summary of a published guide, named with its edition, its year and its publishers. Read the
> published guide, and look at the checklist and at the human factors chapter.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Put two names and a time on every agreement.** *It was agreed* with nobody in it is not a record,
> and the whole point of a joint document is that two named people can be asked about it in a year.
> And when an item cannot be checked, **write a dash and say why** rather than ticking it. A dash on a
> signed checklist is an honest document; a tick on an unchecked item is the opposite, and it is the
> tick that turns a guide into a ritual.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> The ship/shore interface is the clearest example in this industry of a safety system that works
> through disagreement rather than through authority. Nobody is in charge; both sides can stop the
> job; and the only mechanism is a document that forces them to write down what they have agreed. It
> works surprisingly well, and it has two weak points that are both about people rather than hoses.
> The first is fatigue: a representative at the end of a long voyage or a double shift reads forty
> items the way anybody would. The second is politeness — the enormous social pressure not to be the
> person who delays a ship. The 2020 edition's attention to human factors is a response to both.
> **Ask your group: when did you last delay something, and how did it feel?**

A completed ship/shore checklist lies open with every box ticked and two signatures, beside a second checklist from the same berth three years earlier also with every box ticked, an emergency stop button photograph, a stopwatch that has never been used, a note of a captain's request for a figure, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**For three years every ship/shore checklist at a berth has been completed and signed by two
representatives with every box ticked.** Nobody has lied. Both sides were competent, the transfers all
went well, and the documents are a model of compliance.

One item asks for the time from the emergency stop to the flow stopping. For three years it has been
ticked, because the button exists and both parties know where it is. Nobody has measured the time,
because measuring it means stopping a transfer.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Has anybody done anything wrong?
2 A tick and a dash would look different on the page. Which is the honest document?
3 A customer asking for the figure gets it measured; an engineer asking gets a meeting. Why?
4 What would you do on your next shift?

failure that decided the problem was not the weld.
