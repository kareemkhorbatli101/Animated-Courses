%%FIG rc12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the recommended practice, its number, its year and its publisher, a diagram of ten elements arranged around a plan-do-check-act loop, a two-row table contrasting a safety management system with a documentation system, a boxed paragraph headed how a management system fails, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the document that says it was not the weld**
> **Source: **API Recommended Practice 1173**, *Pipeline Safety Management System Requirements*, first
> edition **2015**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **Where this document came from.** It was developed after a series of serious pipeline incidents, in
> which the investigating authority kept reaching the same conclusion: the immediate technical cause
> was real, and behind it sat organizational failures — information that existed and did not travel,
> warnings that were received and not acted on, roles that nobody held, and measures of success that
> rewarded the wrong thing.
>
> So this is not an engineering document. It is a document about **how an organization that operates
> pipelines should be arranged**, and it is the natural last unit of this book.
>
> **Ten elements**, arranged so that they form a cycle of planning, doing, checking and improving.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **1 leadership and management commitment** | **6 safety assurance** |
> | **2 stakeholder engagement** | **7 management review and continuous improvement** |
> | **3 risk management** | **8 emergency preparedness and response** |
> | **4 operational controls** | **9 competence, awareness and training** |
> | **5 incident investigation, evaluation and lessons learned** | **10 documentation and record keeping** |
>
> **Element one is first for a reason.** Everything else in the list costs money, costs production, or
> costs somebody's time on a day when they have something more urgent to do. A risk register that
> nobody senior reads is a spreadsheet. An investigation whose findings nobody funds is a document. A
> competence requirement that is waived whenever the schedule tightens is a preference. **Element one
> is the only element that makes the other nine real**, and that is why it is not a polite
> introduction.
>
> **Element two is the one this industry finds hardest.** Stakeholders include the people who live
> beside the line — the thirty-four buildings at kilometer nine. Engaging them is not public relations;
> it is how an operator finds out that the land use has changed before a reassessment is due.
>
> **Element six, safety assurance, is the quiet one.** It means checking that the controls you believe
> in are actually working. Not that the procedure exists: that it is followed. Not that the alarm is
> installed: that the response time has been measured. Almost every unit in this book has an assurance
> failure in it.
>
> **HOW A MANAGEMENT SYSTEM FAILS**
> There is one way, and it is worth naming plainly.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | a **safety management system** | changes what people do, and can be seen in decisions |
> | a **documentation system** | changes what is on file, and can be seen in audits |
>
> The failure mode is the second thing growing inside the first. Element ten — documentation and
> record keeping — is essential and is the easiest element to do well, because it can be done by
> writing. An organization under pressure will produce the documents for all ten elements, pass an
> audit, and change nothing about how it behaves on a Tuesday at two in the morning. **Nobody decides
> to do this.** It is what happens when the easiest element is the one that gets measured.
>
> The test is not whether the system exists. The test is whether, somewhere in the last year, **the
> system caused a decision to be made differently** — a line derated, a shutdown taken, a figure
> reported that made somebody look bad, a village visited. If you cannot find one, there is a
> documentation system.
>
> **And its own limits.** It is a **recommended practice**, not a regulation: voluntary, adopted by
> choice, with no legal force of its own. It is written to be **scalable**, which means it does not
> tell a small operator exactly what to do. It defines what has to be achieved and leaves the how to
> each organization, which is both its strength and the reason two companies can both claim to have
> implemented it and look nothing alike.
>
> **It also cannot make anybody care.** It is a framework for an organization that wants one.
>
> *Our summary of a published recommended practice, named with its number, its edition and its
> publisher. Read the published document. It is the shortest document in this course and the one most
> likely to change what you do.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Add one column to every safety system document: *what decision did this change?*** It is the only
> column that cannot be filled in by writing, and it is the whole difference between the two kinds of
> system. And when you write anything up, **put a person and a verb in it**. *An investigation was
> undertaken* has no subject; *Yusuf investigated it and found the valve shut* has a name, an action
> and a finding, and it is shorter. Twelve units of this book have been an argument for that sentence.

> This is the last culture check in the book, and the honest thing to say is that the pressure

**An organization documented all ten elements and passed its audit.** Nothing on paper was false.
Everybody involved was competent and most of them were overworked.

Four findings are open, in four different filing systems: the village at kilometer nine, the 2009
weld with no hardness record, the 1.9 kilometers of poor inspection data, and the emergency stop
timing on seven of nine tanks. Each one is somebody's job and nobody's priority. Each one would be
found in an afternoon by anybody who looked for it.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Did the audit fail?
2 Four findings, four filing systems, nobody's priority. Which element of the ten is this?
3 The organization is competent and overworked. Which of those two facts explains the four
   findings?
4 You have finished this book. Name one thing you are going to go and look at, and one person you
   are going to tell.

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** There is a grammar reference, a word list and
the full list of every document quoted in it. Everything else is the next shift.
