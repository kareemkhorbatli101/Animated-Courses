# Unit 10 · The Tank That Overflowed
%%SUB A subtraction nobody has done, and the difference between an alarm and a protection%%

A tank is the simplest object on a plant: a cylinder of steel with a liquid in it. The way it fails is
also the simplest. It gets more liquid than it can hold, and the liquid comes out of the top. Two
published documents cover it, they cover different halves of the problem, and the overflows happen in
the gap between them.

%%FIG rc10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the two documents, their numbers and their publisher, a tank in section with levels marked and the volume above the high-high alarm shaded, a four-row table of layers, a boxed paragraph headed the arithmetic, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the tank, and the seven minutes**
> **Source: **API Standard 650**, *Welded Tanks for Oil Storage*, read alongside **API Recommended
> Practice 2350**, *Overfill Protection for Storage Tanks in Petroleum Facilities*. Printed here in
> our own words.**
>
> **Two documents, two halves of the problem.**
>
> **API 650 is about building the tank.** Shell plate thicknesses, the bottom, the roof, the welding,
> the testing, the fittings. It is a construction standard, and tanks built to it are strong, round
> and tight. **It is not about filling the tank**, and a perfectly built tank will overflow exactly as
> readily as a badly built one.
>
> **API RP 2350 is about not overfilling it.** It is a recommended practice built around the idea that
> overfill protection is a **system**, not a device: levels, alarms, written procedures, response
> times and the people who are supposed to act. Its later editions were substantially revised after
> major overfill events in the industry, which is the usual way a document like this gets better.
>
> **The layers.**
>
> | The layer | What it needs |
> |---|---|
> | the operator **watching** the level | somebody present and looking |
> | the **high level alarm** | the gauge working, and somebody responding |
> | the **high-high alarm** from an **independent** device | a second device that does not share the first one's failures |
> | **automatic shutdown** | an instrument closing a valve with nobody involved |
>
> And then the **bund** — which prevents nothing. It contains what has already come out, and the
> history of this industry includes bunds full of petroleum vapor that then found an ignition source.
>
> **Why *independent* is the most important word in the unit.** If the high alarm and the high-high
> alarm are both driven from the same level transmitter, then a frozen transmitter silences both. Two
> alarms from one instrument are one layer wearing two hats, and on paper they look like two.
>
> **THE ARITHMETIC**
> Here is the calculation the whole subject rests on, and it takes half a minute.
>
> Take the volume between the **high-high alarm** and the point at which the tank overflows. Divide it
> by the **maximum rate** at which the tank can be filled. That gives you the **time you have**.
>
> Now find out how long it actually takes, on the worst shift of the week, from the alarm sounding to
> the flow stopping: somebody noticing, somebody deciding, somebody telephoning, somebody finding the
> right person, somebody closing a valve.
>
> **If the second number is larger than the first, the alarm is not a protection.** It is a record of
> the moment the overflow became certain. That is a hard sentence and it is the only honest reading of
> the arithmetic.
>
> The recommended practice treats this explicitly: the response time is part of the system, the
> category of tank operation matters — attended, unattended, fully automated — and a tank that nobody
> is standing next to needs a layer that does not need anybody.
>
> **What neither document does.** Neither one tells you your fill rate, your volumes or your response
> time: those are your own numbers and you have to go and get them. Neither one covers what happens to
> the vapor if oil does come out. And neither can make an alarm that somebody has silenced into a
> protection.
>
> *Our summary of two published documents, named with their numbers and their publisher. Read the
> published recommended practice, and then do the subtraction for one tank you operate.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> *It has never happened here* is the most reasonable sentence in industrial safety and the most
> dangerous. It is reasonable because eighteen years of evidence is real evidence, and the people
> saying it are not fools. It is dangerous because an overfill is not a frequent event anywhere: a
> tank that overflows once in thirty years across a whole industry will look, from inside any single
> site, exactly like a tank that never overflows. The subtraction in this unit is valuable precisely
> because it does not depend on history. It is arithmetic, and it is true on a site's first day.
