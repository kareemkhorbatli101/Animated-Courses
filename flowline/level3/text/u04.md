One fluid in a pipe is a solved problem. Two fluids in a pipe is not, and the correlation most of the
industry uses was built on a short acrylic loop with air and water in it. The authors said so in
their first paragraph. Almost nobody who uses their work has read it.

### 2 · Six more words, and the three groups

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rc04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its two authors, the journal and its year, a drawing of the test loop with its length and diameter marked and an inclination arrow, a three-row table of the regime groups, a boxed paragraph headed what was actually tested, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: two things in one pipe**
> **Source: H. D. Beggs and J. P. Brill, "A Study of Two-Phase Flow in Inclined Pipes", *Journal of
> Petroleum Technology*, **1973**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> When gas and liquid travel along the same pipe they arrange themselves into patterns, and which
> pattern you get changes everything about the pressure drop and about what arrives at the far end.
>
> **Three groups of pattern.**
>
> | The group | What it looks like |
> |---|---|
> | **segregated** | the two phases in layers: gas above, liquid below, with a smooth or wavy surface between them |
> | **intermittent** | bodies of liquid filling the whole pipe, separated by stretches of gas |
> | **distributed** | one phase carried through the other: bubbles in the liquid, or liquid as a mist in fast-moving gas |
>
> Which group you are in **depends on** the gas rate, the liquid rate, the pipe diameter, the fluid
> properties and — this was the paper's main contribution — **the angle of the pipe**.
>
> **Liquid holdup.** The gas moves faster than the liquid. So the liquid spends longer in the pipe than
> the gas does, and at any moment there is **more liquid inside the line than the flow rates alone
> would suggest**. The fraction of the pipe occupied by liquid is called the **holdup**, and on a line
> that rises and falls, liquid collects in the low points and on the uphill sections.
>
> **Why that matters on a Tuesday.** Stored liquid is not a problem while nothing changes. The moment
> the gas rate goes up, the stored liquid is **swept out ahead of it** and arrives at the far end as
> one body instead of a steady stream. That is a **surge**: a level alarm, a separator at full, a
> possible carry-over into the gas line, and in a bad case liquid reaching equipment designed for gas.
> Starting a line up, increasing the rate, and bringing a well on are the three moments when it
> happens.
>
> **WHAT WAS ACTUALLY TESTED**
> Beggs and Brill built a test loop, measured carefully, and published what they had. Here is what
> they had.
>
> **Air and water.** Not oil and gas. Not a live crude with gas coming out of solution as the pressure
> falls along the line.
>
> **Small pipe.** Approximately one inch and one and a half inches in diameter. Our export lines are
> twelve.
>
> **A short loop**, in a laboratory, at near-atmospheric conditions — not fourteen kilometers of buried
> line at fifty bar.
>
> **Five hundred and eighty-four tests**, across a range of inclinations from steeply downhill to
> steeply uphill, which was the genuinely new part and the reason the paper is still cited.
>
> Everything that happens when you apply that correlation to a twelve inch line of crude and
> associated gas at fifty bar is **extrapolation**. The authors did not hide it; they described their
> apparatus in detail precisely so that readers could see how far they were going beyond it.
>
> So two honest statements sit side by side. This correlation is **the best tool widely available** for
> a very difficult problem, and it is being used thousands of kilometers outside the conditions it was
> measured in. Later correlations exist, they disagree with each other, and **two engineers using two
> accepted methods on the same line can get holdup figures that differ by a large factor**. A number
> for holdup with no method named beside it is not a number.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its authors, its journal and its year. Read the
> published paper, and look at the section describing the apparatus.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Name the method, every time.** *Holdup 94 cubic meters* is not a number; *holdup 94 cubic meters by
> the 1973 correlation* is, and the next engineer can then tell you that another accepted method gives
> 61. When two methods disagree, say both figures and say which one you used and why. Using the larger
> one and saying so is a decision somebody can check; quietly using the smaller one is not. And when
> you raise a rate on a line with a profile, **raise it in steps and write the level at each step**.
> One hour of patience is cheaper than one carry-over.
