# Unit 7 · Keeping the Steel
%%SUB Two defenses that are supposed to be independent, and the way one defeats the other%%
%%GOALS
Say why buried steel corrodes
Name the two defenses and say which is primary
Say what a potential reading does and does not prove
Use prevention language
Say what coating shielding is
%%

A buried pipe is a piece of steel in wet ground, and wet ground takes steel apart. Two defenses stand
between the pipe and the soil. They were designed to be independent, so that one covers for the
other. There is one failure mode in which the first defense stops the second one working, and that
failure mode is invisible from the surface.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rc07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its number and its publisher, a cross-section of a coated buried pipe with a holiday and current reaching the steel, a two-row table of the defenses, a boxed paragraph headed what the reading does not say, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: keeping the soil off the steel, and keeping the metal in it**
> **Source: **NACE SP0169**, *Control of External Corrosion on Underground or Submerged Metallic
> Piping Systems*, published by the corrosion engineers' association now part of AMPP. Printed here in
> our own words.**
>
> **Why it happens.** Steel in wet soil does not rust evenly. Some patches of the surface become
> **anodes**, where iron goes into solution and the wall is lost, and other patches become
> **cathodes**, where nothing is lost. Current flows between them through the soil. It is an electrical
> process, which is why the defense against it is electrical too.
>
> **Two defenses.**
>
> | The defense | What it does |
> |---|---|
> | the **coating** — the primary barrier | keeps the soil and the water away from the steel entirely |
> | **cathodic protection** — the secondary | supplies current to the pipe so that the exposed steel becomes a cathode and stops losing metal |
>
> Coatings always have **holidays**: small gaps, from manufacture, from handling, from the backhoe
> that laid the line. The design philosophy is that the coating does almost all of the work and the
> current covers the small fraction that is exposed. **That is why the money belongs on the coating.**
>
> **Two ways to supply the current.** **Sacrificial anodes**: a more active metal buried near the pipe
> and connected to it, which is consumed instead of the pipe. **Impressed current**: a rectifier
> pushing current from a groundbed. The first is simple and limited; the second is powerful and needs
> somebody to keep it running.
>
> **How you know it is working.** The standard sets out **criteria**, and the best known of them is a
> structure-to-soil potential of at least **−850 millivolts** measured against a saturated copper and
> copper sulfate reference electrode. There is more than one acceptable criterion, and an engineer
> should know which one is being used.
>
> **WHAT THE READING DOES NOT SAY**
> Three things, and each one has caused a failure somewhere.
>
> **A reading taken with the current flowing includes a voltage drop through the soil** which has
> nothing to do with the steel. Switch the current off for an instant and read again, and the figure
> is different — and it is the second figure, the **polarized potential**, that tells you about the
> steel. A site that records only the *on* figure may be recording a number that looks protective and
> is not.
>
> **A reading is taken at a test post.** The pipe between two test posts is kilometers long. The
> reading tells you about the pipe where the electrode is, and about the general state of the current.
> It cannot tell you about one square meter two hundred meters away.
>
> **And then there is shielding, which is the one to remember.** A coating can **disbond**: lift away
> from the steel while remaining physically in place. Water collects in the gap. The steel there is
> wet, so it corrodes. And because the coating is still in the way, it **blocks the protective
> current** from reaching the steel underneath.
>
> So the primary defense, in failing, **prevents the secondary defense from doing its job**. The two
> defenses are not independent. The test post two hundred meters away reads perfectly, because the
> current is distributing itself everywhere it can get to. This is the single most important fact in
> external corrosion, and it is the reason inspection from inside the pipe is not a luxury.
>
> **Other things the standard covers and this note does not.** Interference from other buried
> structures and from traction systems; soil resistivity; how often to survey; how to handle a line
> that crosses somebody else's. All of it matters and none of it is as counter-intuitive as the
> paragraph above.
>
> **And the standard sets criteria, not outcomes.** Meeting a criterion is a reasonable basis for
> believing the pipe is protected. It is not a measurement of wall thickness, and the only thing that
> measures wall thickness is the subject of the last unit.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its number and its publisher. Read the published
> standard, and find the section on the criteria.*

A cathodic protection survey record lies open with eight on readings all passing and two polarized readings failing, beside an inspection report appendix listing the same two kilometers as poor data, a map with the wet section shaded twice, a photograph of a disbonded coating with a pit beneath it, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**Two kilometers of a pipeline cross wet ground.** The cathodic protection survey passes there on *on*
readings and fails on polarized ones. The in-line inspection could not read that section. The coating
has not been assessed since 1998.

Three separate pieces of information point at the same two kilometers, and all three of them say the
same thing: **we do not know**. Each of the three reports was written correctly, by competent people,
and filed in a different place.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Nobody has evidence of a problem. Is there a problem?
2 Three reports, three filing systems. Whose job was it to put them side by side?
3 What would it cost to find out, and what would it cost not to?
4 How would you write a one-page summary that a committee would act on?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 8 is the other way steel fails: not
slowly, and not from the outside.
