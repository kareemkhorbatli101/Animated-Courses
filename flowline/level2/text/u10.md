# Unit 10 · Proving the Meter
%%SUB Two honest parties, one set of readings, and the standard that makes them agree%%
%%GOALS

A meter does not measure volume. It counts something and somebody turns the count into a volume.
Between the count and the number on the ticket there are a prover, a factor, four corrections and a
set of rules about rounding, and every one of them has been the subject of an argument worth money.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rb10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the two chapters, their manual and their publisher, a diagram of a bidirectional prover with a meter in line, a six-row table of the proving run, a boxed paragraph headed what the arithmetic cannot fix, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: checking the counter, and agreeing the arithmetic**
> **Source: American Petroleum Institute, *Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards*, **Chapter 4,
> Proving Systems**, and **Chapter 12, Calculation of Petroleum Quantities**. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> **A meter counts. It does not measure.** A turbine meter counts blade passes. A positive
> displacement meter counts cycles of a chamber. Something has to turn that count into a volume, and
> that something is a number found by experiment: the **meter factor**.
>
> **Chapter 4 is about finding it.** You pass a **known** volume through the meter and compare it with
> what the meter said. The known volume comes from a **prover** — a pipe with a sphere traveling
> between two detectors, a tank with a certified neck, or a master meter that has itself been proved.
> The prover's volume is **traceable**: it was established by a calibration that can be followed back
> to a recognized standard.
>
> | Step | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **1** | the flow is set and settled at the meter's normal rate |
> | **2** | a known volume from the prover is passed through the meter |
> | **3** | the meter's indicated volume is recorded and corrected to standard conditions |
> | **4** | the run is repeated, consecutively |
> | **5** | if the runs **agree within a stated tolerance**, a factor is calculated |
> | **6** | the factor is recorded **with the conditions** it was found at |
>
> Step five is the quiet one. If the runs do **not** agree, you do not have a factor — you have a
> problem with the meter, the prover or the flow, and the standard's answer is to find out which, not
> to average them.
>
> **Chapter 12 is about the arithmetic afterwards.** It sets out the **order** of the steps, the
> **rounding** rules, and how many digits to carry at each stage. That sounds like housekeeping. It
> is the reason two companies can compute the same cargo and agree.
>
> Here is why it exists. The same raw readings, corrected for temperature and pressure and multiplied
> by a meter factor, give **slightly different answers** depending on the order you do it in and where
> you round. Each answer is arithmetically defensible. Only one of them is the agreed one. Over one
> cargo the difference is small; over twenty years of cargoes it is a transfer of value in one
> direction, invisible to both parties, because both tickets look correct.
>
> **WHAT THE ARITHMETIC CANNOT FIX**
> Chapter 12 does not improve a measurement. A perfectly computed quantity from a badly proved meter
> is a precise wrong number, and a precise wrong number is more dangerous than an obviously rough
> one, because nobody argues with it.
>
> A **meter factor belongs to its conditions**. It was found at a flow rate, a viscosity, a
> temperature and a pressure. Run the meter at a third of that rate and the factor is no longer the
> one you measured. The certificate says the conditions. The ticket usually does not.
>
> **Factors drift.** A meter wears, a bearing stiffens, a strainer partly blocks. That is exactly why
> proving is repeated, and **neither chapter tells you how often to prove**: the interval comes from a
> contract, a regulator, or a company's own standard, and that is a decision, not a technical fact.
>
> **The prover has its own uncertainty.** It was calibrated too, by somebody, on a date, with its own
> tolerance. The chain of comparison has to end somewhere, and knowing where it ends is part of
> knowing what your number is worth.
>
> *Our summary of two published chapters, named with their manual and their publisher. Read the
> published chapters, and write down the editions you read.*

### 1 · *Unless* and other conditions

| | |
|---|---|
| Do not issue a factor **unless** the runs agree. | the one exception |
| The factor may be used **provided that** the rate is in range. | a permission with a condition |
| It holds **as long as** the flow stays near the proving rate. | a condition that must continue |
| Accept the run **only if** it is consecutive. | a single strong condition |
| Check the strainer **in case** it is partly blocked. | a precaution, not a condition |
| Prove it again, **otherwise** the ticket is not defensible. | the consequence of not doing it |

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Print the conditions beside the factor.** A meter factor without its flow rate, temperature and
> date is a number somebody will apply for three years at a rate it was never measured at. Six extra
> characters on a ticket prevent an argument that is otherwise unwinnable, because by the time anybody
> asks, the certificate is in a different building. And when two tickets differ, **compare the raw
> readings before anything else.** If the raw readings match, nobody has measured anything wrong and
> the disagreement is arithmetic, which is a much easier conversation than an accusation.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Measurement disputes are the fastest way to spoil a working relationship in this industry, and most
> of them are not about measurement. Two organizations with the same honest data and two slightly
> different arithmetic conventions will produce two different numbers forever, and the first instinct
> in every culture on earth is to suspect the other side. The standards in this unit exist almost
> entirely to prevent that suspicion, which is a strange and rather fine thing for a document about
> rounding to be for. **Ask your group: when two sets of figures differ at your site, what is the first
> question anybody asks?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rb10-f16 | A desk in the evening. **For nineteen years two companies have issued tickets for the same cargoes.** Both are honest. Both
are arithmetically correct. One applies the corrections in one order and rounds at the end; the other
applies them in another order and rounds twice on the way. The difference is about four tenths of one
percent, always in the same direction.

Nobody has ever raised it, because every ticket on both sides looks right. The sale contract names a
calculation standard but not an edition.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Who has lost money, and roughly how much over nineteen years?
2 Both tickets are correct. How is that possible?
3 The contract names a standard but no edition. Why does that matter?
4 Who should have found this, and what would have made them look?
