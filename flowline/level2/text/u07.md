# Unit 7 · The Curve That Goes Down
%%SUB Three shapes of decline, and the sentence that has to go under every forecast%%
%%GOALS

Every well declines. The rate at which it declines can be drawn as a line, the line can be extended
into next year, and three quarters of the forecasting done in this industry is exactly that. A paper
from 1945 set it out, and said what it was: curve fitting.

%%FIG rb07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its author, the transactions it appeared in and its year, a three-row table of the decline types, a graph of three diverging curves, a boxed paragraph headed what the curves assume, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the line through the past**
> **Source: J. J. Arps, "Analysis of Decline Curves", *Transactions of the AIME*, **1945**. Printed
> here in our own words.**
>
> A well's rate falls over time. Arps set out three shapes that the fall can take, and gave an
> equation for each.
>
> | The type | How it falls | The total it predicts |
> |---|---|---|
> | **exponential** | by the same percentage of the current rate every year | the **smallest** |
> | **hyperbolic** | by a percentage that itself gets smaller | in between |
> | **harmonic** | flattest of all, a long tail | the **largest** |
>
> **Exponential decline is the easy one to spot.** Plotted on semi-logarithmic paper — rate on a
> logarithmic scale, time on an ordinary one — it is a **straight line**. That is why production
> engineers have been plotting on semi-log paper for eighty years.
>
> Once you have a curve you can do two things with it. You can **extend** it to find the rate next
> year, and you can add up the area under it down to the **economic limit** — the rate at which the
> well stops paying for itself — to get a total. That total is where a very large share of this
> industry's reserve figures come from.
>
> **The problem is at the left-hand end.** Early data fits all three shapes. The curves only separate
> later, and later is exactly the part being forecast. Two years of production cannot tell you which
> curve you are on; it can only rule out the ones that fit badly.
>
> **WHAT THE CURVES ASSUME**
> Arps was explicit, and the point has been forgotten more often than it has been quoted. These are
> **empirical equations for fitting observed data**. They are not derived from the physics of a
> reservoir. They are shapes that have been found to fit, and they work because reservoirs behave in a
> limited number of ways and not because the mathematics knows anything about rock.
>
> They assume the well is **produced the same way** for the whole period. No change of choke, no
> change in wellhead back pressure, no change in the lift method, no change in the number of hours a
> day it runs.
>
> They assume **nothing is done to the well**. A workover, an acid job, a new perforation or a repair
> moves the well onto a new curve, and the old fit no longer applies to anything.
>
> They assume **nothing changes in the reservoir around it**. A new well drilled into the same
> reservoir, or an injection scheme started nearby, changes the pressure the well is producing against.
>
> Read that list again. **Every item on it is something an operating company does on purpose and
> regularly.** The most widely used forecasting method in the industry rests on an assumption that the
> industry violates deliberately, several times a year, and the forecast is usually not redone.
>
> One more caution, added by later work rather than by Arps. A hyperbolic fit can be made to produce a
> total that **never stops growing** if the exponent is pushed too far. A curve that implies infinite
> oil from a finite reservoir is a sign that the fit has stopped being physics and become arithmetic.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its author, its transactions and its year. Read the
> published paper, and write down the year of the volume you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Put the assumption in the same sentence as the number.** *1.6 million barrels* is not a forecast.
> *1.6 million barrels, on an exponential fit to the last three years, with no further intervention, to
> an economic limit of 40 barrels a day* is a forecast, and it is only two lines longer. And when
> something is done to the well, write the date of the job on the forecast sheet. A forecast with two
> undated workovers inside its history is not a forecast of anything.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> A forecast is a sentence about the future that becomes, within about a week, a target somebody is
> judged against. That change happens quietly and nobody announces it, and it is why forecasting is
> never only a technical activity. The engineer who writes *assuming no intervention* is protecting a
> number that other people need to be simple. The honest habit is not to refuse to forecast; it is to
> make the assumption travel with the number, in the same sentence, every time it is quoted — because
> the moment they separate, the assumption never catches up again.
> **Ask your group: what number are you measured against, and does anybody still know what it assumed?**
