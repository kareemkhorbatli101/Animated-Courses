# Unit 11 · Counting Failures Properly
%%SUB Where the pump stops, what a failure is, and why two sites cannot be compared%%
%%GOALS

A pump broke. That sentence contains two decisions somebody has already made for you: where the pump
ends, and what *broke* means. A standard published in 2016 is almost entirely about those two
decisions, and it is the least glamorous and most quietly useful document in this book.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rb11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its number, its year and its title, a nine-level hierarchy diagram, a pump with a dashed boundary drawn around it, a three-row table of mode, cause and mechanism, a boxed paragraph headed what the standard does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: counting failures so that the count means something**
> **Source: **ISO 14224:2016**, *Petroleum, petrochemical and natural gas industries — Collection and
> exchange of reliability and maintenance data for equipment*. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> This standard exists because reliability data from different places could not be put together. Each
> site called its equipment something different, drew the edges of it somewhere different, and
> counted different events as failures. The data was real and the comparisons were nonsense.
>
> **A taxonomy.** The standard sets out a **hierarchy** for naming equipment, running from the
> industry at the top down through the installation, the plant, the system, the **equipment unit**, its
> subunits, and on down to the individual part. Nine levels. The point of it is that everybody fills in
> the same box with the same kind of thing.
>
> **A boundary.** For each type of equipment, the standard requires a **boundary diagram**: a drawing
> that says what is inside the equipment unit and what is outside it. Is the driver part of the pump?
> Is the lubrication system? Is the control panel? There is no universally right answer. There is only
> a written one, and without it the same work orders give different counts.
>
> **A definition of failure.** Failure is defined as the **termination of the ability of an item to
> perform a required function**. Read the last three words carefully: a failure is relative to what
> the item was required to do. A pump delivering half its rated flow has failed if it was required to
> deliver full flow and has not failed if it was not.
>
> The standard also classifies **how bad** a failure is: a **critical** failure stops the required
> function; a **degraded** failure means the item still works but not properly; an **incipient**
> failure is one found before it has had any effect.
>
> **Three different questions.**
>
> | The question | The term |
> |---|---|
> | how the failure showed itself | the **failure mode** |
> | why it happened | the **failure cause** |
> | the physical process that produced it | the **failure mechanism** |
>
> Most work orders in the world record the mode, sometimes the mechanism, and almost never the cause —
> which is the only one of the three that tells you how to stop it happening again.
>
> **WHAT THE STANDARD DOES NOT DO**
> It does not tell you **how reliable your equipment should be**. It sets no target, no benchmark and
> no acceptable failure rate. It is a standard about how to write things down.
>
> It does not do the **analysis**. Having good data is the condition for analysis, not the analysis,
> and a site can collect faultless data for ten years and never ask it a question.
>
> It depends entirely on **the person filling in the form**. The standard says plainly that the
> quality of the data depends on the collection process and on the competence and motivation of the
> people recording it. A standard cannot make somebody write the cause at the end of a long shift.
>
> And it is only a basis for **comparison if both sides use it the same way**. Two organizations can
> both claim to follow this standard and still be uncomparable, because they drew the pump boundary
> differently or defined the required function differently. The standard makes comparison **possible**.
> It does not make it **automatic**, and almost every confident comparison you will ever hear has
> skipped this paragraph.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its number, its year and its title. Read the
> published standard, and write down the edition you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write the cause even if it is a question.** *Cause: unknown, oil sample sent to the laboratory on
> 15 March* is a real entry. A blank field is not, and a blank field is what turns four identical
> bearing failures into four separate repairs instead of one investigation. And always put the
> boundary reference in the header. A failure count with no boundary behind it cannot be compared with
> anything, including your own count from five years ago.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> A well-run maintenance department fixes things fast, and that is exactly how a repeat failure hides.
> Each repair is a small success: the pump is back, the shift is saved, somebody is thanked. Four
> small successes in eighteen months look like competence and are the signature of a problem nobody
> has looked at. The only thing that breaks the pattern is a field on a form that asks *why*, filled
> in by somebody tired, at the end of a job that is already finished. **Ask your group: what is
> repaired most often at your site, and has anybody ever asked why?**
