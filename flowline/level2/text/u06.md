# Unit 6 · Lifting It With Gas
%%SUB Making the column lighter, and the point past which more gas gives you less oil%%
%%GOALS

A well stops flowing when the column in the tubing weighs more than the reservoir can push. You can
make the column lighter. There is a right amount of gas to do that with, and a wrong amount, and
the wrong amount is more.

%%FIG rb06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its author, the proceedings it appeared in and its year, a two-row table of the two effects, a curve with a peak, a boxed paragraph headed drawn by hand from field data, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: making the column lighter**
> **Source: W. E. Gilbert, "Flowing and Gas-lift Well Performance", in *Drilling and Production
> Practice*, American Petroleum Institute, **1954**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> A well flows if the reservoir pressure is greater than everything in the way: the weight of the
> fluid in the tubing, the friction, and the pressure at the wellhead. As a field ages the fluid gets
> heavier — more water, less gas — and sooner or later the reservoir cannot lift it.
>
> **Gas lift adds gas to the tubing** so that the column becomes lighter. The gas goes down the
> annulus, through a valve in the tubing wall, and up with the oil. The weight of the column falls,
> the pressure at the bottom of the well falls with it, the drawdown increases, and the well produces.
>
> **But two things happen at once.**
>
> | What injecting gas does | Which way it pushes the rate |
> |---|---|
> | the column gets **lighter**, so the bottomhole pressure falls | **up** |
> | the flow gets **faster**, so friction in the tubing rises | **down** |
>
> At low injection rates the first effect is much larger. As you inject more, the gains get smaller
> and the friction losses grow. There is a **peak** — an optimum injection rate — and past it **more
> gas gives you less oil**, while the compressor burns the same fuel or more.
>
> **The gradient curves.** Gilbert's paper gave engineers a way to work out the pressure at any depth
> in a flowing well, for a given rate, tubing size and gas-liquid ratio. He built families of curves
> and published them. Everything since has been a refinement of that idea.
>
> **The choke rule.** The same paper gave an empirical relationship between the pressure upstream of a
> wellhead choke, the liquid rate, the gas-liquid ratio and the choke size. It holds while the flow
> through the choke is **critical** — fast enough that the pressure downstream of the choke stops
> affecting the rate upstream of it. In practice that is taken to be the case while the downstream
> pressure is below roughly **seventy percent** of the upstream pressure. Above that the choke is no
> longer controlling the well, and the relationship does not apply.
>
> **DRAWN BY HAND FROM FIELD DATA**
> Gilbert's curves were constructed from **measurements on real wells**, by hand, in the 1950s, and
> the paper says which wells and which conditions. They are **empirical**. They are not a theory of
> multiphase flow and he did not claim they were.
>
> They were built for **particular tubing sizes and particular fluids**. Used outside that range they
> drift, and every later correlation in this field exists because somebody found a range where
> Gilbert's curves did not fit.
>
> They are in **field units** — pounds per square inch, barrels a day, standard cubic feet per barrel,
> and choke size in sixty-fourths of an inch. Converting them carelessly is one of the commonest
> errors in this subject.
>
> And they say nothing about whether your gas lift system is **actually working**. A well with a
> leaking valve, gas going to the wrong depth, or injection gas disappearing into the formation can
> look lifted on paper and be lifting nothing. The giveaway is the injection pressure, not the oil
> rate.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its author, its proceedings and its year. Read the
> published paper. The curves in it were drawn with a pen.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Report the injection rate beside the oil rate, every month.** An oil rate on its own cannot tell
> anybody whether a well is being lifted well or expensively, and a gas lift well is the one case in
> production where the input is as important as the output. And when you want to find the optimum,
> step the gas **down**, not up. Stepping up costs gas to find out you were already past the peak.
> Stepping down costs nothing and tells you the same thing.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> *When it is not working, do more of it* is one of the most reliable instincts in any workplace, and
> it is right much of the time. Gas lift is one of the places where it is reliably wrong, and the
> reason is worth carrying out of this unit: the thing you are adding has two effects, and the second
> one is invisible until it is bigger than the first. Pushing harder is visible, measurable and feels
> like effort. Stepping down looks like giving up and is actually an experiment.
> **Ask your group: where at your site does doing more of something make it worse, and does anybody
> say so?**
