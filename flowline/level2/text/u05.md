Every operator has been asked to open a well up a bit. Above a certain point, opening a well up stops
giving you more oil, and starts giving you gas and trouble. A paper from 1968 explains why, and says
plainly how it was made.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rb05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its author, the journal and its year, two graphs showing a straight line and a curve, a four-row table of what happens as drawdown increases, a boxed paragraph headed twenty-one computed cases, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: how much more you get when you pull harder**
> **Source: J. V. Vogel, "Inflow Performance Relationships for Solution-Gas Drive Wells", *Journal of
> Petroleum Technology*, **1968**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> A well flows because the pressure in the reservoir is higher than the pressure at the bottom of the
> well. The difference is called the **drawdown**, and the relationship between the drawdown and the
> rate is the **inflow performance relationship**.
>
> **Above the bubble point it is a straight line.** Only liquid is moving in the rock. Double the
> drawdown and you double the rate. The slope of that line is the **productivity index**: so many
> barrels a day for each bar of drawdown. It is simple, it is useful, and it is the thing everybody
> learns first.
>
> **Below the bubble point it is not a straight line.** As the pressure around the well falls below
> the bubble point, gas comes out of solution **in the pores**. The gas takes up space the oil was
> using. The rock's ability to let oil through — its **relative permeability** to oil — falls. So:
>
> | As you increase the drawdown | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | a little, above the bubble point | the rate rises in proportion |
> | past the bubble point | gas starts coming out in the rock near the well |
> | more | the oil has to get past the gas, and its path gets worse |
> | a lot | each extra bar of drawdown buys less oil than the bar before it |
>
> **The more you pull, the less each extra bar gives you.** That is the whole result, and the paper
> gave it a shape.
>
> Vogel plotted his curves **dimensionlessly**: the rate as a fraction of the maximum rate, against
> the bottomhole pressure as a fraction of the reservoir pressure. Plotted that way, cases that looked
> very different collapsed onto nearly one curve. That single curve is what engineers have used ever
> since, and it is the reason the paper is still cited after more than fifty years.
>
> **TWENTY-ONE COMPUTED CASES**
> Here is the part to read twice. The curve was **not** drawn from field measurements. It was drawn
> from **twenty-one hypothetical reservoir cases**, calculated on a computer, with the computers of
> 1968. Vogel said so. He described the cases, and he described how he had varied them.
>
> That is not a criticism. It is why the result can be trusted as far as it goes: a reader can see
> exactly what was varied and what was held fixed, which is more than most correlations offer.
>
> It applies to **solution-gas drive**. It was not built for a reservoir under water drive or with an
> expanding gas cap, and the physics it captures — gas appearing in the pores because the pressure has
> fallen — is not what is happening in those reservoirs.
>
> It assumes a well with **no significant damage**. A well with a damaged zone around it has a worse
> inflow than the curve predicts, for a reason that has nothing to do with the reservoir. If a well
> underperforms the curve, the first question is not about the rock.
>
> And the **maximum rate** in the dimensionless plot is the rate at **zero** bottomhole pressure. No
> well is ever operated there. It is a number used to make the mathematics tidy, and quoting it as
> something a well could do is a misuse of the paper.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its author, its journal and its year. Read the
> published paper. It is short, and the first page says what it is.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Report the gas with the oil, always.** A rate on its own looks like good news. *437 barrels a day
> and a gas-oil ratio of 530, up from 412 and 380* is the actual news, and anybody reading it can see
> what the extra 25 barrels cost. And when a well gives less than the curve says, write *this well is
> below the curve* and not *the reservoir is poor*. The first is an observation. The second is a
> conclusion about the one thing in the system you cannot fix.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Production is reported monthly and reservoirs answer in years, so there is a permanent pull toward
> pulling harder. Nothing about that is unique to oil: it is the same pressure a driver feels on a
> long hill, a nurse feels at the end of a shift and a farmer feels in a dry year. What makes this
> industry unusual is that the cost of over-pulling arrives slowly, somewhere else, in a number
> somebody else will be asked about. The best protection is not a rule. It is the habit of writing the
> two numbers side by side so that whoever decides has to look at both.
