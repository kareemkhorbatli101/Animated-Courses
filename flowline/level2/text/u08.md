interesting object on the plant and the one that decides whether anything downstream of it works.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rb08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the specification, its number and its publisher, a cross-section of a horizontal separator with four numbered zones, a four-row table of the stages, a boxed paragraph headed what the specification assumes, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: what happens inside the drum**
> **Source: American Petroleum Institute, **Specification 12J**, *Specification for Oil and Gas
> Separators*. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> A separator has nothing moving inside it. It works by slowing the stream down and giving gravity
> time. Everything that goes wrong with one goes wrong because it did not get the time.
>
> | Stage | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **1 primary separation** | the stream hits an inlet diverter, loses velocity, and most of the liquid falls out immediately |
> | **2 gravity settling** | in the open space above the liquid, remaining droplets fall out of the gas |
> | **3 mist extraction** | a pad or a set of vanes catches the smallest droplets before the gas leaves |
> | **4 liquid collection** | liquid sits in the bottom; gas bubbles rise out of it; in a three-phase vessel, oil and water separate and leave by different outlets, usually over a weir |
>
> **Two-phase or three-phase.** A two-phase separator splits gas from liquid. A three-phase separator
> also splits the liquid into oil and water, which takes longer and needs more room.
>
> **Retention time** is the heart of it: how long the liquid stays in the vessel. Short retention and
> the oil leaves with gas still in it and water still in it. The specification gives sizing procedures
> based on retention time and on gas capacity, and typical oil retention times for ordinary crudes are
> a small number of minutes — longer where the oil and water do not want to part.
>
> **The two failures have names.** **Carry-over** is liquid leaving with the gas, which damages
> compressors and overloads flares. **Carry-under** is gas leaving with the liquid, which makes the
> metering downstream wrong and can gas-lock a pump. An operator who can say which of the two is
> happening has already done most of the diagnosis.
>
> **WHAT THE SPECIFICATION ASSUMES**
> It is a **specification for a vessel**: how it is designed, built, marked and tested. It is not a
> process design for a facility, and it does not decide what size of separator your plant needs.
>
> Its sizing procedures assume you **know the inlet conditions**: the flow rates of gas, oil and
> water, the pressure, the temperature and the fluid properties. Those were known on the day the
> vessel was ordered. Twenty years later they are all different, and nothing on the nameplate
> changes.
>
> It assumes a fluid that **separates**. A foaming crude occupies the vessel with foam rather than
> liquid, and the retention time that was calculated no longer exists. A tight emulsion does not part
> in minutes at any size, and needs heat, chemical or both — none of which is in this specification.
>
> It says nothing about **how the vessel is operated**. A correctly designed separator run at a low
> level, at the wrong pressure, or with a mist pad that has been fouled for two years, will perform
> badly, and no inspection of the drawings will show you why.
>
> **And a nameplate records an intention, not a capability today.** It says what the vessel was built
> to handle when it was built. It does not say what it will do with the stream you are putting through
> it this morning, and it does not know that the water cut has quadrupled.
>
> *Our summary of a published specification, named with its number and its publisher. Read the
> published specification, and write down the edition you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Compare today against the nameplate, in two columns, on one page.** Nobody intends to overload a
> vessel; it happens because the stream changed slowly over fifteen years and the nameplate stayed
> still. Two columns side by side turn an invisible drift into a visible number in about four seconds.
> And when you record a level change, record **why**, and record the retention time before and after.
> A level is not a setting. It is the design of the vessel, adjusted by hand, by whoever was on shift.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Almost every chronic problem on a process plant lives in the space between two teams. One team's
> solution is the other team's cause, and neither of them is wrong or lazy — they are each looking at
> the page they are given. The separator is the clearest example in this book because the lever is
> literally shared: one setting, two consequences, two buildings, two sets of monthly numbers. Where a
> plant gets this right it is almost never because of a procedure; it is because somebody made a page
> with both numbers on it and refused to let it be split up. **Ask your group: which two teams at your
> site share a lever, and is there a page with both their numbers on it?**
