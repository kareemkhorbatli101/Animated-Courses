### 2 · Read

%%FIG rb12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the system, the four societies that publish it and its revision year, a classification diagram of in place, discovered, undiscovered, commercial and sub-commercial, a three-row table of the certainty levels, a boxed paragraph headed why a reserve figure can fall with no oil moving, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: what a company is allowed to say it has**
> **Source: the **Petroleum Resources Management System**, published by the Society of Petroleum
> Engineers, the World Petroleum Council, the American Association of Petroleum Geologists and the
> Society of Petroleum Evaluation Engineers; **2018** revision. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Start with everything that is down there. The system calls it the total initially in place, and
> almost none of it will ever be produced.
>
> | The class | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | **production** | what has already come out |
> | **reserves** | **discovered**, **recoverable**, **commercial** and **remaining**, as of a stated date |
> | **contingent resources** | discovered and recoverable, but **not yet commercial** |
> | **prospective resources** | **not yet discovered** |
> | **unrecoverable** | there, and never coming out |
>
> **Read the definition of reserves again.** Four conditions, and only the first two are about rock.
> *Commercial* means there is a **committed project**, a reasonable expectation of a **market**,
> evidence of **economic viability** at the assumed prices and costs, and an expectation of production
> starting within a reasonable period. *Remaining as of a stated date* means the number has an
> **effective date** and is meaningless without it.
>
> **So reserves are a property of a project, not of a reservoir.** Two companies holding the same rock
> with different development plans, different costs and different price assumptions will honestly
> report different reserves, and neither is lying.
>
> **The three certainty levels.** Within reserves, quantities are divided by how confident the estimate
> is.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **proved** | at least a **90 per cent** probability that the quantity actually recovered will equal or exceed the estimate |
> | **proved plus probable** | the **50 per cent** level |
> | **proved plus probable plus possible** | the **10 per cent** level |
>
> Those percentages describe the **estimate**, not the rock. The rock has one answer and nobody knows
> it. The percentages say how sure the person doing the work is, which is a different and more honest
> kind of statement.
>
> Reserves are also classified by **project maturity** — on production, approved for development,
> justified for development — because a quantity that depends on a decision nobody has taken is not
> the same asset as a quantity coming out of a pipe this morning.
>
> **WHY A RESERVE FIGURE CAN FALL WITH NO OIL MOVING**
> A price assumption changes and a project stops being economic: the quantity moves from reserves to
> contingent resources. A board defers a development: the commitment is gone, so the quantity moves.
> A permit lapses, a partner withdraws, a market closes. Nothing underground has changed. **Reserves
> have fallen.** This is the single most misunderstood fact in the industry's public conversation, and
> it is stated plainly in the document.
>
> **And here is what the system says about itself.** It is a set of **guidelines** for classification,
> published by professional societies. It is **not a reporting regulation**. Securities regulators have
> their own definitions, which differ, so the same field can honestly carry one reserves number in a
> technical report and a different one in a filing.
>
> It depends on the **evaluator's judgment** and on the data available — which, after eleven units of
> this book, you can now describe precisely: a permeability from a thumb-sized plug, a material
> balance with two missing pressure surveys, a decline curve that assumes nobody intervenes, a meter
> factor measured at a flow rate the meter no longer runs at, and a failure history with blank cause
> fields.
>
> That is not a reason to distrust the number. It is the reason the system insists on ranges,
> categories, effective dates and stated assumptions instead of a single figure. **The careful
> document is the honest one.**
>
> *Our summary of a published system, named with its publishers and its revision year. Read the
> published document. It is free, it is clear, and it is the best-written thing in this course.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Never write a reserve figure without its date, its system and its assumption.** Those three things
> turn a number into a statement somebody can check, and they are the first three things to be dropped
> when the number moves from a report to a slide to a sentence in a meeting. And when reserves fall,
> say in the same sentence whether any oil was produced from the affected area. Otherwise everybody in
> the room will assume the rock disappointed you, which is almost never what happened.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Of everything in these twelve units, this is the one most often got wrong in public. A reserves
> revision is reported as oil vanishing, a failure of geology, or something worse, when most revisions
> are decisions about projects and prices. The industry is not blameless here: a number that rises
> gets announced and a number that falls gets explained, and the explaining happens in a document
> nobody reads. The habit that helps is the one in this unit: one sentence, above the table, saying
> whether any oil was involved. **Ask your group: when a number at your site moves, who explains it,

**A reserves figure of 41 million barrels was signed off.** Behind it: a permeability from five
thumb-sized plugs in one well, a material balance with two missing pressure surveys in the two years
the trend changed, a decline curve fitted before two workovers nobody dated, a production history
metered by an instrument proved at three times its current flow rate, and a failure history with the
