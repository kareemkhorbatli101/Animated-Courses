Nothing lifts oil out of a reservoir. Something pushes it, and the something is already down there.
Which something it is decides how much of the oil you will ever see.

### 2 · Six more words, and the five drives

%%FIG rb02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its publisher and its year, a five-row table of drive mechanisms, a diagram of a reservoir drawn as a single tank, a boxed paragraph headed what the equation does not contain, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: what pushes the oil, and the equation that tells you which**
> **Source: L. P. Dake, *Fundamentals of Reservoir Engineering*, Elsevier, **1978**. Printed here in
> our own words.**
>
> Oil does not rise on its own. Something has to push it, and the push comes from inside the
> reservoir until somebody puts something in from outside.
>
> | The drive | What pushes | What it does to your plant |
> |---|---|---|
> | **solution gas** | gas coming out of solution as pressure falls, and expanding | rising gas-oil ratio, falling pressure, and it does not last |
> | **gas cap** | a free gas cap above the oil, expanding downward | gas breakthrough in the upper wells |
> | **water** | an aquifer below, pushing up | rising water cut, and a water plant you may not have built |
> | **compaction** | the rock closing as the fluid pressure drops | sometimes subsidence at surface |
> | **gravity drainage** | oil draining down a steep layer | slow, patient, and can recover a great deal |
>
> Most reservoirs have more than one of these. The question is always which one is **dominant** now,
> because that changes as the field produces.
>
> **The material balance.** The book's central tool treats the whole reservoir as a **single tank
> with one average pressure**. You put in everything that has been produced, everything that has been
> injected, and the pressure history. What comes out is an estimate of how much oil was there to begin
> with, and which drive is doing the work.
>
> It is a deliberately crude picture, and that is its strength. It has almost no assumptions about
> geology in it, so it is hard to fool. Where a complicated model and a material balance disagree, the
> material balance is usually the one to argue with last.
>
> **WHAT THE EQUATION DOES NOT CONTAIN**
> It contains **no geometry**. There is no map inside it, no thickness, no fault, no well location.
> It cannot tell you where to drill, how many wells you need, or in what order. People ask it those
> questions anyway, and it answers them the way any tank would: by saying nothing.
>
> It needs an **average reservoir pressure**, and that is the hardest number on any field. To measure
> it properly you shut a well in and let it come to rest, which means producing nothing from it for
> hours or days. The information is for next year. The lost production is today. Every field in the
> world has gaps in its pressure history for that reason, and the gaps are not random: they are
> concentrated in the years when the field was busiest.
>
> It needs **accurate cumulative production**, including the water and the gas. A field that has
> been metered loosely for a decade cannot do a material balance at all, and no amount of
> arithmetic repairs it.
>
> The **aquifer is usually the largest unknown**. How much water will come, and how fast, is itself
> estimated from the pressure history you are trying to explain. That is circular, the book says so,
> and the honest result is a range rather than a number.
>
> And it is a **textbook**, not a standard. Nobody is certified against it, it prescribes nothing, and
> the recovery factors anybody quotes from this subject are **typical ranges observed in fields**, not
> predictions about yours.
>
> *Our summary of a published textbook, named with its author, its publisher and its year. Read the
> published book, and write down the edition you read.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every field is run by two clocks. One is this month's production figure, which somebody is asked
> about on a fixed day, by name. The other is the reservoir, which answers in years and asks nobody's
> permission. A pressure survey is the clearest case in the industry of a cost today against knowledge
> later, and the person who pays is never the person who benefits. No procedure solves that. What
> helps is writing down, every single time, what the missing survey would have told you — so that in
> three years the gap in the table has a reason beside it and not just a blank.
