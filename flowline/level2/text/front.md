# Flowline 1
%%SUB English for the Oil and Gas Industries · Syrian Edition · CEFR A2+ to B1%%

## How to use this book

This is the second book of six. Book 1 was the plant and the people. This one goes **down the hole**
and follows the oil forward: the rock it sits in, what pushes it out, the equipment that stops it
coming out too fast, how fast a well will flow, how it is lifted when it will not flow, how it
declines, how the water and the gas are taken off it, where the water goes, how the oil is counted,
how failures are counted, and finally how much a company is allowed to say it has.

**Twelve units**, eighteen pages each, with the same six lessons in the same order every time.

| Lesson | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1 · Vocabulary** | Sixteen words, a second set of six, and a team research task |
| **2 · Reading** | One real paper, standard or report, with its date and its limits |
| **3 · Grammar** | Two structures, with the mistake each one causes |
| **4 · On Site** | Clear English, a dialogue, a culture check and a role-play |
| **5 · Writing** | A model, then your own |
| **Team Up & Review** | A picture, a team project, a survey, and one problem with no easy answer |

**The audio.** 🔊 marks a track. 195 tracks, numbered `FL2-unit-track`.

## What is different about this book

**Four of the twelve sources are older than the industry's own standards.** Darcy published in 1856,
Arps in 1945, Gilbert in 1954 and Vogel in 1968. They are here because the reservoir has not changed
since, and because a paper written by one person solving one problem states its own assumptions far
more plainly than a committee document ever does. Arps said his curves were empirical. Vogel said his
relationship came from twenty-one computed cases. Gilbert drew his curves by hand. Each of them told
you exactly how far to trust the result, and every engineer who has quoted them since has had the
chance to read that sentence.

**One of the twelve is a public inquiry.** Unit 4 is the United States national commission's report
on the 2010 Macondo blowout, and it sits immediately after the well control standard in Unit 3 on
purpose: the standard tells you what equipment must do, and the report tells you what happened when
nine separate decisions each looked defensible on its own.

**The numbers in this book are estimates, and the book keeps saying so.** A permeability is measured
on a core the size of your thumb and applied to a reservoir the size of a town. A decline curve is a
line drawn through the past. A reserve figure is a probability with a word attached. This is not a
weakness of the subject; it is the subject.

**A language course is not a safety course.** This book names well control equipment and quotes the
documents that govern it. It does not authorize you to do anything.

**Money has no symbol in this series.**

## The people

The same company and the same fourteen people as Book 1, in the same jobs.

**Jazira Petroleum Operations** runs the **Tell Sahra** field, the **Mureij** gathering station and
the **Sahra** central processing facility in the northeast. All of it is **invented**.

**Nour Al-Masri**, process engineer, runs the **Mureij Training Center**. **Samir Haddad** is the
field operations superintendent. **Yusuf Khalaf** is the HSE officer. **Samira Qassab** is an
instrument technician. **Ahmad Sweid** is a mechanic. **Nadia Shahin** works on metering and
allocation. **Adeel Rahman** works on pipeline integrity. **Yara Barakat** is a laboratory chemist.
**Lars Eriksen**, **Ida Lindqvist**, **Wei Zhao** and **Tomas Varga** work for contractors.
**Omar Dabbagh** is now four months in, and still asking.

**Mariama Bah** is the reservoir engineer, and this is her book. She appears in every unit, and the
thing she says most often is *that is a model, not a measurement*.
