### 2 · Read

%%FIG rb09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the regulation, its parts, the program it belongs to and the country, a diagram of an injection well in section with a shallow aquifer protected above, a four-row table of requirements, a boxed paragraph headed this is not the law here, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the second well, and what a published rulebook asks of it**
> **Source: the Underground Injection Control program of the United States Environmental Protection
> Agency, and its rules for **Class II** wells at **Title 40 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Parts
> 144 and 146**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **Why we are reading another country's regulation.** Because it is published, it is detailed, it is
> free to read, and it is the clearest public account anywhere of what a produced water injection well
> is supposed to do. It is **not the law here**. We say that twice in this note.
>
> The program divides injection wells into **classes** by what goes down them. The class that
> matters to us is the one for fluids brought to the surface in connection with oil and gas
> production: produced water for disposal, and water injected to improve recovery.
>
> The purpose of the whole program is narrow and worth knowing exactly: it exists to protect
> **underground sources of drinking water**. Everything in it follows from that one aim.
>
> | What the rules require | Why |
> |---|---|
> | **authorization** — a permit, or permission under a general rule | somebody outside the company has to agree the well may exist |
> | **construction** — casing and cement that isolate the injection zone from every shallow water-bearing layer | so the injected fluid cannot reach drinking water |
> | **mechanical integrity** — demonstrated by test, in two separate parts | so the isolation is shown to be intact, not assumed |
> | **a maximum injection pressure** — calculated and not to be exceeded | so the injection does not fracture the confining layer and open a path upward |
> | **monitoring, records and reporting** — volumes, pressures, tests | so a change can be noticed by somebody |
>
> **Mechanical integrity has two halves, and the names matter.** The first is **internal**: no
> significant leak in the casing, the tubing or the packer, usually shown by holding pressure on the
> annulus. The second is **external**: no significant movement of fluid upward through channels beside
> the wellbore, shown by a log or by other approved means. A well can pass one and fail the other, and
> the two tests are looking for completely different failures.
>
> **THIS IS NOT THE LAW HERE, AND HERE IS WHAT ELSE IT IS NOT**
> It applies in **one jurisdiction**, and within that jurisdiction the program may be run by the
> federal agency or by a state that has been given authority to run it, so even there the detail
> differs by place. Nothing in it governs this company, and reading it is not compliance with
> anything.
>
> Its aim is **drinking water**. It is not a general environmental standard. It is not about the
> surface: a leaking tank, a spill off a pad, a corroded line across a field are all real and are all
> somebody else's rulebook.
>
> **Mechanical integrity is a defined term, not a description.** A well that passes demonstrates no
> **significant** leak, by the **test applied**, on the **day of the test**. It is not a certificate
> that a fifty-year-old string of casing is sound, and the rules do not pretend otherwise.
>
> The **maximum pressure is calculated** from an estimate of the strength of the confining layer. Like
> every number in this book, it rests on measurements made somewhere else on something smaller.
>
> And the **time horizon in the rules is unlike anything else in production**. An oil well has an
> economic life. An injection well's requirement not to leak does not end when the field does: that is
> why plugging and abandonment is in the same rulebook as the injection itself.
>
> *Our summary of a published regulation, named with its parts and its country. Read the published
> regulation. It is freely available, and it is written in a language you can learn to read in an
> afternoon.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Record the annulus pressure even when it is boring.** It is the only number on an injection well
> record that is looking at the barrier rather than at the job, and a slow rise over three weeks is
> the clearest early warning in this part of the plant. And when you write an injection pressure,
> write the permitted maximum beside it. *141 bar* means nothing. *141 of a permitted 147* tells the
> next reader that there is almost nothing left, which is a planning problem long before it is a
> compliance problem.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Produced water is the largest stream in oil production and the one with the least status. Nobody is
> promoted for it. It has no price, so it appears in no revenue line, only in costs. And the well it
> goes into carries the longest obligation anybody on a production site takes on: an oil well is
> finished when it stops paying, and a disposal well's duty not to leak runs on past the field, past
> the company, and past everybody who ever worked there. That mismatch — lowest status, longest
> obligation — is worth saying out loud, because it is the reason this part of a plant is usually the
> last to be funded. **Ask your group: who at your site is responsible for the water, and where do
> they sit in the organization?**
