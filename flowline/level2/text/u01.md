In 1856 a municipal engineer in Dijon published a long book about the town's water supply. In an
appendix he described some experiments with sand in a tube. Every reservoir engineer alive still uses
them, and almost none of them have read the sentence where he says what he was measuring.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rb01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its city and its year, a diagram of a vertical sand column with two pressure gauges, a four-row table of the things that decide the flow, a boxed paragraph headed what he was not measuring, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the oldest equation on the site**
> **Source: Henry Darcy, *Les Fontaines Publiques de la Ville de Dijon*, Paris, **1856**. The
> experiments are described in an appendix. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> Darcy was the municipal engineer of Dijon. The town wanted clean water, and clean water meant sand
> filters. He needed to know how fast water would pass through a bed of sand, so he built a vertical
> iron tube, packed it with sand, put a pressure gauge at each end, and measured.
>
> He found something simple and very useful. The flow rate was **proportional to the pressure
> difference** and **proportional to the area**, and it depended on the sand. Later work added the
> fluid: the flow is **inversely proportional to the viscosity**.
>
> | What it is | What it does to the flow |
> |---|---|
> | the **pressure difference** across the sample | doubles it if you double the difference |
> | the **area** of flow | doubles it if you double the area |
> | the **permeability** of the rock | the property of the rock itself |
> | the **viscosity** of the fluid | halves the flow if you double the viscosity |
>
> The unit of permeability is named after him. A **darcy** is a large unit, so reservoir rock is
> usually described in **millidarcies** — thousandths of a darcy. A good sandstone might be a few
> hundred millidarcies. A tight rock might be less than one.
>
> **WHAT HE WAS NOT MEASURING**
> Everything useful about this result comes from reading what he actually did.
>
> He used **water**. One fluid. In a reservoir, oil, gas and water move through the same pores at the
> same time, and they get in each other's way. That needs a different idea — **relative
> permeability** — which Darcy never studied and which is still measured with difficulty today.
>
> He used **slow flow**. The relationship holds while the flow is **laminar**. Close to a wellbore,
> where all the fluid in a large area is squeezed through a small circle of rock, the velocity rises
> and the flow can become **turbulent**. Then the simple law under-predicts the pressure you need,
> and an extra term has to be added.
>
> He used **sand**, loose and uniform. Reservoir rock is cemented, layered and cracked. A limestone
> with fractures can have a laboratory permeability of a few millidarcies and behave in a well test
> like a rock a hundred times better, because the fluid is not going through the pores at all. It is
> going through the cracks.
>
> And he measured **one sample**. A core plug is about the size of a thumb. Our permeability maps
> cover twelve kilometers. The gap between those two is not a detail of the method; it is the main
> source of uncertainty in everything in this book.
>
> **The law is empirical.** It was not derived from theory; it was found by measurement, and it is
> trusted because it has been reproduced for a hundred and seventy years. That is a good reason to
> trust it. It is not a reason to use it where he did not test it.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its author, its city and its year. Read the published
> appendix if you can find it, and write down the edition you read.*

**A field development plan was written around a permeability of 240 millidarcies.** The figure came
from five core plugs in one well, with a sixth excluded for a stated reason. By the time the plan
reached the committee, the first page said *reservoir permeability: 240 mD*, and the note about five
thumb-sized samples was in an appendix that nobody printed.

The wells came in at about a third of the expected rate. Nobody had lied at any stage.
