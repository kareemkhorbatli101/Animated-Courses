# Flowline · sources

**English for the Oil and Gas Industries · Syrian Edition.** Six books, seventy-one units carrying a
source and one review unit in each book that reviews the eleven before it; seventy-two separate
source bodies in all. Nothing in this series reuses a figure or a source body from Wayfinder English,
from the Clearline business series, from Foreign Service English, from Keystone, from Throughline or
from Plumbline.

## Nothing in this series is official

**This is an independent first-edition teaching draft.** It is **not published, endorsed or approved
by the Syrian Ministry of Petroleum and Mineral Resources, by the Syrian Petroleum Company, by any
other Syrian state body, by any standards organization named in it, or by any company.** It carries
no official status, and nothing in it is engineering, legal, safety, commercial or regulatory advice.

**Jazira Petroleum Operations and everything attached to it were written for these books.** The Tell
Sahra field, the Mureij gathering station, the Sahra central processing facility, the Mureij Training
Center, and every person, permit number, tank level, pressure reading, flaring rate, log entry,
incident and conversation in the series are teaching material. None of it is a record of anything
that happened anywhere.

**The real places are named as matters of public record only.** Deir ez-Zor, Hasakah, Homs, Banias,
the Syrian Petroleum Company, the Arab Gas Pipeline and the Kirkuk–Banias line appear because they
are facts about the country, and a course about this industry in this country that did not name them
would be strange. Nothing in these books describes their current condition, ownership, operation or
capacity.

**The published production figures disagree, and the series says so.** Where a book gives a figure
for Syrian production or refining capacity it gives the figure, the year and the kind of source, and
where sources differ it prints both rather than choosing one. Book 1's Starting Point is built on
that fact rather than around it.

**And the standing line, printed in every book:** safety information in a language course is not a
substitute for a competent person, a procedure and a permit. These books teach a reader to say *the
isolation is not confirmed*. They do not tell anybody whether it is.

## The rule this series is built on

Every reading text in every book quotes something real: a measurement standard, an equipment
specification, an industry association's recommended practice, a regulator's guidance, a public
inquiry report, a journal paper, a book, a treaty, a model contract. Three rules apply without
exception.

1. **The text names its document, its number, its edition or date, and its publisher or author.**
   Not "the standard says" but "API Specification 6A, identical to ISO 10423". Not "best practice is"
   but "IOGP Report 459, eighteen rules in 2013 and nine in the 2018 revision". A number and an
   edition are part of a citation, and a reader who cannot give one has not finished the sentence.
2. **The text prints what the document does not cover.** In this series that is the point of reading
   it. A measurement standard assumes a calibrated tank. An equipment specification says what
   equipment must be capable of and not whether this body is fit today. A set of life-saving rules
   addresses personal safety and not process safety, and says so. A management-system standard is
   certifiable and does not certify that a site is safe. A voluntary initiative has endorsers and not
   obligations. Every reading text ends with that line, and every unit's honesty note repeats it.
3. **Every person, company, field, station, figure and conversation is invented, and labeled as
   invented.** Where a unit needs a failure — four correct zeros from an isolated gauge, eight months
   of flaring coded *non-routine*, a permit with one empty box, an award won while the inspection
   backlog grew fivefold — that failure is written from the shape of the document being taught, not
   from any real operator at any real site.

**The series is vendor-neutral.** No contractor, software product, equipment manufacturer, inspection
company or training provider is recommended anywhere in the six books.

**Money has no symbol anywhere in this series.** A figure is written as a number and the unit is
whatever currency the reader deals in. A number without a symbol is readable in every country; a
number with the wrong symbol is readable in none.

## The ladder

The six books follow the **physical value chain**, not a syllabus convenience, so each book
presupposes the one before it: the plant and the people, then the rock, then the pipe, then the
refinery, then the risk, then the contract.

| Book | CEFR | What the book is about |
|---|---|---|
| **Foundation · Book 1** | A2 | *The Wellhead and the Words* — the plant, the units, the people, the permit, the gauge, the flare |
| **1 · Book 2** | A2+ to B1 | *From Reservoir to Tank* — upstream: the rock, the well, the lift, the separator, the meter |
| **2 · Book 3** | B1 | *Pipes, Pumps and Pressure* — midstream: flow, friction, pumps, pigs, corrosion, tanks, ships |
| **3 · Book 4** | B1+ | *The Refinery and the Barrel* — downstream: distillation, cracking, specifications, margins |
| **4 · Book 5** | B2 | *Risk, Integrity and the Permit* — how organizations fail, and what the inquiries said |
| **5 · Book 6** | B2+ to C1 | *Contracts, Capacity and the Long Horizon* — fiscal terms, transparency, and after the oil |

# Book 1 · Flowline Foundation — *The Wellhead and the Words*

CEFR A2 · figure prefix `ra` · audio prefix `FL1` · 196 figures · 195 tracks

Eleven of the twelve units carry a source. Unit 12 reviews the eleven and introduces one more, the
indicator framework that the whole book's record-keeping ends up in.

| Unit | Title | Source, as cited in the text | What the text prints as the document's own limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | What Comes Out of the Ground | American Petroleum Institute, ***Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards***, Chapter 1, ***Vocabulary*** | It defines terms for measurement purposes. It is not a dictionary of the industry, and a term defined for custody transfer may be used differently in a drilling office |
| **2** | The Barrel and the Cubic Meter | API, ***Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards***, Chapter 11.1, published jointly as **ASTM D1250** and **IP 200** | It gives correction factors for stated ranges of density and temperature and does not apply outside them. The 60 degree Fahrenheit and 15 degree Celsius bases are different bases, not a rounding, so two careful parties can correct the same oil and disagree |
| **3** | The Parts of a Wellhead | **API Specification 6A**, identical to **ISO 10423**, *Wellhead and christmas tree equipment* | It specifies what equipment must be capable of. It does not say any particular wellhead is fit for service today, it is not a maintenance or operating document, and a rating is not permission |
| **4** | Who Is on Shift | International Association of Oil and Gas Producers, **Report 423**, *HSE management — guidelines for working together in a contract environment* | Guidance for members, not a standard; nobody is certified against it. It does not define job titles, says nothing about employment law or contract terms, and assumes a written contract and a documented interface that may not exist on paper |
| **5** | Eighteen Rules Became Nine | IOGP **Report 459**, *Life-Saving Rules* — eighteen in the **2013** original, nine in the **2018** revision | The rules address personal safety: the activities that killed individuals. They are explicitly not process safety and not a management system, nine is not the number of ways to be killed, and the data behind them is the association's members' data only |
| **6** | What You Wear, and Why | **ISO 45001:2018**, *Occupational health and safety management systems — Requirements with guidance for use* | It specifies requirements for a system. Certification states that the system meets the requirements; it does not state that a workplace is safe, and the standard says so. It is deliberately generic and names no hazard and no equipment |
| **7** | The Smell You Must Not Trust | **API Recommended Practice 49**, on drilling and well servicing operations involving hydrogen sulfide | A recommended practice for particular operations. Exposure limits are set by regulators and differ between jurisdictions; the document does not set them, and it cannot make a nose into a detector |
| **8** | Before You Touch It | Health and Safety Executive (United Kingdom), **HSG250**, *Guidance on permit-to-work systems*, **2005** | Guidance, not law, written for one jurisdiction. It states that a permit system is only as good as the discipline applied to it, and it describes the ways such systems degrade |
| **9** | Five Minutes Before the Job | IOGP **Report 510**, *Operating Management System Framework*, **2014** | A framework of elements, not a procedure. **It contains no toolbox-talk section at all**, and the unit is built on that absence rather than pretending otherwise |
| **10** | Reading a Gauge | API, ***Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards***, Chapter 3, ***Tank Gauging*** | It assumes a current capacity table, a sound datum plate, an intact reference mark and calibrated equipment. It is a custody-transfer standard, not an operating instruction, and it cannot help with a dead instrument |
| **11** | Why There Is a Flame | The World Bank's ***Zero Routine Flaring by 2030*** initiative, launched **2015**, and the annual ***Global Gas Flaring Tracker Report*** | The initiative is voluntary, with endorsers rather than obligations, and addresses routine flaring only. The volumes are satellite-derived estimates with a stated uncertainty, not meter readings, and they say nothing about how completely the gas burned |
| **12** | One Shift, Start to Finish | IOGP **Report 456**, *Process safety — recommended practice on key performance indicators*, revised **2018** | A recommended practice. The tier thresholds are definitions, and a count depends on them. Reporting is by member companies, so rates between companies are comparable only if both are counting the same events over comparable hours |

**Book 1's honesty core.** The book is about a plant and the people on it, and in eleven of its twelve
units the real lesson is that a correct number can be useless. A rating is correct and is not
permission. A volume is correct and means nothing without its base. A zero is correct and may be a
shut isolation valve. A permit can have every valve in the right position and one empty box. An award
for three years without a lost-time injury can be accurate and not true.

# Book 2 · Flowline 1 — *From Reservoir to Tank*

CEFR A2+ to B1 · figure prefix `rb` · audio prefix `FL2` · 196 figures · 195 tracks

Every one of the twelve units carries a source. Unit 12 is the review, and its source — the resources
classification system — is the document the whole book has been walking toward.

Four of the twelve are single-author papers older than most of the industry's standards: Darcy in
1856, Arps in 1945, Gilbert in 1954, Vogel in 1968. They are here because a paper written by one
person solving one problem states its own assumptions far more plainly than a committee document
does, and because the reservoir has not changed since.

| Unit | Title | Source, as cited in the text | What the text prints as the document's own limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | How Oil Moves Through Rock | Henry Darcy, ***Les Fontaines Publiques de la Ville de Dijon***, Paris, **1856** | Water, one fluid, at low velocity, through loose uniform sand, on one sample. Empirical, and holds while the flow is laminar. Says nothing about oil, gas and water moving together, and nothing about a reservoir twelve kilometers across |
| **2** | What Pushes It Out | L. P. Dake, ***Fundamentals of Reservoir Engineering***, Elsevier, **1978** | A textbook, not a standard. The material balance contains **no geometry at all**, so it cannot say where to drill. It needs an average reservoir pressure and accurate cumulative production, and the aquifer estimate is drawn from the same pressure history it is used to explain |
| **3** | The Stack on the Wellhead | **API Standard 53**, *Blowout Prevention Equipment Systems for Drilling Wells*, **2012** edition | An equipment standard: not a well control procedure and not a well design document. A passed test shows that equipment held that pressure, from that direction, in that configuration, on that day. Shear rams are qualified against particular pipe sizes and grades |
| **4** | Nine Decisions in a Row | National Commission on the BP Deepwater Horizon Oil Spill and Offshore Drilling, ***Deep Water***, Report to the President, **January 2011** | A presidential commission's report, written to a deadline, about deepwater drilling in one jurisdiction. **Not a finding of legal liability.** The commission said publicly it had no power to compel testimony, and other investigations of the same event reached overlapping but not identical conclusions |
| **5** | How Fast Will It Flow | J. V. Vogel, "Inflow Performance Relationships for Solution-Gas Drive Wells", ***Journal of Petroleum Technology***, **1968** | Built from **twenty-one computed hypothetical cases**, not from field measurements, and the paper says so. For solution-gas drive. Assumes no significant well damage. Its maximum rate is at zero bottomhole pressure — a construct, not an operating point |
| **6** | Lifting It With Gas | W. E. Gilbert, "Flowing and Gas-lift Well Performance", in ***Drilling and Production Practice***, API, **1954** | Curves drawn by hand from measurements on particular wells, particular tubing sizes and fluids, in field units. Empirical, not a theory of multiphase flow. The choke relationship holds only in critical flow, and the curves cannot tell you whether a lift system is actually working |
| **7** | The Curve That Goes Down | J. J. Arps, "Analysis of Decline Curves", ***Transactions of the AIME***, **1945** | Empirical equations for fitting observed data, not derived from reservoir physics. They assume the well is produced the same way throughout, that nothing is done to it, and that nothing changes around it — **three assumptions an operating company violates deliberately, several times a year** |
| **8** | Three Streams Out of One Vessel | **API Specification 12J**, *Specification for Oil and Gas Separators* | A specification for a vessel, not a process design for a facility. Its sizing assumes known inlet conditions and a fluid that separates. It says nothing about how the vessel is operated, and a nameplate records what the vessel was built to handle rather than what it will do with today's stream |
| **9** | Where the Water Goes | The **Underground Injection Control** program of the US Environmental Protection Agency, Class II rules at **40 CFR Parts 144 and 146** | Applies in one jurisdiction only and is **not the law in Syria**; the unit says so twice, and says plainly why it quotes another country's regulation. Its purpose is drinking water, not the environment generally, and it does not cover the surface. *Mechanical integrity* is a defined term: no significant leak, by the test applied, on the day of the test |
| **10** | Proving the Meter | API, ***Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards***, **Chapter 4, *Proving Systems***, and **Chapter 12, *Calculation of Petroleum Quantities*** | Chapter 12 makes two parties agree on one answer; it does not improve a measurement, and a precisely computed figure from a badly proved meter is a precise wrong number. A meter factor belongs to the conditions it was found at. Neither chapter says how often to prove, and the prover has its own calibration and uncertainty |
| **11** | Counting Failures Properly | **ISO 14224:2016**, *Petroleum, petrochemical and natural gas industries — Collection and exchange of reliability and maintenance data for equipment* | A standard about how to write things down. It sets no target, no benchmark and no acceptable failure rate, and it does not do the analysis. Data quality depends on the competence of the people recording. It makes comparison **possible, not automatic**: two organizations following it can still be uncomparable if they drew their boundaries differently |
| **12** | How Much Oil Do We Have | The ***Petroleum Resources Management System***, SPE, WPC, AAPG and SPEE, **2018** revision | A set of guidelines for classification, **not a reporting regulation**; securities regulators have their own differing definitions, so one field can honestly carry two numbers. Reserves are a property of a **project**, not of a reservoir, and the certainty percentages describe the **estimate** rather than the rock |

**Book 2's honesty core.** The book follows the oil forward and the numbers get worse as it goes. A
permeability from a thumb-sized plug; a material balance missing the two pressure surveys from the two
years the trend changed; a decline curve fitted before two undated workovers; a meter factor measured
at a flow rate the meter no longer runs at; a failure history with blank cause fields. Unit 12 shows
all five standing behind a single reserves figure that is honest, properly reviewed and almost
certainly the right order of magnitude — and asks how to write it so that a reader knows both things
at once.

**One real event.** Unit 4 is about the Macondo blowout of 20 April 2010, in which eleven people were
killed. It is the only real incident in the series, it is sourced entirely to the named published
inquiry report, and the unit states that other investigations of the same event reached conclusions
that overlap and are not identical.

# Book 3 · Flowline 2 — *Pipes, Pumps and Pressure*

CEFR B1 · figure prefix `rc` · audio prefix `FL3` · 196 figures · 195 tracks

Every one of the twelve units carries a source. Unit 12 is the review, and its source — a pipeline
safety management system recommended practice — is the document that says the organization is what
fails.

Two of the twelve are experiments rather than documents. Reynolds in 1883 put dye into water in a
glass tube and watched; Moody in 1944 drew a chart from other people's measurements and said whose.
Everything else in the book rests on those two pieces of work, and both authors wrote plainly about
what they had and had not shown.

| Unit | Title | Source, as cited in the text | What the text prints as the document's own limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | The Code on the Shelf | **ASME B31.4**, *Pipeline Transportation Systems for Liquids and Slurries*, in the ASME B31 Code for Pressure Piping | Minimum requirements for a new system. It excludes piping within refineries and process plants — a different code with a **lower** design factor — and excludes gas pipelines. A hydrostatic test finds defects large enough to fail at that pressure and proves nothing about smaller ones. The scope boundary is a judgment made once by a person on a drawing |
| **2** | Smooth or Not | Osborne Reynolds, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, **1883** | Water, one fluid, one glass tube, a laboratory. **The transition is not a sharp line**: Reynolds found it depended on how disturbed the water was at the inlet, and laminar flow could be sustained far above the conventional figure with a smooth entry. The two numbers in every textbook are conventions built on a real observation |
| **3** | The Chart Everybody Still Uses | Lewis F. Moody, "Friction Factors for Pipe Flow", *Transactions of the ASME*, **1944** | A **compilation of other people's measurements**, named and credited. For circular pipe, running full, with one fluid. Published roughness values are for **new, clean** pipe, and nobody measures the roughness of a pipe in service — in the fully turbulent region, where most lines run, that unmeasured value is the only thing the answer depends on |
| **4** | When There Are Two Things in the Pipe | H. D. Beggs and J. P. Brill, "A Study of Two-Phase Flow in Inclined Pipes", *Journal of Petroleum Technology*, **1973** | **Air and water**, pipes of about one and one and a half inches, a short laboratory loop, **584 tests** across a range of inclinations. Applying it to a twelve inch line of crude and gas at fifty bar is extrapolation, and the authors described their apparatus so that readers could see how far they were going beyond it. Two accepted methods can give holdup figures that differ by a large factor |
| **5** | The Pump Curve | **API Standard 610**, *Centrifugal Pumps for Petroleum, Petrochemical and Natural Gas Industries*, published as an identical ISO standard | A **purchase specification**: it tells a manufacturer what to deliver and says nothing about the pump you already have, its curve after fifteen years of wear, or whether the duty it was bought for still exists. The required suction head is defined at a **three per cent head drop**, so a pump at exactly the required figure is already cavitating slightly |
| **6** | The Thing That Runs Inside the Pipe | **API Standard 1163**, *In-line Inspection Systems Qualification*, with **API RP 1160**, *Managing System Integrity for Hazardous Liquid Pipelines* | Every reported depth carries a **tolerance**, a **confidence** and a **detection threshold**; at eighty per cent confidence about one feature in five may fall outside the band, and that is the specification working. **The absence of a feature from a report is not evidence of the absence of a feature from the pipe.** Verification digs are the step most often reduced when a schedule tightens |
| **7** | Keeping the Steel | **NACE SP0169**, *Control of External Corrosion on Underground or Submerged Metallic Piping Systems* | It sets **criteria**, not outcomes, and gives more than one. An *on* reading includes a voltage drop through the soil and does not describe the steel; only the polarized potential does. A reading is taken at a test post. And a **disbonded coating shields the protective current** — the primary defense, in failing, prevents the secondary one from working, with nothing showing at the surface |
| **8** | The Wrong Steel | **NACE MR0175 / ISO 15156**, *Materials for use in H2S-containing environments in oil and gas production*, in three parts | About **cracking**, not general corrosion. The environment is defined by hydrogen sulfide partial pressure, pH, chlorides and temperature, and **all four move over a field's life**, so a material correctly selected in 1998 can sit today in a different region with nobody having done anything wrong. It sets hardness limits a welding procedure must achieve; it does not write the procedure, and it will not tell you whether a line has already cracked |
| **9** | One Standard, Many Countries | **ISO 13623:2017**, *Petroleum and natural gas industries — Pipeline transportation systems* | It **does not override national law**, and says so. It is a functional standard: what must be achieved, not how. The design factor follows from a **location class** and a **fluid category**, which makes it a judgment about consequences written as a decimal — and location class is assessed at a moment, while towns grow |
| **10** | The Tank That Overflowed | **API Standard 650**, *Welded Tanks for Oil Storage*, with **API RP 2350**, *Overfill Protection for Storage Tanks in Petroleum Facilities* | API 650 is about **building** the tank, not filling it. RP 2350 treats overfill protection as a **system** including the response time and the people, and neither document gives you your own volumes, fill rate or response time. Two alarms from one transmitter are one layer wearing two hats, and a bund contains a consequence rather than preventing one |
| **11** | Ship and Shore | ***International Safety Guide for Oil Tankers and Terminals***, **sixth edition, 2020**, published jointly by the International Chamber of Shipping, the Oil Companies International Marine Forum and the International Association of Ports and Harbors | A **guide**, not law, though much of its content appears in terminal regulations and charter terms. Almost nothing on the ship/shore checklist is information either party lacked: its purpose is to make the agreement **explicit, written and signed** by two named people. It cannot make two organizations trust each other, or make a representative awake for nineteen hours read forty items carefully |
| **12** | A System, Not a Weld | **API Recommended Practice 1173**, *Pipeline Safety Management System Requirements*, first edition **2015** | A **voluntary recommended practice**, written to be **scalable**: it states what must be achieved and leaves the how to each organization, so two companies can both claim to have implemented it and look nothing alike. Its own failure mode is a documentation system growing inside it, because element ten is the easiest element to do well. And it cannot make anybody care |

**Book 3's honesty core.** Eleven of the twelve units contain a failure, and **not one of the eleven is
a failure of steel**. A scope boundary nobody reviewed. A viscosity that lost its source. Six years of
pressure-drop data nobody subtracted. Three tunings of a controller that was not faulty. Eleven
mechanical seals. A blank in an inspection report read as a clean result. Two reports in two filing
systems. A weld with no hardness record. A village that grew beside a buried line. Seven minutes of
tank against eleven minutes of procedure. A tick where a dash belonged. Unit 12 is the published
document that says the organization is the thing that fails, and the book is arranged so that the
reader reaches it having already seen eleven proofs.

**Two people carry this book.** Adeel Rahman asks what a reported figure actually means, from Unit 6
onward. Tomas Varga's line is repeated in four units: *it is already happening; the question is how
fast, and whether anybody has measured it.*
