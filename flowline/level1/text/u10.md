# Unit 10 · Reading a Gauge
%%SUB Pressure, temperature and level, and the reason zero is the most dangerous number on a dial%%
%%GOALS
Read a pressure, a temperature and a level
Tell innage from outage
Say why a reading needs a reference point
Describe a change with *rose to* and *fell by*
Say what a measurement standard assumes
%%

A dial reads zero. The line is empty, or the gauge is dead, or the isolation valve under it is shut.
Three completely different situations, one needle, and no way to tell them apart by looking. Reading
a gauge is not reading a number. It is knowing what the number is a number **of**.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG ra10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its chapter and its publisher, a tank cross-section with the datum plate, the reference gauge point and both measurements marked, a short note on capacity tables, a boxed paragraph headed a standard that assumes a calibrated tank, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for new operators: what a level reading is a reading of**
> **Source: American Petroleum Institute, *Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards*, Chapter 3,
> *Tank Gauging*. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> A level is not a number. It is a **distance between two points**, and the standard spends most of its
> length on fixing those two points so that two people measuring one tank get the same answer.
>
> | The term | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | the **datum plate** | a fixed plate on the tank bottom, directly under the hatch, that the tape bob touches |
> | the **reference gauge point** | a permanent mark at the hatch, from which every outage is measured |
> | **innage** | the depth of liquid, measured **up** from the datum plate |
> | **outage** or **ullage** | the empty distance, measured **down** from the reference gauge point |
> | the **capacity table** | the table that converts a level into a volume, for that one tank |
>
> **Innage and outage are both correct and they are not interchangeable.** Four point six meters of
> innage in a twelve-meter tank and four point six meters of outage in the same tank differ by about
> seven and a half meters of liquid. Which one a site uses is a local decision. **Writing which one you
> used is not.**
>
> **A level is not a volume.** It becomes one only through the tank's own capacity table, produced by
> physically measuring that tank. Two tanks of the same nominal size have different tables, because
> steel is not made to the nearest liter.
>
> **The standard also covers the method.** Manual gauging with a tape and bob, automatic gauging with
> a float or a radar, and hybrid systems all appear, and they do not have the same uncertainty. A
> figure from an automatic gauge and a figure from a tape are not the same kind of figure, and a
> careful ticket says which.
>
> **A STANDARD THAT ASSUMES A CALIBRATED TANK**
> Everything above assumes a tank with a **current capacity table**, a **sound datum plate**, an
> **intact reference gauge point** and equipment that has been **calibrated**. The standard is written
> for custody transfer, where somebody is paying.
>
> It does not cover a tank whose bottom has **settled**, whose datum plate has **corroded away**,
> whose table was produced before a repair, or whose reference mark has been painted over. Those are
> not edge cases in a mature oil province; they are Tuesday. What the standard gives you there is the
> knowledge that your reading has no reference point, which is worth more than a number.
>
> It is also a **custody transfer standard**, not an operating instruction. It does not say what level
> is safe, what the alarm should be set at, or what to do when the level is rising and should not be.
> Those are in the operating procedure, and the overfill question has a standard of its own that Book
> 3 will reach.
>
> And none of it helps with a **dead instrument**. A gauge reading zero on an empty line, a gauge
> reading zero because its isolation valve is shut, and a gauge reading zero because it has failed
> look identical. The standard assumes you know which one you have.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its chapter and its publisher. Read the published
> chapter, and write down the edition you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **A gauge reading zero is a question, not an answer.** Before you write it down, do one thing: check
> that the gauge is connected to what you think it is connected to. Crack the isolation valve, watch
> the needle move, let it settle. Four seconds. The alternative is a log sheet full of zeros from an
> instrument that stopped working on Tuesday, and a shift that believes the vessel is empty.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every mature field runs on instruments older than the people reading them, and every mature field
> has the same quiet problem: a reading that has been the same for a long time stops being read. The
> needle becomes part of the scenery. That is not carelessness and it is not local — it is how
> attention works, and the only defense anybody has found is a second number that ought to agree with
> the first. **Ask your group: which reading on your plant has not changed in a month?**
