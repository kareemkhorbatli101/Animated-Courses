# Unit 8 · Before You Touch It
%%SUB A piece of paper that stands between a person and a live system, and the four signatures on it%%
%%GOALS
Say what a permit to work is for
Name the four roles on a permit
Say what hand-back means
Use *before*, *after* and *until*
Say why a permit system can fail
%%

The permit is a sheet of paper. It does not isolate anything, it does not drain anything and it does
not make anything safe. What it does is record who did those things, who checked them, who is doing
the work, and who gets the plant back — and it does that because a place where nobody recorded it
once killed a hundred and sixty-seven men.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG ra08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the guidance document, its number, its publisher and its year, a four-row table of the roles, a short list of the ways a permit system degrades, a boxed paragraph headed guidance, not a template, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for new operators: what the permit does, and what it does not**
> **Source: Health and Safety Executive (United Kingdom), *HSG250: Guidance on permit-to-work systems
> — a guide for the petroleum, chemical and allied industries*, **2005**. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> A permit to work is a **formal written system** used to control work that has been identified as
> potentially hazardous. The guidance is careful about what that means, and it is worth being equally
> careful.
>
> **A permit is a record, not a control.** The isolation is the control. The drain is the control. The
> gas test is the control. The permit records that those things were done, by whom, and what the work
> party may therefore do.
>
> | The role | What they are confirming |
> |---|---|
> | **issuing authority** | the work is authorized and the necessary precautions are specified |
> | **isolating authority** | the equipment is isolated, drained, depressurized and proved dead |
> | **performing authority** | the work party has been briefed and understands the permit |
> | **area authority** | the work is compatible with other activities in the area |
>
> **Hand-back** is the other half of the system, and the half that is forgotten. When the work is
> finished, the performing authority hands the equipment back, the permit is canceled, and the
> isolations are removed in a controlled order. A permit left open on a board is a permit that somebody
> will act on tomorrow.
>
> The guidance then does something unusual for a safety document: it sets out how permit systems
> **fail**.
>
> > **Too many permits.** If everything needs a permit, nothing gets read.
> > **Permits as a substitute for assessment.** A permit records controls; it does not decide what the
> > controls should be.
> > **Blanket and long-duration permits.** A permit covering a week of unspecified work on a system
> > authorizes nothing precisely.
> > **Weak hand-back.** Work finished, permit still open, isolation still in place, nobody certain.
> > **Handover across shifts.** The person who signed is gone, and the person who inherits the permit
> > did not see the plant.
>
> That list is the most valuable page in the document, because every item on it is a system that looks
> like it is working.
>
> **GUIDANCE, NOT A TEMPLATE**
> This is **guidance from a national regulator**, written for one country's legal framework. It is not
> itself law, and in other countries neither it nor the law behind it applies. What is portable is the
> reasoning.
>
> It is deliberately **not a template**. The guidance says each site must design a system that fits
> its own plant, its own work and its own organization, and it declines to supply a form. A permit
> form copied from another site is a form nobody here designed.
>
> It does not cover **every kind of work**. Permits apply to work identified as needing them, and
> deciding which work that is happens somewhere else — in a risk assessment, by a competent person.
>
> And the guidance **cannot make anybody read the form**. Every failure on its own list is a failure of
> use rather than of design. A system that has degraded still prints forms, still has signatures on
> them, and still looks exactly like a system that works.
>
> *Our summary of a published guidance document, named with its number, its publisher and its year.
> Read the published guidance, and write down the edition you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Read the boxes that are always signed.** A permit develops a shape: the same four boxes, filled in
> by the same four people, in the same order, every day. Within a month your eye stops reading them
> and starts recognizing the pattern. The box most likely to be missing is the one you have never
> once found missing, and the four seconds it costs to read it is the whole value of the system.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Permit systems arrived in this industry from a particular place and a particular disaster, and they
> carry that history: a formality that feels bureaucratic in a culture of personal trust, and a
> personal trust that feels reckless in a culture of formality. Both readings are honest. The
