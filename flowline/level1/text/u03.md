# Unit 3 · The Parts of a Wellhead
%%SUB The stack of valves on top of the hole, what each one is for, and what the plate on it does not tell you%%
%%GOALS
Name the parts of a wellhead and a christmas tree
Say which valve does what
Use *on top of*, *beneath*, *upstream of*
Read a pressure rating correctly
Say what an equipment specification covers
%%

A well is a hole with a stack of steel on top of it. Every piece in the stack has a name, a job and a
rating stamped into it. Learning the names takes an afternoon. Learning what the stamped rating does
**not** promise takes this unit.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG ra03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the specification, its publisher and the identical international standard, a table of six rated working pressures, a short note on product specification levels and material classes, a boxed paragraph headed a specification for making equipment, not for operating it, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for new operators: what the plate on the valve means**
> **Source: American Petroleum Institute, *Specification 6A: Wellhead and Christmas Tree Equipment*,
> published in a form identical to the international standard **ISO 10423**. Printed here in our own
> words.**
>
> This specification governs how wellhead equipment is **designed, made, tested and marked**. It is
> the reason a valve body from one country bolts to a spool from another.
>
> **Rated working pressure.** Equipment is made in a short list of rated pressures, and the rating is
> stamped on the part.
>
> | Rated working pressure | Where you meet it |
> |---|---|
> | **2,000 psi** | shallow, low-pressure wells |
> | **3,000 psi** | common on older onshore wells |
> | **5,000 psi** | the most common rating in general service |
> | **10,000 psi** | higher-pressure wells |
> | **15,000 psi** | deep or high-pressure wells |
> | **20,000 psi** | a small number of very high-pressure applications |
>
> **Product specification level.** The specification also defines **levels of testing and
> documentation** — the higher the level, the more inspection, the more traceability and the more
> paperwork. Two valves with the same pressure rating can be made to different levels, and the
> difference is in what was checked and what was recorded.
>
> **Material class.** Separate classes cover what the equipment is made of, because a well producing
> hydrogen sulfide destroys materials that are perfectly adequate in sweet service. Unit 7 is about
> the gas; this is about the steel that has to survive it.
>
> **Temperature class.** A rating is a rating **at a stated temperature range**. Steel behaves
> differently at minus twenty and at a hundred and twenty degrees.
>
> **A SPECIFICATION FOR MAKING EQUIPMENT, NOT FOR OPERATING IT**
> This document tells a **manufacturer** what to build and how to prove it. It does not tell an
> operator what to do. There is no procedure in it for shutting in a well, no instruction about which
> valve to close first, and no permit.
>
> A rating is **not permission**. A valve rated to five thousand pounds per square inch is rated for
> that pressure when it is **in the condition the specification assumes**: correctly assembled, within
> its temperature class, not corroded, and tested. A rating describes equipment as manufactured, and
> equipment in the field has a history.
>
> The specification is also an **edition**. It has been revised many times, it is harmonized with the
> international standard but the two have not always moved together, and a part made in 1981 was made
> to a document that no longer exists in that form.
>
> And it assumes a **document trail** that may not exist. Much equipment in service anywhere in the
> world is older than its paperwork. A part with no legible plate, no certificate and no history is
> not covered by this specification or by any other; what it is rated to is then a question for a
> competent person and an inspection, not for a number somebody remembers.
>
> *Our summary of a published specification, named with its publisher and the identical international
> standard. Read the published specification, and write down the edition you read.*
