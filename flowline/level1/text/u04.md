# Unit 4 · Who Is on Shift
%%SUB Fourteen people on one site, two employers, and one question: who do you take an instruction from?%%
%%GOALS
Name the roles on a production site
Say who reports to whom
Say what a contractor is, and what changes
Use the present simple for a routine
Say what a set of guidelines can and cannot settle
%%

Fourteen people were on the Mureij site this morning. Ten of them work for Jazira Petroleum
Operations and four work for somebody else. Everybody wore the same coveralls. The question that
matters is not what they wore: it is whose instruction each of them has to obey.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG ra04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the report number, its publisher and its year, a five-row table of the phases of a contract, a short note on bridging documents, a boxed paragraph headed guidelines, not a standard, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for new operators: who you take an instruction from**
> **Source: International Association of Oil & Gas Producers, *Report 423: HSE management — guidelines for working together in a contract environment*. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **1** Much of the work here is done by people employed by somebody else. It makes one problem: two sets of rules, two supervisors, one piece of plant.
>
> **2** The guidelines give a contract five phases, each with safety work.
>
> | The phase | What happens in it |
> |---|---|
> | **planning** | the company decides what work it is buying and what risks come with it |
> | **selection** | contractors are assessed, including on their safety record |
> | **pre-mobilization** | the interface is agreed in writing, before anybody arrives |
> | **execution** | the work is done, supervised, watched and reviewed |
> | **final evaluation** | what happened is written down, and it is used in the next selection |
>
> **3** The idea at the center is the **interface**: the written place where the company's system meets
> the contractor's. They always differ, and the difference goes in a **bridging document**, which says
> which rule applies to what.
>
> **4** For an operator it comes down to three lines. You take instructions from your own line.
> A contractor's supervisor may ask you for something. The request goes through the interface. A permit crosses the interface; a conversation does not. And everybody may stop a job, at any rank.
>
> **5** This is guidance from an industry association. It is not a standard and not law, and nobody is
> certified against it. It does not define job titles: *operator* and *supervisor* mean
> different things from site to site. Learn your own chart, not a list.
>
> **6** It says nothing about employment law, pay or contract terms. Those are another
> document. And it assumes a written contract and interface. Sometimes work is informal, urgent, or left
> over from the last operator. Then the interface is not on paper at all, and the
> report does not say what to do. Ask who is accountable, in writing, before the
> work starts.
>
> *Our summary of a published guidance report, named with its number and its publisher. Read the published report, and write down the edition you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **A request is not an instruction.** When somebody who is not in your line asks you to do something,
> the words to use are *I will ask my supervisor*. That sentence is not obstruction and it is not
> rudeness; it is how two companies share a piece of plant without one of them being surprised. And if
> the job is unsafe, you do not need anybody's line: you stop it.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Titles do not travel. *Superintendent*, *supervisor*, *foreman*, *coordinator*, *team leader* and
> *in-charge* all exist, they overlap, and the same word is three ranks apart in three companies on
> one road. International contractors bring their own vocabulary with them and so does every new
> operator of an old field. The safe habit is not to learn a list of titles but to learn the names of
> the two or three people whose instruction you must obey. **Ask your group: what is your own title,
> and what does it mean in the company next door?**
