# Unit 9 · Five Minutes Before the Job
%%SUB The briefing at the toolbox, and the system behind it that nobody can see%%
%%GOALS
Run a five-minute pre-job briefing
Check that somebody has understood
Say what the briefing cannot do on its own
Use instructions and comprehension checks
Say what a management framework is
%%

Five people at a toolbox. Five minutes. Somebody says what the job is, what could go wrong and what
to do if it does, and somebody checks that the other four heard it. That is the whole of the toolbox
talk, and it is the visible end of something very large.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG ra09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the report number, its publisher and its year, a diagram of four fundamentals around ten elements, a short note on where the toolbox talk sits, a boxed paragraph headed a framework, not a procedure, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for new operators: the five minutes, and the system behind them**
> **Source: International Association of Oil & Gas Producers, *Report 510: Operating Management
> System Framework for controlling risk and delivering high performance in the oil and gas
> industry*, **2014**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> This report describes how a large operating company organizes itself so that risk is controlled on
> purpose rather than by luck. It sets out a small number of **fundamentals** — leadership, risk,
> people, implementation — and a set of **elements** beneath them, covering everything from how
> contractors are selected to how incidents are investigated.
>
> There is no section in it called *toolbox talk*.
>
> That absence is the point of this unit. The briefing you give at a toolbox is where several of those
> elements arrive at the same time, in the mouth of one person, in five minutes.
>
> | What the briefing relies on | Which part of the system supplies it |
> |---|---|
> | the hazards you name | the risk assessment somebody did earlier |
> | the controls you name | the procedures and the engineering somebody built |
> | the competence of your party | selection, training and assessment |
> | the escape route and muster point | the emergency plan |
> | the right to stop the job | the stated expectations of the people in charge |
>
> So the briefing cannot invent any of that. What it can do is **three things nothing else in the
> system can do**: say which of the general hazards apply to **this** job today; find out whether the
> five people in front of you actually understood; and give one of them the moment in which to say
> that something is different from the plan.
>
> That third thing is why a briefing is a conversation and not an announcement.
>
> **A FRAMEWORK, NOT A PROCEDURE**
> This is a **framework**. It says what a management system should cover; it does not say how to do
> any of it. There is no procedure in it, no form, no checklist and no script.
>
> It is written for **large operating companies**, with departments to put in the boxes. A small
> contractor with eleven employees has the same hazards and none of the structure, and the report does
> not address that gap.
>
> It is also **not auditable in the way a standard is**. Nobody is certified against it. It is a
> description of good organization offered by an industry association to its own members.
>
> And a framework **cannot make a briefing honest**. Every element in it can be in place and the
> five-minute talk can still be four sentences and a nod, given by somebody in a hurry to a party who
> have heard it ninety times. The system makes a good briefing **possible**. The person makes it
> happen.
>
> *Our summary of a published framework, named with its number, its publisher and its year. Read the
> published report, and write down the edition you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **End the briefing with a question, not with *any questions?*** The second one asks four tired
> people to admit in public that they did not follow you. The first one asks one of them to tell you
> something, which is easy, and which tells you whether the five minutes worked. *Omar, what will you
> do if the gas alarm sounds?* takes eight seconds and it is the only part of the briefing you get
> evidence from.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Asking a question in front of colleagues is not equally easy everywhere. In some workplaces a
> question reads as engagement and in others as a challenge to the person briefing, or as an admission
> in front of people who will remember it. A briefing that only works where asking is easy is a
> briefing that does not work. The fix is the same everywhere: the person in charge asks the questions
> rather than inviting them. **Ask your group: in your workplace, is it easy to say *I did not
> follow that*?**
