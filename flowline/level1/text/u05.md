# Unit 5 · Eighteen Rules Became Nine
%%SUB A card of rules built from the industry's own count of how people died, and cut down because eighteen was too many%%
%%GOALS
Name the nine rules
Say what each one stops
Use the imperative and *must not*
Say why the number was reduced
Say what a rule is not
%%

There is a card. It fits in a pocket, it has nine rules on it, and each rule is there because people
died doing that one thing. The card used to have eighteen rules. The reason it has nine is that
eighteen rules is a list, and nine is something a person can carry in their head at two in the
morning.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG ra05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the report number and its publisher with both the original and the revised version dated, a nine-row table of the rules, a short note on how the set was reduced, a boxed paragraph headed personal safety, not process safety, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for new operators: nine rules, and where they came from**
> **Source: International Association of Oil & Gas Producers, *Report 459: Life-Saving Rules*. The
> original set was published in **2013**; the revised set of nine was issued in **2018**. Printed here
> in our own words.**
>
> The rules were not written by somebody in an office deciding what would be prudent. They were
> derived from **counting deaths**. The association collects fatal incident data from its member
> companies, and a small number of activities kept appearing in it year after year.
>
> | The rule | What it requires |
> |---|---|
> | **Bypassing safety controls** | obtain authorization before overriding or disabling a safety control |
> | **Confined space** | obtain authorization before entering a confined space |
> | **Driving** | follow safe driving rules |
> | **Energy isolation** | verify isolation and zero energy before work begins |
> | **Hot work** | control flammables and ignition sources |
> | **Line of fire** | keep yourself and others out of the line of fire |
> | **Safe mechanical lifting** | plan lifting operations and control the area |
> | **Work authorization** | work with a valid permit when required |
> | **Working at height** | protect yourself against a fall when working at height |
>
> **Why the set was cut.** The first version had **eighteen** rules. The revision reduced them to
> **nine** on the reasoning that a set small enough to be remembered is worth more than a set complete
> enough to be defensible. Each rule was also given a single icon and a single short sentence, for the
> same reason.
>
> That decision is the most useful thing in the document for a new operator. **Nine is not the number
> of ways to be killed on a production site.** It is the number of rules somebody judged a person
> could carry.
>
> **PERSONAL SAFETY, NOT PROCESS SAFETY**
> These rules address the activities that killed **individuals** — a fall, a vehicle, a lift, a
> confined space. They are **not about process safety**: nothing on this card would have prevented a
> refinery explosion, and Book 5 of this series is about the difference. A site can comply with all
> nine rules on the day it destroys itself.
>
> They are also **not a safety management system**. There is no risk assessment in them, no
> competence framework, no maintenance regime and no design standard. They sit on top of all of that
> and they do not replace any of it.
>
> The data behind them is the **association's members' data**. Those members are mostly large
> international companies with reporting systems. Work done by small contractors, in informal
> arrangements, or in places where nothing is reported, is not in that dataset — and the activities
> that kill people there may not be the same nine.
>
> And a **rule is not a procedure**. *Verify isolation and zero energy before work begins* tells you
> what must be true. It does not tell you how to isolate this pump, or who signs, or what to do when
> the isolation valve will not seat. That is Unit 8, and after Unit 8 it is still a competent person
> and a written procedure.
>
> *Our summary of a published report, named with its number, its publisher and both of its dates. Read
> the published report, and write down the version you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **The card is not the point. The stop is.** Every one of the nine rules ends in the same place: if
> the rule is not satisfied, the work does not start, and the person who says so does not need a rank.
> Learn the nine so that you can name the one you are stopping the job for. *This is a line of fire
> problem* is a sentence a supervisor has to answer. *I do not like it* is a sentence they can
> dismiss.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Nine rules on a pocket card is a particular way of doing safety, and it came from somewhere:
> large international companies working across many countries, needing something that survives
> translation and does not depend on a local regulation. It has real strengths and one real cost —
> rules on a card tell you nothing about why, and a workforce that is only ever given rules does not
> become a workforce that can judge. **Ask your group: were you ever told the reason for a safety rule,
> or only the rule?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG ra05-f16 | A desk in the evening. A pocket card lies open beside a withdrawn permit, an open padlock, a stop-work report with one rule name underlined, a bar chart of nine rules, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**Ahmad took a padlock off for thirty seconds and was halfway through a job when the officer arrived.**
The risk in those thirty seconds was, in all probability, nil. The job was stopped, the permit was
withdrawn, and the whole isolation had to be done again — about ninety minutes.

Ninety minutes for thirty seconds, and the risk really was probably nil.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Was stopping the job proportionate?
2 If the rule bends for thirty seconds, what is the next number after thirty?
3 The nine rules came from counting deaths. Is *probably nil* a count?
4 Would you have stopped it?
