# Unit 7 · The Smell You Must Not Trust
%%SUB One gas, measured in parts per million, and the reason your nose stops working at the worst possible moment%%
%%GOALS
Say what hydrogen sulfide does at different concentrations

%%

The crude from the northeast is sour. That word means one thing on a laboratory report and another
thing in a cellar at two in the morning: a colorless gas, heavier than air, that smells of rotten
eggs at concentrations that cannot hurt you — and stops smelling of anything at the concentration
that can.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG ra07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the recommended practice, its number and its publisher, a four-row table of concentrations and effects, a short note on detection and contingency planning, a boxed paragraph headed a recommended practice for one kind of operation, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for new operators: the gas, and why you carry an instrument**
> **Source: American Petroleum Institute, *Recommended Practice 49: Drilling and Well Servicing Operations Involving Hydrogen Sulfide*. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **1** Hydrogen sulfide has no color. It is heavier than air. It burns. It is toxic far below the
> level of other gases. The crude in the northeast of this country is sour. This gas is a
> daily condition of work here, not an emergency.
>
> | Roughly | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **a fraction of one part per million** | the rotten-egg smell can be detected |
> | **around ten parts per million** | a limit often applied at work |
> | **around a hundred parts per million** | the sense of smell is lost; the eyes and airway are hurt |
> | **several hundred and above** | consciousness is lost fast; death in minutes |
>
> **2** Your nose is not a detector. At higher levels the gas kills the sense of smell. A person
> walking into worse air may notice the smell going and think the danger has passed. That is how this
> gas kills people who knew about it.
>
> **3** It collects low. It sits in cellars, sumps, trenches, tank bottoms and downwind of a leak. The
> air at head height can be clean while the air at your knees is not. So do not lean into a pit. A
> dropped tool is not worth a life.
>
> **4** It kills rescuers. The reports read the same. One person goes down. A second goes in
> without a mask. The report has two names on it.
>
> **5** So the practice asks for four things together.
>
> | What it asks for | Why that one |
> |---|---|
> | **detection** that does not rely on people | because the nose fails at the worst moment |
> | **training** for everybody who may be exposed | because the signs have to be known before the day |
> | **escape sets** within reach | because you may have seconds, not minutes |
> | a written **contingency plan** | because somebody has to count who is out |
>
> **6** This is a recommended practice, not a standard and not law. It is written for drilling and well
> servicing. The limits are not the institute's: regulators set them, and they differ from country to
> country. The number on your alarm came from somewhere, and you may ask where. A practice cannot
> supply equipment either.
>
> *Our summary of a published recommended practice, named with its number and its publisher. Read the published practice, and write down the edition you read.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **If the smell stops, go up and go out.** Not *investigate*, not *check the other monitor*, not
> *tell somebody first*. The one piece of information your body gives you about this gas is unreliable
> in exactly one direction: it tells you the hazard has gone when it has grown. Treat the smell
> disappearing as an alarm, and treat an alarm as true until an instrument says otherwise.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Sour fields produce sour habits. Where the gas is present every day, people stop treating it as
> remarkable, and that is both inevitable and the thing that kills them: the hazard that is always
