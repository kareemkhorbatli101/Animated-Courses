Every vessel on a plant has a device on it that will open if the pressure rises too far. The device
was sized for the worst event somebody thought of. Everything about whether it protects anybody
depends on how good that list was.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rd09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its number, its publisher and the identical international standard, a six-row table of causes with relief loads beside them and one circled, a diagram of a flare header and knock-out drum, a boxed paragraph headed what the system cannot protect against, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the last line, and how it was sized**
> **Source: **API Standard 521**, *Pressure-relieving and Depressuring Systems*, published as an
> identical international standard. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What the document is for.** A vessel has a design pressure. If something pushes the pressure above
> it, either the vessel fails or something lets the contents out. This standard is about working out
> **what could push it up**, **how much would have to be let out**, and **where that goes**.
>
> **Step one is a list.** The standard sets out the causes of overpressure to be considered. Among
> them:
>
> | The cause | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **blocked outlet** | the outlet is closed while the inlet keeps flowing |
> | **external fire** | the contents boil and the vapor must leave |
> | **loss of cooling or reflux** | vapor that was being condensed stops being condensed |
> | **control valve failure** | a valve fails open and a high pressure source reaches a low pressure vessel |
> | **exchanger tube rupture** | a tube fails between the high and low pressure sides |
> | **thermal expansion** | liquid blocked in between two valves warms and expands |
> | **utility failure** | power, instrument air or cooling water lost |
>
> **Step two is arithmetic, done separately for each cause.** Each one gives a relief load: how much
> has to leave, per hour, to hold the pressure.
>
> **Step three is the choice.** The **governing case** is the largest of those loads, and the device
> is sized for it. Different devices on the same plant are commonly governed by different cases, and
> the governing case belongs on the record beside the device.
>
> **Where it goes.** The relieved material goes to a header, usually to a **knock-out drum** that
> separates any liquid, and then to a flare. The header and the drum have to be sized for the relief
> cases too — and for the possibility of **several devices lifting at once** if one event affects a
> whole plant, which is why a power failure case is often the largest load the flare system ever
> sees.
>
> **Depressuring** is a related idea: deliberately letting the pressure down, in a controlled way,
> so that a vessel in a prolonged fire does not stay at high pressure while its wall gets hotter and
> weaker.
>
> **WHAT THE SYSTEM CANNOT PROTECT AGAINST**
> Four things, and they are the whole reason this unit exists.
>
> **A scenario nobody listed.** This is the big one. The device is sized for the largest case on the
> list. A relief valve is a spring and a disc; it does not know what caused the pressure, so it will
> lift. But if the event nobody considered needs twice the flow the valve can pass, **the vessel fails
> with the relief valve fully open**. An undersized valve looks like a protection and is not one, and
> nothing on the plant shows the difference.
>
> **A fire case built on stale assumptions.** The fire load depends on how much of the vessel's
> surface is assumed to be heated, over what area, for how long, with what drainage and what
> fire-fighting. Those assumptions were made when the plot plan looked different. New equipment,
> new paving, a new drain or a new building changes them, and nobody revalidates a fire case when a
> plot plan changes.
>
> **Two failures at once.** The convention — and the standard discusses it — is that **unrelated
> simultaneous failures are not normally considered**. That is called excluding **double jeopardy**,
> it is sensible nearly always because otherwise nothing could be designed, and it is a **judgment**
> rather than a law of nature. Some real events have been two things happening together.
>
> **And a device that cannot open.** A relief valve with a closed isolation valve beneath it, a
> corroded seat, or a header full of liquid is a calculation, not a protection. Which is why
> inspection and testing of relief devices is a program and not an event.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its number and its publisher. Read the published
> standard, and then go and find the scenario list for one vessel you work near.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Pressure relief is the clearest case in this industry of a protection that depends entirely on a
> piece of paper from decades ago. The hardware is visible, testable and tested. The assumption list
> is invisible, untested and almost never revisited, and it lives in a calculation file owned by
> nobody currently employed. Every plant change — a new unit, a bund, a paved area, a bigger pump —
> potentially moves a number in that file, and no change process in the world routinely asks the
> question, because the people doing projects and the people who own relief studies are different
> people in different buildings.

**Four relief devices are tested on schedule and every certificate is current.** The testing is done
properly by competent people and the records are complete. On every audit, overpressure protection at
this plant comes out clean.

The scenario list the devices were sized from was reviewed in 1979. Since then a compressor house has
been built eighteen meters away, a bund has been constructed where the drainage used to run, and the
area has been paved. Each of those was a properly managed project with its own approvals.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is the plant protected?
2 Every document is current and correct. Where is the gap?
3 Three managed projects produced it. What question was each one not asked?
4 What would you add to a project approval form?
