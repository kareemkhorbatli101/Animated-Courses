# Unit 5 · Two Numbers for One Fuel
%%SUB An engine from the 1920s, two defined liquids, and why 95 and 91 can be the same petrol%%
%%GOALS
Say what an octane number is
Say why there are two of them
Say what the pump number means in different markets
Use contrast language
Say what the number does not predict
%%

There is no octane number inside a fuel waiting to be found. There is a single-cylinder engine whose
design goes back to the 1920s, maintained to a specification, run under prescribed conditions, and
compared against two liquids that were defined as 100 and 0. What that engine says is the octane
number. There are two sets of conditions, so there are two numbers.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rd05-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the two test methods, their numbers and their publisher, a drawing of the test engine with the compression adjustment and the inlet heater marked, a two-column table comparing the conditions, a boxed paragraph headed what the number is, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: why one petrol has two numbers**
> **Source: **ASTM D2699**, the research method, and **ASTM D2700**, the motor method, for determining
> the knock characteristics of spark-ignition engine fuels. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What knocking is.** In a spark-ignition engine the mixture is squeezed and then ignited by the
> spark. If the fuel is squeezed too hard it starts to burn on its own, in the wrong place and at the
> wrong moment, and the result is a metallic knocking sound and, in time, damage. A fuel that
> tolerates more squeezing before it does that is more valuable, because an engine designed for it can
> be more efficient.
>
> **How the number is obtained.** Both methods use a **single-cylinder laboratory engine** whose
> compression ratio can be changed while it runs. The design dates from the 1920s and is maintained
> to a specification, which is precisely why the results mean anything: every laboratory in the world
> is using the same engine.
>
> Two liquids were **defined** as the ends of the scale: one as **100** and one as **0**. The sample
> is run, the compression is raised until knock of a defined intensity appears, and then blends of the
> two reference liquids are run until one of them knocks the same way under the same conditions. **The
> octane number is the percentage of the high reference liquid in that matching blend.**
>
> So an octane number is not a measurement of a quantity in the fuel. It is **the result of a
> comparison**, on a defined engine, against two defined liquids, and that is a different kind of
> number.
>
> **Two methods, two sets of conditions.**
>
> | | Research method | Motor method |
> |---|---|---|
> | engine speed | the **lower** speed | the **higher** speed |
> | inlet mixture | not significantly preheated | **heated** |
> | severity | the **less** severe test | the **more** severe test |
> | the result for the same fuel | the **higher** number | the **lower** number |
>
> The two numbers for one fuel are therefore different, and the gap between them is called the
> fuel's **sensitivity**.
>
> **AND SO THE PUMP**
> Markets label fuel differently, and this is the single most practical consequence of the whole unit.
>
> In Europe and much of the world, the number on the pump is the **research** number.
>
> In North America, the number on the pump is the **antiknock index**: the **average of the two**.
>
> A fuel with a research number of 95 and a motor number of 87 has an antiknock index of 91. **It can
> be sold as 95 in one country and 91 in another, and it is the same fuel.** Nobody is being deceived
> and both labels are correct under their own conventions. A great deal of confusion, and some
> genuinely bad commercial decisions, come from comparing the two directly.
>
> **WHAT THE NUMBER DOES NOT PREDICT**
> Neither method claims to tell you how the fuel will behave in a particular modern engine, and it is
> worth being exact about why.
>
> The test engine is a **single cylinder** running at a **constant** speed under **constant**
> conditions. A modern engine has several cylinders, varies its speed and load continuously, controls
> its own ignition timing, and in many cases listens for knock with a sensor and retards the timing
> when it hears any. The relationship between the laboratory number and real behavior is a subject of
> its own.
>
> And both references and both numbers rest on a **convention**. One hundred and zero are definitions,
> not natural limits — fuels exist with research numbers above 100, which is why the scale has
> extensions.
>
> **None of this makes the numbers worthless.** It makes them what they are: a universally comparable
> ranking, obtained by one method, that everybody in the world can check. That is an enormous
> achievement, and it is the reason the English for it has to include which method produced it.
>
> *Our summary of two published test methods, named with their numbers and their publisher. Read the
> published methods, and look at the description of the engine.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Always say which number, and which method.** *95 research, 87 motor, 91 index* is six words and it
> makes every later comparison honest. And when a customer compares your grade with a grade from

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rd05-f16 | A desk in the evening. A gasoline certificate lies open with three figures on it, beside two photographs of fuel pumps from two countries labeled ninety-five and ninety-one, a complaint letter, a single-cylinder test engine photograph, a note of two conventions chosen seventy years ago, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A customer complained that a fuel labeled 95 was worse than one labeled 91.** Both labels were
correct. The two fuels were, to within the precision of the methods, the same fuel. The customer was
not stupid, was not being difficult, and had done exactly what anybody would do with two numbers on
two signs.

The certificate had all three figures on it and named both methods. The customer had never seen the
certificate, because customers see labels.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Whose job was it to prevent this?
2 Every document was correct. Where did the misunderstanding live?
3 The convention could be printed on the label. Why is it not?
4 Where else does your site publish a number without its convention?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 6 is a fuel specification where the most
important clauses are the ones with no numbers in them.
