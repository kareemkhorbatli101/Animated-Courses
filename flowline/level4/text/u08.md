# Unit 8 · The Heat You Already Have
%%SUB A thermodynamic target, an economic choice disguised as a constant, and four hundred meters of pipe%%

A refinery heats streams and cools other streams, often at the same time, often a few meters apart.
Any heat you move from a hot stream into a cold one is heat you do not buy and do not throw away. A
paper from 1983 showed how to work out, before designing anything, how much of it you could in
principle keep.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rd08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its two authors, the journal and its year, a temperature-enthalpy diagram with two composite curves and the pinch marked, a four-row table of the steps, a boxed paragraph headed what the method cannot see, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the heat you already have**
> **Source: B. Linnhoff and E. Hindmarsh, "The Pinch Design Method for Heat Exchanger Networks",
> *Chemical Engineering Science*, **1983**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **The situation.** A process plant has streams that must be **heated** and streams that must be
> **cooled**. If you heat the cold ones with a furnace and cool the hot ones with air or water, you
> buy fuel at one end and throw heat away at the other, often within a hundred meters of each other.
> Any heat you can move directly from a hot stream to a cold one is bought twice over: once in fuel
> not burned and once in cooling not needed.
>
> **The old way of doing it** was to look at the flowsheet, spot the obvious matches, and design
> exchangers between them. It works and it has one weakness: you never know whether you have found
> most of the available heat or half of it.
>
> **What the paper gave the industry** was a way of calculating the answer **first**.
>
> | Step | What happens |
> |---|---|
> | **1** | list every stream with its start temperature, its end temperature and its heat capacity flow |
> | **2** | add all the hot streams into one **hot composite curve** and all the cold ones into a **cold composite curve** |
> | **3** | choose a **minimum temperature approach** and separate the curves by that much at their closest |
> | **4** | read off the **pinch** and the two utility targets |
>
> **The pinch** is the point where the two curves come closest. It divides the process in two: above
> it the process needs net heating, below it net cooling. The **overlap** of the two curves is the
> heat that can be recovered inside the process. What is left over at each end is the **minimum hot
> utility** and the **minimum cold utility** — the least heating and cooling you will ever have to
> buy, whatever network anybody designs.
>
> **That is the whole value of the method.** You get a **target** before any design exists, and any
> proposal can then be judged against it. A network using twice the minimum heating is not necessarily
> wrong, but somebody now has to say why.
>
> **The design rules follow.** Do not transfer heat **across** the pinch: doing so costs you the same
> amount in extra heating and extra cooling. At the pinch itself the stream matches are the most
> constrained, so design outward from there.
>
> **THE NUMBER THAT LOOKS LIKE A CONSTANT**
> The minimum temperature approach is where judgment enters, and it is almost always written down as
> though it were a property of nature.
>
> A **smaller** approach recovers **more** heat — the curves can be pushed closer together — and needs
> **larger** exchangers, because transferring heat across a small temperature difference needs a lot
> of area. A **larger** approach recovers less heat and needs less steel.
>
> So the number is an **economic choice**: energy cost against capital cost, at the prices of the day
> the decision was made. A plant designed in 1975, when energy was cheap, will have been designed with
> a wide approach and will have poor heat recovery for a completely rational reason. Those decisions
> are extremely hard to reverse afterwards, which is Unit 8's quiet point: **the energy performance of
> a refinery was mostly decided by somebody choosing one number before it was built.**
>
> **WHAT THE METHOD CANNOT SEE**
> The targets are thermodynamically correct and the following four things are invisible to them.
>
> **Streams nobody listed.** The whole calculation rests on step one, and a real plant has streams
> that are intermittent, streams on units that were added later, streams whose flows vary with the
> crude, and streams that are simply not on anybody's list. The target is only as complete as that
> list.
>
> **Distance.** The method will happily match a hot stream in one unit with a cold stream four hundred
> meters away. Thermodynamics does not know about pipe, pumps, pressure drop, plot space or the road
> in between, and in a real plant those often make the match impossible.
>
> **Operability.** A heavily integrated plant is a plant where everything depends on everything. Start
> it up, shut one unit down, or change the crude, and a network designed for one steady condition may
> not work. The paper is about design; a plant has to be run.
>
> **Fouling and reality.** An exchanger recovering heat between two dirty streams fouls, and a network
> whose performance was calculated clean is not the network you have in year four.
>
> **And it is a design method, not a retrofit method.** Working out the target for an existing plant is
> easy; changing an existing plant to reach it means moving pipe through a working refinery, and that
> is a different and much harder problem.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its authors, its journal and its year. Read the
> published paper, and then go and look at how far apart two units on your own plant actually are.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write the approach temperature beside every energy target.** A target without it is not a number:
> the same plant has a different target at every approach, and quoting one without the other is how
> energy projects get approved and then disappoint. And when you list candidate matches, **put the
> distance in meters in the next column**. Thermodynamics cannot see pipe, and the column that shows
> four hundred meters is the column that decides whether a project is real.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Energy targets are among the most dangerous numbers in engineering, and not because they are wrong.
> They are exactly right about a world in which pipe is free, plants are empty and nothing fouls. The
> word *target* carries all of that, and the word falls off within about two meetings, after which a
> thermodynamic limit is sitting in a budget as a commitment. The people who put it there are not
> careless; a target is genuinely the right number to aim at, and the distance column is in an
> appendix. The habit that survives is simple and unpopular: the target and the achievable figure on
> the same line, every time, with the distances beside them.
> **Ask your group: which target at your site has become a commitment, and when did the word
> change?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rd08-f16 | A desk in the evening. An energy study lies open with a seventeen megawatt gap labeled as a target, beside a budget page where the same figure appears as a saving, a plot plan showing four matches over two hundred meters apart, a 1975 design basis sheet with a twenty degree approach on it, a full pipe rack photograph, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**An energy study gave a theoretical target and labeled it a target.** It named the approach
temperature, listed six candidate matches, and put the distance beside each one. It was a careful,
honest piece of work and the achievable figure — six megawatts — was in the recommendation.

The seventeen megawatt figure went into a budget as a saving. Two years later the project is late and
over cost, the four long matches have not been built, and the study is being described as
over-optimistic.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Was the study over-optimistic?
2 Who moved the number, and why would they have thought it was reasonable?
3 The achievable figure was in the recommendation. Why did the other one travel?
4 What would you have put on page one?
