# Unit 2 · The Book That Taught the Industry
%%SUB A thousand charts from other people's data, and an author who said whose%%
%%GOALS
Say what a correlation is
Say what a rule of thumb is good for
Hedge a generalization properly
Say what a 1958 chart has and has not kept
Say what a complexity index measures
%%

For decades the way an engineer learned to design a refinery was to open one book, find the chart,
read the number off it, and get on with the job. The charts came from other people's measurements,
their author said whose, and he said how accurate he thought each one was. The book is from 1958 and
parts of it are still the fastest way to get an answer.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rd02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its publisher and its edition year, a reproduction of a scatter with a fitted line and a shaded band, a three-row table of the kinds of answer, a boxed paragraph headed what has aged and what has not, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the book of charts**
> **Source: W. L. Nelson, *Petroleum Refinery Engineering*, McGraw-Hill, **fourth edition, 1958**.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> For most of the twentieth century this was the book. An engineer who needed a boiling point, a
> density, a heat duty, a vessel size or a cost opened it, found the chart, read the number, and
> carried on. Generations of refineries were designed from it.
>
> **What a correlation is.** Somebody measures a quantity on a number of samples, plots the results
> against something easier to measure, and draws a line through them. Anybody can then read a value
> off that line without doing the measurement. That is enormously useful and it has one obvious
> property: **the line is as good as the data under it, over the range the data covers, and no
> better.**
>
> Nelson's charts are built from other people's measurements, and the book **names them**. Each chart
> carries its sources, and the text is explicit about how closely the points fell to the line and
> therefore how much to trust a value read from it. That is unusual then and unusual now.
>
> **Three kinds of answer.** The book is full of all three and it distinguishes them.
>
> | The kind | Good for | Not good for |
> |---|---|---|
> | a **rule of thumb** | deciding whether an idea is worth pursuing | ordering steel |
> | a **correlation or chart** | sizing a first pass; checking somebody's calculation | a final design outside the fitted range |
> | a **full calculation** | a design, with its assumptions recorded | being believed without a check against the plant |
>
> **The cost index.** Nelson also built an index of refinery construction costs, published
> periodically, so that a cost from one year could be brought to another. An index is a correlation
> about money rather than about physics, and it has the same property: it is as good as the basket of
> things it was built from.
>
> **The complexity figure.** Later work associated with the same author produced a way of expressing
> how much processing a refinery has, by weighting each unit's cost against the cost of the crude
> distillation unit. A simple refinery that only distills has a low figure; one with cracking,
> reforming and treating has a high one. It is a **single number describing a whole plant**, which
> means it is very useful for comparing refineries and tells you nothing about any particular one.
>
> **WHAT HAS AGED AND WHAT HAS NOT**
> This is the part to think about, because the book does not say it.
>
> **What has not aged.** Thermodynamics. Heat and material balances. The relationships between
> density, boiling range and the other physical properties of hydrocarbons. The geometry of a
> distillation column. The arithmetic of a yield.
>
> **What has aged completely.** Catalysts, which have been transformed several times over. Product
> specifications, which in 1958 permitted sulfur levels that are now illegal in most markets.
> Metallurgy. Instrumentation and control. Energy costs, which were low enough in 1958 that heat
> integration barely appears — and that is Unit 8 of this book.
>
> **And what is hardest.** The book does not mark the boundary between those two lists, because in
> 1958 nobody could. An engineer using it today has to supply that judgment, which means the book is
> most dangerous in exactly the hands it is most useful in: somebody who does not yet know which
> chapters have moved.
>
> **One more honest point.** A modern process simulator will give you an answer to eight decimal
> places in a second, from correlations and equations of state that are themselves fitted to
> measurements. It has not escaped the problem in this unit. It has made it harder to see.
>
> *Our summary of a published textbook, named with its author, its publisher and its edition. Read the
> published book if you can find it, and read the captions under the charts.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write the accuracy beside the figure, and say what the figure is for.** *Approximately 10
> megawatts, from a chart good to about five percent, good enough to decide whether to study this*
> is one sentence and it closes off three bad conversations. And when you go outside the fitted range,

> the next reader to be careful, and the people who avoid it are the people whose numbers get
> believed.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every organization has a number that gained precision as it traveled. It starts as *about ten*,
> gets typed into a spreadsheet as 9.64, appears on a slide as 9.6, and is quoted back six months
> later as *the* duty. Nobody added the decimal places dishonestly; a spreadsheet simply cannot
> represent *about*. The only defenses are rounding on purpose and writing the accuracy in the next
> cell, and both of them feel like making the work look worse than it is. They make it look exactly as
> good as it is, which is different.

**A screening study said *approximately 10 megawatts, good to about five percent*.** It was correct,
it was properly sourced, and the word *extrapolation* was on the page.

Somebody typed it into a spreadsheet, where it became 9.64. The spreadsheet fed a slide, where it
became 9.6. Six months later an exchanger was ordered for 9.6 megawatts by a competent buyer who had
never seen the study. The real duty, when it was finally measured, was 12.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 At which step did the number stop being an estimate?
2 Nobody was dishonest. What made the precision appear?
3 The word *extrapolation* was on the page. Why did that not help?
4 What one habit, applied once, would have caught this?
