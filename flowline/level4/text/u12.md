%%FIG rd12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the agency, its glossary and its explanatory publication, a diagram of three barrels in and two plus one out with prices, a five-row table of what the indicator assumes against what a plant has, a boxed paragraph headed what it is good for, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the number the market watches**
> **Source: the **United States Energy Information Administration**'s published glossary and its
> explanatory series on energy topics, which set out what a **crack spread** is and what it
> approximates. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What it is.** The **crack spread** is the difference between the price of crude oil and the prices
> of the products refined from it. A common construction is the **three-two-one** spread: take the
> price of **three** barrels of a benchmark crude, subtract it from the combined price of **two**
> barrels of gasoline and **one** barrel of distillate, and divide by three to get a figure per barrel.
>
> Why those proportions? Because they roughly resemble the output of a typical refinery in the market
> the figure was built for. They are an **approximation of a yield**, chosen to be simple, and they are
> fixed.
>
> **What it is good for.** Telling you, every day, whether refining in that market is getting more or
> less profitable. It is published daily, it is consistent, everybody uses the same construction, and
> it moves when the market moves. For that purpose it is excellent, and the agency that publishes
> explanations of it says plainly that it is an approximation.
>
> **AND WHY IT IS NOT YOUR MARGIN**
> Five differences, and every one of them is the subject of an earlier unit of this book.
>
> | The indicator assumes | Your plant actually has |
> |---|---|
> | a **benchmark** crude at its published price | the crude you buy, at a **differential** — a heavy sour crude sells below a light sweet benchmark, which is Book 1 Unit 1 |
> | a **fixed assumed yield** | your own **yield slate**, set by which units you have — which is Unit 3 of this book |
> | **published** product prices | the prices you **realized**, in the markets you can actually sell into — which is Unit 4 |
> | **no operating cost at all** | energy, people, maintenance, catalyst and chemicals — and the largest of those is usually energy, which is Unit 8 |
> | **no transport** | getting crude in and product out |
>
> **The consequence is arithmetic, not an excuse.** A refinery can have a poor month when the spread
> is strong — because its crude differential widened, or its units were down, or its energy bill rose —
> and a good month when the spread is weak. Anybody who manages a plant against the published spread
> will be puzzled regularly.
>
> **Complexity is the other half of it.** A simple refinery that only distills makes what the crude
> gives it. A complex one, with cracking and treating, can turn the heavy end into the light products
> that are worth more — which is the whole content of Unit 3. Two refineries in the same market, on the
> same day, with the same spread in front of them, therefore earn very different amounts, and the
> difference is mostly what they are able to make rather than what they are able to sell.
>
> **WHAT THIS BOOK HAS BEEN ABOUT**
> Look back at the eleven units before this one. Every one of them is about a number whose meaning
> lives in its definition.
>
> A boiling range is defined by **a test method**. An octane number is defined by **an engine and two
> reference liquids**. A correlation is defined by **whose data and over what range**. A fuel limit is
> defined by **a law, in a place**. A capacity is defined by **which of four capacities**. An energy
> target is defined by **an approach temperature**. A relief valve is defined by **a scenario list**. An
> emissions total is defined by **a boundary**. A transport classification is defined by **properties on
> a date**. And the crack spread is defined by **an assumed yield**.
>
> **In every single case the definition is short, it is published, and it does not travel with the
> number.** That is the whole argument of this book, and it is not a point about carefulness. It is
> about what a number is: a figure plus the thing that produced it, and nothing else.
>
> *Our summary of a published glossary and explanatory series, named with the agency that publishes
> them. Read the published explanation. It is the plainest writing in this course and it is written
> for the public.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Reconcile, line by line, every month.** A published spread and your own margin with five named
> adjustment lines between them turns an argument about performance into arithmetic, and every one of
> those five lines belongs to somebody who can do something about it. The crude differential belongs
> to the people buying. The yield belongs to the units. The energy line belongs to Unit 8. And when
> you explain the spread to anybody, **lead with what it is for**, not with what it leaves out: *it is
> a daily indicator of the market* earns you the right to say *and it has no costs in it*.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> This is the last culture check in the book, and the pattern it describes has appeared in all twelve
> units. A number is created with a definition attached. The definition is short and published. The
> number travels and the definition does not. And then somebody compares two numbers whose definitions
> are different, in good faith, and reaches a conclusion that is wrong in a way nobody in the room can
> see. It happens with test methods, octane conventions, capacity denominators, emissions boundaries,
> energy targets and market indicators, in every company, everywhere. Nothing in this book is a story
> about carelessness. It is about the ordinary fact that a number is portable and its definition is
> not, and that the only remedy is to attach them by hand, every time, at the cost of about six words.
> **Ask your group: which number at your site has traveled furthest from its definition?**
