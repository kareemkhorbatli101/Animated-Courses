# Starting Point · What a Barrel Becomes
%%SUB Six things you already have, and the question the whole book answers%%
%%GOALS
Say what a barrel is separated into
Say why the products are worth more than the crude
Say what a product slate is
%%

A refinery does not make oil into anything. It takes a barrel apart into pieces that boil at
different temperatures, changes some of the pieces chemically, and sells them separately for more
than the barrel cost. Everything in this book is one step of that.

## 2 · The one idea

%%FIG rd00-f2 | A tall distillation column in section with five side draws leaving it at different heights, labeled from the top down as gas, gasoline range, kerosene range, diesel range and residue, with a thermometer scale beside the column rising from top to bottom.%%

**2A  Look at the picture. Say which product leaves at the top and which at the bottom.**

Crude oil is a mixture of thousands of different molecules. The small ones boil at low temperatures
and the large ones at high temperatures. Heat the mixture and take off what boils at each level, and
you have separated it.

| Roughly, from the top down | What it becomes |
|---|---|
| gas | fuel for the plant, or liquefied and sold |
| the lightest liquid | gasoline range |
| next | kerosene and jet range |
| next | diesel and gas oil range |
| what will not boil | fuel oil, bitumen, or feed for another unit |

**That is the whole of distillation**, which is Unit 1, and which has not changed in principle in a
hundred years. What has changed is everything that happens to the pieces afterwards.

**2B  Say the five levels aloud with a partner.** Then say which one you think is worth the most, and
why.

## 3 · Why it is worth doing

%%FIG rd00-f3 | Two bars side by side. The left bar is one barrel of crude with one price label. The right bar is divided into five product segments, each with its own price label, and the total is higher than the left bar. A gap between the two totals is marked with a bracket and the word margin.%%

**3A  Look at the two bars with a partner. Say which total is bigger.**

A barrel of crude has one price. The products made from it have several prices, and the total is
normally higher. The difference pays for the plant, the people, the energy and the risk, and what is
left is the margin.

**The whole business of a refinery is that difference**, and the English for it is in Unit 12. It is
worth knowing from the first page, because it explains why every unit in this book exists: each one
is either making the products worth more, or stopping something expensive from happening.

**3B  Discuss in your group.** If the difference between the two bars got smaller every year for ten
years, what would a refinery do? Say three things, and say which is easiest.

## 4 · The two refineries, as a matter of record

%%FIG rd00-f4 | A two-row table of the two refineries with columns for the place, the year it opened and the published nameplate capacity, and beneath it a line in bold reading nameplate is what it was built to do.%%

**4A  Read the two rows aloud with a partner.**

| Where | Opened | Published nameplate capacity |
|---|---|---|
| **Homs** | 1959 | about 107,000 barrels a day |
| **Banias** | 1979 | about 133,000 barrels a day |

**Nameplate capacity is what a plant was built to do.** It is not what it did last year, and
published figures for any country come from compilations whose provenance differs. Unit 7 is about
exactly that difference, and this book gives the figure, the year and the kind of source every time.

**4B  Ask and answer with a partner.** Why do you think a published capacity figure might be
different from what a plant actually processes?
