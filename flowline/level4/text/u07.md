%%SUB Four different capacities, and the one that gets published%%
%%GOALS

Ask what a refinery's capacity is and you will get a number. There are at least four different
numbers, they can differ from each other by a third, and the one that appears in published
compilations is usually the oldest of them.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rd07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the publication, the organization that publishes it and its annual frequency, a four-bar diagram of the capacities, a two-row table comparing direct communication with secondary sources, a boxed paragraph headed what a compilation can and cannot tell you, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: where published capacity figures come from**
> **Source: the ***Annual Statistical Bulletin*** published each year by the Organization of the
> Petroleum Exporting Countries. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **Why read a statistical compilation at all?** Because sooner or later somebody will ask you what
> your country's refining capacity is, or what a competitor's is, and the answer will come from a
> table like this one. Knowing how such a table is made is the difference between quoting a figure and
> understanding it.
>
> **What the publication is.** An annual compilation of oil and gas statistics: reserves, production,
> refining capacity, trade, by country and in world totals. It is compiled from two kinds of input,
> and the publication is open about this.
>
> | The kind of input | What it is |
> |---|---|
> | **direct communication** | figures supplied by a member country's own authorities |
> | **secondary sources** | figures estimated by the compiler from other published information |
>
> For some tables the two sets of figures are published **side by side**, and they do not always
> agree. That is not an embarrassment; it is the compiler telling you what it knows and how.
>
> **Which figures are which matters here in a specific way.** This country is **not a member** of that
> organization. So any figure for it in that publication, or in most others like it, comes from
> **secondary sources** — estimated by a compiler from other published material, not supplied by a
> national authority to that compiler. That is worth saying out loud before quoting one.
>
> **AND WHICH CAPACITY IS IT?**
> Refining capacity in such a table is a **nameplate** figure: the distillation capacity the plant was
> designed for. There are at least four different numbers that could be called capacity, and the gaps
> between them are large.
>
> **Nameplate capacity** is the design figure, from the year it was built. For the two refineries in
> this country those years are 1959 and 1979.
>
> **Current maximum sustainable capacity** is what a plant can actually sustain today. Over decades
> this moves in **both directions**. Pumps are replaced, columns are retrayed, exchangers are added,
> restrictions are removed — a well-maintained plant that has been debottlenecked may sustain
> **more** than its nameplate. Equally, a furnace may have been derated, a unit may be permanently
> out, and the figure may be **well below** nameplate. From a one-column table you cannot tell which.
>
> **Available capacity** is the sustainable figure minus the time the plant is not there: planned
> turnarounds, which take weeks, and unplanned outages, which take whatever they take.
>
> **Actual throughput** is what was processed.
>
> **Utilization** is throughput divided by capacity — and since *capacity* could be any of the first
> three, the same plant can honestly be described as running at ninety-four percent or at
> sixty-eight, depending on the denominator. **Nobody is lying. Ask which denominator.**
>
> **WHAT A COMPILATION CAN AND CANNOT TELL YOU**
> It is **good** at comparisons across countries and across years, on a consistent basis, which is
> genuinely hard to do and is what it exists for.
>
> It is **not** a description of any particular plant on any particular day. It carries one figure
> where four exist. It is annual, so it is at best a year out of date. And its footnotes — which is
> where the provenance lives — are in small type and almost never travel with the number.
>
> **So the rule for quoting one is short.** Give the figure, the year it refers to, the publication,
> and whether the publication says where it got it. Four things. A capacity figure quoted without them
> is a number that will be argued about by people who do not know they are arguing about two different
> definitions.
>
> *Our summary of a published annual compilation, named with its publisher. Read the published
> bulletin, and read the notes to the table rather than the table.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Never write *capacity* alone.** There are four of them and the gaps between them can be a third of
> the plant. *Nameplate*, *current sustainable*, *available* and *throughput* each take one extra word
> and remove an entire category of argument. And when you quote a published figure, quote it with
> **four things**: the figure, the year it refers to, the publication, and whether that publication
> says where it got it. The fourth is the one nobody includes and the one that decides whether anybody
> can check you.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Capacity figures are the most quoted and least defined numbers in this industry, and the reason is
> that four different people need four different ones. A board wants to know how much asset it owns.
> An operations manager wants to know how well the plant ran when it was running. A regulator wants a
> consistent basis across companies. A journalist wants one number for a sentence. All four are
> reasonable and they are not the same question, so the figure splits — and then travels without its
> denominator, and the arguments start. The fix is never a better number. It is one extra word.
> **Ask your group: which number at your site means four different things to four departments?**

ninety-four percent of available capacity, and the plant had run well whenever it was available.

An investor compared that figure with another company's sixty-eight percent and concluded that this
plant was far better run. The other company had used nameplate as its denominator. Both reports were
accurate, neither stated its denominator, and the comparison was meaningless.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Did either company mislead anybody?
2 The comparison was meaningless. Who was harmed by it?
3 One extra word in each report would have prevented it. Why was it not there?
4 Where at your site is a number published without its denominator?
