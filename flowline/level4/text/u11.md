%%SUB Four digits on an orange plate, and the laboratory report they came from%%
%%GOALS

There is an orange plate on the back of a road tanker with four digits on it. Those digits are the end
of a chain that starts with a flash point measured in a laboratory, passes through a decision made by
whoever is sending the load, and ends with a firefighter at the side of a road at three in the morning
who has about ninety seconds and that plate.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rd11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the recommendations, the committee that publishes them and the modal instruments that give them force, a nine-class chart, a table of three products with their UN numbers and shipping names, a boxed paragraph headed who classifies, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the orange plate, and where the number comes from**
> **Source: the United Nations ***Recommendations on the Transport of Dangerous Goods — Model
> Regulations***, published by the committee of experts and revised every two years, read alongside
> the road transport agreement **ADR**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **Start with what the document is.** The Model Regulations are **recommendations**. They are not law
> anywhere by themselves. They become law through **modal instruments** — a road agreement, a rail
> agreement, a sea code, air instructions — and through each country's own legislation. So the thing
> that binds a driver is the **national law** of the country they are driving through, which gives
> effect to a modal instrument, which is based on the recommendations.
>
> That three-step chain is why the recommendations are revised every two years and why a road tanker
> crossing a border may meet two slightly different versions of the same rules.
>
> **Nine classes.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **1** explosives | **6** toxic and infectious substances |
> | **2** gases | **7** radioactive material |
> | **3** flammable liquids | **8** corrosive substances |
> | **4** flammable solids and related | **9** miscellaneous |
> | **5** oxidizing substances and organic peroxides | |
>
> **Every substance gets three things.** A **UN number**, four digits, which identifies it. A **proper
> shipping name**, which is the name it must be described by on the transport document — not the
> trade name and not the plant's own name for it. And a **packing group**, I, II or III, which says
> how dangerous it is within its class.
>
> | What we move | UN number | Proper shipping name |
> |---|---|---|
> | crude oil | **1267** | petroleum crude oil |
> | gasoline | **1203** | gasoline |
> | gas oil, diesel, light heating oil | **1202** | gas oil, or diesel fuel, or heating oil, light |
>
> For flammable liquids the packing group is assigned from the **flash point** and the **initial
> boiling point** — which means it is assigned from laboratory results, by the methods of Unit 1 and
> their precision sections.
>
> **A substance can also carry a subsidiary hazard.** A crude oil with enough hydrogen sulfide in it
> is still a flammable liquid and is **also** toxic, and the classification has to say so. That is not
> a technicality: it changes the label, it changes what a first responder expects, and it changes how
> the load is handled.
>
> **WHO CLASSIFIES**
> The **consignor** — the party sending the goods. Not the carrier, not the driver, not the receiving
> terminal. The company putting the load on the road is responsible for determining the
> classification, for declaring it correctly on the transport document, and for the placards being
> right.
>
> And classification rests on **properties**, which rest on **test results**. So the orange plate is
> the last link in a chain that starts with a flash point on a laboratory bench. If the flash point was
> measured on a sample that is not representative of the load, the plate is wrong and everything
> downstream of it is confidently wrong.
>
> **WHY A CORRECT CLASSIFICATION CAN BECOME WRONG**
> This is the part that connects to the rest of the series.
>
> A classification is correct for a product with particular properties. **Products change.** A crude
> sours over the life of a field and its hydrogen sulfide content rises — exactly the situation in
> Book 3's materials unit. A blend changes when a new stream is added. A specification change alters a
> cut point and therefore a flash point.
>
> **Nobody reclassifies a product because a field aged.** The classification was determined once, by
> somebody competent, from a sample that was representative **then**, and it is now sitting in a
> system being printed onto plates. A subsidiary toxic hazard that should now be declared may not be,
> and no document in the company will show that, because every document is a correct copy of a
> decision that was right when it was made.
>
> **And what the recommendations do not do.** They do not test your product. They do not tell you
> whether your sample was representative. They are revised every two years and they do not come and
> find you. And they are recommendations: the applicable law is national, and it is the national law
> a court will read.
>
> *Our summary of published recommendations and a road transport agreement, named with the body that
> publishes them. Read the published volume, and find the entry for a product you handle.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write the date the classification was determined, beside the classification.** A UN number with no

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Transport classification is the only piece of paperwork in this industry whose primary reader is a
> stranger in an emergency. Everything else is written for colleagues, auditors, buyers or regulators —
> people who can ask a question. The orange plate is read once, in the dark, by somebody who cannot
> ask anybody anything, and who will make a decision about how close to stand. That changes what
> accuracy means. In most documents an out-of-date figure is an inconvenience. Here it is a stranger
> being made confident about the wrong thing.

A transport document lies open with a UN number on it, beside a classification record dated 1998, a laboratory report from this May showing a hydrogen sulfide figure five times higher, an orange plate photograph with one diamond on it, a note of who signs as consignor, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A classification was determined correctly in 1998 by a competent person from a representative
sample.** Every transport document since has been a correct copy of it. Every plate has matched every
document. Every audit has found the paperwork in order, because it is in order.

The crude has soured. The hydrogen sulfide content is now about five times what it was. A subsidiary
toxic hazard may apply and has never been considered, because nothing in the system asks whether a
product has changed — only whether the documents agree with each other.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is the load incorrectly classified? Say what you would need to know.
2 Every document agrees with every other document. Why is that not enough?
3 Who should have asked, and what would have prompted them?
4 The primary reader of that plate cannot ask a question. What does that change?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 12 is the number traders look at every
morning and almost nobody on a plant has ever seen.
