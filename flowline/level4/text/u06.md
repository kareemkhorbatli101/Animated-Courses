# Unit 6 · The Fuel That Has to Reach a Ship
%%SUB A specification whose most important clauses have no numbers in them%%
%%GOALS

Use *maximum*, *minimum* and *free from*

%%

A ship buys fuel in one port and burns it a week later in the middle of an ocean, with nobody to
complain to. The specification that stands between those two moments has about twenty numerical
limits in it. The clauses that have caused the most argument and the most damage are the two with no
numbers at all.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rd06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, its number and its publisher, a specification table with columns for the characteristic, the unit, the limit and the test method, a two-row boxed extract of the clauses with no numbers, a boxed paragraph headed what the specification does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the fuel a ship has to live with**
> **Source: **ISO 8217**, *Petroleum products — Fuels (class F) — Specifications of marine fuels*.
> Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **Why this one is different.** A ship takes fuel in one port and burns it days later at sea. If
> something is wrong with it, the engineer on board has a problem, a long way from anybody, with the
> ship's propulsion depending on the answer. That shapes the whole document.
>
> **Grades.** The standard sets out two families. **Distillate** fuels, which are lighter and cleaner.
> **Residual** fuels, which are heavier, cheaper, and contain the parts of the barrel that will not
> boil. Each grade has a designation: letters for the family and the grade, then a **number giving the
> maximum viscosity** at a stated temperature. A designation is therefore a short sentence rather than
> a code.
>
> **What is limited.** Twenty or so characteristics, each with a unit, a limit and a **named test
> method**. Among them:
>
> | Characteristic | Direction | Why it is there |
> |---|---|---|
> | **viscosity** at the stated temperature | maximum | it decides whether the ship can heat and pump it |
> | **density** | maximum | it affects separation of water and solids on board |
> | **flash point** | **minimum** | a safety limit, not a performance one |
> | **water** | maximum | it does not burn and it carries salt |
> | **ash**, **vanadium**, **sodium** | maximum | they attack hot metal in the engine |
> | **aluminum and silicon**, together | maximum | **catalyst fines**: hard, abrasive particles |
> | **total sediment** | maximum | it blocks filters and separators |
> | **pour point** | maximum | below it the fuel will not flow at all |
>
> Note that **flash point is a minimum** while almost everything else is a maximum. Mixing those two
> words up in a specification conversation is the fastest way to be identified as somebody who has not
> read it.
>
> **The two clauses with no numbers.** These are the ones that matter most.
>
> The fuel **shall be a homogeneous blend of hydrocarbons derived from petroleum refining**, and it
> **shall be free from** inorganic acid and used lubricating oils.
>
> It **shall not contain** any added substance or chemical waste that jeopardizes the safety of the
> ship, adversely affects the performance of the machinery, is harmful to personnel, or contributes to
> additional air pollution.
>
> Those two sentences are the reason the standard works. Every numerical limit can be satisfied by a
> blend that is nonetheless not fuel — and a prohibition with a number in it would read, to the wrong
> kind of supplier, as a permitted quantity.
>
> **WHAT THE SPECIFICATION DOES NOT DO**
> **It is commercial, not statutory.** This is a standard agreed between buyers and sellers. The
> **legal** limit on sulfur in marine fuel comes from somewhere else entirely — an international
> maritime convention and the national or regional rules that implement it — and in some sea areas
> the legal limit is tighter still. A cargo can meet the specification and be illegal to burn where
> the ship is going. **Both documents have to be satisfied, and only one of them is in this
> standard.**
>
> **It does not guarantee that the fuel will run in your engine.** The characteristics are those
> considered adequate for the grade; engine builders publish their own requirements, which are
> sometimes tighter, and the standard does not override them.
>
> **And the most damaging items are the hardest to catch.** Catalyst fines and total sediment do their
> damage slowly, inside pumps and injectors, and they cannot be assessed at a bunker manifold in the
> hour available. What the ship has instead is a **sample**, taken at the manifold, sealed, signed by
> both parties and retained — which is the same idea as Unit 11 of the last book, in a different
> place: where two organizations share a hazard and neither is in charge, the answer is a jointly
> witnessed document.
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its number and its publisher. Read the published
> standard, and read the two clauses with no numbers in them.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Write the grade designation in full, and write what it means.** *RMG 380* is a short sentence —
> residual, that grade, maximum viscosity 380 — and a delivery note that spells it out once cannot be
> misread by anybody. And always record the **sample**: drawn where, over what period, sealed with
> which number, signed by whom, and how many retained. Everything else on the document can be argued
> about later. The sample is the only thing that will still be true in six months.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Bunkering is the most pressured commercial moment in this industry: a ship is burning money while it
> sits, a barge is on a schedule, and the people doing the paperwork are the two most junior staff
> present. Everything about the situation pushes toward signing quickly, and the most valuable thing
> on the page is the one that takes longest — a sample drawn throughout the delivery rather than
> grabbed at the start. Nobody is being lazy. The incentives are simply all pointing one way, and the
> only defense is that both sides know the sample is what they will have.

**Every numerical result on a bunker delivery note was inside its limit.** The certificate was correct.
The two clauses in words were signed by both parties in good faith, and nobody had any reason at the
time to believe otherwise.

Three weeks later, two fuel pumps on the ship were scored. The sample that had been taken was a single
bottle drawn at the start of the delivery. The engineer who took it was experienced and in a hurry,
and the barge was on a schedule.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 What can anybody now prove?
2 Everything on the note was correct. Why does that not help?
3 The sampling method is the one thing that cannot be fixed afterwards. Why is it the thing that gets
   rushed?
4 What would you change on your next delivery, and who would you have to persuade?
