%%FIG rd04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the directive, its number, its year and the body that made it, a specification table with four columns, a timeline of phased dates with a shaded transitional band, a boxed paragraph headed why somebody else's law matters here, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the law that decided what refineries had to build**
> **Source: **Directive 98/70/EC** of the European Parliament and of the Council, of **13 October
> 1998**, relating to the quality of petrol and diesel fuels, **as amended** — in particular by
> instruments of 2003 and 2009. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **It does not apply here.** We are not in that market and this law does not bind this company. Read
> on anyway, because it has changed more refineries in more countries than any standard in this
> course.
>
> **What a directive is.** It is not a regulation that takes effect directly. It is an instrument that
> requires each member state to **transpose** it: to write its own national law achieving the same
> result, by a stated date. So the law that actually binds a refiner selling into that market is the
> **national law of the country of sale**, and the directive is what that law has to deliver.
>
> **What it sets.** Annexes give specification tables for petrol and for diesel: a list of
> parameters, each with a unit, a limit, and a **reference test method**. Among them:
>
> | Parameter | Direction | What the amended instrument requires |
> |---|---|---|
> | **sulfur content** | maximum | **10 milligrams per kilogram**, with effect from 1 January 2009 |
> | **benzene** in petrol | maximum | a limit expressed in percent by volume |
> | **aromatics** in petrol | maximum | a limit expressed in percent by volume |
> | **lead** | prohibited | leaded petrol withdrawn |
> | distillation and density parameters | maxima and minima | as tabulated |
>
> **Read the sulfur figure again.** Ten milligrams per kilogram is ten parts per million. A crude at
> four percent sulfur is forty thousand parts per million. The specification therefore requires the
> removal of about **three thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine parts out of every four thousand**,
> and that cannot be done by distillation at all. It takes a hydrotreater, a hydrogen supply and a
> sulfur recovery unit.
>
> **It was phased.** The limits did not arrive at once. Earlier instruments brought the figure down in
> steps, with dates, and some member states were granted **transitional derogations** allowing them
> longer. That is ordinary in law-making and awkward in engineering: a refinery has to build for the
> final date, not the transitional one, because a plant takes years and a derogation expires.
>
> **WHY SOMEBODY ELSE'S LAW MATTERS HERE**
> Because a refinery that wants to **export** must meet the specification of the place it is selling
> into. Not its own national specification. The buyer's.
>
> That single fact is the most important commercial statement about product quality anywhere in the
> world, and it explains a great deal of what has been built in this industry since the late 1990s.
> When one large market set a ten milligram limit, refineries in many countries that had no such
> domestic requirement built the units anyway, because the alternative was to be locked out.
>
> It also produces a familiar situation: a plant making two grades of the same product, one for export
> and one for the domestic market, to two different specifications, out of the same crude. That is not
> a scandal. It is what happens when two jurisdictions have different laws, and the English for
> explaining it clearly is in this unit.
>
> **And its limits.** It is **one bloc's law**, amended several times, and what applies on any given
> day is the **transposed national law** in the country of sale, in its current form. It covers
> **road** fuels, not every petroleum product. Vehicle emission requirements are a **separate** set of
> instruments and should not be confused with fuel quality. And every limit in it depends on a
> **named test method** — which brings us back to Unit 1, and to the reproducibility figure that
> decides who wins an argument.
>
> *Our summary of a published directive, named with its number, its date and the body that made it.
> Read the published instrument, and read an annex table rather than the articles.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Name the market beside every specification.** *On specification* is meaningless on a plant that
> serves three markets, and the sentence *on specification for market B, not for market A* is twelve
> words that stop a cargo being loaded into the wrong ship. And when somebody says the domestic grade
> is worse, **say what it is**: compliant with a different instrument written by a different
> legislature. That is not a defense of anything. It is the only accurate description, and the
> alternative is a conversation about quality that is actually a conversation about law.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> There is a conversation in this industry that goes wrong in every country, and it goes wrong the
> same way. Somebody notices that the fuel sold at home has a looser specification than the fuel
> exported, and concludes that the home market is being cheated. The technical answer — two
> legislatures, two instruments, both complied with — is completely true and completely useless as a
> reply, because the question underneath it is not technical. It is about who decides what is good
> enough, and that is a fair question with a political answer. The useful skill is being able to give
> the accurate technical description without pretending it settles the other thing.
> **Ask your group: who decides what is good enough where you work, and does anybody ever ask them?**

**A cargo was nominated for a market with a ten milligram sulfur limit.** The certificate of quality
said 500 and was correct. The product met the domestic specification and the certificate named the
specification it had been tested against.

Nobody checked the destination's limit, because the nomination form has no line for it. The ship was
chartered, the cargo was rejected at the discharge port, and the cost of that one voyage was more than
the annual cost of adding a line to a form.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Who made the mistake?
2 Every document involved was correct. How did this happen?
3 The missing line costs nothing. Why does nobody add it?
4 What would you do on your next shift?
