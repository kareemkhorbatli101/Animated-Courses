# Unit 4 · The Payment You Do Not Make
%%SUB A customs post, a clock running, and the only safe thing to do%%
%%GOALS
Say what the convention obliges states to do
Use prohibition language
Report a demand in writing
Say what is not a defense
Say what to do and what not to decide alone
%%

Somewhere in a career in this industry, somebody will ask one of you for a payment. It will almost
certainly not be in an office. It will be at a border, a port, a customs post or a checkpoint, there
will be a clock running and a cost attached to every hour, and you will be alone.

This unit is about the document that stands behind that moment, and about the four things to do when
it happens.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rf04-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the convention, its full name, the organization under which it was adopted and its year, a diagram showing a company, an intermediary and an official with an arrow crossed through, a table of what parties undertake to do, a boxed paragraph headed what the convention does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the document behind the four steps**
> **Source: the **Convention on Combating Bribery of Foreign Public Officials in International
> Business Transactions**, adopted under the **Organisation for Economic Co-operation and
> Development**, **1997**. Printed here in our own words. **This is not legal advice and it does not
> state the law of any country, including ours.**
>
> **What kind of document this is, and it matters.** It is a treaty between states. **It does not
> itself make anything a crime.** It obliges each **Party** to make it a criminal offense **under its
> own law** to bribe a foreign public official, and to back that up with enforcement, sanctions and
> cooperation. So the thing that can prosecute you is a national statute; the Convention is why that
> statute exists and why it looks similar in many countries.
>
> **What the offense is.** Intentionally **offering, promising or giving** any **undue pecuniary or
> other advantage** — money, or anything else of value — to a **foreign public official**, directly
> or **through intermediaries**, for that official or a third party, in order that the official act or
> refrain from acting in relation to official duties, **in order to obtain or retain business or
> other improper advantage** in international business.
>
> **Read that again for the three things most people get wrong.**
>
> **It covers offering and promising**, not only paying. Nothing has to change hands.
> **It covers payments through intermediaries** — agents, consultants, brokers, joint venture
> partners. You cannot put a layer between yourself and the act and be outside it.
> **The advantage need not be money.** Employment for a relative, a trip, a contract for a connected
> company.
>
> **What Parties undertake to do.** Make it an offense, with **effective, proportionate and
> dissuasive** penalties. Establish the **liability of legal persons** — companies, not only people.
> Take measures on **accounting**: prohibiting **off-the-books accounts**, unrecorded transactions,
> the recording of non-existent expenditure, the use of false documents. Treat bribery as a predicate
> offense for **money laundering**. Provide **mutual legal assistance** between Parties, and treat the
> offense as extraditable. And submit to **peer monitoring**: the Parties examine each other's
> implementation and publish the reports, which is unusual and is the main reason the Convention has
> teeth.
>
> **Why the accounting provisions are the practical ones.** Very few of these cases are made by
> somebody witnessing a payment. They are made from **documents**: an invoice with no description, a
> consultancy fee with no deliverable, a round-number payment to an agent in a third country, an
> expense coded to the wrong account. **The books are the evidence**, in both directions: they are how
> wrongdoing is proved, and a complete, honest record is also how an innocent person is cleared.
>
> **On small payments.** Some national laws have historically treated small payments to secure routine
> administrative action differently from bribery to win business. That distinction has narrowed
> considerably: the OECD has recommended that Parties review it, many companies prohibit such payments
> outright, and **the fact that a payment is small, or customary, or was demanded rather than offered,
> does not of itself make it lawful**. If you take one sentence from this note, take that one.
>
> **WHAT THE CONVENTION DOES NOT DO**
> **It does not state the law that applies to you.** Your national law does, and so does the law of
> every country your company is connected to. Only a lawyer can tell you what applies.
>
> **It is about the supply side.** It addresses those who **offer or give**. It does not create an
> offense of demanding a bribe; other instruments and national laws address that.
>
> **It is about foreign public officials.** Domestic bribery and private-sector bribery are dealt with
> elsewhere.
>
> **Enforcement varies enormously between Parties**, and the Convention's own peer review reports say
> so, in public, by name.
>
> **And it cannot help you at a barrier.** It is the reason your company has a policy. The policy is
> the reason you have four steps. The four steps are the only thing that is any use at the moment it
> happens.
>
> *Our summary of a published international convention, named with its full title, its organization
> and its year. This is not legal advice. Read your own company's policy, and find out tonight who you
> would telephone.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Learn two sentences by heart in the languages you work in.** *I am not able to do that* and *I
> would have to ask*. They are not refusals, they do not accuse anybody of anything, and they move the
> decision to a place where it can be made properly. **Then write the note before you do anything
> else.** In the vehicle, with the time on it, in the words that were used. Nobody has ever regretted
> a contemporaneous note, and the note protects **you** at least as much as it protects the company.
> **And report it the same day, to two places.** Your manager, and the channel. If one of those is
> where the problem is, the other one is why you used both.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> This is the hardest unit in the series to teach honestly, because the person who faces the demand is
> almost never the person who made the policy, and the cost of saying no is immediate, visible and
> theirs. A delay costs money that somebody will ask about this week; the consequence of paying is
> somebody else's problem in three years. In many places the demand is not even made by a person
> benefiting from it: it is a system that has worked that way for a long time, operated by people on
> very small salaries, and treating every one of them as a criminal is both unkind and useless. None
> of that changes the four steps, and none of it changes the one sentence in the reading. What a
> company can actually do is make saying no **cheap** for the individual: a channel that answers, a
> manager who backs them, and a written culture in which the delay is reported as a cost of doing the
> right thing rather than as somebody's failure.
> **Ask your group: at your organization, who pays the price when somebody says no?**
