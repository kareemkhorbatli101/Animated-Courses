somebody else's percentage. By the end of it you will be able to work out, from a page of terms, who

%%FIG rf01-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its publisher and its year, a waterfall diagram of a hundred barrels through five deductions, a two-row table comparing the two families, a boxed paragraph headed what a take figure does not tell you, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: who gets what out of a hundred barrels**
> **Source: Daniel Johnston, *International Petroleum Fiscal Systems and Production Sharing
> Contracts*, PennWell, **1994**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What the book is.** The standard plain-language account of how petroleum fiscal systems work. It
> was written to let people who are not tax lawyers read a set of terms and work out what they mean,
> and it is still the book people are given when they move into this part of the industry.
>
> **Two families.** In a **concessionary** system the company takes **title** to the production at the
> wellhead and pays the state a **royalty** on gross production and **income tax** on its profits. In
> a **contractual** system — a **production sharing contract**, or a service agreement — the **state
> keeps title**. The contractor is permitted to recover its **costs** out of production and to take a
> share of what is left, and that share is usually taxed.
>
> **The difference is real and it is not the difference people think.** Title is a political and legal
> fact, and it matters. But **whether a government ends up with more depends entirely on the numbers
> inside the system**, not on which family it belongs to. A generous sharing contract can leave the
> state with less than a tough concession.
>
> **The order of operations, which is the whole of this unit.** Out of **gross production**:
>
> | Step | What comes off |
> |---|---|
> | 1 | **royalty**, if there is one, taken off the top, before any cost is considered |
> | 2 | **cost oil**: the contractor's recoverable costs, **up to a stated ceiling** |
> | 3 | any costs above the ceiling are **carried forward** to the next period |
> | 4 | what remains is **profit oil**, divided between state and contractor |
> | 5 | the contractor normally pays **income tax** on its share of profit oil |
>
> **The split is rarely a single number.** It commonly moves on a **sliding scale**, by production
> rate, by cumulative production, or by a ratio of what the contractor has received to what it has
> spent. So *the split* has no answer without a condition attached: at this rate, in this tranche, at
> this stage of the project.
>
> **Government take and entitlement are not the same thing.** **Take** is the government's share of
> the **profits**. **Entitlement** is the share of the **production volume** a party may lift and
> sell. A contractor can have a large entitlement and a small take at the same time, because most of
> its entitlement is **cost oil**, which is not income.
>
> **WHAT A TAKE FIGURE DOES NOT TELL YOU**
> A published take percentage is almost never comparable with another one, and the reasons are all
> arithmetic.
>
> **It does not say what the denominator is.** Share of profits, or share of gross revenue. Those two
> figures differ by a factor of two or more on the same contract.
> **It does not say at what production rate.** On a sliding scale the answer moves, sometimes by ten
> points or more.
> **It does not say at what cost level.** The ceiling binds at high costs and does not bind at low
> ones, and the answer changes.
> **It does not say whether bonuses, rentals, fees and domestic supply obligations are included.**
> **And it says nothing about timing.** A high take with **fast** cost recovery can be worth more to a
> contractor than a low take with slow recovery, because money arriving sooner is worth more than the
> same money arriving later. **A take figure has no time in it at all.**
>
> **And one limit of this book itself.** It was published in **1994**. The mechanics it explains have
> not changed. The illustrative terms and country data in it are **thirty years old**, and terms are
> renegotiated.
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its author, publisher and year. Read the book, and then
> go and reproduce one published take figure with a pencil.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Never write a percentage in this subject without its denominator.** *77.6 per cent of profits* and
> *38.8 per cent of gross revenue* describe the same page of terms and the gap between them is the
> whole argument. **And when you quote somebody else's take figure, write the four facts beside it:**
> the number, the publication, the year, and whether the publication states its denominator. If it
> does not, say so. Half the comparisons in public circulation cannot be reproduced from the terms
> they claim to describe, and saying that is not an accusation — it is an arithmetic observation
> anybody can check.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Fiscal terms are the most consequential numbers in this industry and almost nobody who works in it
> has ever seen them. They are negotiated by a small number of people, often under confidentiality,
> frequently in a hurry, and almost always at a particular moment in the price cycle — and then they
> govern a field for thirty years, through a cycle nobody predicted. The asymmetry is not usually
> corruption. It is that one side does this for a living in forty countries and the other side does it
> once a decade. The single most useful professional habit in this subject is also the least
> glamorous: be the person in the room who can do the arithmetic with a pencil, slowly, in front of
> everybody.
> **Ask your group: who in your country negotiates these terms, and where would you go to read one?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf01-f16 | A desk in the evening. A waterfall worksheet lies open with four percentages at the foot, beside a published comparison table printing sixty-two per cent with no methodology note, a board paper quoting sixty-two, a note recording a telephone call to somebody who left two years ago, a pencil, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A figure is in circulation.** It is printed in a respected international comparison, it is quoted in
your own board papers, and it has been used in two public statements. It is a number somebody
calculated carefully, from real terms, for a real purpose.

It cannot be reproduced from the terms it describes, on any definition you can construct. The
publication does not state its denominator. The person who built the table left two years ago, and
the methodology note was internal.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is the figure wrong?
2 What would you write in the next board paper, in one sentence?
3 Nobody did anything careless. What is missing from the publication?
4 What would you ask for, and from whom?
