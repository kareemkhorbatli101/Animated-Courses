# Unit 8 · Energy and the State
%%SUB An argument about pipes, strikes and what a country becomes%%

This unit contains the largest claim in the series: that the **physical form** of an energy system —
how it is produced, who has to touch it, and how it moves — shapes what kind of politics is possible
in the country that has it. It is a work of argument, not a measurement, and reading it well is a
different skill from reading a standard.

%%FIG rf08-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book, its author, its publisher and its year, two diagrams side by side of a concentrated coal system and a dispersed oil system, a chain of reasoning drawn in four steps, a boxed paragraph headed what kind of claim this is, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: an argument about the shape of an energy system**
> **Source: Timothy Mitchell, *Carbon Democracy: Political Power in the Age of Oil*, Verso, **2011**.
> Printed here in our own words. **This is a work of political argument and history. It is contested,
> and this note says so more than once.**
>
> **What the book argues.** That the political arrangements we call modern democracy, in the countries
> where they first took hold, were shaped in part by the **material properties of coal** — and that the
> shift to **oil** altered those properties, with political consequences.
>
> **The coal half of the argument, in four steps.**
>
> **One.** Coal was produced by very large workforces, concentrated underground in a small number of
> places, doing work that could not be closely supervised and that required them to rely on each other
> for their lives.
> **Two.** Coal moved along **narrow, fixed channels**: rail lines, canals, specific loading points.
> Energy for an entire industrial economy passed through a small number of physical bottlenecks.
> **Three.** That combination gave those workforces an unusual capacity: **by stopping work, or by
> stopping a flow at a chokepoint, they could halt an entire economy.**
> **Four.** On this account, that capacity is part of how mass political rights — the vote, union
> recognition, limits on hours, pensions, welfare provision — came to be conceded in those countries.
> Not granted from above out of principle, but won by people who had become able to interrupt
> something.
>
> **The oil half.** Oil, as a physical system, is different in ways that matter for this argument. It
> is produced by **far smaller workforces**. It flows through **pipelines** and across **oceans**,
> along routes that can be re-routed. It is loaded at many ports onto many ships. The result is that
> the same capacity to interrupt does not exist in the same way: there are fewer people and more
> alternatives. **On this account, the shift from coal to oil weakened a particular kind of leverage**,
> and the author traces consequences of that, including the ways in which markets, scarcity and
> economic management were organized around the new system.
>
> **WHAT KIND OF CLAIM THIS IS, AND IT MATTERS MORE HERE THAN ANYWHERE ELSE IN THIS BOOK**
> **It is an argument, not a measurement.** There is no regression. There is a chain of reasoning
> supported by historical material. It is tested by asking whether the steps follow and whether the
> cases fit, not by checking a coefficient.
>
> **It is contested, in print, by historians and political scientists.** Objections include: that the
> account gives too much weight to material form and not enough to ideas, movements and institutions;
> that the history of democratic expansion is more varied than the argument allows; that oil workers
> have in fact exercised considerable leverage in some countries and periods; and that the argument
> reads too much continuity into a long and uneven history. Those are serious objections from serious
> people.
>
> **It is not the claim it is usually reported as making.** It is **not** that oil causes
> dictatorship. It is **not** that coal produces democracy. It is a claim about **capacities** — what
> particular people, in particular physical systems, became able to do — and about consequences nobody
> intended.
>
> **It is not predictive.** It does not tell you what any country's politics will be, and it was never
> offered as a forecast.
>
> **And the honest reason it is in this course is not that it is right.** It is that most people who
> work in this industry will at some point be handed an argument of this kind — in a newspaper, a
> speech, a report, or a conversation at a wedding — and the professional skill is to be able to state
> it **at its strongest**, say what kind of claim it is, name one objection, and only then say what you
> think. **That sequence is the unit.**
>
> *Our summary of a published book, named with its author, publisher and year, and identified as an
> argument. Read it, and read one review that disagrees with it.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **When somebody hands you an argument, do four things in order.** State it **at its strongest**, in
> two sentences, better than they did. Say **what kind of claim** it is. Name **one published
> objection**. Then say what **you** think, with a hedge in it. **That order is the whole skill**, and
> the first step is the one people skip, because improving an argument you are about to disagree with
> feels like losing ground. It is the opposite: nobody can dismiss you as having misunderstood, and
> the conversation stops being about whether you read it properly.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> People who work in this industry are handed arguments about it constantly, usually by somebody who
> has read a headline, and the two standard responses are both bad. The first is to dismiss the whole
> field — *that is just politics* — which concedes the argument to whoever made it. The second is to
> accept a summary and feel vaguely guilty, which is worse, because a vague guilt produces no action
> and no answer. The professional response is the four-step one, and it has a side effect worth
> noticing: once you can state an argument better than the person who handed it to you, you discover
> which parts of it you actually agree with. In this case most people in this industry, asked
> carefully, find they agree with more of it than they expected and less than it is usually reported
> as saying.
> **Ask your group: what argument about this industry are you asked about most, and what do you

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf08-f16 | A desk in the evening. A book lies open with four markers in it, beside a printed review that disagrees with it, a circulated one-sentence summary with no author, a four-step list with the fourth step circled and a question mark beside it, a map with pipeline and shipping routes, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A summary of an argument has gone to forty people.** It makes a point that may well be worth making.
It has no author on it, it overstates the argument in one sentence, and it draws a conclusion about a
country that the book itself does not draw.

The book is serious. The objections to it are serious. The person who circulated the summary meant
well and will be embarrassed.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 What do you send to the forty people?
2 The conclusion may be right and the summary wrong. Does the summary matter?
3 What would you say to the person who sent it?
4 Which step of the argument would you question, and on what grounds?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 9 is a model from 1931 whose assumptions
are almost never met, and which you cannot think about this industry without.
