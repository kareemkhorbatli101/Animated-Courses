# Unit 12 · Can It Be Escaped?
%%SUB Review · the last unit of the last book%%
%%GOALS

Eleven units, eleven documents, and one question underneath all of them. The last book of this series
ends where a language course has no business going: with you saying what you think, in English, with
reasons, about something nobody has settled.

The good news is that this is the easiest unit in the book to do well and the hardest to do
confidently, and the difference between those two things is the whole point.

%%FIG rf12-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the volume, its three editors, its publisher and its year, a diagram of five sequential decisions, a table of what the volume argues is decidable and what is not, a boxed paragraph headed what a book of essays cannot do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the last document in the course**
> **Source: *Escaping the Resource Curse*, edited by Macartan Humphreys, Jeffrey D. Sachs and Joseph E.
> Stiglitz, Columbia University Press, **2007**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What it is.** An edited volume: chapters by different authors on how resource wealth is actually
> handled, and on what can be done differently. It comes after a decade in which the resource curse
> literature of Unit 7 had become widely known, and its title is a deliberate reply to it.
>
> **The central claim, and it is a claim about agency.** The outcomes associated with resource wealth
> are **not destiny**. They are the result of **decisions**, the decisions can be identified, and most
> of them are made in a **sequence** in which each one constrains the next. That is why this volume and
> the charter in Unit 11 fit together: one is a framework, the other is the argument for why a
> framework is the right shape.
>
> **The decisions it concentrates on.**
>
> **How rights are awarded.** Several chapters argue for **competition** — auctions and transparent
> allocation — on the ground that a negotiated award depends on the state having information and
> expertise it usually does not have, while a well-designed competitive process extracts value without
> requiring either.
>
> **How the fiscal system is designed.** The arithmetic of Unit 1, with attention to **robustness**: a
> system that produces a reasonable outcome across a wide range of prices is worth more than one that
> is optimal at a price nobody can forecast.
>
> **How revenue is managed.** Volatility, saving, smoothing, and the design of funds — with the
> repeated observation that **a rule without an institution to enforce it does not survive a
> downturn**.
>
> **How it is spent.** Public investment, absorptive capacity, and the uncomfortable finding that
> spending capacity is often the binding constraint rather than money.
>
> **And who can see any of it.** Transparency, scrutiny, and the role of a press and a parliament —
> with the qualification of Unit 6: **disclosure is a precondition and not the thing itself**.
>
> **WHAT A BOOK OF ESSAYS CANNOT DO**
> **It is not a single argument.** It is a collection, and the chapters do not fully agree with each
> other. Reading it as one position is a misreading.
>
> **It does not settle the empirical question.** Unit 7's argument is still an argument after this
> book. What this volume adds is that **it may not matter as much as it seems**: if outcomes depend on
> decisions, then the right response to a contested correlation is to improve the decisions, whichever
> way the correlation eventually falls.
>
> **Its authors have a position**, stated openly: that better institutions, more competition and more
> transparency produce better outcomes. That is argued for in the book rather than assumed, and it is
> contested by others.
>
> **And it cannot make the sequence available to you.** The volume is clearest about decisions taken
> before production starts. A reader thirty years into production has most of them behind them, and
> the honest version of the advice for that reader is smaller and less satisfying: fix what is still
> open, document what is not, and do not pretend the earlier decisions can be unmade.
>
> **SO HERE IS THE QUESTION THIS COURSE ENDS WITH**
> Not *is this industry good or bad for a country*. That question has no answer at the level at which
> it is usually asked.
>
> The question is: **of the decisions that are still open to you — in your job, this year — which ones
> affect whether the money and the barrels and the land leave anything behind?** Six books of this
> course have been an attempt to make that question answerable by somebody who is not a minister and
> not an economist: a figure with its definition attached, an obligation with a date, a permit with a
> conversation behind it, a sample taken before anybody argues, a payment declined and recorded, a
> number nobody can reproduce challenged in a sentence, and an estimate published badly rather than
> not at all.
>
> **None of that escapes anything on its own. All of it is available on Monday.**
>
> *Our summary of a published edited volume, named with its editors, publisher and year. Read two
> chapters that disagree with each other, and then write down what you think, with a hedge in it and a
> reason.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> This series has spent six books on a single habit: say what you know, say how you know it, and say
> what you do not know, in that order. It is not a style preference. In this industry the people who
> can do it are listened to for thirty years and the people who cannot are listened to until the first

> than somebody who has read less, and in a short meeting that will lose you the room. The compensation

> This industry has been a net benefit to the country, as the development of the last fifty years

> **Answer 2**
> **The question, as usually asked, has no answer at the level it is asked at**, so I will answer a
