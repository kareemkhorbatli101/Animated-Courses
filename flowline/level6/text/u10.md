Everything else in this book is uncertain: prices, growth, politics, whether a well finds anything.
This unit is about the only thing in the whole series that is **certain**. Every well will one day be
plugged, every pipeline emptied, every structure removed, and somebody will pay for it. That bill is
the most reliably ignored number in the industry.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rf10-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the decision, its number, the commission that adopted it and its year, a diagram of a prohibition with four narrow derogation categories beside it, a table of the four stages of decommissioning, a boxed paragraph headed what this decision does not cover, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the steel at the end**
> **Source: **OSPAR Decision 98/3** on the Disposal of Disused Offshore Installations, adopted by the
> **OSPAR Commission**, **1998**. Printed here in our own words. **It applies to the North-East
> Atlantic maritime area and does not apply here.**
>
> **What it does.** It establishes, for the maritime area it covers, that the **dumping** of disused
> offshore installations, and the **leaving of them wholly or partly in place**, **is prohibited**.
> That is the rule, and the structure of the rule is the lesson: it starts from a **prohibition** and
> then allows narrow, procedural exceptions, rather than starting from a case-by-case assessment.
>
> **The derogations.** A Contracting Party may, **by way of derogation**, permit something to remain —
> but only for specified categories and only after a process. The categories include very large
> **steel** installations placed before a stated date, **concrete** installations of certain kinds, and
> installations where **exceptional and unforeseen circumstances** arising from structural damage or
> deterioration make removal unsafe. The **footings** of the largest steel structures may be
> considered.
>
> **And the process is the substance.** A derogation requires an **assessment** of the specific
> installation, **consultation with the other Contracting Parties**, and the issue of a **permit**
> stating the conditions. Derogations granted are **recorded**. The effect is that leaving something in
> place is possible, visible, documented and comparatively rare — which is a very different regime
> from one in which each case is simply judged on its merits.
>
> **Why it was adopted in that form.** The Decision followed a period of substantial public
> controversy in the mid-1990s about a proposal to dispose of one installation in deep water. The
> controversy is a matter of public record; what matters here is the drafting consequence. The
> Commission did not respond by writing a better assessment method. It responded by **reversing the
> default** — and that is a choice available to anybody writing an obligation about anything.
>
> **The four stages of the work itself**, offshore or onshore, are the same in principle: **plug the
> wells**; **clean and make safe**; **remove**; **restore and verify**. And then **monitor**, because
> a plugged well is a set of barriers and barriers have a service life.
>
> **WHAT THIS DECISION DOES NOT COVER**
> **It is regional.** It binds its Contracting Parties in its maritime area. **It does not apply
> here**, and onshore obligations — wells, flowlines, stations, land restoration — come from national
> law and from contracts.
>
> **It does not settle the environmental comparison.** There are circumstances in which a structure
> left in place provides habitat, and in which complete removal does considerable damage and consumes
> considerable energy. That argument exists and is conducted seriously in other regions. This Decision
> takes a **presumption against leaving in place** as a matter of policy, and it does not claim to have
> resolved every case on scientific grounds.
>
> **It says nothing about who pays.** And that, for everybody reading this in a producing country, is
> the question that matters most.
>
> **SO HERE IS THE QUESTION THE DECISION DOES NOT ASK**
> Decommissioning costs are, under very many agreements, **recoverable costs**. They come out of
> production **before** profit oil is divided. That means the fiscal system decides who actually bears
> them. **If the state's share of profits is high, the state bears most of the decommissioning bill**,
> even though every invoice is paid by a company.
>
> Which produces the central problem: **the obligation falls due at the end of a field's life, when
> there is little or no production left to recover it from.** A cost that is recoverable in principle
> and unrecoverable in practice lands somewhere, and where it lands depends on security
> arrangements, on residual liability provisions, and on whether the companies that incurred it still
> exist.
>
> *Our summary of a published regional decision, named with its number, its commission and its year.
> It does not apply here. Read your own national requirements and your own abandonment clause, and
> then ask who would pay.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Publish a bad estimate.** Plus or minus half, with its basis on the page, is one of the most useful
> documents an operating company can produce, and the reason is purely practical: a wide number
> becomes an argument, and an argument becomes a budget line, and a missing number stays missing for
> twenty years. **And do the recovery arithmetic.** Three sentences: the cost is recoverable; recoverable
> costs come out before the split; therefore at our take the state bears most of it. Almost nobody on
> either side of the table has written those three sentences down, and they change what the
> conversation is about.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Decommissioning is the one subject in this industry where every incentive points the same way: away
> from it. The cost is large, the benefit is invisible, the obligation falls on a future management, a
> future government and sometimes a future company that does not exist yet. Nobody has ever been
> promoted for publishing a decommissioning estimate. And unlike almost everything else in this book,
> it is not contested: no reasonable person argues that the wells will not need plugging. The
> obstacle is purely that the first person to write a number down becomes responsible for a problem
> that was previously nobody's. Which is why the practical advice in this unit is so modest and so

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf10-f16 | A desk in the evening. A first abandonment estimate lies open with a plus or minus fifty per cent range across it, beside a two-paragraph abandonment clause, a fiscal waterfall with a cost recovery line circled, a photograph of a cut wellhead below ground level, a note reading sixty years with a question mark, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A field has twenty years left.** A hundred and forty wells, sixty kilometers of flowline and two
stations will all have to be dealt with. Nobody disputes any of that, and nobody has ever written down
what it will cost.

The cost is recoverable under the contract, so most of it is borne by the state after the fiscal
split. It falls due when production is nearly over, when there is almost nothing left to recover it
from. The agreement has no fund, no methodology, no security requirement and no residual liability
provision. A cement barrier has a service life, and nobody has asked about year sixty.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Who will pay for this?
2 Everything above is known and none of it is in anybody's objectives. Why?
3 A bad estimate is attackable. What does publishing one actually achieve?
4 What would you put in the agreement at the next review, in one clause?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 11 is twelve recommendations covering
everything in this book, in order, and the question of where a country that cannot do all twelve
should start.
