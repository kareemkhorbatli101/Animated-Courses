%%SUB A result from 1995, forty years of objections, and a measure that may be the outcome%%

There is a sentence circulating in a ministry paper: **resource-rich countries grow more slowly**. It
comes from real research, by careful people, and it has been enormously influential. It is also forty
years into an argument that is not settled, and the most serious objection to it is about the
measurement rather than the politics.

%%FIG rf07-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the book and its author and year and the working paper with its authors, number and year, a scatter plot with a line, a five-row table of mechanisms, a boxed paragraph headed four objections, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the biggest claim in this subject, and the argument about it**
> **Sources: Richard M. Auty, *Sustaining Development in Mineral Economies: The Resource Curse
> Thesis*, Routledge, **1993**; and Jeffrey D. Sachs and Andrew M. Warner, "Natural Resource Abundance
> and Economic Growth", **National Bureau of Economic Research Working Paper 5398**, **1995**. Printed
> here in our own words.**
>
> **The claim.** The phrase *resource curse* comes from the 1993 book. The result that made it famous
> comes from the 1995 working paper: across a large sample of countries over roughly two decades,
> economies with a **higher share of natural resource exports in gross domestic product** grew more
> slowly, and the relationship survived controlling for a range of other factors — initial income,
> trade openness, investment rates and others.
>
> **That is a serious piece of work and it should be stated fairly before it is argued with.** It was
> not a slogan. It was a measured association, published with its method, which is why it has been
> argued about productively for thirty years.
>
> **The proposed mechanisms.** The literature offers five main explanations, and they are
> explanations, not findings.
>
> | Mechanism | The account |
> |---|---|
> | **real exchange rate appreciation** | export earnings strengthen the currency; other tradable sectors become uncompetitive and shrink |
> | **volatility** | resource revenue swings hard; expenditure built on good years must be cut in bad ones, and cutting is more damaging than not having spent |
> | **rent-seeking** | a large, concentrated, capturable revenue stream rewards competing for a share of it over producing anything |
> | **human capital** | where income flows from a resource, the perceived return to education falls |
> | **debt** | borrowing against expected revenue transfers a good year's optimism into a bad year's obligation |
>
> **FOUR OBJECTIONS, AND THEY ARE NOT FROM THE INDUSTRY**
> **One: the measure may be partly the outcome.** Resource exports **as a share of the economy** is a
> ratio. A country that develops other sectors sees that share fall; a country that develops nothing
> sees it rise. So the variable may be measuring **failure at everything else** rather than resource
> endowment. When later researchers used measures of what is actually **in the ground** — subsoil
> wealth per person — several found the relationship **weakened or reversed**. This is the most serious
> objection and it is a measurement objection.
>
> **Two: the period.** The original work covered a particular span of decades with a particular price
> history. Extending or shifting the window changes results, which is a general problem with
> cross-country work and not a criticism of the authors.
>
> **Three: institutions appear to decide the sign.** A substantial body of later work argues that the
> effect depends on the quality of institutions: where institutions reward production, resource wealth
> helps, and where they reward capture, it harms. On that account there is no curse and no blessing;
> there is a multiplier.
>
> **Four: the counterexamples are not marginal.** Several resource-rich countries have sustained
> growth, built funds, diversified and maintained institutions over decades. A general claim has to
> account for them, and *they are exceptions* is not an account.
>
> **AND THE METHOD ITSELF**
> Cross-country regression establishes **association**, not **direction**. It cannot easily tell you
> whether resources slowed growth, or whether the things that slow growth also make an economy look
> resource-dependent, or whether a third factor produces both. The authors knew this. Everybody in the
> field knows it. It gets lost the moment the result is summarized in a sentence.
>
> **What survives all of this, and it is not small.** Nobody seriously disputes that resource revenue
> is **volatile**, that volatility is **damaging** when budgets are built on peaks, that large
> concentrated rents **can** be captured, or that these are **manageable** with instruments that
> exist: stabilization and savings funds, fiscal rules, publication, and deliberate investment in
> capability. **The curse is contested. The volatility is arithmetic.**
>
> *Our summary of a published book and a published working paper, named with their authors, publishers
> and years. Read them, and read two of the objections, and then decide what you think.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **When you cite this literature, cite two things: the result and one objection.** It takes one extra
> sentence and it is the difference between being read as informed and being read as repeating
> something. **And separate the contested part from the arithmetic part.** Whether resources cause
> slower growth is argued about by serious people. Whether resource revenue is volatile is not argued
> about by anybody, and almost every practical instrument in this field — a stabilization rule, a
> savings fund, a fiscal anchor — is aimed at the part nobody disputes. **Argue about the curse in the
> evening. Work on the volatility on Monday.**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf07-f16 | A desk in the evening. A ministry paper lies open with one bold sentence and one footnote from 1995, beside two journal articles arguing the opposite way, a book from 1993, two bank statements showing a threefold swing in receipts, a draft savings rule, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A sentence is in a ministry paper.** It is drawn from real research, it cites its source, and the
policy it supports — diversification — is almost certainly sensible. The person who wrote it is not
careless and the research is not bad.

The measure behind it may be partly the outcome it is trying to explain. Later studies using a
different measure found something different. The institutions literature says the sign depends on the
country. And the writer's own country has bank statements showing receipts swinging by a factor of
three, twice, which nobody disputes at all.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Should the sentence stay in the paper?
2 What would you add, in one sentence?
3 The policy may be right and the evidence for it weak. What do you do?
4 Which would persuade a finance minister: the regression, or the bank statements?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 8 is a much larger claim — that the kind
of energy a country has shapes the kind of state it becomes — and it is an argument rather than a
measurement.
