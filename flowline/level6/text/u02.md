### 2 · Read

%%FIG rf02-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the chapter, its author, the volume it appears in, the two organizations that published it and the year, a six-part diagram of an agreement, a table of clauses with who each one protects, a boxed paragraph headed what a primer is not, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: what is actually in a petroleum agreement**
> **Source: Jenik Radon, "The ABCs of Petroleum Contracts: License-Concession Agreements, Joint
> Ventures, and Production-sharing Agreements", a chapter in *Covering Oil: A Reporter's Guide to
> Energy and Development*, published by the Open Society Institute and the Revenue Watch Institute,
> **2005**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What the document is, and why that matters.** It is a **primer**, written to allow somebody who is
> not a lawyer — originally a journalist — to pick up a petroleum agreement and understand what they
> are looking at. That is an unusual thing to write and it makes it the clearest short account
> available. It was published as part of a volume intended to help people report on this industry,
> and it is written from the position that these documents **should be readable by the public**. You
> should know that about it before you read it.
>
> **The agreement has six parts, and they are not equal in length or in trouble.**
>
> **The parties and the area.** Who the state is contracting with, and exactly where, usually by
> coordinates. The area is a definition, and areas are argued about.
>
> **The phases.** An **exploration period** of some years, often extendable. At the end of it, part of
> the area is **relinquished** — given back — and the contractor keeps only what it has worked up. If
> a discovery is declared commercial, a **development** phase and then a **production period**, which
> is the long one.
>
> **The work obligations.** The **minimum work program**: so many kilometers of seismic, so many
> **commitment wells**, so much spend, by stated dates. This is what the state is really buying in an
> exploration agreement, and it is usually what the competitive bidding was about.
>
> **The fiscal terms.** Unit 1. Typically the shortest part of the document that anybody argues
> about, and the only part most people have heard of.
>
> **The administration.** Committees, approvals, work program and budget procedures, reporting, and
> **audit rights** — who may come and look at the books, how far back, and with how much notice.
>
> **The protections and the exits.** And this is the part that decides disputes.
>
> **The four clauses that decide what happens when it goes wrong.**
>
> **Stabilization.** A clause protecting the agreed terms against subsequent changes in the host
> state's law — or requiring the parties to restore the economic balance if the law changes. It is the
> most politically contested clause in the industry, because it constrains a sovereign legislature by
> contract, and because a government that signed one twenty years ago may now be unable to do what its
> voters want.
>
> **Governing law and dispute resolution.** Which law the agreement is read under, and where a dispute
> goes: usually **international arbitration** rather than the national courts. Who appoints the
> arbitrators, where they sit, and in what language are not small details.
>
> **Confidentiality.** Whether the agreement itself may be published. Historically, very many could
> not be. This has changed in a number of countries, in part because of documents like this one.
>
> **Assignment.** Whether a party may transfer its interest, to whom, with whose consent, and whether
> the state has a right of first refusal. The company you negotiated with may not be the company you
> end up with.
>
> **And the ones everybody forgets.** **Force majeure** — what excuses performance. A **domestic
> supply obligation** — a volume that must be sold into the local market, often at a stated price
> basis. **Training and employment obligations**, usually with a number in them and almost never
> reported against. And **abandonment**, which in older agreements is two paragraphs and in newer ones
> is a funded obligation.
>
> **WHAT A PRIMER IS NOT**
> **It is not a legal text and does not pretend to be.** It explains categories. The operative words
> in any particular agreement are the operative words in that agreement.
>
> **It describes practice as at 2005.** Practice has moved: more contracts are published now, and
> abandonment and transparency provisions are handled differently.
>
> **It is written from a position.** The volume it appears in was published to support public
> scrutiny of this industry. That is a legitimate position, it is stated openly, and it is not the
> same as neutrality.
>
> **And no agreement is typical.** Every one is the record of one negotiation between particular
> parties at a particular moment.
>
> *Our summary of a published chapter, named with its author, its volume, its publishers and its year.
> Read it, and then go and find out whether the agreement governing the field you work on is public.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Build an obligation register and put a date against every row.** Clause number, what it obliges,
> when it falls due, who owns it. An agreement is not a document; it is a list of dated promises, and
> an obligation with no date is an obligation that will be discovered by somebody else. **And when you
> report on a clause, quote its number.** *Clause 4.3 requires relinquishment of half the area in
> March* is a sentence somebody can check in ninety seconds. *The contract requires us to give back
> some of the area at some point* is a sentence that will be ignored.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> In a great many countries these agreements have historically been confidential, and the arguments
> for that were not frivolous: commercially sensitive terms, bidding positions, the negotiating
> leverage of a state in the next round. The consequence, though, was that the people most affected
> by an agreement — including nearly everybody who works under one — could not read it. The document
> in this unit was written in a push against that, and a number of countries now publish. What has
> not changed is the asymmetry inside a company: the agreement is held by legal and commercial
> functions, the obligations fall on operations, and the operations people have usually never seen the
> clause that governs what they do. An obligation register is the cheapest possible fix, and almost
> nobody has one.
> **Ask your group: who in your organization has read the agreement, and who has to comply with it?**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf02-f16 | A desk in the evening. A bound agreement lies open at clause four point three, beside an obligation register with three rows highlighted, a map of a contract area with no dotted line drawn on it yet, a calendar with a date circled eighteen months ahead, a training record file with four years missing, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**An agreement was signed nine years ago.** It is a competent document, negotiated by competent
people, and the company has complied with everything anybody has asked about.

In eighteen months half the contract area must be given back, and the choice of which half is the
company's. Choosing well requires about two years of appraisal work that has not started. A training
obligation with a number in it has no report against it for four years. The abandonment clause is two
paragraphs, and nobody holds an estimate.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Is the company compliant?
2 Nobody has broken anything. What is actually missing?
3 The relinquishment date was known for nine years. How does a known date stay out of a calendar?
4 What would your register look like, and who would own it?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 3 is the other agreement — the one
between the companies — and what happens when a partner will not pay.
