Unit 2 was the agreement between a company and a state. This unit is the other one: the agreement
between the companies themselves. Nobody outside the industry has heard of it, it is usually longer
than the first one, and it governs what happens on the worst day a joint venture has.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rf03-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the model agreement, the association that publishes it and its year, a table of three interests with a pass mark, a flow of a cash call through to default, a boxed paragraph headed what a model form is not, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: the agreement between the companies**
> **Source: the **Model International Operating Agreement** published by the **Association of
> International Petroleum Negotiators**, **2012** form. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What it is for.** When two or more companies hold an interest in the same contract area, they need
> an agreement between **themselves**, separate from the agreement with the state. It is called a
> **joint operating agreement**, and this is the most widely used model for an international one.
>
> **The operator and the non-operators.** One party is appointed **operator** and actually does the
> work: it contracts, it hires, it drills, it operates, and it issues the bills. The others are
> **non-operators**: they pay their share and they vote. The operator is not the owner of the venture
> and it is not the boss of the others; it is the party that acts, under instructions from a committee.
>
> **Participating interests.** Each party holds a percentage. It pays that percentage of the costs, it
> takes that percentage of the production, and it votes with that percentage.
>
> **The operating committee and the pass mark.** Decisions are taken by vote, weighted by interest, and
> the agreement states a **pass mark** — a percentage of interests required to approve something. The
> arithmetic of the pass mark against the actual interests decides who really controls the venture,
> and it is often not what the headline percentages suggest. A party holding less than half can be in
> every winning combination, and two parties holding a majority between them can be unable to pass
> anything.
>
> **Work programs, budgets and cash calls.** The committee approves an annual **work program and
> budget**. The operator then issues **cash calls** — demands for each party's share, in advance, to a
> stated account by a stated date. The operator is spending other people's money and must have it
> before it spends.
>
> **Default.** A party that does not pay is in **default**. The procedure escalates: interest accrues,
> a notice is served, the defaulting party's rights are suspended — it loses its vote and its
> entitlement to production — the other parties may cover the shortfall in proportion, and ultimately
> the interest may be **forfeited**. The provisions are severe on purpose, because one party's failure
> to pay stops the work for everybody.
>
> **Sole risk and non-consent.** If the committee will not approve something, a party or parties may be
> permitted to do it **at their own risk and cost**. Those who did not participate have no share in the
> result unless they later buy in on stated, usually penal, terms. This is the mechanism by which a
> minority partner who is certain about a prospect can get a well drilled.
>
> **The operator's liability.** The operator is normally **not liable** to the others for losses caused
> in the conduct of operations **except** in cases of **gross negligence or wilful misconduct**. That
> standard is one of the most heavily negotiated phrases in the industry, and those two terms are
> defined in the agreement itself, because their meaning differs between legal systems.
>
> **Assignment, withdrawal and the area of mutual interest.** A party's right to **sell** its interest,
> and whether the others may **pre-empt** the sale. A party's right to **withdraw**, and what it still
> owes — normally including its share of **decommissioning**. And an **area of mutual interest**: a
> zone around the contract area in which a party that acquires an interest must offer the others a
> share.
>
> **WHAT A MODEL FORM IS NOT**
> **It is not a contract.** It is a **starting point for a negotiation**, published with alternative
> provisions in brackets, precisely because the parties are expected to choose between them. A
> sentence in a model form is a sentence somebody proposed, not a sentence anybody agreed.
>
> **It does not bind the state.** It governs the relationship **among the contractor parties**. The
> state's agreement governs the relationship with the state, and where the two conflict, the state's
> agreement does not simply give way.
>
> **It is a members' publication of an industry association**, prepared by practitioners who negotiate
> these agreements for a living. That is why it is good, and it is also why its defaults tend to
> reflect the concerns of the parties who were in the room.
>
> **And it cannot make a venture work.** Everything in it is a procedure for a disagreement. A joint
> venture in which the parties are using these procedures regularly is a joint venture in trouble,
> whatever the drafting.
>
> *Our summary of a published model agreement, named with its association and its form year. Read the
> form, and then go and work out the pass mark arithmetic for a venture you know.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Do the pass mark arithmetic before any meeting, and write it on one line.** *Pass mark 65; A 40, B
> 35, C 25; A is in every passing combination; B and C together make 60 and cannot pass anything.*
> That one line tells you who you are actually negotiating with. **And when you describe a default,
> name the step.** *Nineteen days overdue, interest accruing, no notice served* is precise, and it is
> very different from *in default*, which to some readers means a few days late and to others means
> about to lose a field.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Joint ventures in this industry are long marriages between organizations that compete everywhere
> else. The same two companies may be partners in one country and opponents in a bid round in the
> next, and the people are the same people: a small professional world in which everybody remembers
> who used a procedure as a weapon. That is why the sharpest provisions in a joint operating agreement
> are so rarely used in full, and why the arithmetic of a pass mark is more important than the drafting
> around it — because once you know who is decisive, the sensible parties stop voting and start
> talking. The failure mode is not aggression. It is a venture in which the procedures are used
> monthly, by people who have stopped trusting each other and are correct not to.
> **Ask your group: who are your partners, and would you want to be in a venture with them again?**

**A1  Look at the picture with a partner. Say eight things you can see.**

**A2  Find in the picture:** seven combinations listed · a model agreement with bracketed alternatives
· an area of mutual interest on a map · a sole risk election form.

**A3  Ask your partner three questions about the picture using *is entitled to* and *at its sole risk
and expense*. Then ask them which party they would rather be.**

**A4  Listen to two accountants describe the same venture.** %%AUD FL6-03-16%% Which one names the
step?

### B · Write a team summary

**B1  In your team, write six lines: what a participating interest determines, what an operator is and
is not, the default escalation in order, what sole risk is for, the operator's liability standard, and
the one line of pass mark arithmetic you will write before any meeting.**

### C · Report

**C1  One Presenter reads your six lines to another team. That team asks two questions. Answer
them.**

### D · Team project — the combinations

**D1  In your team, invent four parties with four interests and a pass mark. List every possible
combination and mark which pass.**

**D2  Give it to another team and ask them which party they would want to be, and why.**

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf03-f16 | A desk in the evening. A joint venture status note lies open with a cash call nineteen days overdue, beside a voting table with all seven combinations and two marked exactly at the pass mark, a default clause with five numbered steps, an appraisal well proposal, a model agreement open at bracketed alternatives, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A partner is nineteen days late with a cash call.** The agreement entitles the operator to charge
interest, to serve a notice, and in time to suspend that partner's rights and eventually to forfeit
its interest. Every step is lawful and every step is drafted clearly.

The vote next week passes whether or not that partner is suspended. The three companies expect to hold
this field together for about twenty years. The partner's finance function has not been telephoned.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 What would you do this week?
2 What would you write in the board paper, in one sentence?
3 The agreement allows a suspension. What would using it cost, and when would you find out?
4 What would you have negotiated differently if you were the twenty-five per cent party?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 4 is the payment somebody will eventually
ask one of you for, and the only safe thing to do about it.
