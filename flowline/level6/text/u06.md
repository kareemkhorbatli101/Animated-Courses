# Unit 6 · What May Be Published
%%SUB Two figures for one payment, and a difference that is only a date%%
%%GOALS

The company says it paid a figure. The government says it received a different one. Nobody has stolen
anything: one of them counted in December and the other in January. This unit is about the standard
that puts those two figures on the same page, what it can establish, and the much larger thing it
cannot.

### 2 · Read

%%FIG rf06-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the standard, the initiative that publishes it and its edition year, a diagram of company payments and government receipts meeting at an administrator, a table of what is disclosed, a boxed paragraph headed what disclosure does not do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: what may be published, and who decides**
> **Source: the **EITI Standard**, published by the **Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative**,
> **2023** edition. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What it is.** A standard that a **country** chooses to implement. A country that does so
> establishes a **multi-stakeholder group** — government, companies and civil society, sitting
> together — and publishes, every year, what companies paid the state and what the state says it
> received, reconciled by an **independent administrator**, together with a substantial amount of
> contextual information.
>
> **The core idea is very simple and was once radical.** A payment has two ends. Publish both, from
> both sides, and let anybody compare them.
>
> **What gets disclosed.** Beyond the payments themselves, the standard requires a country to publish:
> the **legal and fiscal framework**; **licenses** and how they were awarded; **contracts**, with
> contract transparency now a requirement for agreements entered into from a stated date; the
> **beneficial ownership** of companies holding licenses — who actually owns them; **state
> participation** and the role of state-owned enterprises; **production and export** data;
> **revenue allocations**, including **transfers to subnational governments**; and social and
> environmental payments. More recent editions also address energy transition and gender
> disaggregation.
>
> **The reconciliation, and what it actually finds.** Companies complete a template. Government
> completes one. The administrator compares them line by line and publishes the matches and the
> differences.
>
> **Most differences are boring, and that is the single most useful thing to know about this
> process.** They are caused by **timing** — a payment made before a cut-off date and received after
> it — by **exchange rates**, by one side reporting **gross** and the other **net**, and by
> classification: the same payment recorded under two different headings. A reconciliation that finds
> a small number of timing differences and nothing else is a good result, and it reads as dull.
>
> **The differences that cannot be explained by any of those are the ones worth attention**, and the
> report states them as **unreconciled differences**, without asserting a cause, because asserting a
> cause is not what the exercise does.
>
> **Materiality.** The group agrees a **threshold**: payments below a stated size are not reported
> separately. That is a practical necessity — otherwise the exercise collapses under small items — and
> it is also a decision about what becomes invisible. Where the threshold is set, and who argued for
> that level, is a legitimate question about any report.
>
> **Validation.** The initiative assesses each implementing country's progress against the
> requirements and publishes the assessment. A country can be found to have made inadequate progress,
> and that is a public finding.
>
> **WHAT DISCLOSURE DOES NOT DO**
> **It is voluntary at the country level.** A country chooses to implement, and can leave. Companies
> operating in an implementing country report because the country requires it.
>
> **It covers what is in scope.** Scope and threshold are agreed by the group. A payment below the
> threshold, or a stream outside the agreed scope, does not appear — and its absence does not mean it
> does not exist.
>
> **A reconciliation is not an audit.** It compares two reported figures. It does not verify either of
> them against underlying records, and it does not investigate.
>
> **The data is old by the time it is published.** Typically a year or two. It is good for patterns and
> for accountability after the fact; it is not a live control.
>
> **It cannot create the third chair.** The process requires civil society to participate freely, and
> where that is difficult the standard says so and the validation records it. A multi-stakeholder
> group with a silent third chair meets the form and not the purpose.
>
> **And here is the big one. Disclosure is not accountability.** Publishing what was paid and received
> tells nobody whether the money was spent well, or spent at all on what it was meant for. That
> requires budget scrutiny, audit institutions, a parliament, courts and a press — none of which this
> standard provides, and all of which it quietly depends on. **Transparency is a precondition. It was
> never the thing itself.**
>
> *Our summary of a published standard, named with its initiative and its edition year. Read the
> standard, and then go and find out whether your own country publishes, and how old the latest data
> is.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **When you report a difference, give the two dates.** *Paid 27 December, received 4 January* is
> eight words and it closes the question before anybody opens it. Without those two dates, a
> difference in a published table will be read by somebody as a missing amount, and you will spend
> three weeks explaining arithmetic. **And when a payment falls below the threshold, say so in the
> same sentence as the total.** A number that is invisible because of a rule is very different from a
> number that is invisible because nobody mentioned it, and only one of those survives being asked
> about.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Twenty years ago almost none of this was public anywhere. The idea that a company would publish what
> it paid a government, and that the government would publish what it received, and that a third party
> would compare the two in a document anybody could download, was treated in much of the industry as
> naive at best. It is now ordinary in a substantial number of countries, which is a genuine change
> and worth saying plainly. What has not followed automatically is the thing it was for. A published
> figure needs somebody with the time, the training and the safety to ask about it — an audit office,
> a committee, a journalist, a district council — and a country can have excellent disclosure and
> almost none of that. The honest position is that transparency turned out to be necessary,
> insufficient, and much easier than the rest.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf06-f16 | A desk in the evening. A reconciliation table lies open with five nil differences and one row showing two dates, beside a reporting pack with an incomplete beneficial ownership entry, a materiality threshold line excluding a district social payment, a report cover dated twenty-two months earlier, a newspaper cutting with the word missing in the headline, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A country publishes. A company reports. An administrator reconciles.** Five of six streams match
exactly, which is a real achievement and did not happen ten years ago. The sixth differs by eight days
across a year end, and both figures are audited and correct.

A newspaper prints the word *missing*. A social payment to a district does not appear anywhere because
it is below the threshold. One beneficial ownership entry is incomplete. The year being discussed
ended twenty-two months ago.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Has transparency worked here?
2 The newspaper is wrong and the table is right. Whose fault is the story?
3 The district payment is invisible because of a rule. Who should know that?
4 Twenty-two months. What would a live control look like, and who would run it?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 7 is the biggest claim in this subject —
that having all of this makes a country poorer — and the forty years of argument about whether it is
true.
