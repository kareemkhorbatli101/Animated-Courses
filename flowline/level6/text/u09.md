A barrel left in the ground is an asset. An asset has to earn a return, or its owner should sell it
and put the money somewhere that does. That one sentence is a paper from 1931, it is almost never
true in the form it is stated, and you cannot think clearly about this industry without it.

%%FIG rf09-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the paper, its author, the journal and its year, a four-step chain of reasoning, a two-column table of assumptions against reality, a boxed paragraph headed what the model abstracts from, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: how fast a finite thing should be used up**
> **Source: Harold Hotelling, "The Economics of Exhaustible Resources", *Journal of Political
> Economy*, **1931**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **The question the paper asks.** Suppose you own a fixed quantity of something that cannot be
> replaced. You can produce it now or later. **What is the right rate?**
>
> **The reasoning, in four steps.**
>
> **One.** A unit left in the ground is an **asset**. It has value and you are choosing to hold it.
> **Two.** Any asset you hold has to earn a **return**, because the alternative is to sell it and put
> the proceeds somewhere that earns one.
> **Three.** The return on a unit left in the ground is the **rise in its net price** — the price it
> will fetch later, less the cost of producing it, compared with the same thing today.
> **Four.** Therefore, if an owner is indifferent between producing now and producing later, the
> **net price must be rising at the rate of interest**. If it rises faster, hold. If it rises more
> slowly, produce now.
>
> **That is the rule**, and it has an elegance that has kept it in every resource economics course for
> ninety years: it turns a question about geology and politics into a comparison between two rates of
> return.
>
> **What the model assumes, and the paper says so.** A known, fixed stock. No new discoveries. No
> technical change. Extraction cost that does not change as the stock depletes. Perfect capital
> markets. A known demand curve. No uncertainty. No political risk. An owner maximizing the present
> value of the whole stream.
>
> **WHAT THE MODEL ABSTRACTS FROM, AND WHY IT MATTERS HERE**
> **The stock is not fixed.** Reserves are an economic category, not a geological one: they grow when
> prices rise and when technology improves, and they shrink when prices fall. A model that begins with
> a fixed number begins with something that does not exist.
>
> **Prices have not behaved as the rule predicts.** Over long periods, real resource prices have not
> risen smoothly at anything like an interest rate. They have been volatile, and at times they have
> fallen for decades. This is the best-known empirical problem with the rule and it is not in dispute.
>
> **Extraction is constrained by capacity, not only by choice.** You cannot produce a field at any rate
> you like. Wells, facilities, and above all **reservoir behavior** set limits. Producing faster than a
> reservoir will tolerate reduces **ultimate recovery** — you do not get the barrels later, you do not
> get them at all. The model has no reservoir in it.
>
> **And the model is silent on who owns it.** It describes an owner maximizing value. It says nothing
> about a state, a generation not yet born, a budget deficit this year, or a government with four years
> in office and a thirty-year asset.
>
> **So why is it indispensable?**
>
> Because step one survives all of it. **A barrel produced this year is a barrel that cannot be
> produced in twenty years**, and almost every argument about production rates in every
> resource-producing country is an argument about that sentence without anybody saying it. The model
> is not a prediction and was not offered as one. It is a **benchmark**: it tells you what question you
> are actually answering when you decide a rate, and it tells you that *produce as fast as possible*
> is a decision about the future and not merely a decision about this year.
>
> **The honest summary.** The rule is almost never satisfied. The question it poses cannot be avoided.
> Those two sentences are both true, and holding both of them is what it means to use a model properly.
>
> *Our summary of a published paper, named with its author, journal and year. Read the paper — it is
> short — and then ask what rate your own field is producing at, and who chose it.*

### 📝 ON THE JOB

> **Never give a revenue figure for an acceleration without a recovery figure beside it.** Two numbers
> and two periods: *plus this much in years one to three; minus this much over twenty-five years*.
> With both, the decision is a decision. With one, it is an announcement. **And say the word
> *lost*.** When a reservoir is produced too fast, the barrels are not postponed; a proportion of them
> never come out at all. *Deferred* is the word people reach for because it sounds recoverable, and
> using it accurately is the single most useful thing a reservoir engineer can do in a budget meeting.

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Every producing country in the world has had this argument, and the structure of it is almost always
> the same: a real need now, a cost later, a political term shorter than a reservoir, and a technical
> person who can see the whole of it and sits four levels below the decision. What makes it hard is
> that the people asking to accelerate are usually not doing anything wrong — a hospital this year is
> a real hospital — and the people resisting cannot promise that the barrels saved will be well used
> in 2048 either. The only defensible professional position is not to win the argument. It is to make
> sure the comparison is visible, in both periods, in writing, before the decision; and then to
> accept the decision and record what it was.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf09-f16 | A desk in the evening. An acceleration proposal lies open with one revenue figure on its front page, beside an attachment giving a probable reduction in ultimate recovery, two production profiles on one sheet, a reservoir pressure chart, a budget page showing a real shortfall this year, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A budget shortfall is real.** The hospital it would fund is real. The people asking to accelerate
production are not doing anything improper, and the request is lawful, technically feasible and
supported by a competent revenue forecast.

Producing faster will probably reduce ultimate recovery, by an amount that is uncertain in size and
not in direction. A proportion of the barrels will not be produced later; they will not be produced at
all. The gain falls inside this budget period. The cost falls on whoever is operating the field in
twenty years.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 Would you approve it?
2 What has to be on the front page before anybody can decide honestly?
3 A model from 1931 with six false assumptions framed this question. What did it get right?
4 Who, in your own organization, could put a twenty-five-year figure in front of a decision?

**There is no answer printed at the back of this book.** Unit 10 is the other end of the same horizon:
what you do with the steel when there is nothing left to produce.
