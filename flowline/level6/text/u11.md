# Unit 11 · Twelve Precepts
%%SUB A chain of decisions, and where to start when you cannot do all of it%%
%%GOALS
Describe the decision chain
Recommend and sequence
Say what a framework is
Identify what is available now
Say what needs an institution that does not exist
%%

Everything in this book so far has been one document at a time. This unit is the attempt to put all
of it in order: twelve recommendations covering the whole chain, from a discovery to what a country
does with the money. The useful question is not whether the twelve are right. It is which four a
country can actually start on this year.

%%FIG rf11-f5 | A one-page internal note on headed paper: a title, a line giving the charter, the institute that publishes it, its edition and its year, a chain of twelve numbered links, a table grouping the twelve into four stages, a boxed paragraph headed what a framework cannot do, and a source line in small type at the foot.%%

**2A  Read the note. Then answer the questions in section 3.**

> **JAZIRA PETROLEUM OPERATIONS — Mureij Training Center**
> **Note for operators: twelve recommendations, in order**
> **Source: the ***Natural Resource Charter***, second edition **2014**, published by the **Natural
> Resource Governance Institute**. Printed here in our own words.**
>
> **What it is.** Twelve **precepts** — general recommendations — covering the whole sequence of
> decisions a country faces when it has a resource. It is not a standard, not a treaty and not a
> ranking. It is an attempt to set out, in order, what the decisions are and what good practice looks
> like at each one.
>
> **The organizing idea is the chain.** The decisions are **sequential**, and each one **constrains**
> the ones after it. A badly allocated license cannot be fixed by good taxation. A good fiscal system
> cannot survive revenue volatility with no rule. A well-managed fund is wasted by poor spending. The
> charter's claim is that most failures attributed to corruption or bad luck are actually traceable to
> an **earlier decision in the chain** that nobody revisits.
>
> **The twelve, grouped.**
>
> **The foundation.** What the resource is **for** — the development strategy, and the proposition that
> extraction is only worthwhile if citizens are better off. And **accountability**: an informed public,
> with the information it needs to hold decisions to account.
>
> **Getting it out of the ground.** Whether, when and how to **explore**; how **rights** are
> allocated, with competition and disclosed criteria; how it is **taxed**, with a system that is
> robust across prices; the management of **local effects**, environmental and social; and the role,
> mandate and accountability of **state-owned enterprises**.
>
> **Managing the money.** **Volatility** — smoothing, saving, and a published rule rather than a
> discretion; and **spending** — public investment chosen for return and constrained by the economy's
> **absorptive capacity**, because money spent faster than an economy can use it buys very little.
>
> **The wider setting.** The development of the **private sector**, so that something exists beyond the
> resource; the role and conduct of **international companies**; the role of **international standards
> and norms**; and the responsibilities of **home governments and the international community**,
> including on bribery, tax and transparency.
>
> **The most practically useful idea in the whole document is not a precept.** It is the
> recommendation to read the chain **backwards**. Start from what is going wrong — a budget that
> cannot be sustained, a region with nothing to show, a figure nobody can reproduce — and walk up the
> chain to the decision that made it inevitable. That is the diagnostic use, and it is available to
> anybody, in any country, this afternoon.
>
> **WHAT A FRAMEWORK CANNOT DO**
> **It is not law and not a standard.** Nobody is assessed against it and nothing follows from
> ignoring it.
>
> **It cannot sequence itself for you.** It presents twelve recommendations and a country has the
> capacity for a few. Choosing which, in what order, given the institutions that actually exist, is
> the hardest decision in the document and the document does not make it. It can tell you that a
> savings rule without a credible oversight body is a rule that will be suspended in the first bad
> year. It cannot tell you whether to build the body or write the rule first.
>
> **It is written from a position.** The institute that publishes it exists to improve resource
> governance, and the charter reflects a view about transparency, competition and accountability that
> is argued for rather than neutral. That view is stated openly, which is the honest way to hold one.
>
> **And it assumes you can start at the beginning, which almost nobody can.** A country fifty years
> into production has already allocated, already taxed, already signed. For those readers the charter
> is not a plan. It is a **map of where the constraints came from**, which is worth more.
>
> *Our summary of a published framework, named with its institute, edition and year. Read the charter,
> and then color in your own chain: what could start this year, what needs a body that does not
> exist, and what is a political choice.*

### 🌐 CULTURE CHECK

> Frameworks of this kind are read in two bad ways. Governments read them as a report card and get
> defensive; companies read them as somebody else's homework and say nothing. Both responses miss what
> the document is for, which is to show that the thing going wrong today was decided earlier by
> somebody who had no idea. It is also the only document in this book where an operating company is
> itself one of the twelve items — not an observer of the problem, but a link in the chain with its own
> procurement, payment, employment and disclosure decisions. That link requires no permission from
> anybody. Which makes it, in practice, the most neglected of the twelve, and the easiest to point at
> in ten years.

### 🧩 THINK IT THROUGH

%%FIG rf11-f16 | A desk in the evening. A colored chain of twelve links lies open with four green, three amber and five blank, beside a draft national strategy, a savings rule with no formula, an absorptive capacity note with a question mark, a procurement page with ninety days on it, and a glass of tea. A clock shows seven.%%

**A country has been producing for fifty years.** It cannot start at the beginning of the chain:
licenses are awarded, agreements are signed, taxation is in force. The strategy in front of it is
competently drafted and addresses eleven of twelve precepts.

Four of the twelve could be started this year. Three need a body that does not exist. Five are
political choices that nobody in a technical meeting can make. One of the four — the conduct of the
operating companies themselves, including procurement and payment — needs nobody's permission at all,
and nothing has happened on it in nine years.

**Discuss in your group.**

1 What would you start with?
2 The precept that needs nobody's permission is the one nothing has happened on. Why?
3 The five political precepts are the most important. What is a technical person's role there?
4 Which link does your own country's biggest problem point back to?
