# Starting Point · Where the Money Goes
%%SUB Five books of plant, and the question none of them asked%%

You know what a barrel is, how it gets out of the ground, how it moves, what it becomes, and how the
organizations that handle it fail. You do not yet know what happens to the money, and neither does
almost anybody who works on a plant.

This book is about that, and it is the last one.

### 1 · One barrel, four claims on it

%%FIG rf00-f1 | A diagram of one barrel with four arrows leaving it, labeled the cost of getting it out, the share that goes to the state, the share that goes to the companies, and what is left for everything else, with a note that the four are negotiated and not natural.%%

**1A  Look at the four arrows with a partner. Say which one you think is biggest, and say why.**

| Who has a claim | On what basis |
|---|---|
| **whoever spent the money** | costs have to come back before anybody has a profit |
| **the state** | the resource belongs to the country, in almost every system in the world |
| **the companies** | they took the risk of drilling something that might have been empty |
| **everybody else** | roads, schools, a currency, a generation that will not have this barrel |

**1B  Read the four rows aloud, taking turns. Then discuss: why is the second row true in almost every
country and still argued about in every one of them?**

### 2 · The sentence this book exists to teach

%%FIG rf00-f2 | A single sentence set large on a page, reading the terms are not natural facts, they were negotiated, with four small callouts around it reading by whom, when, under what conditions, and with what information.%%

**2A  Look at the four callouts. Say which one is hardest to find out.**

Every number in this book was **negotiated**. A royalty rate, a cost recovery ceiling, a profit share,
a local content target, a decommissioning obligation. None of them is a law of nature, all of them
were argued over by people with opposing interests who were all acting reasonably, and almost all of
them were settled **at a particular moment**, under particular conditions, with particular
information.

**2B  Ask and answer with a partner.** Would you expect terms agreed in a year of low prices to look
different from terms agreed in a year of high ones? Say why.

### 3 · Four things that take decades

%%FIG rf00-f3 | A timeline of about sixty years with four bars across it: exploration and appraisal, development, plateau production, and decline and decommissioning, with a small human figure beneath it labeled a career and a shorter bar beneath that labeled a political term.%%

**3A  Look at the timeline with a partner. Say which bar is longest and which is shortest.**

| What | Roughly how long |
|---|---|
| finding it and proving it | years, sometimes a decade |
| building it | three to six years |
| producing it at plateau | five to fifteen years |
| declining, and then taking it away | decades, and the last part is all cost |
| **a career** | thirty-five years |
| **a political term** | four or five |

**3B  Discuss in threes.** The decisions that matter most in this book are taken on the fourth line and
paid for on the fifth. **Say what that does to the quality of those decisions, and say who would have
to care for it to be otherwise.**

### 4 · What is contested, and said so

%%FIG rf00-f4 | A table of three columns headed the claim, who makes it, and what is disputed, with four rows: resource wealth slows growth, oil shapes political systems, a resource should be depleted on a price path, and transparency improves outcomes.%%

**4A  Read the four claims aloud. Say which one you have heard before.**

| The claim | What is disputed |
|---|---|
| Resource wealth tends to slow economic growth | the measure, the period, the countries, and whether institutions change the answer |
| Oil shapes the political system of the country that has it | it is an argument from history, not a measured finding |
| A finite resource should be depleted along a particular price path | the assumptions are almost never met in practice |
| Publishing payments improves what happens to them | disclosure is not the same as accountability |

**4B  Ask and answer with a partner.** Which of the four would be hardest to test, and what would you
have to measure?

### 5 · What this book will and will not do

**5A  Read the five lines aloud with a partner. Then say which one matters most to you.**

> · It will give you the **English to read a contract, a standard and a paper** and to say what each
> one claims and on what basis.
> · It will give you the **arithmetic** of a fiscal system, well enough to check somebody's figure.
> · It will **not** tell you what the right terms are. Nobody can, and anyone who says otherwise is
> selling something.
> · It will **not** give you a position on whether this industry is good for a country. Two of the
> twelve units are the argument, printed with its objections.
> · It will ask you, in the last unit, what you think. **And it will expect you to say so with the
> word *probably* in it.**

**5B  Say the last line aloud.** Then ask your partner: why should a careful answer be more persuasive
than a confident one, and when is it not?

**Unit 1 is the arithmetic of who gets what**, and it is the only unit in this series where you will
be asked to check somebody else's percentage with a pencil.
