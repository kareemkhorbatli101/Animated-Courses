Three programmes and what happened next

If early differences predict later results, the obvious response is to spend money early. Three large programmes have done so, have been followed for long enough to be judged, and between them illustrate why the question has not been settled by evidence that is now forty years old.

The first was small, intensive and expensive: a few dozen children, daily sessions, trained staff, home visits. Its results are the ones everybody quotes. The children who took part were, decades later, more likely to have finished school, more likely to be employed and less likely to have been arrested, and economists calculated a return of several pounds for every pound spent.

The second was the same idea at national scale. Funding went to thousands of settings, staff were existing nursery workers given additional training, and the daily contact was an hour instead of a morning. Measured gains at the end were real and much smaller, and by the second year of school the difference between participants and others was not statistically detectable.

The third tried to explain the distance between the first two. It ran the intensive model and the diluted model side by side in the same city. The intensive arm reproduced roughly two thirds of the original effect. The diluted arm reproduced almost none of it, and the difference between the arms was larger than the difference between the diluted arm and no programme at all.

The usual conclusion drawn from this sequence is that the programmes do not scale, and that is a reasonable summary with a misleading word in it. Nothing failed to scale in the sense of becoming less effective as more children joined. What happened is that the thing delivered at scale was a different thing: fewer hours, less trained staff, no home visiting.

That distinction has a practical consequence. If the problem is scale, the policy conclusion is to stop. If the problem is dilution, the conclusion is to deliver fewer children the full programme, which is politically much harder, because it means choosing who receives it and defending that choice.

There is a further reading that deserves more attention than it gets. The original programme ran in the 1960s among families whose alternative was no provision at all. The comparison group in a modern trial has nursery places, television designed to teach early literacy and parents who have themselves been to school longer. A programme can be as good as it ever was and measure smaller, because what it is being compared with has improved.

It is worth naming what the three programmes have in common, because it is easy to miss while comparing them. All three measured the same outcomes: school completion, employment and contact with the criminal justice system. None measured what the children or their parents thought of the experience, and none followed the brothers and sisters who were in the house throughout and received nothing.

The sibling question is the more interesting of those two omissions. A programme that changes how a parent talks to one child is unlikely to leave the others untouched, and the two small studies that have looked found gains in siblings who never attended a session. If that holds, the return on the original spending is higher than any of these evaluations reports, and it has gone unmeasured at scale for sixty years.

This last point cuts against both camps, which is probably why it is so rarely made. It denies the sceptics their claim that the original results were a fluke, and it denies the enthusiasts their claim that the same returns are available today. It also implies that the question "does early intervention work" has no general answer, because the answer depends on what the children concerned would otherwise have had.
