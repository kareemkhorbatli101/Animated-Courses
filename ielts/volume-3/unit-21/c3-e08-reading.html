Earnings by subject, five years on

The chart shows median earnings five years after graduation, by subject group and by sex. Medicine leads on both measures, at £52,000 for men and £48,000 for women, and creative arts comes last, at £22,000 and £21,000. The spread between the top and the bottom is larger than the spread between men and women in any single subject.

That second observation is the one most reports lead with, and it is worth stating carefully. The gap between the sexes is present in every subject shown and is widest in economics, at seven thousand. It is narrowest in creative arts, at one thousand, which is partly a fact about pay and partly a fact about there being less to differ about.

What a chart of this kind cannot show is what the same people would have earned without the degree. Medicine attracts students with high grades who would have done well in many fields, and part of the medical figure is those students and not that training. Economists call this selection, and separating it from the effect of the course itself is the central difficulty in the subject.

There is also a question about which five years. These are the earnings of people who graduated during a particular period and entered a particular labour market. A chart published next year describes a different cohort, and the subject order has in fact changed twice in the past fifteen years, with engineering rising and law falling.

None of which makes the figures useless. They are a reasonable guide to what a subject currently leads to, provided the reader holds two things in mind: the numbers describe people who are not the reader, and they describe a market that will have moved by the time the reader enters it.
