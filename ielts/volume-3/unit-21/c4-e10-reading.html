What is already different at five

Most public argument about educational inequality concerns schools, and most of the gap that argument is about exists before a child has attended one. By the first term of formal schooling, measurable differences in vocabulary, in the ability to hold attention and in early number concepts are already large, and they predict results at sixteen better than almost anything that happens in between.

The vocabulary finding is the most widely quoted and the most widely misunderstood. Children from different backgrounds arrive at school knowing different numbers of words, and the difference is substantial. What the figure does not show is a cause: the same households differ in income, in parental education, in housing stability and in a dozen other ways, and the vocabulary gap travels with all of them.

Attempts to separate these have produced one reasonably solid finding. What predicts a child’s early language is not income itself but the quantity and kind of talk directed at them. Conversation in which a child is addressed and answered, rather than talk they merely overhear, is what the measurements track, and this is true within income groups as well as between them.

That distinction matters because it changes what can be done. A gap caused by income needs income. A gap caused by a pattern of interaction can in principle be addressed by showing parents the pattern, which is cheap, and several programmes have tried exactly that, with results that are genuinely mixed.

The mixed results are instructive. Programmes that train parents in specific interaction techniques produce measurable gains in language at the end of the programme. In most studies those gains shrink over the following two years, and in several they disappear entirely by the time the children are seven. This pattern is common enough in education research to have a name: fade-out.

Fade-out is often reported as failure and that reading is too quick. A gain that fades may still have mattered, if the child entered school better able to follow a lesson and the school then failed to build on it. On this account fade-out is a finding about schools and not about the programme, and the evidence does not currently allow us to say which account is right.

A separate line of work asks whether the measured gap is a gap in skill or a gap in what has been practised. Children differ at five in how often they have been asked to sit still, to answer a stranger and to perform on request, and all three are part of what an early assessment records. Where the same children have been scored informally, at home, across several visits, the measured difference falls by about a third without any change in what the children can actually do.

That finding does not make such assessments worthless. School itself demands sitting still and answering strangers, and a child who cannot yet do those things will struggle whatever their vocabulary. It does mean the number records readiness for a particular setting alongside language, and a policy built on it is aimed at something broader than the word it uses.

There is a further complication that the public debate rarely reaches. The measurements are of what children can do at five, and what they can do at five is partly what they have been asked to do. A child who has never been asked to name colours on request may know the colours. Assessments of this kind measure performance in an unfamiliar setting with an unfamiliar adult, and that setting is itself less familiar to some children than to others.

None of this supports the conclusion that early differences do not matter. They predict later results robustly, whatever they are measuring. It does suggest that the common policy inference, which is that we should intervene earlier and harder on the measured skill, rests on reading a predictor as though it were a cause.

A trial in one city followed four hundred children from the age of two to the age of eleven, with half offered a place in a staffed setting and half not. At five the difference in vocabulary was about four months of development. At eleven it was two months, and on reading it had gone altogether. The programme had not failed; the comparison group had caught up, which is a different result and a harder one to put in a headline.
