### 3B · See


![A branching diagram sorting the subject pronouns into three groups, one for am, one for is and one for are.](figures/fig_a11_u01_p03_v09a.png){width=6.6in}

*Who gets which word — One person speaking. One person spoken about. More than one.*


### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | **am** | I am a student. |
| **he · she · it** | **is** | She is a teacher. |
| **you · we · they** | **are** | They are in room 3. |

**Short forms — what people actually say.**

> I'm · you're · he's · she's · it's · we're · they're

> **Watch out.** *You* is one person **and** more than one person. It is always **are**.
> *You are my teacher.* *You are my class.*

> **Why it matters.** This is the most common verb in English. You will say it a hundred times
> today.


![Two sentence diagrams showing not inserted after the verb, and the verb and the pronoun changing places to make a question.](figures/fig_a11_u01_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*Not, and the swap — For a negative, put not after the verb. For a question, swap the first two words.*


| | |
|---|---|
| **–** | I **am not** / I'm not · she **is not** / she isn't · they **are not** / they aren't |
| **?** | **Am** I…? · **Is** she…? · **Are** you…? |

## Part 4 · Speaking ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### 4A · Fluency starter — the circle

Stand in a circle. Say **three things** about yourself. Ten seconds.
Move one place to the left. Say the **same** three things to a new person. Eight seconds.
Move again. **Six seconds.**

Same words. Faster every time. That is the whole exercise.

### 4B · The functional core — saying who you are, and who somebody else is

**Language Bank**

|---|---|
| **you** | I'm ______ . · I'm from ______ . · I'm a ______ . |
| **somebody else** | This is ______ . · He's from ______ . · She's a ______ . |
| **asking** | What's your name? · Where are you from? · Sorry — how do you spell it? |
| **answering a mistake** | Not quite — it's ______ . · Almost! · That's it. |

### 8A · Read the model

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Yusuf, or Joseph?

Yusuf is from Cairo. His name is **Yusuf**.

At the café next door, Marco calls him **Joseph**. In the first week, Yusuf corrected him twice.
Marco said sorry twice. Now it is week three and Marco still says **Joseph**, and he says it in a
friendly way, every morning, in front of other people.

Yusuf has two choices. Neither one is free.

### 11A · The evidence


![Two sets of pigeonhole labels compared, one ordered by family name and one by first name, with the same eleven people.](figures/fig_a11_u01_p11_v04b.png){width=6.6in}

*Two ways to put the names on the pigeonholes — Learner A has the list as it is. Learner B has the list by first name.*


🔊 **Track 1.6.** Lin, 25 seconds: *"I changed mine in my first month here. It was easier. Now
nobody at work knows my real name and I don't know how to start."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame. Everybody says one.

> *I think Yusuf should ______ , because ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *easy · difficult · polite · his name · every day · in front of people ·
friendly · tired*

### 11C · Decide

Write **two sentences**. Choose, and say why.

> I think Yusuf should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> I think Yusuf should say it one more time, but differently. Two corrections did not work, and
> Marco is not unkind — he is friendly, and that makes it harder. So: say it when nobody is
> waiting, and write it down. People remember a name they have seen.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** Yusuf wrote his name on a napkin and gave it to Marco on a quiet afternoon.
> Marco kept it. He now says **Yusuf**, and he says it slightly too carefully, which Yusuf finds
> funny.
>
> Lin never went back to her name. She says she does not mind. **She also says she minds.**

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> Yusuf's reasoning was good **and** it worked. Would it still have been good reasoning if Marco
> had not kept the napkin?

| | | |

| **they** | **work** | They work at night. |

> **The verb does not change.** Not yet. In Unit 3 it changes for *he*, *she* and *it*, and that
> is the only place it changes in the whole language.


![Two sentence diagrams. In the first, do not is inserted before the verb; in the second, do moves to the front of the sentence. The verb itself does not move in either.](figures/fig_a11_u02_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*Do, and where it goes — For a negative, do not drops in before the verb. For a question, do jumps to the front.*


| | |
|---|---|
| **–** | I **do not** work / I **don't** work · They **don't** open on Sunday |
| **?** | **Do** you work on Saturday? · **Do** they open early? |

> **Where the time goes.** *I get up at six.* · *At six I get up.* Both are English. The first one
> is normal. The second one puts the time first because the time is the news.

> **Why it matters.** This is how you say anything that is true every day, and most of what you
> say about your life is true every day.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The six o'clock class

Escola Monte has an evening class at seven. Eleven students come.

Four of them work until seven and arrive late, every week. They miss the first fifteen minutes.
Teresa starts again for them, every week, and the seven students who came on time wait.

The school can move the class to **half past seven**. Then nobody is late. But the class finishes
at nine, and the last tram is at ten past nine, and three students walk home.

### 11A · The evidence


![Two drawings of the same classroom. On the left, seven students and four empty chairs at seven o'clock. On the right, eleven students at half past seven, with a dark window behind them.](figures/fig_a11_u02_p11_v04b.png){width=6.6in}

*Room 3 at seven, and at half past seven — The same room, drawn twice, from the same place in the corridor.*


🔊 **Track 2.6.** Lin, 20 seconds: *"I come at seven. I wait. I am not angry. But I get up at
six and now I go home at half past nine."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame. Everybody says one.

> *I think the class should start at ______ , because ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *work · late · tired · tram · wait · early · every week*

### 11C · Decide

Write **two sentences**. Choose, and say why.

> I think the class should start at ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> Keep it at seven and do not start again. The first fifteen minutes is speaking practice, and
> you can walk into speaking practice late. Put the new grammar at half past. Nobody waits and
> nobody misses anything.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** Teresa moved the grammar to half past seven and left the first fifteen
> minutes for speaking. The four late students still came late. The seven others stopped waiting.
>
> Then something she did not expect: two of the seven started coming at twenty past instead of
> seven, because they had worked out what the first fifteen minutes was.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> Teresa solved the problem for the four. Did she make a new one for the seven?

### 3A · Notice

### 3B · See


![Five boxes in a row. The first four read I check, you check, we check and they check in the same colour; the fifth reads he checks with the final s in a second colour and larger than the rest of the word.](figures/fig_a11_u03_p03_v09a.png){width=6.6in}

*Four the same, and one with a letter on it — The fifth box is the whole of the present simple. One letter, one group of people.*


### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **I / you / we / they** | **check** | They check their phones at night. |
| **he / she / it** | **checks** | She checks her phone before she gets up. |

> **One letter, one person.** The **s** goes on for *he*, *she* and *it* and nowhere else. It is
> the only ending the present simple has, and it is the one everybody forgets.


![Two sentence diagrams. In the first, she checks carries the s on the verb; in the second, does has taken the s to the front and the verb is left bare.](figures/fig_a11_u03_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*The s is only in one place at a time — When does arrives at the front, it takes the s with it. The verb gives it up.*


| | |
|---|---|
| **?** | **Do** you check your phone at night? · **Does** she check her phone at night? |
| **–** | I **don't** check it. · He **doesn't** check it. |
| **? + word** | **Where** do you put it? · **How often** does he look at it? |

> **The s is only in one place at a time.** *Does she checks?* is wrong because the **s** has
> already gone onto *does*. One **s** per question, and the helper gets it.

> **Why it matters.** Every question you will ever ask about somebody else's life has this shape.
> It is the whole of asking about habits.

*One word, three destinations — Can does not change. What changes is the situation it lands in.*


### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **ability** | what a body or a mind does | *I can carry it. I can't carry it up four floors.* |
| **permission** | somebody decided, and they could decide otherwise | *You can use the lift. Students can't.* |
| **prohibition** | a rule, and the person who made it is gone | *You can't smoke here.* |

> **Can never changes.** No **s** for *he* and *she*. No *do* in the question. The verb after it is
> always plain: *can go*, never *can goes* and never *can to go*.


![Two sentence diagrams. In the first, not joins can to make can not; in the second, can moves to the front of the sentence. The verb after it is plain in both.](figures/fig_a11_u04_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

| | |
|---|---|
| **–** | I **cannot** use it · I **can't** use it · She **can't** come today |
| **?** | **Can** I leave this here? · **Can** you help me? · **Can** she use the lift? |
| **short answers** | *Yes, you can.* · *No, I'm sorry, I can't.* |

> with the sharp edge taken off. *Is it OK if…?* asks whether the rule allows it rather than

> **There is not a place.** It does not mean *in that place over there*. It is an empty word that
> holds the front of the sentence open while the real subject arrives behind the verb.

| | |
|---|---|

### 3A · Notice

### 3B · See


![Two groups of boxes. On the left, countable foods with numbers in front of them; on the right, uncountable foods with containers in front of them instead.](figures/fig_a11_u06_p03_v09a.png){width=6.6in}

*Things you count and things you weigh — The same wheat is bread and a sandwich. The food did not change. The language did.*


### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **countable** | you can put a number in front | *an egg · two eggs · eleven apples* |
| **uncountable** | no number, no plural | *bread · water · rice · cheese · coffee* |
| **asking** | **how many** + countable · **how much** + uncountable | *How many eggs? How much rice?* |
| **the bridge** | a container makes anything countable | *two loaves of bread · three bottles of water* |

> **It is not about the food.** It is about how the language decided to look at it. *Bread* is
> uncountable and *a sandwich* is countable, and they are the same wheat.


![Two sentence diagrams. In the first, a number tries to attach directly to bread; in the second, the number attaches to loaves and of bread hangs beneath it.](figures/fig_a11_u06_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*The container is the bridge — Bread cannot take a number. A loaf can, and it carries the bread across with it.*


| | |
|---|---|
| **some / any** | *some bread · some eggs* · *Is there any rice? There isn't any milk.* |

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **like / love / hate + -ing** | the activity, as an activity | *I love playing. I hate running.* |
| **like / love / hate + a noun** | the thing itself | *I love music. I hate football.* |

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|

| **an before a vowel sound** | *an engineer · an artist · an office worker* |

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Saturday at the café

Marco's café is open eight hours on Saturday. He cannot do eight hours alone any more.

**One person, eight hours.** One wage, one person who learns the job properly, one person who is
exhausted by two.

**Two people, four hours each.** Two wages for the same money, nobody exhausted, and twice as much
training. The handover at twelve is twenty minutes of nobody serving anybody.

**Nobody.** He closes at two. He loses the afternoon, which is a third of Saturday's money and
the only part he enjoys.

### 11A · The evidence


![Two drawings of the same cafe counter at half past two, one with a single worker eight hours into a shift and three customers waiting, the other with the second of two workers two hours in and nobody waiting.](figures/fig_a11_u08_p11_v04b.png){width=6.6in}

*The counter at half past two — One person eight hours in, and the second of two people two hours in.*


🔊 **Track 8.6.** Marco, 20 seconds: *"Eight hours is not the problem. Hour seven is the problem.
In hour seven I am still working and I am not working well, and the customer can see it."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame. Everybody says one.

> *I think Marco should ______ , because ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *job · shift · customer · break · work · team · uniform*

### 11C · Decide

Write **two sentences**. Choose, and say why.

> I think Marco should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> Two people, with the handover at eleven and not at twelve. The twenty minutes of nobody serving
> is the whole argument against two people, and it disappears if you move it to the quietest hour
> instead of the busiest one.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** Marco hired two people and moved the handover to eleven. The twenty minutes
> cost him almost nothing, because at eleven there are four customers and at twelve there are

> they started telling each other things about the customers. By March they knew more about his
> regulars than he did.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> Marco was buying hours. What did the twenty minutes of overlap turn out to be worth?

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Five minutes from the metro

One in four new students does not arrive at the first lesson. Six out of seven of those who
telephone are standing in the square at the bottom of the hill.

**Option one:** rewrite the website. Two landmarks, one warning about the hill, and the three
streets to ignore. Free, and it takes twenty minutes.

**Option two:** put a sign in the square. The city charges one hundred and forty euros a year for
a sign on a pole, and it has to be approved.

**Option three:** meet every new student at the metro for the first week of each term. It works
perfectly, it costs Ana four hours a term, and it stops the moment she is busy.

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Thirty-nine euros

Lin bought a jacket on the first of December for thirty-nine euros. It was eighty-nine in
November. She did not try it on. At home it was too small.

The shop will give her a credit note for thirty-nine euros, valid for six months. It will not give
her cash, and the receipt says so in small type at the bottom.

She can keep the jacket and give it to somebody. She can take the credit note and buy something
else from a shop she now does not trust. Or she can argue, which will take forty minutes and
probably fail.

### 11A · The evidence

### 11C · Decide

> I think Lin should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

### 11E · The Reveal

## A1.1 · Unit 6 · Two Streets from the Café

**0B** · Guess it
Because one of them has a small handwritten sign on it and the other has nothing. The tomatoes and the price are identical, so the sign is the only difference — and at seven in the morning a sign is the only thing a stranger has to go on.

**1C** · Sort — can you count it?
> **BOTH** — chicken · fish
> **YOU CANNOT COUNT IT** — water · cheese · milk · rice · bread
> **ONE, TWO, THREE** — apple · tomato · egg
**The two in both:** *chicken* and *fish*. Alive in a field or a river they are countable — *two chickens*, *three fish*. On a plate they are a substance — *some chicken*, *a bit of fish*. English counts the animal and weighs the meat, and the word does not change.

**2A** · Seven o'clock at the stall 🔊 Track 6.1
**1** T · **2** T · **3** F · **4** T

2 Nine.

1 Five euros thirty-four.  
2 Six litres — the line says 6 x 1 L, so the amount is in the line, not only the price.

**5F** · Two texts

## A1.1 · Unit 7 · Friday at Six

**0B** · Guess it
The one watching the game. Three of the four are looking at a phone, which means the park is where they are and not what they are doing. The fourth has chosen to be there, and that is the whole difference between watching and sitting.

**1C** · Sort — alone, or with people?
> **ALONE** — a book · drawing · a film · running · swimming
> **WITH PEOPLE** — football · a game · dancing
> **BOTH** — cooking · music
**The two in both:** *cooking* and *music*. Both work completely alone and completely in company, and they are the only two on the list that change their nature when you add people rather than just becoming louder. Accept *a film* in either column with a reason — the point is that most film-watching is now solitary and did not used to be.

**2A** · On the bench 🔊 Track 7.1

Open answers. The useful observation is that nothing in the square tells them: three of the four ways out look the same, and the one piece of information that would decide it — which street goes up — is the one the phone map flattens.

**1C** · Sort — a place, or a direction?
> **A PLACE** — hill · station · square · church
> **A DIRECTION** — left · right · behind · between
> **BOTH** — opposite · corner

## A1.1 · Unit 10 · Thirty-Nine Euros

**0B** · Guess it
The one whose sign gives only the new price. Without the old price there is nothing to compare the new one with, so twenty looks like a decision somebody else has already made for you. The three honest signs invite arithmetic; the fourth does not.

**1C** · Sort — the thing, or the money?
> **BOTH** — change · receipt
> **THE MONEY** — cost · cash · price
> **THE THING** — colour · shirt · size · shoes · jacket
**The two in both:** *change* and *receipt*. Change is money, and it is also a handful of coins you can drop. A receipt is a piece of paper you can hold, and it is also the only record that the money moved. Both of them are objects whose whole purpose is financial.

**2A** · The second of December 🔊 Track 10.1

**Noticing** — the same thing that decides *is* and *are*. **Was** goes with I, he, she and it; **were** goes with you, we and they. One jacket was; all of them were.

2 Eleven.  
3 Thirty-nine euros.
