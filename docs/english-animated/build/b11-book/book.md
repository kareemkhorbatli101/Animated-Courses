### Three past tenses, working together

### 3B · See


![A timeline with three bands: a dot for a completed event, a lens bracket for background, and a dotted span ending at a boundary for what was already true.](figures/fig_b11_u01_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*Three past tenses, one story — The shapes carry the meaning. The names come afterwards.*


### 3C · Know

| | Use it for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **past simple** | one completed event; the spine of the story | *They **took** the timber out.* |
| **past continuous** | what was going on around that event; the background | *The building **was standing** empty.* |
| **past perfect** | what was already true before the story started | *By then they **had** already **started**.* |

> **Watch out.** The past perfect is not "more past". It marks **what was already true when the
> story began**. If every past sentence in your paragraph is a past perfect, none of them is doing
> any work.

> **Why it matters.** Get this wrong and your reader puts the events in the wrong order. *When I
> arrived, they left* and *When I arrived, they had left* describe two different mornings.

### 3D · Use — controlled

Complete the text. Use the past simple, the past continuous or the past perfect.

> Mwangi (1) __________ (open) the stall in 1994. At that time the lane (2) __________ (be) mostly
> workshops, and people (3) __________ (repair) radios and bicycles all day. He (4) __________
> (rent) his two metres of frontage from a man called Otieno, who (5) __________ (die) in 2009.
> By the time the first tower (6) __________ (go) up, Otieno's son (7) __________ already
> __________ (raise) the rent twice. For eleven days Mwangi (8) __________ (work) by lamp, because
> the new building (9) __________ (block) the sun until ten in the morning. He (10) __________

---

**The last shop on the corner**

Mwangi Njoroge has been taking in trousers on the same corner of Ngong Lane for thirty-one years.
In that time three buildings have gone up around him and two have come down. His stall has not
moved by a metre.

When he started, the lane was mostly workshops. People repaired things here — radios, shoes,
bicycles, trousers. The building that now houses Jengo House was a timber store, and the plot next
to it was open ground where matatu drivers parked and argued. Mwangi rented his two metres of
frontage from a man called Otieno, who died in 2009, and whose son raised the rent twice and then
forgot about it.

The first tower went up in 2014. Mwangi remembers the week it was finished, because for eleven days
he could not see the sun until after ten in the morning, and he was working by lamp. The second one
took the open ground. The drivers moved three streets down and most of them never came back.

"People ask me if business is worse," he says. "It is not worse. It is different. Before, I repaired
work clothes. Now I take in suits for young people who have just started earning and bought the
wrong size online."

He is not sentimental about it. He points out that the new buildings brought him the customers he
has now, and that the lane had been dangerous after dark for years before anyone built anything.

But he also notes that of the fourteen businesses that were on this stretch when he arrived, his is
the only one still trading. The others did not fail. Their rent went up, and they moved somewhere
cheaper, and then that place got expensive too.

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### How a place changed · 140 words


![A four-storey building drawn in section, each floor labelled with its original and current use, with the ground floor showing three changes of use.](figures/fig_b11_u01_p08_v02.png){width=6.6in}

### 8A · Read the model

> The road I grew up on is called Station Road, and there has not been a station on it since 1968.
> `[opens in the present, then goes back — the reader needs the now first]`
>
> When we moved there in 2004 it was mostly houses, with a post office at one end and a garage at
> the other. The garage had already been there for forty years, and the man who ran it had learned
> the trade from his father. `[past perfect — the only one in the text, and it is doing real work]`
>
> Then the bypass opened. Traffic that had come through the village went around it instead, and
> within three years the post office had closed, the garage had closed, and a developer had bought
> both sites.
>
> There are eleven new houses now, and they are good houses. The people in them drive to the
> supermarket. Nobody walks up Station Road any more, because there is nothing on it to walk to.
> `[who gained and who lost — stated, not implied]`

**Questions about the writer's choices, not the rules:**

1. Why does the writer start in the present and then go back?
2. There is one past perfect in paragraph two and three in paragraph three. Why the cluster?
3. The last sentence gives a reason. What would be lost if it were the first sentence?

### 8B · The anti-model

> Station Road was built in 1890. The station closed in 1968. We moved there in 2004. There was a
> post office and a garage. The bypass opened in 2011. The post office closed in 2012. The garage
> closed in 2013. A developer bought both sites. There are eleven new houses now. The people drive
> to the supermarket.

This is grammatically correct. **Why is it worse?** Name three specific things the model does that
this does not.

A developer has offered to buy Jengo House. The price is good — better than the building is worth

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

A report extract called **"The cost of the cheapest option."** The first line is:

> *Procurement teams are rewarded for the price on the invoice, and for nothing else.*

### 5B · Skim — sixty seconds

Where does the argument turn? Mark the paragraph.

---

**The cost of the cheapest option**

Procurement teams are rewarded for the price on the invoice, and for nothing else. This is not a
criticism of the people in them. It is a description of what their targets measure, and people
hit the targets they are given.

The effect is well documented in manufacturing and almost undocumented in small-scale production,
which is where most of the world's clothing is actually made. A buyer with a unit-price target
will move an order to a workshop that quotes eight per cent lower. On the invoice, the saving is
real. What the invoice does not carry is the four days of rework when the first batch comes back
with the seams wrong, the two weeks of delay while a replacement is cut, or the senior fitter's
time spent on a problem that the cheaper workshop was never equipped to avoid.

None of those costs appear in the procurement figure. All of them appear somewhere else.

The pattern repeats at every scale. A workshop that has been underpricing for years is not
cheaper; it is subsidising its buyers with unpaid hours, deferred maintenance and, eventually,
its own capacity to keep trading. When it closes, the buyer records no loss at all. The cost
shows up as a sourcing problem eighteen months later, and nobody connects the two.

What makes this hard to correct is not that it is unknown. It is that the invoice is a single
number with a name on it, and the other costs are spread across four departments, three quarters
and one person's weekend. A number that can be pointed at will always beat a number that has to
be assembled.

The workshops that survive are not the cheapest. They are the ones that learned to put the
assembled number on the page before anybody asked for it.

**The second name**

For eleven years my mother called me by a name that is not on any document I own. It is four
letters long and it means nothing in particular. She says she chose it before I was born and then
put something longer on the certificate because the registrar was watching.

At school I used the long one. At home I was the short one. It did not feel like a secret; it
felt like weather — something that changed depending on which room you were in.

The trouble started when the two rooms met. A teacher came to the house and used the long name in
front of my mother, and I watched my mother decide, in about half a second, not to correct her.
That was the first time I understood that one of the names was going to have to win.

I am going to say that I chose the long one, and that is almost true. What actually happened is
that the long one was already on things. It was on the register, the library card, the
inoculation record, the bank account somebody opened for me when I was nine. Choosing the short
one would not have been a choice; it would have been nineteen separate arguments with nineteen
institutions, each of which would have asked me for a document carrying the name I was trying to
leave.

So I did not choose. I let the paperwork choose, which is what most people mean when they say
they chose.

My mother still uses the short one. I am forty-one. When she dies, nobody alive will call me by
it, and I have started to mind about that in a way I did not expect, because for eleven years I
thought the short name was the one that did not count.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Joseph, or the other thing

Didier runs the café on the ground floor. His full name is Didier Nsengimana. For two years the
biggest account — a company that orders ninety coffees a week — has called him **Dee**.

It began as a shortening. It is now on their internal system, on the standing order, and on the
reference that appears on every payment. Changing it means changing the reference, which means
their finance team reopening the account.

- **Leave it.** It is a nickname, they are friendly, and ninety coffees a week is ninety coffees.
- **Correct it once, properly, in writing.** Make it easy. Accept the risk that it is a nuisance
  to them.
- **Correct it and ask them to change the payment reference too.** The whole thing, done once.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The standing order record** — the name as their system holds it.
2. **The chart**: how often a correction is actually made, and by whom.
3. **The voice.** Lin, from A1.1, twenty-five seconds: *"I changed mine in my first month here.
   It was easier. Now nobody at work knows my real name and I don't know how to start."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think he should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> I think he should correct it once, in writing, and not ask them to change the payment reference.
>
> The full correction is the one I would want to be right, and I think it is wrong here. The
> payment reference is their system's problem and it is genuinely expensive to change — reopening
> an account is a week of somebody's attention. Asking for it turns a thirty-second courtesy into
> a favour, and favours get remembered as difficulty.
>
> But leaving it entirely is worse, and Lin's recording is why. The cost of saying nothing is not
> felt in the first year. It is felt when the wrong name has been true for so long that
> correcting it would need an explanation, and the explanation is the thing nobody has time for.
>
> So: one line, friendly, with the spelling, and no request attached to it. The reference can stay
> wrong. The person saying it out loud ninety times a week is the one that matters.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** He sent one line. Four people started saying Didier within a week. The
> finance system still says **D NSENG** and the payment reference has never changed.
>
> Eight months later a new account manager arrived, read the system, and called him Dee.

**He fixed the people and not the paperwork, and the paperwork refilled the people.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And what, exactly, would the full fix have cost?

![A vertical ladder of modal forms from must at the top through should and may to needn't and mustn't, with their meanings.](figures/fig_b11_u04_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*Obligation, from binding to free — Two of these are opposites that look like variants.*


### 3C · Know

| | Now | Past |
|---|---|---|
| **obligation** | must / have to | **had to** *(must has no past)* |
| **no obligation** | don't have to / needn't | didn't have to / didn't need to |
| **prohibition** | mustn't / can't | wasn't allowed to / couldn't |
| **permission** | can / may / be allowed to | could / was allowed to |

> **Watch out.** *Mustn't* and *don't have to* are opposites, not variants. *You mustn't sign it*
> forbids. *You don't have to sign it* releases.

> **Why it matters.** In a meeting about who is responsible, the difference between *we weren't
> required to* and *we weren't allowed to* is the whole argument.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The toilet on the ground floor

Jengo House has no accessible toilet. The building predates the requirement, so nobody has broken
a rule. Priya, who is retraining as a dentist after a spinal injury, has used the café next door
for seven months and has never mentioned it.

- **The landlord pays.** He is not obliged to. Asking costs the relationship and probably fails.
- **The tenants pay.** About 160,000, shared six ways. It is their building for four more years.
- **Nobody pays, and it is minuted.** Honest, cheap, and in writing for ever.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The inspection report** from Part 5, and the forty-one-word reply.
2. **The chart**: what the survey, the works and the disruption would actually cost.
3. **The voice.** Priya, thirty seconds: *"I didn't say anything because I didn't want it to be
   about me. Now it's been seven months and saying something would be about me."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think they should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> I think the tenants should fund the survey, and only the survey, and should say in writing that
> they are not accepting liability by doing it.
>
> Paying for the works outright is the option that feels right and I think it is wrong. It is
> 160,000 of other people's money for a building they will leave in four years, and it lets the
> landlord establish that this is a tenant cost. Once that is established it stays established.
>
> Minuting it and doing nothing is the cheapest thing and the one Priya would have to live in. She
> has already spent seven months not mentioning it, which tells you what the cost of the honest
> option actually is, and who pays it.
>
> The survey is the only move that changes what anybody knows. A number on a page converts an
> argument about principle into an argument about money, and arguments about money get settled.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The survey came back at 96,000 — considerably less than the 160,000 everyone
> had been assuming, because the ground-floor plumbing was already in the right place.
>
> Mr Kamau paid half, having refused to pay anything for four months, after his insurer asked him
> a question about the 2019 report.
>
> Priya left in November for a practice forty minutes away. The works were finished in January.

**The reasoning worked. The number came down, the landlord moved, the thing got built.
And the person it was for had gone.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And what would it have taken to move faster — and who would have had
> to pay for *that*?

### 3B · See


![A branching diagram from one if-clause into zero, first and second conditionals with their forms and what each one claims about reality.](figures/fig_b11_u05_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*Three conditionals, side by side — The past form in the third one is not about the past. It marks distance from reality.*


### 3C · Know

| | *if* clause | result clause | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **zero** | present simple | present simple | a rule, always true — *If you work nights, you get two days off.* |
| **first** | present simple | will / may / might | a real possibility — *If she asks, I'll explain.* |
| **second** | past simple | would / could | not real, or very unlikely — *If it were up to me, I'd let people swap.* |

> **Watch out.** The past form in the second conditional is **not about the past**. It marks
> distance from reality, not distance in time.

> **Why it matters.** *If we switch it off, we go back to the whiteboard* and *if we switched it
> off, we'd go back to the whiteboard* are the same sentence with two different politics. The
> first treats it as on the table. The second treats it as unthinkable.

### 3D · Use — controlled

> If a shift (1) __________ (go) unfilled for two hours, the system (2) __________ (reallocate) it
> automatically — that is what it does every time. If Grace (3) __________ (ask) to swap tomorrow,
> Tom (4) __________ (have to) do it by hand, which he can. If the rules (5) __________ (be)
> written by the people on the rota, they (6) __________ (look) completely different — but they
> were not.

Move each sentence one step further from reality, then one step closer. Say what changes.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

An interview transcript. The first exchange is:

> **Q:** So the system was working correctly?
> **A:** It was working exactly as specified. That's a different thing.

What do you expect the rest to be about? One sentence.

### 5B · Skim — sixty seconds

Find the moment the interviewee stops defending the system.

---

**"Exactly as specified"**

**Q:** So the system was working correctly?

**A:** It was working exactly as specified. That's a different thing, and I want to be careful
about it, because people hear "working correctly" and stop asking questions.

**Q:** What's the difference?

**A:** The specification said: allocate shifts so that no one does more than two nights in a row,
weight by availability, and break ties by seniority. It did all three, every time, for fourteen
months. If you audit it against the spec, it passes. The problem is that nobody writing the spec
had ever covered a Sunday.

**Q:** Meaning?

**A:** Meaning the spec had no word for the thing people were actually doing, which was swapping.
Before the system, if you couldn't do a Sunday you found somebody who could, and you owed them
one. That's not in the spec because it isn't a rule — it's a favour economy, and it's how the
place ran. We automated the rota and the favours stopped, because there was nothing to swap.

**Q:** Did anybody raise it?

**A:** One person. Six weeks in. She said it had got harder to change a shift, and I said yes,
that's correct, that's the point — the whole reason we built it was that the old rota got changed
twelve times a week and nobody could plan. And I was right. I was answering a different question
from the one she was asking, and I did it confidently, which is worse.

**Q:** Would you build it again?

**A:** Yes. But I'd build the override first and the allocator second, and I'd make the override
cost somebody thirty seconds instead of an email. If the cheapest thing to do is the thing the
system wanted, you haven't built a tool. You've built a rule and called it a tool.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Switch it off, or build the override

Six weeks of data. The rota changes twice a week instead of twelve. Nobody is doing more than two
nights in a row for the first time in years. And Grace has worked four Sundays running, because
she is the most available person in the building and availability is weighted first.

- **Switch it off.** Back to the whiteboard. Everybody can swap. Nobody can plan.
- **Leave it.** It is working against every measure anybody wrote down.
- **Build the override.** Three weeks of Tom's time, which is three weeks of something else.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The specification** from Part 5 — three pages, three rules, one missing word.
2. **The chart**: what changed in six weeks, including the thing nobody was measuring.
3. **The voice.** Grace, thirty seconds: *"I'm not complaining. It's fair. I just used to be able
   to say no, and I can't work out who to say it to now."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think they should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> Build the override, and accept that it costs three weeks.
>
> Switching it off is the option I have most sympathy with and I think it is wrong. The twelve
> changes a week were real, and the people who suffered most from them were the people with the
> least control over their own time — which is the same group this system is now hurting. Going
> back does not return their discretion; it returns everybody's, and theirs was always the first
> to be overruled.
>
> Leaving it is indefensible once you have heard Grace, and it is indefensible for a reason that
> has nothing to do with fairness: the system is *working*, and nobody can tell you what it would
> look like if it stopped. There is no measurement. An override log is the cheapest instrument
> anybody is going to get.
>
> Three weeks of Tom's time is the real cost, and I would rather spend it on knowing than on
> guessing.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The override took five weeks, not three. In the first month it was used
> eleven times. Nine of the eleven were the same two people swapping a Sunday, which is what the
> system had removed.
>
> The log made the pattern visible, and the fairness rule was rewritten in March to weight
> *consecutive weekends* rather than availability. Grace worked two Sundays in the next quarter.
>
> Tom's other project slipped by five weeks and was eventually cancelled.

**The fix worked. The measurement was worth more than the fix. And something else died quietly to
pay for it.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And: the cancelled project had no voice in the room. Should it have?

### 3C · Know

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| present | *They enforce it.* | *It **is enforced**.* |
| past | *They enforced it.* | *It **was enforced**.* |
| present perfect | *They have enforced it.* | *It **has been enforced**.* |
| past perfect | *They had enforced it.* | *It **had been enforced**.* |
| future | *They will enforce it.* | *It **will be enforced**.* |
| modal | *They must enforce it.* | *It **must be enforced**.* |

**by** names the doer. **with** names the instrument.

> *The door was wedged open **by** a tenant **with** a fire extinguisher.*

> **Watch out.** The passive is not more polite and not more formal. It is a decision about
> **what goes first in the sentence** — and therefore about what the reader thinks the sentence is
> about.

> **Why it matters.** *Mistakes were made* is grammatically unremarkable and does a specific job:
> it puts the mistakes first and leaves no room for a name.

---

**The rule that was never repealed**

Every organisation I have worked in has a rule that no living person has obeyed. It is written
down. It has never been repealed. And if you ask why nobody follows it, you are told, with real
confidence, that it does not apply any more — a claim that is never produced in writing, because
there is nothing to produce.

Dead rules accumulate for a simple reason: creating one requires a decision, and killing one
requires the same decision with none of the credit. A policy that is brought in after an incident
is brought in by somebody who can point at it afterwards. A policy that is quietly dropped is
dropped by somebody who then has to explain, if anything goes wrong, why they dropped it. The
asymmetry does the rest.

For a long time I thought this was simply untidy, and that the answer was a periodic cull. I now
think that is wrong, and that the dead rules are doing something.

What they are doing is holding a space. A rule that nobody follows is still a rule that can be
produced at a moment of crisis, and the fact that it was ignored for a decade will not help you
when it is. It also marks a boundary: everybody knows the door should not be propped open, and
the reason they know is the notice, not the enforcement. Remove the notice and you remove the
thing the shrug is aimed at.

The real danger is not the dead rule. It is the live rule that has quietly become dead without
anybody noticing the moment it happened — because then nobody is shrugging. They simply do not
know. The organisation believes the rule is operating. The people believe it was never serious.
Neither side is lying, and the gap between them is where the incident comes from.

So: audit, by all means. But the question is not "which rules are nobody following?" It is
"which rules do we believe are working?" And the honest version of that question can only be
answered by somebody who does not already know the answer.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The fire doors

The notice says fire doors must not be propped. They have been propped every working day for two
years, because the corridor is dark and the doors are heavy and nobody has ever said anything.

The insurance renewal is in six weeks. The form asks whether fire doors are kept closed.

- **Enforce it.** Remove the wedges, tell everyone, and accept that four people will be angry and
  two will keep doing it.
- **Formally drop it** and write down what you do instead — hold-open devices on a closer, which
  cost 94,000 for three doors.
- **Say nothing**, answer the form honestly about the policy rather than the practice, and
  continue.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The notice** from Part 5, with its unenforceable sentence.
2. **The chart**: what the three options cost, including the one with no money in it.
3. **The voice.** Obi, twenty-five seconds: *"I didn't know it was a rule. I knew it was a sign."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think they should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> They should buy the hold-open devices, and I accept that 94,000 is a lot of money for a problem
> nobody has had.
>
> Enforcing it is the honest-looking option and I think it fails. The reason the doors are propped
> is that the corridor is dark and the doors are heavy, and removing the wedges does not change
> either of those things. In six weeks the wedges come back, and now they come back knowing it is
> forbidden, which is worse than not knowing — that is Obi's line, and it is the only new
> information in the room.
>
> Saying nothing is the option I would most like to take and it is the one that cannot survive a
> question. The insurance form does not ask about the policy. It asks whether the doors are kept
> closed. Answering about the policy is a lie with a defensible sentence wrapped round it.
>
> The devices cost money and solve the actual problem, which is that the rule and the building
> were never compatible. Somebody should have noticed that in 2019.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** They bought devices for two of the three doors — the third was quoted at
> 61,000 on its own because of the frame — and propped the third one.
>
> The insurance form was answered *"fire doors are fitted with automatic closers"*, which was true
> of two doors out of three and was not queried.
>
> The third door is still propped. Nobody has put a wedge under it since March, because somebody
> moved a filing cabinet in front of it instead, and a cabinet is not a wedge.

**They solved two thirds of it, told two thirds of the truth, and the remaining third found a new
way to be propped that the notice does not cover.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And: the notice still says "must not be propped". Is a filing cabinet
> propping?

### 3B · See


![A sentence with brackets showing the three patterns that can follow an object: to plus verb, bare verb, and -ing.](figures/fig_b11_u07_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*What can follow an object — The pattern lives in the verb, not in the meaning. let and allow mean the same and behave differently.*


### 3C · Know

| Pattern | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **object + to + verb** | want · ask · tell · advise · remind · expect · allow · teach · persuade · encourage · force · invite · enable | *I want you **to hold** it.* |
| **object + bare verb** | let · make · have · (and *help*, either way) | *Let her **try**.* · *She made me **do** it again.* |
| **object + -ing** | see · hear · watch · notice · find · catch | *I saw her **doing** it wrong.* |
| **object + bare verb**, with the senses | see · hear · watch | *I saw her **do** it.* — the whole thing, start to finish |

> **Watch out.** *Let* and *make* take the bare verb. *Allow* and *force* mean almost the same and
> take *to*. The meaning is not the guide; the verb is.

> **Why it matters.** *I saw her doing it* describes a moment. *I saw her do it* describes the
> whole act — which is what a witness statement needs.

### 3D · Use — controlled

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Four days, or forty pages

Felix leaves on the 30th. His replacement starts on the 27th. That is four days of overlap, and
Felix has eleven days of work left that only he can do.

- **Spend the four days teaching.** His eleven days become somebody else's problem, and two of
  them cannot be done by anybody else before March.
- **Spend the four days finishing, and write the forty pages in the evenings.** He has done this
  before. The pages are good and the gaps are the things he has stopped noticing.
- **Ask for the start date to move.** It costs the business three weeks of an empty seat, and he
  has no standing to ask.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **Page 19** from Part 5, before and after.
2. **The chart**: what survives one handover, two handovers, and six months.
3. **The voice.** Ruth, twenty-five seconds: *"The forty pages were the only reason I knew where
   to put the thing I found out."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think he should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> He should write the pages and finish the eleven days, and I think the four days of teaching is
> the romantic answer.
>
> Teaching in person transmits more per hour than any document — that is not in dispute, and if
> he had four weeks I would say spend them. He has four days, and four days of sitting beside
> somebody produces a person who can do the normal case and has met none of the exceptions,
> because the exceptions do not happen to order. In March she will hit the 6% case and he will be
> gone either way.
>
> What the forty pages do is different. They are not a teacher; they are a place to put things.
> Ruth's own sentence is the argument: the pages were why she knew where p.19 was. A document that
> is wrong in a findable place beats a conversation that was right and is now unrecoverable.
>
> He should spend one of the four days on the exceptions only, and let the normal case go in the
> document.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** He wrote the pages and worked the eleven days. He gave Ruth one afternoon, not
> four days, and spent all of it on the things that go wrong.
>
> Eighteen months later the document is on version 4 and about a third of it is Ruth's. The
> section Felix was proudest of — a long explanation of why the system was designed that way — was
> deleted in version 2 by Ruth, who said nobody needed it.
>
> He found out, and minded, and said nothing.

**The handover worked. The part he most wanted to pass on was the part that did not survive.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And: was Ruth right to cut it?

### 3C · Know

**Reducing a relative clause**

| Full | Reduced | When you can |
|---|---|---|
| the farm **which supplies** us | the farm **supplying** us | active verb → *-ing* |
| the crates **which were packed** yesterday | the crates **packed** yesterday | passive → past participle |
| the grower **who I buy** from | the grower **I buy** from | drop *who/which/that* when it is the **object** |
| the grower **who supplies** me | ✗ *the grower supplies me* | **never** when it is the **subject** |

**Moving the preposition**

> *the cooperative **she's a member of*** — normal speech
> *the cooperative **of which she is a member*** — formal writing
> ✗ *the cooperative **of that** she is a member* — *that* never follows a preposition

> **Watch out.** You can drop the relative pronoun only when it is the object of its clause. If
> dropping it leaves two verbs with no subject between them, you have dropped the wrong one.

> **Why it matters.** A reduced clause packs more into a sentence, which is what reports and
> labels do. It also hides things: *produce grown locally* does not say by whom, or how local.

### 5A · Predict

### 5B · Skim — sixty seconds

---

**What the word local is doing**

There is no legal definition of local, which is not an oversight. Several have been proposed, and
each has failed for the same reason: any distance you pick is correct somewhere and absurd
somewhere else. Thirty miles is a reasonable radius in a dense country and meaningless in a
sparse one. A rule written for one landscape fails in the next valley.

In the absence of a number, the word is doing something other than measuring. It is signalling.
When a label says local, the claim it is making is not geographic; it is a claim about the kind of
supply chain the buyer is joining — short, visible, and with a person at the end of it who could
in principle be visited.

Most of the time this is roughly honest. The produce travelled a distance the buyer would accept
if they knew it, and the grower is a person the shop could name. The failure cases are specific.
The first is seasonality: a shop that sells a crop twelve months a year is importing for at least
part of it, and the label does not usually change in July. The second is aggregation: a
cooperative, of which the grower is one of four hundred members, can truthfully describe produce
as coming from its members while a given crate has no traceable field at all. The third is
processing: a thing grown four hundred kilometres away and packed ten kilometres away is, under
several voluntary schemes, locally packed, which on a label looks identical.

The defence of the word is worth stating, because the critique is easy and the alternative is
worse. A buyer who knows nothing uses the word as a weak signal and is usually not badly misled by
it. A buyer who insists on a number ends up with a certification scheme, and certification costs
money, and the growers who cannot afford it are precisely the smallholders the word was supposed
to protect. Replace a vague word with a precise one and you have not made the system more honest.
You have made it more expensive to enter.

What would help is not a definition but a habit: printing the distance, printing the month, and
printing the name of the farm the crate came from. None of those requires a scheme. All of them
are harder to write on a label than the word local, which is the actual reason they are not there.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Esther, or Meru

The stall's tomatoes come from Esther at Limuru, forty kilometres away, from November to June. For
the other four months she has none, and the stall buys from Meru, four hundred kilometres away, at
about two thirds of the price.

The stall next door sells Meru tomatoes all year and is eleven shillings a kilo cheaper. Customers
have started saying so.

- **Buy from Esther all year** — she can grow under netting, but the cost rises and the quality in
  July is honestly worse.
- **Buy from Meru all year** and stop saying local. Cheaper, consistent, and Esther loses a third
  of her income.
- **Split it, and say so on the board** — Esther in season, Meru out of season, with the distance
  and the month printed.

1. **The two labels** from Part 5.
2. **The chart**: what the price difference is, and how much of it reaches the grower.
3. **The voice.** Esther, thirty seconds: *"November to June I can plan. July I do other work. If
   you take November to June, I don't do other work — I stop."*

### 11B · Steelman

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think they should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> Split it and print the month and the distance on the board.
>
> Buying from Esther all year is the answer I want and I think it is the sentimental one. Netting
> raises her cost, the July crop is worse, and a stall that sells a bad tomato in July does not
> get the customer back in November. It also quietly makes her dependent on a single buyer, which
> is not a kindness.
>
> Going to Meru all year is coherent and it is the one I would defend if the stall were failing.
> It is not. Eleven shillings a kilo is a real difference and it is not an existential one, and
> Esther's thirty seconds is the whole argument against it: the eight months are what make the
> other four survivable.
>
> Printing the month and the distance costs nothing and it is the only option that makes the
> customer's complaint answerable. "Why is it dearer?" currently has no answer at the stall. It
> should have one, in writing, before anybody asks.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** They printed it. In the first month three customers said they had not known,
> and two of them bought the cheaper ones anyway and said so pleasantly.
>
> Takings fell about four per cent over the year. Esther's November-to-June order grew by a fifth,
> because two restaurants saw the board and asked who she was.
>
> The stall next door copied the board in March and now prints the distance too. Theirs says 400 km
> every month of the year, and their takings did not move at all.

**The honest board cost them four per cent and found Esther two restaurants. And the competitor
proved the information changes nothing for people who were never going to pay more.**

*Five things a reporting verb can do — The reported words do not change. The verb changes what the reader concludes.*


### 3C · Know

| Group | Verbs | What the writer is doing |
|---|---|---|
| **neutral** | say · tell · state · report · add · note | reporting, taking no position |
| **doubting** | claim · allege · maintain · purport | signalling distance from the claim |
| **conceding** | admit · concede · acknowledge · accept | the speaker said something against their own interest |
| **confirming** | confirm · recall · point out · stress | the writer already had it |
| **refusing** | deny · refuse · decline to comment · dispute | the speaker pushed back |

**And the pattern each takes:**

> *admit / deny / recall* **+ -ing** or **+ that** · *refuse / claim* **+ to + verb** or **+ that** ·
> *insist / stress / point out* **+ that** only

> **Watch out.** *Admit* makes the speaker's own words costly to them. Using it where *say* would
> do is not neutral reporting — it is an edit with no fingerprint on it.

> **Why it matters.** Change one verb and you have changed what the reader thinks, without
> changing a single reported word. This is the most powerful thing in this book and the hardest to
> see.

**The quote I gave**

I said eleven words to a journalist in 2019 and I have been described by them ever since. The
words were accurate. I said them. They are in my voice on her recording, and if I heard somebody
else say them I would think exactly what everybody thought about me.

What I did not say, in the forty minutes either side of them, was the part that made them make
sense. She did not leave it out to damage me. She left it out because it took two minutes to
explain and the eleven words took four seconds, and she had six hundred words for the whole piece.
I have since written to a length limit myself and I know what it does to you.

For about three years I told this story as something that was done to me. I was good at telling
it. There is a version with a pause in it that gets a reaction.

The version is not honest, and I worked out why in an argument with somebody who had been in the
room. She pointed out that I had chosen the eleven words. Not the edit — the words. I had reached
for a phrase that was sharper than what I actually thought, because I was being recorded and
because sharp phrases get agreed with. The journalist did not put a performance in my mouth. She
printed one I had already given her.

That is a less comfortable story and it has no pause in it.

What I do now is simple and I recommend it without being certain it is right. I say the dull
version first. If it is the only thing I say, it is the only thing that can be used. The cost is
that I am quoted much less often, and that the people who are quoted instead are the people
reaching for the sharp phrase, which is how the sharp phrase became the thing people believe.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The profile

A journalist wants to write about Jengo House. She is good, the piece will be read, and two of the
six tenants would benefit from it enormously.

The story she wants is about the accessible toilet: four months, an insurer, a landlord who moved
only when his own money was at risk, and a woman who left before it was built.

Priya has not agreed to be named. She has not refused either. She has said *"I'd rather not be the
reason."*

- **Do the piece with the story she wants.** Priya is central whether or not she is named — in a
  building of six people, "a tenant" is not anonymity.
- **Offer a different story** — the workspace, the economics, the survey that came in at 96,000.
  She will probably decline.
- **Refuse.** Keep the relationship, lose the coverage, and the story goes untold.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The transcript and the published piece** from Part 5.
2. **The chart**: what happens to a quote between recording and publication.
3. **The voice.** Priya, thirty seconds: *"I'd rather not be the reason. I'm not saying don't. I'm
   saying I'd rather not be the reason."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think they should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> Go back to Priya and ask her the actual question, which nobody has asked her.
>
> I am aware this is not one of the three options and that it delays everything by a week. It is
> the only move that does not decide something about her without her.
>
> "I'd rather not be the reason" is not a refusal and treating it as consent is the thing this
> whole unit is about. It is also not an invitation to protect her by deciding for her, which is
> what refusing the piece would be. She is an adult with an interest in the building and in her
> own privacy, and she is the only person who can weigh them.
>
> The question is not "may we name you". It is: "the piece will exist, you will be identifiable
> whether or not you are named, and here are the three versions. Which would you rather read about
> yourself?" That is a harder question and it is the honest one.
>
> If she says no after that, refuse the piece and say why.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** They asked her. She thought about it for four days and said yes, named, on
> condition that the piece said she had left for a job and not because of the building — which was
> true, and which she had never said to anybody.
>
> The piece ran. It was fair. Two sentences about her were lifted into a follow-up article
> elsewhere without the condition attached, and that version is the one that comes up when you
> search her name.
>
> She has never mentioned it. Wanjiru found it eight months later.

**They did the honest thing, and it was honest, and a second publication they had no contact with
undid the only condition she asked for.**

*How big is the difference? — The number does not change. The modifier is the offer.*


### 3C · Know

| Size | Modifier | Example |
|---|---|---|
| tiny | slightly · marginally · a little · a bit | *slightly better* |
| moderate | somewhat · rather | *somewhat better* |
| large | much · far · a lot · considerably · substantially · significantly | *far better* |
| enormous | a great deal · infinitely · appreciably | *a great deal better* |
| not at all | nowhere near · not nearly · barely | *nowhere near enough* |
| equal | just as · every bit as | *just as good* |

**The *more … the more* structure**

> **The more** hours she takes, **the less** she can choose.
> **The sooner** we write it down, **the better**.

Comparative first in each half. No *that*. The second half is the consequence.

> **Watch out.** *Very* modifies an adjective; it cannot modify a comparative. *Very good* ✓
> *very better* ✗. Use *much*, *far* or *a lot*.

> **Why it matters.** In a negotiation, the modifier is the offer. *Slightly more* and
> *considerably more* can describe the same number, and the person across the table hears the
> word, not the number.

### 5A · Predict

### 5B · Skim — sixty seconds

Find the paragraph where the reviewer praises the book most.

---

**The chapter that isn't there**

There is a chapter missing from this otherwise excellent book, and I think I know why.

*Handshakes* is a study of informal work arrangements across four cities, and it is far better
than the literature it joins. The fieldwork is exhaustive — four hundred and eighty interviews
over three years — and the author resists almost every temptation available to her. She does not
romanticise informality, which is the standard failure of the sympathetic account. She does not
treat it as simply exploitation waiting to be formalised, which is the standard failure of the
policy account. She is considerably better than both camps at holding two things at once: that
informal arrangements are often chosen, and that being able to choose them is not the same as
having a choice.

The best chapter is the fourth, on what she calls the goodwill ledger. Her argument is that a
long informal arrangement accumulates a kind of credit on both sides — the worker who has stayed,
the employer who has paid in hard months — and that this credit is real, is valuable, and is
destroyed by formalisation. A contract converts six years of mutual obligation into twelve months'
notice. That is a far worse deal than it looks, and nobody records it as a loss because it was
never recorded as an asset.

What is missing is the chapter on what happens afterwards. The book follows people up to the
moment of formalisation and stops. In the two cases where she does follow through, both workers
end up marginally better paid and substantially less free, and in one of them the arrangement
ends within a year — and the author notes this in a footnote.

A footnote. The most important finding in four hundred and eighty interviews, in a footnote.

I do not think this is carelessness. I think the finding is uncomfortable for everybody who would
otherwise agree with her, including the reviewer, and that she knew it and did not want the book
to be read as an argument against writing things down. It would have been. It would also have
been the honest version, and the book is good enough that it could have carried it.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Grace's contract

Grace has cleaned Jengo House for six years. There is no contract. She is paid weekly in cash,
sets her own hours, and does other work on Tuesdays and Thursdays that nobody has ever asked about.

The tenants want to put her on a written agreement: twelve fixed hours, nine per cent more an
hour, three months' notice, paid by transfer.

- **Formalise it as proposed.** More money, legal protection, and her Tuesdays become a problem
  she has to raise.
- **Leave it.** She has said she is happy. Six years says something. And she has no notice, no
  record, and nothing if she is ill.
- **Formalise it on her terms** — she sets the hours, the rate is benchmarked, and the agreement
  says so in writing. It is harder to administer and two of the six tenants will object.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The two sides of the agreement** from Part 5.
2. **The chart**: what she earns now, what she would earn, and what she loses.
3. **The voice.** Grace, thirty seconds: *"It's a lot more than I thought. I'd just want to know
   what happens on a Tuesday, and I don't want to have to ask every week."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think they should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> Formalise it on her terms, and accept that it is harder and that two people will object.
>
> Leaving it is the option that respects her stated view and I think it fails her. Six years with
> nothing written down is not an arrangement; it is an arrangement that has not yet been tested.
> The test arrives when somebody is ill, or when the building changes hands, and at that point
> goodwill is held entirely by people who will have moved on.
>
> Formalising it as proposed is worse than leaving it. It takes the one thing she has — control of
> her own week — and converts it into a clause she would have to negotiate for, having just been
> given a rise. Her thirty seconds is explicit about this and it is the only new information
> anybody has: she did not ask about the money.
>
> The third option costs the tenants administrative effort and costs her nothing. That asymmetry
> is the argument. The two who object will be objecting to effort, and it is not their Tuesdays.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** They wrote it her way. The hours clause says *at times set by Grace, not less
> than twelve hours weekly*, which took four drafts because nobody had written a clause like it
> before and two of the drafts were worse than no clause at all.
>
> Eleven months later the building changed hands. The new owner's managing agent asked for all
> service arrangements in writing within fourteen days. Grace's was the only one that existed.
>
> She was kept on. Two of the other arrangements in the building — a window cleaner and a man who
> did the gutters — were not.

**The clause nobody knew how to write was the only thing in the building that survived the sale.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And: the window cleaner had been there nine years. Whose job was it to
> write his down?
