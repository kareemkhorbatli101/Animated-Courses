## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### If it happened again

### 3A · Notice

Look at the four sentences in Part 2.

1. In sentence 1, has it happened again? **So why is the verb in the past?**
2. Sentence 2 says *if I were you*. **Not *was*. Why?**
3. Sentence 3 compares two bad outcomes. **Which does he prefer, and is that a fact or a value?**
4. **First conditional or second?** *If it rains, we'll move the car.* / *If it flooded again,
   we'd move it.* What is the difference in how likely each one is?
5. Try *"If it would happen again, I'd move it."* **What is wrong?**

### 3B · See


![A branching diagram from one situation into a first conditional for a real possibility and a second conditional for an imagined one.](figures/fig_a22_u01_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*Two conditionals, two distances — The grammar is not about time. It is about how far away you think the thing is.*


Now look at the second picture. **The ladder runs from certain to impossible.
Where does the second conditional sit?**


![A ladder from a certain future at the top through likely and unlikely to impossible at the bottom, with the matching form on each rung.](figures/fig_a22_u01_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*From certain to impossible — Five rungs. The second conditional lives on the middle three and is comfortable there.*


### 3C · Know

| | Shape | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **first conditional** | *if* + present , *will* | a real possibility · *If it floods, we'**ll** move.* |
| **second conditional** | *if* + **past** , *would* | unlikely, imagined, or hypothetical · *If it **flooded** again, we'**d** move.* |

> **The past tense is not about time.** It is about distance. The further a thing is from
> happening, the further back the verb goes.

> ***were*, not *was*, after *if I*.** *If I **were** you* is the fixed form. *If I was you*
> is heard everywhere and is still the one to avoid in writing.

> **would in four jobs:** *I'd move it* (result) · *I'd rather* (preference) ·
> *Would you mind* (request) · *I'd have gone* (a past you cannot change)

> **Watch out.** ✗ *If it would happen* ✓ *If it happened*
> *would* lives in the result half, never in the *if* half.

### 3D · Use — controlled

> If it (1) __________ (happen) again, Donal (2) __________ (move) the car on the Wednesday.
> If Eimear (3) __________ (be) ready, she (4) __________ (not lose) the kitchen floor. If I
> (5) __________ (be) you, I (6) __________ (not wait) for the council. And if the wall
> (7) __________ (be) two feet higher, none of us (8) __________ (be) having this conversation.

### 3E · Use — guided

Make each one a second conditional.

1. I don't have the money, so I don't move.
2. We aren't ready, so we worry.
3. He doesn't ask, so nobody helps him.
4. The hall isn't open, so people sleep in cars.
5. I'm not you, so I can't decide.

### 3F · Use — open

**Ask three people:** *If you had to leave your home in an hour, what three things would you
take?* **Then ask them why the third one.**

### 3G · Check — six items

> 1. If it ______ (happen) again, I ______ (move) it earlier.
> 2. If I ______ you, I ______ (not wait).
> 3. We ______ (be) safe if the wall ______ (be) higher.
> 4. I ______ rather look foolish than lose a car.
> 5. What ______ you do if the water ______ (come) tonight?
> 6. If she ______ (have) insurance, she ______ (not be) in that house now.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

An interview transcript with a resident. The title is
**"I had sandbags in the shed since 2019."**
**What do you expect her to say about them?** One sentence.

### 5B · Read — one hundred seconds

---

**"I had sandbags in the shed since 2019"**
*Eimear Nolan, 14 Strand Road*

**When did you know it was coming in?**
About ten. The drain in the yard started going the wrong way. That is the thing nobody tells you.
It does not come in the door first.

**You had sandbags.**
I had sandbags. I had had them in the shed since 2019, when there was a warning that came to
nothing, and on the Thursday I thought about putting them out and I did not, because Donal had
moved his car and everyone was being funny about it and I did not want to be the second one.

**Would you do it differently?**
I would put them out. But I want to be careful here, because that is easy to say in September.
If you asked me in January, with a forecast and a shed and everybody watching, I honestly do not
know that I would.

**What would you change, then?**
I would not wait to see what anybody else did. That is the one. Not the sandbags — the waiting.

**And twelve centimetres.**
Twelve centimetres is nothing until it is in your kitchen. It takes eight months to dry a floor.
You cannot put a new one down until it is dry and you cannot tell when it is dry, so you wait,
and then you put one down and sometimes you are wrong.

5. Why does it take eight months to dry a floor?
6. **What is the difference between "the sandbags" and "the waiting"?**

### 8A · Read the model

> If it happened again I would not wait to see what the neighbours did. That is the only thing I
> would change and it is not the thing people expect me to say. `[the change, named, and the
> surprise acknowledged]`
>
> I would still keep the sandbags in the shed rather than by the door, because the shed is dry
> and the door is not, and a wet sandbag is a bag of mud. `[something kept, with a technical
> reason, which proves she has thought about it]`
>
> I would not promise to be braver. I would promise to be two hours earlier. `[the honest
> limit — and it is specific and achievable]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The second paragraph defends something she did **right**. **Why include it?**
2. The last line refuses to promise courage. **Is that weakness or precision?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> If it happened again I would be much more prepared. I would act faster and I would not make the
> same mistakes. We have all learned a lot from this experience and we would all do things
> differently next time.

Correct English, and there is nothing in it. **Name two things it does not say.**

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The wall, or the three houses

There is 1.8 million. It is enough to build the sea wall badly along its whole length, or to
move the three nearest houses and do nothing else.

- **The wall.** Protects fourteen houses to the standard that failed in January, plus about
  thirty centimetres.
- **The three houses.** Buys and demolishes them. The three families are rehoused and the gap
  becomes a flood meadow. The other eleven houses are no safer than they were.
- **Neither, yet.** Spend 90,000 on a proper survey and come back in a year with a number that
  means something.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The policy summary** from Part 5E — and what is not covered.
2. **The chart** — water height at each house on the night, against floor level.
3. **The voice.** One of the three, twenty seconds: *"I would take the money. I want to be clear
   that I would take it, because I think people will say afterwards that we were pushed out, and
   we would not be. We would be glad."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Everybody says one. Use the frame.

> *If they built ______ , then ______ . So I think they should ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *risk · at risk · damage · protect · recover · cost · safe · in time*

### 11C · Decide

Write **two sentences**. Choose, and say why.

> I think they should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> They should do the survey. Both of the other options are being chosen on the basis of one
> night's water, measured afterwards by people wading through it, and a wall built to the wrong
> height is worse than no wall because it is believed. Ninety thousand to find out is cheap
> against 1.8 million to find out you were wrong.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** They did the survey. It took fourteen months rather than twelve and cost
> 118,000.
>
> It found that the water in January had not come over the wall at all. It had come **up through
> the old storm drain**, which was built in 1961 and connects to the harbour below the wall line.
> A higher wall would have made no difference to eleven of the fourteen houses.
>
> Fixing the drain is costed at 340,000. There is now an argument about what to do with the rest
> of the money, and two of the three families have said they no longer want to move.

**The survey was right. And it cost fourteen months, during which nothing was built.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say what you would do with the remaining 1.34
> million.

### 3C · Know

| | Shape | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **present perfect simple** | *have* + past participle | the **result**, and how many · *We'**ve done** the roof. I'**ve phoned** four times.* |
| **present perfect continuous** | *have been* + *-ing* | the **activity**, and how long · *We'**ve been doing** the roof since June.* |

> **The question each one answers.**
> *How many?* → simple. *How long?* → continuous.

> **Some verbs have no continuous.** *know, believe, own, belong, cost, mean*.
> ✓ *I've known him for years.* ✗ *I've been knowing him.*

> **Both take *for* and *since*.** *for* + a length, *since* + a point.
> You met this in A2.1 Unit 1 and it has not changed.

> **Watch out.** The continuous dislikes numbers.
> ✗ *I've been phoning him four times.* ✓ *I've phoned him four times.* ·
> ✓ *I've been phoning him all week.*

# Her footage, their broadcast

A national news channel has been using Aoife's phone footage of the flood for eight months.
Nobody asked her. The coverage has brought in about 90,000 in donations to the town.

- **Say nothing.** The money is real. She is eighteen and does not want a fight.
- **Ask for a credit.** Her name on the footage. No money. It is still her work.
- **Ask to be paid.** The channel has a rate card for user footage. It is about 250 a clip,
  and they have used four.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The progress report** from Part 5E — what the donations have actually paid for.
2. **The chart** — donations over the eight months, against each broadcast.
3. **The voice.** Aoife, twenty seconds: *"I've been watching it on the television since
   January. I don't mind. What I mind is that my mother is in one of those clips and nobody
   asked her either, and she is not eighteen and she did not film anything."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Everybody says one. Use the frame.

> *I think Aoife should ______ , because ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *fair · her work · donations · credit · ask · permission · progress · funding*

### 11C · Decide

Write **two sentences**. Choose, and say why.

> I think Aoife should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> She should ask for the credit and raise her mother separately, in writing, as a different
> question. Mixing the two makes both easy to refuse: the credit is a small thing a producer can
> grant in an afternoon, and the consent is a thing a lawyer has to look at. Asked together they
> will be answered together, which means slowly.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** She asked for the credit by email on a Tuesday and had it by Friday. Her
> name has been on the footage since.
>
> She raised her mother in a separate letter. That took eleven weeks and three people, and ended
> with the clip being edited rather than removed, which her mother says she is fine with and
> Aoife is not sure about.
>
> She has not asked to be paid. She says that if she had asked for money first, she does not
> think she would have got the credit.

**The small ask worked in three days. The important one took eleven weeks and was half met.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say whether she should ask for the money now.

## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### The past before the past

### 3A · Notice

Look at the four sentences in Part 2.

1. Sentence 1 and sentence 2 are about the same forecast. **What does sentence 2 add?**
2. In sentence 3, which happened first — arriving, or starting?
3. In sentence 4, could you use a plain past? **Would anything be lost?**
4. **When do you actually need the past perfect?** Try: *First he moved the car. Then it flooded.*
5. Try *"By the time we got there, it already started."* **What is missing?**

### 3B · See


![A timeline showing a past-simple event and, before it, an earlier event drawn with had plus past participle.](figures/fig_a22_u03_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*The past before the past — Two events in the past. The earlier one takes had.*


Now look at the second picture. **Two events, one line. Which one gets the *had*?**


![Two parallel sentences compared, one telling two events in the order they happened and one telling them in reverse with the past perfect.](figures/fig_a22_u03_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*Forwards, and backwards — The same two events, told in two directions. Only one of them needs had.*


### 3C · Know

| | Shape | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **past simple** | *moved, changed, decided* | events in the order they happened |
| **past perfect** | *had* + past participle | an event **before** the one you are already talking about |

> **You do not need it when the order is already clear.**
> ✓ *He moved the car. Then it flooded.* — the order is the sentence order.
> **You do need it when you go backwards.**
> ✓ *It flooded. He **had moved** the car two days earlier.*

> **The words that invite it:** *by the time · already · before · until · once · previously ·
> the night before · up to that point*

> **Watch out.** *had* is the auxiliary for everybody. ✗ *he hadded* ✓ *he had moved*
> And in speech it is almost never a full word: *it'd already started*.

> **Why it matters.** An inquiry is not asking what happened. It is asking **what had already
> happened when each person decided.**

### 3D · Use — controlled

> By the time the hall opened, the water (1) __________ (already / reach) the doors. Donal
> (2) __________ (move) his car on the Thursday, two days before, because the forecast
> (3) __________ (change) that morning. Eimear (4) __________ (decide) against the sandbags
> after he (5) __________ (move) it, and she says now that she
> (6) __________ (not know) the forecast (7) __________ (change) at all.

### 3E · Use — guided

### 3C · Know

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| *"The water **is** at the door."* | She said the water **was** at the door. |
| *"I **checked** the clock."* | She said she **had checked** the clock. |
| *"I **will** phone you."* | She said she **would** phone me. |
| *"I **can** see it."* | She said she **could** see it. |

> **Everything shifts one step back**, and so do *I/you*, *here/there*, *now/then*,
> *today/that day*.

> **say** and **tell** are not the same shape.
> ✓ *She **said that** she had checked.* ✓ *She **told me that** she had checked.*
> ✗ *She said me…* ✗ *She told that…*

> **You do not have to shift** if the thing is still true.
> ✓ *She said the drain **is** still blocked.* — because it is.

> **The reporting verb is never neutral for long.** *said* is the flat one.
> *insisted*, *claimed*, *admitted* and *revealed* all add a judgement, and a reader hears it.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# One time goes in the report

Three accounts, forty minutes apart, all honest. The report can carry one time in its summary.

- **23.30, the metadata.** The most accurate instrument. It is also the only one with no person
  behind it.
- **A range: 23.00 to 23.40.** Honest about the uncertainty. Harder to use, and a range in a
  summary tends to be read as its lowest number.
- **All three, named, with their instruments.** Six lines instead of one. Nobody is wrong, and
  nobody can quote it.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The newspaper article** from Part 5E — and what it quoted.

   summary. But a time that nobody in that town recognises is not a finding either, and I have

### 3B · See


![A reported question with brackets marking the reporting verb, the question word and the statement word order that follows.](figures/fig_a22_u05_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*What happens to a reported question — The question word stays. The inversion goes, the auxiliary goes, and so does the mark.*


Now look at the second picture. **Three shapes: a wh- question, a yes/no question, and an
instruction. Which one needs *if* or *whether*?**


![A branching diagram from direct speech into a reported wh- question, a reported yes/no question with whether, and a reported instruction with to.](figures/fig_a22_u05_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*Three things to report, three shapes — A wh- question, a yes/no question and an instruction each take their own form.*


### 3C · Know

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| *"**How deep was** it?"* | He asked **how deep it was**. |
| *"**Did you** measure it?"* | He asked **whether / if I had measured it**. |
| *"**Measure** it."* | He told me **to measure** it. |
| *"**Don't** guess."* | He told me **not to guess**. |

> **Three things happen to a reported question.**
> 1. Statement word order — no inversion. 2. No *do/did*. 3. No question mark.

> **Yes/no questions need *if* or *whether*.** *whether* is the safer one in writing, and the
> only one possible before *or not*.

> **Instructions take *to* + verb**, and the verb that reports them takes a person:
> *tell **somebody** to*, *ask **somebody** to*, *want **somebody** to*, *advise **somebody** to*.
> ✗ *He said me to measure it.* — *say* does not take this pattern at all.

> **Why it matters.** The shape of the question changes the answer. A survey is only ever as
> good as the shape of its questions, and nobody checks the shape afterwards.

### 5B · Read — one hundred and ten seconds

---

**Fifty-one doors and the wrong question**

A survey is two things: a list of questions, and a decision about who is asked. Both can be done
carefully and the result can still be useless.

The Ballinmore survey was done carefully. Fifty-one households, six weeks, forty-four responses,
which is a response rate most professional surveys would envy. Every question was clear. Nobody
was led.

It asked how deep the water had been, when it arrived, what was damaged, whether people had
received a warning, and whether they intended to stay. It did not ask where the water came from,
and that was the question the money depended on.

This is the commonest failure in survey design and it has a name: **you cannot find an answer to
a question you did not ask.** The data is not wrong. It is simply about something else.

The second failure is quieter. Everybody asked was a household on the flood plain. Nobody asked
the harbour master, who has watched that drain for twenty-two years, because he does not live in
a flooded house and so was not on the list. The list was drawn up correctly, from the correct
register, and it excluded the one person who could have answered the question that was not asked.

Redoing the survey takes six weeks. Publishing it as it stands takes an afternoon, and it will be
quoted for ten years.

### 3C · Know

| | About now | About the past |
|---|---|---|
| **almost certain yes** | *It **must be** the current.* | *It **must have been** the current.* |
| **possible** | *It **might / could be** the engine.* | *It **might / could have been** the engine.* |
| **almost certain no** | *It **can't be** him.* | *It **can't have been** him.* |

> **The negative of *must* is *can't*, not *mustn't*.**
> *mustn't* is about rules. *can't have* is about evidence.

> **These are not about ability or permission.** They are about **how much you know**.
> *He can't swim* is ability. *He can't have swum* is a deduction.

> **Adverbs do the same job and can be stacked — carefully.**
> *definitely · surely · obviously · clearly · presumably · apparently · possibly*
> ✗ *It must have been the current, possibly.* — pick one level and stay there.

> **Watch out.** In speech these are /mʌstəv/ and /maɪtəv/. In writing they are **never**
> *must of* or *might of*.

| **future continuous** | *will be* + *-ing* | a future that will simply be running · *I'**ll be** taking the boat out at four.* |

> **The future continuous is the least dramatic of the three**, and that is its point.
> It describes a future as **ordinary**, as something already in motion, with no decision
> being made in the sentence.

> **It is also the polite one.** *Will you be coming?* is softer than *Are you coming?*,
> because it asks about your arrangements rather than your intentions.

> **The time expressions it likes:** *this time next year · by then · by the end of ·
> in a year's time · eventually · meanwhile*

> **Watch out.** State verbs still have no continuous.
> ✗ *I'll be knowing by March.* ✓ *I'll know by March.*

### Adding something, rather than narrowing it

### 3A · Notice

### 3B · See


![One sentence shown with brackets marking the relative clause and the commas that make it non-defining.](figures/fig_a22_u08_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*Two marks, two meanings — The commas are the difference between describing a hall and choosing between halls.*


Now look at the second picture. **One sentence, two readings, and the only difference is two
marks.**


![Two parallel sentences compared, one with a defining relative clause and no commas and one with a non-defining clause between commas.](figures/fig_a22_u08_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*Defining, and non-defining — The same words. One of them tells you there is another hall.*


### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **defining** | no commas · tells you **which one** | *The hall **that was built in 1884*** *(so there is another hall)* |
| **non-defining** | commas · **adds** information | *The hall, **which was built in 1884**,* *(there is one hall)* |

> **Three rules for non-defining clauses.**
> 1. Always commas — one before and one after, unless the sentence ends.
> 2. Never ***that***. Use *which*, *who*, *whose*, *where*, *when*.
> 3. You can always delete the whole clause and the sentence survives.

> **With a proper name it is almost always non-defining**, because a name already identifies.
> ✓ *Sinéad, who has run it for nine years, …* ✗ *Sinéad who has run it for nine years …*

> **Quantity phrases take *of which* and *of whom*.**
> *eleven buildings, **most of which** are below the line* ·

### Saying how many, exactly

### 3A · Notice

### 3B · See


![A ladder of quantifiers from all at the top down through most, half and several to none at the bottom, with the of rule on each rung.](figures/fig_a22_u09_p03_v09.png){width=6.6in}

*One bar, cut six ways — Six quantifiers, from everything to nothing, and two of them are only ever about two.*


Now look at the second picture. **One bar, cut six ways.
Which cut is *most*, and which is *several*?**


![A sentence with brackets marking the quantifier, the optional of, and the pronoun that makes it compulsory.](figures/fig_a22_u09_p03_v09b.png){width=6.6in}

*Where of is compulsory — Three slots, and only one of them can be empty.*


### 3C · Know

| | How many | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **all** | 100 % | *all **the** claims* · *all **of** them* |
| **most** | more than half | *most **of** them* |
| **half / several / a couple of** | in between | *half **of** it* · *several **of** them* |
| **both** | exactly 2 | *both **of** them* — only two |
| **either** | one of 2 | *either **of** them*, singular verb |
| **neither** | 0 of 2 | *neither **of** them* **is** — singular verb |
| **none** | 0 of any number | *none **of** it* / *none **of** them* |

> **The *of* rule.** Before a pronoun, *of* is compulsory: *all **of** them*, never *all them*.
> Before *the* or *my*, *of* is optional: *all (of) the claims*.
> Before a bare plural, there is no *of*: *all claims are honest* — and that is a general
> statement about every claim in the world.

> **Countable or not.** *none of them* (the claims) · *none of it* (the money).
> *few* and *several* go with countables. *little* and *much* go with uncountables.

> **Watch out.** *both*, *either* and *neither* are only ever about **two**.
> ✗ *both of the four claims* ✓ *all four claims*

**Everybody wants a fair share and nobody means the same thing**

There is €180,000. There are four claims, totalling €246,000. All four are honest. That is the
whole situation and it has no solution, only decisions.

When people in that room say *fair*, they mean one of four things, and the argument is not about

quietly assumed into the budget ever since.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# One hundred and eighty thousand

Four claims. €238,500 asked for. €180,000 available. All four honest.

- **Equal.** €45,000 each. Two of the four cannot start work with that, so two of the four
  projects do not happen and the money sits.

  get nothing and wait for their insurers.

### 11D · Model decision

> By need, and the committee should say the word *need* out loud and vote on it by name. Equal

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

A feature article. The title is **"The town that came back, and the seven people who did not."**
**What do you expect the writer to be doing?** One sentence.

### 5B · Read — one hundred and twenty seconds

---

**The town that came back, and the seven people who did not**

The campaign is good. I want to begin there, because the easy piece to write about Ballinmore
this autumn is the cynical one and it would also be wrong.

The photographs are of real people doing real things and nobody is pretending the hall is
finished. The line — *the town that came back* — is three years of work by people who were not
paid for most of it, and the agency did not invent it. Somebody in the town said it first, in the
hall, in February, and nobody wrote down who.

Here is what it leaves out. Of the fifty-one households surveyed, seven have gone. Two sold, two
were bought out, and three are simply not here and the houses are shut. Seven out of fifty-one is
fourteen per cent, which is the figure that does not appear in the brochure, and I understand why
it does not: nobody markets a place with a number like that, and the people who left are not
available to be photographed.

The town did come back. It came back smaller, and it is carrying a sea wall decision it has not
made and a hall it has decided to keep without quite saying why.

This is not an objection to the campaign. It is a note that the story is true and the story is
also an edit, and that a place which has spent a year insisting on accurate times and honest
reporting is about to agree to a sentence about itself that it would not have accepted about
anybody else.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# A sentence about yourselves

The agency will run *the town that came back* for three years. It would bring about nine thousand
visitors a year. Seven of the fifty-one households have gone.

- **Agree.** It is true. The town did come back, and the money would pay for the drain.
- **Refuse.** Eimear says it is a story about what happened to the town told by people it did not
  happen to, and that the town spent a year insisting on accuracy.
- **Agree, with a page.** The campaign runs. The town writes one page of the brochure, in its own
  words, including the seven.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The brochure page** from Part 5E — and the numbers that are not on it.
2. **The chart** — households, before, after, and now.
3. **The voice.** One of the seven, by phone, twenty seconds: *"I've no objection at all. I'd
   like to be in it, actually. I'd like somebody to put in writing that we were there, because
   in about ten years the only record of it will be the brochure, and we are not in the
   brochure."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Everybody says one. Use the frame.

> *I think the town should ______ , because ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *identity · truth · story · reputation · community · belong · outsider · genuine*

### 11C · Decide

Write **two sentences**. Choose, and say why.

> I think the town should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> Agree, with the page, and put the seven on it by name with their permission. The line is true
> and refusing it costs the drain; but a town that has spent a year arguing about eleven-minute
> clocks and forty-one-word notices cannot then accept a sentence about itself without a footnote.
> The page is the footnote. It also solves the thing the woman on the phone actually asked for,
> which nobody in that room had thought to ask her about.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** They agreed, with the page. The agency accepted in one meeting and was,
> by every account, relieved — it had expected a fight about the line itself.
>
> Six of the seven agreed to be named. The seventh asked not to be, and the page says *and one
> household who has asked not to be named*, which Eimear says is the best sentence on it.
>
> The campaign ran. Visitor numbers were about six thousand in the first year, not nine. The
> drain was funded in the second year, partly from visitor levy and mostly from the county, who
> had been going to fund it anyway and had not said so.

**They got the page. The numbers were two thirds of the promise. And the money was coming
regardless.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say what the page is actually for.
