## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### A modal is a promise about how sure you are

### 3A · Notice

Look at the five sentences in Part 2 again.

1. Which one commits the speaker most, and what evidence would it need?
2. Which one commits the speaker least while still asserting something?
3. Sentence 4 is about the past. How does the form show it?
4. Sentence 5 says nothing about how probable the effect is. **What does it say instead?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u03_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **must · is bound to** | the strongest inference short of proof | *It must be a real effect.* |
| **will · is likely to** | confident, and routine in forecasting | *That is likely to be the explanation.* |
| **should** | expected, with room to be wrong | *The replication should settle it.* |
| **may · might · could** | possible; no commitment at all | *It may well be chance.* |
| **can't · could hardly have** | excluded by the evidence | *It could hardly have been chance.* |

> **The epistemic modals are a scale, and the scale is public.** A reader who sees *must* and
> later learns the interval ran from 1.2 to 3.3 has caught the writer overclaiming, and will not
> forget it.

> **Deduction about the past takes the perfect.** *must have been*, *might well have been*,
> *could hardly have been*. The modal sets the certainty; the perfect sets the time. The two
> choices are independent, which is why every combination exists.

| | |
|---|---|
| **adverbs run the same scale** | *conceivably · arguably · presumably · demonstrably · unequivocally* |
| **and some of them do not** | *ostensibly · allegedly · reportedly* — these report somebody else's certainty, not yours |
| **the passive reporting frame** | *is said to · is reported to · has yet to be confirmed* |
| **the balanced statement** | *may or may not* — which is a refusal to place the claim at all |

> **Why it matters.** Every sentence about the future, about a cause, or about somebody else's
> data is a bet, and the modal is the size of the bet. Writers who always bet large are read once.

### 3D · Use — controlled

Complete with the modal that the evidence in brackets will support.

> The finding (1) ______ be chance, given the interval. *(it cannot be excluded)*
> At that cohort size the effect (2) ______ be real. *(as sure as the data allows)*
> The authors (3) ______ have seen the release before it went out. *(the evidence suggests not)*
> A replication (4) ______ settle it within two years. *(expected, with room)*
> Whether it applies to day workers (5) ______ or ______ ______ be the same question. *(explicitly balanced)*

### 3E · Use — guided

Rewrite each one so that the certainty matches the evidence in brackets.

1. Night shifts cause the condition. *(one cohort, not peer reviewed)*
2. The release was written by somebody who had not read the paper. *(a guess, past)*
3. The effect is chance. *(excluded by the interval)*
4. The paper will replicate. *(a hope, not a forecast)*
5. The authors disagree with the release. *(you have not asked them)*

### 3F · Use — open

**In pairs.** One of you states a claim at the top of the scale. The other asks *what would you
need to have seen?* **Keep going until the claim comes down to where the evidence actually is.**

### 3G · Error autopsy

> ✗ *It must to be a real effect.*
> ✗ *It might be chance last year, we cannot say.*
> ✗ *The evidence doesn't support that it is demonstrably possible.*

For each, name the rule and say what the writer was reaching for.

### 3H · Check — eight items

> 1. It ______ be chance; the interval includes 1.2. *(possible, uncommitted)*
> 2. At that cohort size it ______ be real. *(strongest inference)*
> 3. They ______ ______ seen the release first. *(past, strong inference, negative evidence)*
> 4. It ______ ______ ______ been chance at that interval. *(past, all but excluded)*
> 5. It ______ ______ ______ be the same in day workers. *(explicitly balanced, five words)*
> 6. ✗ *It must to be a real effect.* → correct it.
> 7. Rewrite *Night shifts cause it* so that it survives peer review.
> 8. Rewrite *We cannot rule it out* so that a reader can tell how likely it is.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

The title is **"Four in ten thousand."**
**What do you expect the headline on this story to have said instead, and why?**

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**Four in ten thousand**

The release arrived at nine. Night work, it said, doubles the risk of a rare heart-rhythm
condition, in a cohort of sixty-one thousand Danish adults followed for nine years.

The preprint says something narrower. In the group that worked no night shifts, the incidence was
four cases per ten thousand people per year. In the group working four or more nights a week it
was eight. That is a doubling of risk, and it is also four extra people in ten thousand.

The confidence interval runs from 1.2 to 3.3, which means the study is consistent with an effect
of almost nothing and with an effect three times larger than the one reported. The result is
statistically significant, just. The authors say, twice, that they cannot establish a causal link
and that the cohort was not designed to.

The release says *doubles* three times. It does not give the base rate anywhere in three hundred
and ten words.

By four o'clock the health reporter had the epidemiologist on the phone. "It may well be real,"
she said. "We cannot rule it out, and we cannot scale it up to the whole workforce either. Bear in
mind what it is out of."

The first edition closes at six. The desk has two numbers, both true, and room for one of them in
a headline.

---

### 5C · Comprehension — eight questions

1. How many people were in the cohort, and for how long?
2. What was the incidence in the group that worked no night shifts?
3. What does the confidence interval run from and to?
4. How many times does the release use the word *doubles*?
5. What do the authors say twice?
6. **Why does the writer give the length of the release in words?**
7. **"Both true" — what is the difference between the two numbers, if neither is wrong?**
8. **The last sentence says *room for one of them*. What is the real constraint being described?**

### 7B · Say

> *They might have read it, they should have read it, and they could have asked.* — three
> perfect modals in one breath, and not one full *have* among them.

### 7C · Make it mean something

> *It might be real.* — unstressed modal: the ordinary, uncommitted statement

why a stressed modal in a meeting is never neutral.

### A risk story, with every claim at its evidence · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> A Danish cohort of sixty-one thousand adults, followed for nine years, reports four extra cases
> of a rare heart-rhythm condition per ten thousand night workers per year. `[the absolute figure
> first, with what it is out of]`
>
> That is a doubling of the baseline, which was four in ten thousand. `[the relative figure
> second, and it is now interpretable]`
>
> The result is statistically significant, but the confidence interval runs from 1.2 to 3.3, so
> the effect may well be very small. `[the modal is set at the interval, not at the headline]`
>
> The authors could hardly have been clearer that they cannot show a causal link; the university's
> press release, which has yet to be corrected, says *doubles* three times and gives no base rate.
> `[past deduction with a perfect, then a reporting frame that places the blame precisely]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The absolute figure comes first. **What would the reader have done with the relative one alone?**
2. *May well be* appears once. **Why not *is*?**
3. The last sentence does not accuse anybody of lying. **What does it do instead, and how?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> Night work doubles your risk of a serious heart condition, a major new study has found. Experts say the findings are highly significant and could have implications for millions of workers. The research, which has yet to be published, is said to be the largest of its kind.

Three sentences, one real number hidden inside a ratio, and *significant* used in the wrong sense.
**Rewrite it so that a reader could decide what to do on Monday.**

### 8C · Plan

> the absolute figure, with its base rate → the ratio, now interpretable → the uncertainty, with
> the modal set at it → who said what, and what has yet to be done

### 8D · Write — 200 words
### 8E · Peer review — three criteria

> ☐ Does every number say what it is out of?
> ☐ Is each modal set where the interval puts it, not where the story would like it?
> ☐ Is every claim you did not verify attributed to whoever made it?

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The headline at six o'clock

Two numbers, both true, and room for one.

**Option one:** *Night work doubles heart risk, study finds.* True, accurate to the release, and
the number readers will remember.

**Option two:** *Night work linked to four extra cases in ten thousand.* True, accurate to the
preprint, and nobody will click on it.

**Option three:** run option one, and adopt a standing rule that no risk figure appears anywhere
in the paper without its base rate in the same sentence.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u03_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 3.6.** The standards editor, 30 seconds: *"We have run a hundred and forty risk stories
in a year. I asked two hundred readers what a doubling meant, and the median answer was fifty per
cent. Not fifty per cent more — fifty per cent of people. We are not informing anybody. We are
producing a number that happens to be correct and a belief that is wildly wrong, and then
defending the number."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and state your certainty setting every time you use it.

> *I would ______ , because ______ , and the evidence for that is ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and the thing you would accept as proof you
were wrong.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Option two is honest and unread, which helps nobody, and option one on its own
> produces the fifty-per-cent belief the standards editor measured. The base-rate rule costs nine
> words a story and is the only version where the figure the reader remembers is a figure they can
> use. It also has a cost worth naming: some stories will not survive their own base rate, and
> those are the stories the rule exists for.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald ran the first headline and adopted the rule. Nine months later the
> preprint failed peer review on the exposure definition and was withdrawn.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: the health reporter began returning press releases that gave no
> base rate, with a one-line note. Within a year, three university press offices in the city were
> putting the base rate in the first paragraph — not because anybody asked them to, but because
> the alternative was not being covered.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The rule was about the Herald's own sentences. Why did it change what other people wrote?
