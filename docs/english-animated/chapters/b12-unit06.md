### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b12_u06_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual · mirror**

### 3C · Know

| | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **modal passive** | *modal* + *be* + pp | *It **can be done** under local.* |
| **perfect passive** | *have* + *been* + pp | *She **has been referred**.* |
| **modal perfect passive** | *modal* + *have been* + pp | *She **should have been seen** in March.* |
| **causative: have** | *have* + object + pp | *She**'s having it done** on Thursday.* |
| **causative: get** | *get* + object + pp | *I **got my notes sent** to the clinic.* |

> **The causative keeps the patient as the subject** while making it clear that somebody else is
> doing the work. *She is having it done* is not the same as *she is doing it*, and it is not
> the same as *it is being done to her*: it says she arranged it, or that it is happening to her
> as part of her plan.

> ***get* is the informal causative** and carries a faint sense of effort or arrangement:
> *I got it sorted.* *Have* is neutral.

> **The verbs that take an object and a to-infinitive:** *cause, allow, permit, require, oblige,
> entitle, get.* The verbs that take an object and a bare infinitive: *make, let*.
> ✓ *They **made her wait**.* ✓ *They **required her to wait**.* ✗ *They made her to wait.*

> **In the passive, *make* takes *to* again.** ✓ *She **was made to wait**.*

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

A feature article. The title is
**"The four-hour queue exists to protect her, and she has never used it."**
**What do you expect the argument to be?** Two sentences.

### 5B · Read — two minutes and a quarter

---

**The four-hour queue exists to protect her, and she has never used it**

The policy is correct. I want to establish that before anything else, because the piece that
writes itself here is the one about heartless bureaucracy, and that piece would be wrong.

Family members should not interpret in clinical settings. The reasons are not procedural. A
daughter will soften a prognosis. A son will omit a question about alcohol. A sixteen-year-old
will be asked to say the word *terminal* to her own mother, and will be in a room for the rest of
her life afterwards. Every hospital that has studied this has found the same thing, and the
policy that follows from it is the right policy.

The telephone interpreting service that the policy requires has a median wait of four hours and
eleven minutes.

So here is what actually happens, and it happens in every hospital I have visited in three
countries. The clinician makes a judgement. For anything serious, they wait, and the four hours
are real and the clinic runs over and other patients are seen late and the policy works. For
anything routine — a consent for a minor procedure, a discharge explanation, a follow-up date —
they use the daughter, and they do not write down that they used the daughter, because writing it
down is a breach.

The policy therefore achieves two things. It protects patients in serious conversations, which is
what it was for. And it produces a permanent, undocumented, unmeasurable practice in routine
ones, which is the opposite of what a policy is for.

The queue is not a bureaucratic failure attached to a good policy. The queue **is** the policy,
in the sense that a rule that cannot be followed is a rule about what gets written down.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The daughter in the corridor

A patient is being consented for a minor procedure. Her daughter, who is forty-one, is in the
corridor. The policy requires a registered interpreter. The telephone service has a median wait
of four hours and eleven minutes.

- **Follow the policy.** Wait. The clinic runs over by four hours, eleven other patients are
  seen late, and three of them will not be seen today.
- **Use the daughter and record it.** The policy permits this in an emergency only, so recording
  it is recording a breach, which goes to the governance committee and names the clinician.
- **Use the daughter and do not record it.** What happens in practice, everywhere, and the reason
  nobody knows how often.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b12_u06_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual**

1. **The policy and the service figures** from Part 5E — and the emergency clause.
2. **The chart** — requests, waits and abandonments, by time of day.
3. **The voice.** The patient, through her daughter, thirty seconds: *"I would like my daughter
   to do it. I am aware that she will make it sound better than it is. I have thought about that
   and I would still like my daughter to do it, and nobody has asked me this before, which is the
   part I mind."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In fours. Everybody speaks twice. Use the frame.

> *If the policy is followed, ______ . If it is not, ______ . What is not recorded is ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *consent · procedure · interpreter · carer · be allowed to · be required
to · on behalf of · written consent · nobody explained any of it*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**. Choose, say why, and name what it costs.

> I think the clinician should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .
> What this costs is ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> Use the daughter, record it as a breach, and write one line saying why. The third option is the
> one everybody takes and it is the only one that guarantees nothing will ever change, because an
> unmeasured practice cannot be argued about. A breach that appears eleven times in a quarter,
> each with a four-hour wait beside it, is the only document that will ever get the service
> funded properly. It costs the clinician something. That is what it is for.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** Dr Benítez recorded it as a breach, with one line: *telephone service wait
> 4 h 20 min, routine consent, patient's explicit request, daughter aged 41.*
>
> The governance committee reviewed it in six weeks and recorded no concern. Over the following
> quarter, eleven more breaches were recorded with similar lines, nine of them by people who had
> seen his.
>
> The service's contract was renegotiated in the following year with a two-hour target for
> routine requests. The business case quotes the twelve breach records, which are the only
> documentation of the problem that has ever existed in this hospital.

**He took the cost. Nine people copied him. And twelve one-line records did what four years of
complaints had not.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say what should happen to the policy now.
