### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b13_u06_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual · branch**

### 3C · Know

| Pattern | Verbs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **verb + sb + *of* + -ing** | accuse · remind | *She accused them of writing a veto.* |
| **verb + sb + *to* + infinitive** | persuade · urge · advise · warn · remind | *They persuaded the Dean to wait.* |
| **verb + sb + *for* + -ing** | blame · congratulate | *He blamed them for the delay.* |
| **verb + sb + *on* + noun** | congratulate | *She congratulated them on the gift.* |
| **verb + sb + *against* + -ing** | warn · advise | *She warned them against saying it.* |
| **verb + *on* + -ing** | insist | *He insisted on seeing the draft.* |
| **verb + -ing** | deny · admit | *He denied writing it.* |
| **verb + *that* + clause** | insist · deny · admit · warn · remind | *He insisted that thirty days was normal.* |

> **The pattern is part of the word.** *Accuse* takes *of*; *blame* takes *for*; *congratulate*
> takes *on*. There is no rule behind this and no shortcut: learn the verb with its preposition
> or you have not learned the verb.

> **Four of them take two patterns**, and the meaning does not change: *insist on / insist
> that*, *deny doing / deny that*, *warn against / warn that*, *remind to / remind that*.

> **A preview of Unit 7.** *He is said to have written it* reports a claim without saying whose
> it is. That is the whole of next unit, and it is what this unit's grammar exists to make
> unnecessary.

> **Why it matters.** A report that loses the pattern loses the person, and a claim with no
> person attached is the most dangerous sentence in any document.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

**What do you expect the writer to admit?** Two sentences.

### 5B · Read — two minutes and a quarter

---

**I WROTE THE PRESS RELEASE**
*a personal essay*

I have worked in university communications for eleven years and I have written somewhere around
nine hundred press releases. I am going to describe one paragraph of one of them, because it is
the only thing I have written that I still think about.

The gift was four million dollars for a research wing. The agreement had nine pages. Clause 9.4
said that the sponsor would be given thirty days' sight of any publication arising from work
done in the wing, and could request amendments. Not require. Request.

I was given the agreement on a Tuesday and asked for a release by Thursday. I read all nine
pages, which I want on the record, because the thing people assume about my job is that we do
not read them.

I wrote: *The partnership includes standard arrangements for the review of research outputs
prior to publication.*

Every word in that sentence is true. *Standard* is doing the most work. The arrangement is not
standard — I checked four comparable agreements and three had no review clause at all — but the
sentence does not say it is common. It says it is standard, which is a word that means *of a
known type* and is heard as *normal*. I knew that when I wrote it. That is the craft, and I am
not going to pretend it was an accident.

What I did not do was lie, and what I also did not do was put the clause in the release, which
would have taken eleven words.

A researcher found it eighteen months later, in the agreement, which had been public the whole
time on a page three clicks from the homepage. She accused the university of hiding it. She was
wrong about hiding and right about everything else, and the thing I have not been able to get
past is that if she had been wrong about everything, I would have been fine.

### 9B · Three ways a turn can end

> **a gap** — one speaker stops, a beat, the next begins
> **an overlap** — the next begins slightly early, as agreement
> **a competitive interruption** — the next begins early, to take the floor

**Which two are easily mistaken for each other, and by whom?**

### 9C · The length of a comfortable silence

Reported averages: around **0.2 seconds** between turns in much of northern Europe; longer in
some conversational cultures, and noticeably longer in others again. The number matters less
than this: **whatever yours is, a pause longer than it feels like a problem**, and you will
start talking to fix a problem that is not there.

**Whose silence have you filled this week?**

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Four million dollars, and a paragraph about publishing

The gift funds a research wing for fifteen years. Clause 9.4 gives the sponsor thirty days'
sight of any publication and the right to request amendments. The sponsor will not remove it.
The Dean has until November.

- **Sign it.** Four million, a wing, forty researchers. The clause says *request*, not *require*,
  and no comparable agreement has ever been used to block anything.
- **Refuse the clause.** The sponsor has said it is not negotiable. They have said that about
  two other things already and moved on both.
- **Sign it and publish it.** Take the clause, and put it, in full, on the front page of the
  wing's website, alongside every request ever made under it.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b13_u06_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual**

1. **Clause 9.4 and the press release** from Part 5E — and the handwritten amendment.
2. **The chart** — review clauses in twelve comparable agreements, and what was requested under
   each.
3. **The voice.** A postdoctoral researcher, thirty seconds: *"Nobody will ever block a paper.
   They will not need to. I will know on the day I start what the thirty days are for, and in
   four years nobody will be able to point at a single thing I did not write, including me."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In fours. Everybody speaks twice. Use the frame.

> *They ______ us of ______ . We ______ on ______ . The sticking point is ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *claim · clause · leverage · concession · undertaking · stakeholder ·
spin · retraction · the small print · strings attached*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**. Choose, say why, and name what it costs.

> I think the university should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .
> What this costs is ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> Sign it and publish it. The clause is not the danger; the clause plus silence is. A review
> right that everybody can see is a review right nobody can use quietly, and publishing every
> request made under it turns the sponsor's leverage into a public record of how little they
> use it — or into evidence, if they use it a lot. The cost is that the sponsor will read this
> decision as hostile, and they will be partly right.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The university signed and published. The clause went on the wing's website
> in week one, with a page beside it headed *Requests made under clause 9.4*, which stayed empty
> for two years and eleven months.
>
> Then one request was made, on a paper about a competitor's product. It asked for the removal
> of one sentence. The university refused, published the request and the refusal on the same
> page, and the paper went out unchanged four days later.
>
> The sponsor renewed for a second fifteen years. Their head of partnerships said in a
> conference talk, without mentioning the university by name, that the published-requests page
> was the single most useful thing any partner had ever done for them, because it meant nobody
> internally ever asked them to make a request again.

**The clause was never the problem. Writing it down where everybody could see it was what made
it harmless, and it took four days and one refusal to prove it.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say what the researcher in 11A would say now.
