### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b11_u10_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| Size | Modifier | Example |
|---|---|---|
| tiny | slightly · marginally · a little · a bit | *slightly better* |
| moderate | somewhat · rather | *somewhat better* |
| large | much · far · a lot · considerably · substantially · significantly | *far better* |
| enormous | a great deal · infinitely · appreciably | *a great deal better* |
| not at all | nowhere near · not nearly · barely | *nowhere near enough* |
| equal | just as · every bit as | *just as good* |

**The *more … the more* structure**

> **The more** hours she takes, **the less** she can choose.
> **The sooner** we write it down, **the better**.

Comparative first in each half. No *that*. The second half is the consequence.

> **Watch out.** *Very* modifies an adjective; it cannot modify a comparative. *Very good* ✓
> *very better* ✗. Use *much*, *far* or *a lot*.

> **Why it matters.** In a negotiation, the modifier is the offer. *Slightly more* and
> *considerably more* can describe the same number, and the person across the table hears the
> word, not the number.

### 3D · Use — controlled

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

A review of a book about informal work. The first line is:

> *There is a chapter missing from this otherwise excellent book, and I think I know why.*

What do you expect? One sentence.

### 5B · Skim — sixty seconds

Find the paragraph where the reviewer praises the book most.

---

**The chapter that isn't there**

There is a chapter missing from this otherwise excellent book, and I think I know why.

*Handshakes* is a study of informal work arrangements across four cities, and it is far better
than the literature it joins. The fieldwork is exhaustive — four hundred and eighty interviews
over three years — and the author resists almost every temptation available to her. She does not
romanticise informality, which is the standard failure of the sympathetic account. She does not
treat it as simply exploitation waiting to be formalised, which is the standard failure of the
policy account. She is considerably better than both camps at holding two things at once: that
informal arrangements are often chosen, and that being able to choose them is not the same as
having a choice.

The best chapter is the fourth, on what she calls the goodwill ledger. Her argument is that a
long informal arrangement accumulates a kind of credit on both sides — the worker who has stayed,
the employer who has paid in hard months — and that this credit is real, is valuable, and is
destroyed by formalisation. A contract converts six years of mutual obligation into twelve months'
notice. That is a far worse deal than it looks, and nobody records it as a loss because it was
never recorded as an asset.

What is missing is the chapter on what happens afterwards. The book follows people up to the
moment of formalisation and stops. In the two cases where she does follow through, both workers
end up marginally better paid and substantially less free, and in one of them the arrangement
ends within a year — and the author notes this in a footnote.

A footnote. The most important finding in four hundred and eighty interviews, in a footnote.

I do not think this is carelessness. I think the finding is uncomfortable for everybody who would
otherwise agree with her, including the reviewer, and that she knew it and did not want the book
to be read as an argument against writing things down. It would have been. It would also have
been the honest version, and the book is good enough that it could have carried it.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Grace's contract

Grace has cleaned Jengo House for six years. There is no contract. She is paid weekly in cash,
sets her own hours, and does other work on Tuesdays and Thursdays that nobody has ever asked about.

The tenants want to put her on a written agreement: twelve fixed hours, nine per cent more an
hour, three months' notice, paid by transfer.

- **Formalise it as proposed.** More money, legal protection, and her Tuesdays become a problem
  she has to raise.
- **Leave it.** She has said she is happy. Six years says something. And she has no notice, no
  record, and nothing if she is ill.
- **Formalise it on her terms** — she sets the hours, the rate is benchmarked, and the agreement
  says so in writing. It is harder to administer and two of the six tenants will object.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The two sides of the agreement** from Part 5.
2. **The chart**: what she earns now, what she would earn, and what she loses.
3. **The voice.** Grace, thirty seconds: *"It's a lot more than I thought. I'd just want to know
   what happens on a Tuesday, and I don't want to have to ask every week."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think they should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> Formalise it on her terms, and accept that it is harder and that two people will object.
>
> Leaving it is the option that respects her stated view and I think it fails her. Six years with
> nothing written down is not an arrangement; it is an arrangement that has not yet been tested.
> The test arrives when somebody is ill, or when the building changes hands, and at that point
> goodwill is held entirely by people who will have moved on.
>
> Formalising it as proposed is worse than leaving it. It takes the one thing she has — control of
> her own week — and converts it into a clause she would have to negotiate for, having just been
> given a rise. Her thirty seconds is explicit about this and it is the only new information
> anybody has: she did not ask about the money.
>
> The third option costs the tenants administrative effort and costs her nothing. That asymmetry
> is the argument. The two who object will be objecting to effort, and it is not their Tuesdays.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** They wrote it her way. The hours clause says *at times set by Grace, not less
> than twelve hours weekly*, which took four drafts because nobody had written a clause like it
> before and two of the drafts were worse than no clause at all.
>
> Eleven months later the building changed hands. The new owner's managing agent asked for all
> service arrangements in writing within fourteen days. Grace's was the only one that existed.
>
> She was kept on. Two of the other arrangements in the building — a window cleaner and a man who
> did the gutters — were not.

**The clause nobody knew how to write was the only thing in the building that survived the sale.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And: the window cleaner had been there nine years. Whose job was it to
> write his down?
