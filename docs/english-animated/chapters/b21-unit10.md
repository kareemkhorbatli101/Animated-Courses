## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Putting the verb first, and why you would

### 3A · Notice

Look at the six sentences in Part 2 again.

1. In sentence 1, what comes before the subject, and what has happened to the auxiliary?
2. Sentence 3 inverts the second clause, not the first. **Why that one?**
3. Sentence 4 has two clauses and only one inversion. **Which, and what joins them?**
4. Sentence 6 is a fixed expression. **Could you say *little did they know* about yourself?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u10_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **negative adverb first** | *never · rarely · seldom · nowhere · little* | *Never had she seen it.* |
| **near-negative first** | *hardly · scarcely · no sooner* | *Hardly had it arrived when…* |
| **restrictive first** | *only when · only then · only if · not until* | *Only then did we count them.* |
| **absolute negatives** | *at no point · under no circumstances · not once* | *Not once did they name it.* |
| ***so* + adjective or adverb** | *so rarely does · so great was* | *So rarely does it appear that…* |
| ***such*** | *such was the* | *Such was the reaction that…* |

> **Inversion is the written language's way of shouting.** It costs nothing in words and it cannot
> be used twice in a paragraph without sounding theatrical, which is the whole constraint.

> **The auxiliary does the inverting, and if there isn't one, *do* is supplied.** *They never
> counted them* becomes *Never did they count them*, not *Never counted they them*. This is the
> same machinery as a question, which is why the pattern feels familiar and the register does not.

| | |
|---|---|
| **the fixed frames** | *never before had · not once did · only when* |
| **what writers do with a place** | *set out · single out · point up* |
| **the degree words** | *rarely · seldom · scarcely · hardly · little · only* |

> **Why it matters.** Marked order tells a reader that this sentence is the one to remember. Use it
> where the emphasis is real and it is the strongest device in the language; use it where it is not
> and the reader hears a performance.

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**Eighty-one headlines**

The letter ran to a page and a half and contained a table. Over two years the Herald published
eighty-one pieces mentioning Ward 7. The writer, a resident of thirty-one years, had sorted them
by section and counted how often the ward appeared in the headline.

In crime stories it appeared in seventy-one per cent of headlines. In planning stories, eighteen.
In business, six. Not once did it appear in a culture headline, although eleven culture pieces
over the two years were about events in the ward, including the reopening of a hall the paper
itself had campaigned to save.

There is no rule in the style guide about naming a ward. Nobody in the building could produce one.
Asked, three editors gave three answers, and all three were about specificity: a crime happens
somewhere, and readers want to know where.

The letter's last paragraph points out that a concert also happens somewhere.

The ward is forty thousand people and eleven thousand households, of which fifty-eight per cent
are rented. The six streets around the Lake and Seventh junction account for sixty-two of the
eighty-one pieces. The writer lives four streets away and has never been quoted in any of them.

## Part 7 · Pronunciation & Fluency Lab ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u10_p07_v10` · V10 Phonetics Visual**

### Marked order has a marked tune

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 10.3

> *NEVer before had anybody counted them.* — the jump is on the first word
> *Not ONCE did the culture pages name it.* — and here on the second
> *Only when it is CRIME do we name it.* — the jump waits for the content word

### 7B · Say

Say each one three times. Then say this:

> *Never before had anybody counted them — and not once did anybody ask why.* — two inversions in
> one breath, two jumps, and the second must be lower than the first or it sounds like a speech.

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *We rarely name it.* — flat, ordinary, and forgettable
> *RARELY do we name it.* — the jump on *rarely*, the verb inverted, and it is now the sentence
> the reader repeats

**Inversion without the pitch jump sounds like a mistake,** and the jump without inversion sounds
like emphasis in the wrong place. The two travel together or not at all.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **three inverted sentences, each with one jump, each jump lower than the last.**
Three times, faster.

### A case about a place · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> Over two years the Herald published eighty-one pieces mentioning Ward 7, of which sixty-two
> concerned the same six streets. `[ordinary order, and the figures do the work; nothing is marked
> yet]`
>
> Not once did the ward appear in a culture headline. `[the first inversion, on the sentence that
> is the point of the piece]`
>
> Eleven culture pieces over the same period were about events there, including the reopening of a
> hall this paper campaigned to save. `[back to ordinary order immediately; the marked sentence
> only works if it is surrounded by unmarked ones]`
>
> So rarely does a neighbourhood appear in good news that its readers come to believe there is
> none — and such was the response to this letter that the rule, which had never been written down,
> was written down in a week. `[two marked structures in the last sentence, where the piece is
> allowed to raise its voice because it has not done so before]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The first sentence is unmarked. **What does that buy the second one?**
2. The third sentence returns to ordinary order. **Why not keep going?**
3. The last sentence uses two marked structures. **Would three have worked?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> Never before has a community been so ignored. Not only is the coverage unfair, but so biased is the framing that no resident can recognise themselves in it. Rarely has a newspaper so comprehensively failed. At no point has anybody listened.

Four sentences, five inversions, and not one number anywhere.
**Rewrite it with one inversion and four facts.**

### 8C · Plan

> the figures, unmarked → one inversion, on the point → back to ordinary order, with the

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The naming rule

There is no rule. There has been a practice since at least 1994.

**Option one:** keep it. A crime happens in a place and readers are entitled to know which place.
Specificity is the job.

**Option two:** name a neighbourhood only when the story is about the neighbourhood, and not when
it is about one event that happened inside it.

**Option three:** name it everywhere — in culture, in business, in planning, in sport — at the same
rate as in crime, and write the rule down.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u10_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 10.6.** Priya Raghavan, news editor, 30 seconds: *"I have defended the crime practice
for eleven years and I would defend it again, because it is right. A crime happens somewhere. What
I cannot defend is the other four sections, and nobody has ever asked me to, because nobody ever
noticed that the defence only works if you apply it everywhere. We applied it in one place. That
is not a rule. That is a habit with a reason attached afterwards."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and invert exactly once per person.

> *I would ______ , because ______ . Only when ______ would I ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and one inverted sentence you would stand
behind in print.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Option two is the comfortable answer and it makes the ward disappear, which is what
> the letter is complaining about in the first place. The defence of naming is sound and it has
> only ever been applied where the news was bad. Applying it everywhere costs nothing, writes the
> rule down, and is the only version that can be checked a year from now by counting, which is how
> this started.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald wrote the rule down and applied it to every section. In the
> following year Ward 7 appeared in a hundred and forty headlines instead of eighty-one, and the
> crime share of those headlines fell from seventy-one per cent to twenty-nine.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: readers outside the ward, asked to free-associate on its name,
> produced a list in which crime words fell from first to fourth. Nothing had changed in the ward.
> Six hundred more headlines had changed, and all of them were true.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> Every one of the eighty-one original headlines was accurate. What exactly was wrong?
