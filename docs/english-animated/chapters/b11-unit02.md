### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b11_u02_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | What it points at | Takes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **have/has + past participle** | the **result**, counted up to now | *how many*, *how much* | *I've made forty.* |
| **have/has been + -ing** | the **activity**, running up to now | *how long*, *since*, *for* | *I've been making these for nine years.* |

> **Watch out.** Both forms reach now. The difference is not time — it is **what you are pointing
> at**. *I've painted the room* (it is finished) against *I've been painting the room* (there is
> paint on me).

> **Why it matters.** *We've been speaking to a few workshops* and *we've spoken to three
> workshops* are both true. One of them is a negotiating position.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

A report extract called **"The cost of the cheapest option."** The first line is:

> *Procurement teams are rewarded for the price on the invoice, and for nothing else.*

What do you expect it to argue? One sentence.

### 5B · Skim — sixty seconds

Where does the argument turn? Mark the paragraph.

---

**The cost of the cheapest option**

Procurement teams are rewarded for the price on the invoice, and for nothing else. This is not a
criticism of the people in them. It is a description of what their targets measure, and people
hit the targets they are given.

The effect is well documented in manufacturing and almost undocumented in small-scale production,
which is where most of the world's clothing is actually made. A buyer with a unit-price target
will move an order to a workshop that quotes eight per cent lower. On the invoice, the saving is
real. What the invoice does not carry is the four days of rework when the first batch comes back
with the seams wrong, the two weeks of delay while a replacement is cut, or the senior fitter's
time spent on a problem that the cheaper workshop was never equipped to avoid.

None of those costs appear in the procurement figure. All of them appear somewhere else.

The pattern repeats at every scale. A workshop that has been underpricing for years is not
cheaper; it is subsidising its buyers with unpaid hours, deferred maintenance and, eventually,
its own capacity to keep trading. When it closes, the buyer records no loss at all. The cost
shows up as a sourcing problem eighteen months later, and nobody connects the two.

What makes this hard to correct is not that it is unknown. It is that the invoice is a single
number with a name on it, and the other costs are spread across four departments, three quarters
and one person's weekend. A number that can be pointed at will always beat a number that has to
be assembled.

The workshops that survive are not the cheapest. They are the ones that learned to put the
assembled number on the page before anybody asked for it.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The order at 4,100

The buyer will take forty jackets at **4,100**. Mariam's honest cost is **3,720**.

At 4,100 she makes 380 a jacket. At that margin, one batch of rework wipes out the order. She has
been quoting 4,800 for two years and has never been asked to justify it until now.

- **Take it.** Forty jackets is six weeks of guaranteed work and the first order of this size.
- **Hold at 4,800.** Defensible, honest, and he has three other workshops on his phone.
- **Take it, and cut something.** Cheaper lining. Nobody would see it for a year.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The costing sheet** from Part 5.
2. **The chart** from Part 8 — where the money actually goes.
3. **The voice.** The buyer, thirty seconds, from Track 2.1: *"We've been looking at a few workshops."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue for the option you like least, as well as its best advocate would. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think she should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> I think she should hold at 4,800 and say exactly why, in writing, on one page.
>
> Taking 4,100 is not the disaster it looks like — 380 a jacket on forty jackets is still money,
> and six weeks of booked work has a value that does not appear on the sheet. I understand the
> argument and on a different week I might make it.
>
> But the sheet is the thing that changes this. Two years of quoting 4,800 with nothing behind it
> is a position. A costed page is an argument, and she has never put one in front of a buyer. If
> he walks away from a costed page, he was always going to walk, and she will know that for the
> next buyer. If he does not, she has set the price for every order after this one.
>
> Cutting the lining is the only option I would rule out entirely. It is the one choice that she
> could not put in writing.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** He went to another workshop and came back eleven weeks later, because the
> first batch had the seams wrong and the rework ate the saving — which is the article in Part 5,
> happening. He paid 4,650.
>
> Mariam lost eleven weeks of work she had counted on. Two of her four machinists left during it,
> and one did not come back when the order arrived.

**She was right, and it cost her two machinists.**

> Was the reasoning sound?
> And: is "I was right" worth anything, if you could not afford to be?
