### Four futures, and why you would choose each one

### 3A · Notice

5. Try swapping 1 and 3. What goes wrong socially — not grammatically?

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b11_u03_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| Form | What it signals | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **present continuous** | an arrangement — somebody else knows too | *I'm seeing him on Thursday.* |
| **going to** | an intention formed before now | *I'm going to ask about the lease.* |
| **will** | a decision made at this moment, often for someone | *I'll ask him, if you want.* |
| **present simple** | a timetable — not yours to change | *The lease runs out in March.* |

> **Watch out.** *I'll send it* and *I'm sending it* are both correct and they promise different
> things. The first is a decision. The second is a plan that already exists — and is therefore
> harder to get out of.

> **Why it matters.** Choosing the wrong future does not produce a grammar mistake. It produces a
> commitment you did not mean to make, or a vagueness the other person can hear.

### 3D · Use — controlled

> Wanjiru (1) __________ (meet) the landlord on Thursday; it has been in the diary for a week.
> She (2) __________ (tell) him about the lift, because she decided that on Monday and has been
> rehearsing it since. If he refuses, she (3) __________ (take) it to the tenants — she has just
> this second thought of that. The lease (4) __________ (run out) in March whatever anybody does.
> "I (5) __________ (not do) this by email," she says. "I (6) __________ (sit) in front of him."

### 3E · Use — guided

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

A fiction extract called **"The second name."** The first line is:

> *For eleven years my mother called me by a name that is not on any document I own.*

What do you expect? One sentence.

### 5B · Skim — sixty seconds

Where does the narrator change their mind? Mark it.

---

**The second name**

For eleven years my mother called me by a name that is not on any document I own. It is four
letters long and it means nothing in particular. She says she chose it before I was born and then
put something longer on the certificate because the registrar was watching.

At school I used the long one. At home I was the short one. It did not feel like a secret; it
felt like weather — something that changed depending on which room you were in.

The trouble started when the two rooms met. A teacher came to the house and used the long name in
front of my mother, and I watched my mother decide, in about half a second, not to correct her.
That was the first time I understood that one of the names was going to have to win.

I am going to say that I chose the long one, and that is almost true. What actually happened is
that the long one was already on things. It was on the register, the library card, the
inoculation record, the bank account somebody opened for me when I was nine. Choosing the short
one would not have been a choice; it would have been nineteen separate arguments with nineteen
institutions, each of which would have asked me for a document carrying the name I was trying to
leave.

So I did not choose. I let the paperwork choose, which is what most people mean when they say
they chose.

My mother still uses the short one. I am forty-one. When she dies, nobody alive will call me by
it, and I have started to mind about that in a way I did not expect, because for eleven years I
thought the short name was the one that did not count.

---

### 5C · Close reading

1. Why did the long name go on the certificate?
2. What does *"it felt like weather"* describe?
3. What did the narrator understand when the teacher visited?
4. **The narrator says "I am going to say that I chose… and that is almost true." What is the
   gap between those two things?**
5. List the five institutions named. What do they have in common?
6. What changed when the narrator reached forty-one?

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Joseph, or the other thing

Didier runs the café on the ground floor. His full name is Didier Nsengimana. For two years the
biggest account — a company that orders ninety coffees a week — has called him **Dee**.

It began as a shortening. It is now on their internal system, on the standing order, and on the
reference that appears on every payment. Changing it means changing the reference, which means
their finance team reopening the account.

- **Leave it.** It is a nickname, they are friendly, and ninety coffees a week is ninety coffees.
- **Correct it once, properly, in writing.** Make it easy. Accept the risk that it is a nuisance
  to them.
- **Correct it and ask them to change the payment reference too.** The whole thing, done once.

### 11A · The evidence

1. **The standing order record** — the name as their system holds it.
2. **The chart**: how often a correction is actually made, and by whom.
3. **The voice.** Lin, from A1.1, twenty-five seconds: *"I changed mine in my first month here.
   It was easier. Now nobody at work knows my real name and I don't know how to start."*

### 11B · Steelman

Argue the option you like least. Two minutes.

### 11C · Deliberate

> *I think he should ______, because ______. The strongest argument against me is ______, and my
> answer to it is ______.*

### 11D · Decide — eighty words, two reasons

### 11E · Model decision

> I think he should correct it once, in writing, and not ask them to change the payment reference.
>
> The full correction is the one I would want to be right, and I think it is wrong here. The
> payment reference is their system's problem and it is genuinely expensive to change — reopening
> an account is a week of somebody's attention. Asking for it turns a thirty-second courtesy into
> a favour, and favours get remembered as difficulty.
>
> But leaving it entirely is worse, and Lin's recording is why. The cost of saying nothing is not
> felt in the first year. It is felt when the wrong name has been true for so long that
> correcting it would need an explanation, and the explanation is the thing nobody has time for.
>
> So: one line, friendly, with the spelling, and no request attached to it. The reference can stay
> wrong. The person saying it out loud ninety times a week is the one that matters.

### 11F · The Reveal

> **What happened.** He sent one line. Four people started saying Didier within a week. The
> finance system still says **D NSENG** and the payment reference has never changed.
>
> Eight months later a new account manager arrived, read the system, and called him Dee.

**He fixed the people and not the paperwork, and the paperwork refilled the people.**

> Was the reasoning sound? And what, exactly, would the full fix have cost?
