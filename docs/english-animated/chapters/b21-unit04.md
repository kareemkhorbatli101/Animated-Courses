## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Standing in 2014 and looking forward

### 3A · Notice

Look at the five sentences in Part 2 again.

1. Where is the speaker standing in each one — in 2014, or now?
2. Which sentence contains an obstacle, and which word puts it there?
3. Sentence 4 is the most formal of the five. **What register does *was to have* belong to?**
4. Sentence 5 uses *would* about something still ahead. **How is that possible?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u04_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **was going to** | the plan or expectation, neutral | *It was going to open in 2019.* |
| **was due to** | a scheduled commitment, often contractual | *It was due to open in 2019.* |
| **was about to** | on the point of happening, interrupted | *They were about to sign.* |
| **would have** | and something prevented it | *It would have opened, but for the harbour.* |
| **was to have been** | formal, written, and faintly reproachful | *It was to have been the first line in forty years.* |
| **would later** | the narrator, who knows how it ends | *It would later be revised four times.* |

> **Future in the past is a vantage point, not a tense.** You stand at a moment in the past and
> look forward from it, and the reader knows you also know what happened. That double knowledge is
> the whole effect.

> **The narrator's *would* is the most useful form in this unit.** *It would later overrun by nine
> years* is only available to somebody telling the story afterwards, and it tells the reader there
> is more coming.

| | |
|---|---|
| **standing at the start** | *originally · formerly · the plan was to · was due to* |
| **moving through** | *subsequently · latterly · would later · as it would turn out* |
| **arriving at the end** | *eventually · ultimately · in the event · as things turned out* |
| **choosing a direction** | *prospectively · retrospectively · with hindsight* |

> **Why it matters.** Every institution has a record of things that were going to happen. The
> grammar that narrates those honestly is the grammar that stops a newsroom repeating them.

**The line that was going to open in 2019**

In October 2014 the city announced a new transit line to the east end. It was to have been the
first new line in forty years. It was due to open in the spring of 2019, at a cost of two point
four billion dollars.

The Herald's front page that morning carried a projection of journey times and a photograph of a
model of the station. The model is still in the lobby of the planning office. The station is not
built.

The opening date has since been revised to 2023, to 2027 and, in March, to 2031. The budget has
been revised downwards once, in 2016, and upwards three times. The central estimate is now six
point one billion.

The paper has published two hundred and forty pieces about the line. Thirty-one of them contain a
forecast date. None of the thirty-one mentions the forecast it replaced.

Nobody saw it coming, the city's transport chair said in 2023, of the second delay. The 2016
contingency documents, released under access law in 2024, model the harbour problem on page
eleven and put a worst case at 2029.

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The first paragraph has no hindsight in it at all. **What does that buy, and what does it cost?**

**Rewrite it from inside 2014, with a date in every sentence.**

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The forecast audit

The business desk has proposed that the paper publish, once a year, every forecast it made twelve
months earlier and what became of each one.

**Option one:** do not. A newspaper's job is to report what people say, and a public scoreboard of
its own wrong guesses will be quoted against it for years.

**Option two:** publish it in full, including the names of the writers.

**Option three:** publish it in full, without names, and require that every new forecast in the
paper carry the date and value of the forecast it replaces.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u04_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 4.6.** Laran Bakare, business desk, 30 seconds: *"We ran forty forecasts in five years
and eleven came off. That is worse than a coin, and I am not embarrassed by it, because the ones
we got wrong were the ones everybody got wrong and the chart shows exactly why. What I am
embarrassed by is that we never once printed the number we were replacing."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and state which vantage point you are speaking from each time.

> *I would ______ , because ______ , and in five years it would ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and what you would expect to see in the
first audit if you were right.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Names turn an audit into a disciplinary record and the desk will quietly stop
> forecasting, which is the one outcome that helps nobody. The replacement rule is the part that
> does the work: a forecast printed beside the forecast it supersedes cannot be re-announced as
> news, and it is the re-announcement, not the error, that has cost this paper its credibility on
> the line.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald published the audit without names and adopted the replacement rule.
> Eleven of forty was the headline figure, and it was quoted against the paper for a fortnight.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: the city's own press office began including the superseded date
> in its announcements, because the Herald printed it anyway and the office preferred to be the
> one who said it. The number of re-announcements fell by half in a year.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The rule applied only to the Herald's own pages. Why did it change what the city published?
