## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Clauses that hang off a noun

### 3A · Notice

Look at the six sentences in Part 2 again.

1. Sentences 1 and 4 differ by two commas. **How many berths are cabled in each?**
2. Sentence 2 has no relative pronoun and no verb *be*. **What has been removed, and from where?**
3. Sentence 3 uses a perfect participle. **What does it add that sentence 2 does not?**
4. Sentence 5 puts the quantity first. **What register does that belong to?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b22_u04_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **defining relative** | no commas; it tells you which ones | *the berths that were cabled in 2021* |
| **non-defining relative** | commas; it tells you about all of them | *the berths, which were cabled in 2021,* |
| **reduced relative** | the pronoun and *be* go; the participle stays | *the berths cabled in 2021* |
| **present participle clause** | an active reduced clause | *the berths serving the inland fleet* |
| **perfect participle clause** | and this one happened first | *the berths, having been cabled in 2021,* |
| **the formal fronting** | the quantity goes first, the clause follows | *Of the nine berths, four, cabled in 2021,…* |

> **A reduced relative is the engine of academic English.** *The samples which were collected in
> March* becomes *the samples collected in March*, and a nine-thousand-word paper does it four
> hundred times. The reader stops noticing; the writer saves four words each time; and the
> ambiguity is that a reduced clause has lost its commas and therefore its defining or
> non-defining signal.

> **Two commas are a claim about quantity.** *The berths, which were cabled, are used* says all of
> them were cabled. *The berths that were cabled are used* says some were. In a sentence with a
> number in it, those two commas are worth five berths.

| | |
|---|---|
| **pointing inside the document** | *herein · aforementioned · the foregoing · as noted above · see below* |
| **pointing at a thing already named** | *thereof · respective · those referred to above* |
| **pointing at a time or an event** | *hitherto · whereupon* |
| **pointing at an authority** | *pursuant to · as amended · in respect of · with reference to* |

> **Why it matters.** This is the grammar of every document that decides anything: papers, audits,
> contracts, regulations. It is learned by reading and almost never produced without being taught,
> which is why a learner who can build it reads as a professional and one who cannot reads as a
> student.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

The title is **"A significant reduction."**
**What has to be true for a sixty-two per cent reduction and an eight per cent reduction to be the
same finding?**

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**A significant reduction**

The port commissioned a study of shore power at its inland berths. The paper runs to nine thousand
four hundred words and carries a hundred and twelve references.

Its finding is in two parts. At the four berths of nine that are cabled, a call using shore power
produces sixty-two per cent less at-berth emission than one that does not. Across all eleven
hundred and forty calls in the year, including those at the five berths with no cable, the fleet
reduction is eight per cent.

The note in the annual report runs to a hundred and eighty words. It says that a significant
reduction in at-berth emissions has been achieved and that a provision of eight point four million
euros is held in respect of the remaining berths. It carries no references.

The note is accurate. Sixty-two per cent is a significant reduction and it has been achieved. The
word *significant* appears in the paper eleven times, in every case meaning *unlikely to be
chance*, and in the note once, meaning *large*.

Three sentences in the note correspond to no paragraph of the paper. The board secretary added
them, in good faith, from a meeting. They are the three a reader remembers.

---

### 5C · Comprehension — eight questions

1. How long is the paper, and how many references does it carry?
2. What are the two parts of the finding?
3. How long is the note, and how many references does it carry?
4. What provision is held, and what for?
5. How many sentences in the note have no source in the paper?
6. **Why does the writer say "the note is accurate" before making the next point?**
7. **The word *significant* is used in two senses. What is the writer showing by counting them?**
8. **"They are the three a reader remembers." Why those three, and what follows from it?**

### A long formal sentence is three beats, not forty

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 4.3

> *The BERTHS — cabled in twenty twenty-ONE — are used on a THIRD of calls.*
> three beats, two resets, and the middle clause said fastest
> *The PROvision, held in respect of the remaining BERTHS, appears in note FOURteen.*

### 7B · Say

Say both. Then say this forty-word sentence with exactly three beats:

> *Of the nine inland berths, four, cabled in 2021 and serving the barge fleet referred to above,
> are used on approximately a third of calls, a proportion which has not changed since the second
> year of operation.*

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *The berths, cabled in 2021, are used.* — a pause each side: all of them were cabled
> *The berths cabled in 2021 are used.* — no pause: only some of them were

**Two commas in writing are two pauses in speech, and both of them are a claim about how many.**
A reduced clause said without the pauses means something different from the same words with them.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **one forty-word formal sentence, three beats, said three times**, each time
slightly faster and with the same three resets.

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### A formal paragraph that carries three clauses · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> Of the nine inland berths, four, cabled in 2021, are equipped for shore power. `[formal
> fronting, then a reduced relative with commas: the quantity is stated twice and cannot be
> misread]`
>
> Calls using shore power at those berths produce sixty-two per cent less at-berth emission than
> comparable calls that do not. `[a present participle clause and a defining relative in one
> sentence, and the defining one is what makes the comparison valid]`
>
> Across all 1,140 calls in the year, including those at the five berths having no cable, the
> fleet-level reduction is eight per cent. `[a perfect-free participle carrying the exclusion, and
> the number the board needed]`
>
> The provision held in respect of the remaining berths, referred to in note 14, is 8.4 million
> euros; the foregoing figures are drawn from the commissioned study and not from management
> accounts. `[two reduced relatives, one authority frame, and a pointing-back word doing the work
> of a whole sentence]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. Sentence one states the quantity twice. **Why, when one of them would be grammatical alone?**
2. Sentence two contains two clause types. **What would happen if the defining relative were
   non-defining?**
3. The last sentence distinguishes two sources. **Why is that worth four words of formality?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> It should be noted that a significant reduction in at-berth emissions has been achieved at the berths, which have been cabled, and that a provision has been made, as set out below, for the avoidance of doubt, in respect of the aforementioned.

One sentence, forty-two words, four pointing-back words, one comma that claims nine berths were
cabled, and no number.
**Rewrite it in three sentences with the quantities right.**

### 8C · Plan

> formal fronting with the quantity → the comparison, defining → the exclusion, in a participle
> clause → the authority frame and the pointing-back word

### 8D · Write — 200 words
### 8E · Peer review — three criteria

> ☐ Does every comma around a clause make the quantity claim you meant?
> ☐ Is each reduced clause recoverable to a full one?
> ☐ Does each pointing-back word point at exactly one thing?

### 8F · Write it again · 8G · Publish

Rewrite after review. **Publish both, and mark each clause defining or non-defining.**

# STUDY FILE · The register that will not hold

### 9A · Starting formal and ending spoken 🔊 Track 4.4

Almost every learner at this level can open in formal register and almost none can hold it for a
paragraph. The first sentence has a reduced relative and a nominalisation; by the fourth, there is
a contraction and a phrasal verb, and the reader has noticed the moment it happened.

| | |
|---|---|
| **sentence one** | *The samples, collected in March, were analysed…* |
| **sentence four** | *…and then we had a look at what came out.* |
| **what gives it away** | the contraction, the phrasal verb, the vague noun |
| **the fix** | a list of four substitutions, memorised, not derived |

### 9B · Hold it for four

Write a four-sentence paragraph in formal register.
**Give it to somebody and ask them to mark the sentence where it slipped.** It is almost always
the third.

### 9C · Role play — the substitution

In pairs. One of you says a sentence informally. The other says it formally, **changing exactly
one thing**. **Eight rounds each**, and name the thing each time.

### 9D · The four you need

Most learners need the same four: *look at* → *examine*, *a lot of* → *a substantial number of*,
*get* → *obtain*, *so* → *accordingly*.
**Find your own four** and learn them as a pair each, not as a rule.

### 9E · Transfer — before next week

**Take a paragraph you wrote informally and raise its register, changing nothing else.** Bring both
and count the words you added. The usual answer is between nine and fourteen per cent.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# What the port publishes

**Option one:** publish the summary. The paper is nine thousand four hundred words and will be
read by eleven people, four of whom wrote it.

**Option two:** publish the paper and nothing else. Whoever needs the finding can read it.

**Option three:** publish both, and require every sentence of the summary to cite the paragraph of
the paper it comes from.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b22_u04_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 4.6.** Dr Ilse Brandt, who wrote the study, 30 seconds: *"I do not mind being
summarised. I have been summarised accurately twice in nine years and I remember both. What I mind
is a summary with nothing to check it against, because the writer of such a summary has no way of
knowing whether they have gone wrong either. The citation is not a leash. It is a mirror."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and attach one clause to a noun in every turn.

> *I would ______ , because the reader, ______ , cannot ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and one sentence of a summary with its
citation.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Publishing the paper alone is a decision to be read by nobody, and publishing the
> summary alone is what produced the three sentences. The citation costs the summary writer about
> twenty minutes and it is the only mechanism here that works on the writer rather than on the
> reader: a sentence that cannot be anchored to a paragraph announces itself while it is being
> written, which is the only moment at which anybody can do anything about it.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The port published both, with citations. The summary came back from its first
> draft with four sentences uncited, three of them the board secretary's and one of them a figure
> nobody could source at all.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: the summary got longer, from a hundred and eighty words to two
> hundred and sixty, and the extra eighty words were almost entirely scopes — *at cabled berths*,
> *across all calls*, *in the year to December*. Nobody had asked for them. They appeared because
> a sentence being anchored to a paragraph acquires the paragraph's qualifications on the way.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The citation rule was about where sentences came from. Why did it change what they said?
