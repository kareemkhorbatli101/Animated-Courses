## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### The first position and the last position do different jobs

### 3A · Notice

Look at the six versions in Part 2 again.

1. Version 1 ends on *prefix*. **Why is that the wrong word to end on, if the prefix is the problem?**
2. Version 3 fronts a phrase. **What does fronting it promise, and does the sentence keep the promise?**
3. Version 5 is two sentences. **What has the split bought?**
4. Version 6 is a pseudo-cleft. **Where does it put the prefix, and what does that cost?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b22_u07_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **the first position (theme)** | what the sentence is about; the reader's starting point | *Including the prefix, …* |
| **the last position (focus)** | the new information; where the voice falls | *…including the prefix.* |
| **fronting** | moving something to the front for contrast or linkage | *The prefix you must include.* |
| **end-weight** | long and heavy elements go last, short ones first | *Enter, in full, the reference printed on your order.* |
| **extraposition** | a heavy subject is moved to the end and *it* holds its place | *It is essential to include the prefix.* |
| **the conflict** | you cannot front a thing and also end on it | and most bad instructions try |

> **English puts old information first and new information last.** The first position tells the
> reader where they are; the last position tells them what to do with it. A sentence that puts the
> new fact first and a familiar one last has been read backwards, and the reader will have to do
> it twice.

> **End-weight is not a style rule.** *Enter the reference printed on your transport order in
> full* is hard because a long noun phrase has been pushed into the middle. Move it to the end and
> the sentence works: *Enter in full the reference printed on your transport order.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **fronting for emphasis** | *more important is · what matters most is · central to this is* |
| **marking position in a list** | *first · foremost · last of all · chief among them · last but not least* |
| **adding weight at the end** | *worse still · no less important · and finally* |
| **what writers do with order** | *lead with · end on · put off* |

> **Why it matters.** An instruction is read once, at speed, by somebody who is already doing
> something else. The last word of each sentence is the one that survives, which means the order
> of the words is the design.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**The card somebody taped to the kiosk**

Two thousand nine hundred trucks arrive at the east gate each day. Each driver passes through six
kiosk screens. Screen three asks for the booking reference.

The instruction on screen three reads: *Please ensure that the booking reference, which can be
found on your transport order, is entered in full including the prefix.* Twenty-four words. The
error rate on that screen is fourteen per cent, which is about four hundred trucks a day entering
a queue they will have to leave.

Every error is the same error. The prefix is two letters. Drivers enter the six digits and omit
them.

The sentence ends on *prefix*, which is the one piece of information that matters, and that ought
to be right. It is not, because by the time a driver reaches the end of a twenty-four word
sentence in a queue at ten to six in the morning, they have already started typing.

At some point in the last year somebody taped a card to the kiosk. It says, in marker pen,
*2 LETTERS FIRST*. Four words, at the top, where the hand is.

The error rate at that kiosk is three per cent. It is the only one of the twelve gates where
anybody has measured it, because it is the only one where anybody noticed.

### The last stressed word is the instruction

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 7.3

> *Enter the reference in full, including the PREfix.* — one fall, at the end, on the point
> *Please ensure that the booking REFerence, which can be found on your transport ORder, is
> entered in full including the PREfix.* — three falls, and the first two are not instructions
> *Two letters FIRST.* — one fall, three words, and the hand is already there

### 7B · Say

Say all three. Then say this, with exactly one fall:

> *Enter your reference in full, with the two-letter prefix.*

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *Enter the reference in FULL.* — and somebody has been entering part of it
> *Enter the REFerence in full.* — and somebody has been entering something else

**The last stressed word names the error you are correcting,** which is why an instruction with
three falls in it is three different corrections and a driver has to guess which one applies.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **four instructions, each with exactly one fall, each on a different word.** Three
times, faster.

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### An instruction set that ends in the right places · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> Enter your booking reference. `[four words, one fall, and the fall is on the thing being
> entered]`
>
> It begins with two letters. `[extraposed and short: the new fact is alone at the end of a
> five-word sentence, where it cannot be missed]`
>
> More important than the digits is the prefix, which is the only part most drivers omit.
> `[fronted comparison, and the heavy relative clause has been pushed to the end where it
> belongs]`
>
> Last of all, check the trailer number against the order in your cab; without it, the gate will
> not open. `[a list marker at the front, the heaviest element last, and the consequence in the
> final position because that is the one drivers repeat to each other]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The second sentence is five words long. **What would putting it inside the first have cost?**
2. The third sentence fronts a comparison. **What does the front position promise here?**
3. The last sentence ends on a consequence rather than an action. **Why, in an instruction?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> Please ensure that all required fields, including the booking reference, which can be found on your transport order, and the trailer number, are completed in full before proceeding to the confirmation screen, which will then be displayed.

One sentence, thirty-six words, four heavy elements in the middle, and it ends on something that
happens by itself.
**Rewrite it as four sentences, each ending on something the driver does.**

### 8C · Plan

> the action, four words → the new fact, alone at the end → the fronted comparison with its heavy
> clause last → the list marker, the heavy element, and the consequence

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# How the kiosk gives an instruction

**Option one:** keep the full sentence. It is complete, it is legally precise, and every field it
mentions is a field the system requires.

**Option two:** one imperative per screen, the action first, everything else deleted.

**Option three:** the action first, the qualification at the end, and the single thing that varies
— the prefix — in the final position of its own short sentence.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b22_u07_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 7.6.** The driver who taped the card, 30 seconds: *"I did not design anything. I wrote
down the thing I kept getting wrong, and I put it where my hand already was. Four words, because
by the fifth word the gate arm has started to move and I have stopped reading. If somebody wants
to pay me for that, they can send it to the yard and I will be asleep."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and end every turn on the word that matters.

> *I would lead with ______ , and end on ______ , because the reader is ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and the new text of screen three.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Deleting everything is the right instinct and goes one step too far: the
> transport-order reference is genuinely needed and a driver who has not brought it needs to know
> that before they start typing. The split is what does the work — one short sentence whose last
> word is *letters* — and the full qualification can stay, provided it is in a second sentence
> where it cannot push the point out of the final position.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The gate adopted the split instruction. The error rate on screen three fell
> from fourteen per cent to three — the same rate as the kiosk with the taped card.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: drivers began reading the second line. Observed reading of line
> two rose from four per cent to sixty-one. Nothing on line two had changed. What had changed was
> that line one now ended, and a sentence that ends gives a reader somewhere to stop and therefore
> somewhere to start again.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The second line was always there. Why did ending the first one make it readable?
