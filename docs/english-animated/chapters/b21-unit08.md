## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Saying less and meaning the same

### 3A · Notice

Look at the six exchanges in Part 2 again.

1. In exchange 1, how many words has the second speaker not said?
2. In exchange 2, *I have, both* — what is *both* doing, and what has gone?
3. In exchange 4, *so can the third* — what does *so* stand for?
4. Exchange 6 contains two ellipses in six words. **Find both.**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u08_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **ellipsis** | leaving words out where the reader can recover them | *Dev has. → Dev has filed.* |
| **substitution with *one / ones*** | standing in for a countable noun | *the long version and the short one* |
| **substitution with *do so / do it*** | standing in for a whole verb phrase | *She cut it, and I would have done so too.* |
| **substitution with *so***  | standing in for a clause | *— The quote can go. — So can the colour.* |
| **substitution with *such*** | standing in for a kind of thing | *no such paragraph survived* |
| ***neither / either*** | carrying a negative across to a second speaker | *— I wouldn't. — Neither would I.* |

> **Ellipsis is only possible where recovery is certain.** *Dev has* works because the question
> supplied *filed*. Leave out something the listener cannot rebuild and you have not been brief;
> you have been unclear, and the two are confused daily.

> ***Do so* is formal and written; *do it* is neutral; bare *do* is British and spoken.** *She
> asked me to file and I did* is spoken; *and I did so* is a report; *and I did it* is in between.
> Choosing wrongly is one of the clearest register mistakes an advanced learner makes.

| | |
|---|---|
| **pointing back** | *the former · the latter · the same · as such* |
| **carrying a condition** | *if so · if not* |
| **adding a second speaker** | *likewise · so does mine · neither did I* |
| **pointing somewhere else** | *elsewhere* |

> **Why it matters.** Half of spoken English is what was not said, and every edit is a bet about
> what the reader can rebuild. A good cut leaves a hole the reader fills correctly without noticing.

### 3D · Use — controlled

Complete each second turn with the shortest form that works.

### 3F · Use — open

**In pairs.** One of you makes a statement. The other agrees or disagrees **in four words or
fewer**, using substitution. **Eight rounds, and nobody may repeat a form.**

### 3G · Error autopsy

> ✗ *— Who has filed? — Dev has filed it the story.*
> ✗ *— I would not cut it. — Either would I.*
> ✗ *She asked me to file and I did so it.*

For each, name the rule and say what the writer was reaching for.

### 3H · Check — eight items

> 1. — Who has filed? — Dev ______ .
> 2. — Have you cut both? — I ______ .
> 3. — The second can go. — ______ can the third.
> 4. — I would not cut it. — ______ would I.
> 5. — Is it down to length? — If ______ , it goes now.
> 6. ✗ *— I would not. — Either would I.* → correct it.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

The title is **"Seventy-three words."**
**If a story has to lose eight hundred words, which paragraph is most likely to go, and why?**

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**Seventy-three words**

The story was filed at four o'clock at fourteen hundred words. The hole on page one is six
hundred. That is not a negotiation; it is a measurement.

The first pass takes twenty minutes and finds three hundred words of repetition, two sentences
that say the same thing in different registers, and a quotation that adds nothing the paragraph
above it has not said. Nobody will miss any of it.

That leaves five hundred to go, and five hundred words is four paragraphs.

Three of the four are easy. The description of the junction, which took the reporter an afternoon,
goes first. The second and third quotations go next; the first one carries the argument and the
others agree with it.

The fourth is seventy-three words long and names the two councillors who voted for the contract,
and the dates of the two meetings at which they did so. It is the least readable paragraph in the
story. It is also the only one that could not be written by somebody who had not been there.

At two minutes past six the subeditor rings it, and does not cut it, and says: *If this goes, what
is the story for?*

---

### 5C · Comprehension — eight questions

1. How long was the story as filed, and how big is the hole?
2. What does the first pass find?
3. How many words are left to cut after the first pass?
4. Which three paragraphs go, and in what order?
5. How long is the fourth paragraph?
6. **"That is not a negotiation; it is a measurement." What is the writer establishing early?**
7. **Why does the writer mention that the junction description took an afternoon?**
8. **The piece ends on a question, not a decision. What does that leave the reader to do?**

### 5D · Vocabulary in context

Infer from the text, then check.

> says the same thing in different registers · carries the argument · the least readable
> paragraph · could not be written by somebody who had not been there · rings it

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### Two versions and one note · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> The contract was approved on 14 March by seven votes to two. `[the plain sentence, and nothing
> has been left out that a reader would want]`
>
> Two councillors voted for it twice: once in committee and once in the chamber. The former had
> declared an interest; the latter had not. `[the former and the latter carry two whole noun
> phrases, and the sentence is half the length it would otherwise be]`
>
> The reporter asked both to comment. One did; one did not. `[two clauses of three words each,
> and the reader rebuilds comment from the sentence before]`
>
> The printed version of this story says only that the contract was approved. It is not untrue, and
> nobody will miss the seventy-three words unless they go looking — which, as such, is the whole
> argument. `[as such points back to the entire preceding clause, which is what it is for]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The second sentence uses *the former* and *the latter*. **What would repeating the names cost?**
2. *One did; one did not.* **What is the reader rebuilding, and from where?**
3. The last sentence calls its own brevity the argument. **Is that fair? Say why in one sentence.**

### 8B · The anti-model

> The report was published and the report was criticised. The councillors who voted for the contract were the councillors who had attended the committee meeting. The committee meeting took place in March and the chamber vote also took place in March.

Three sentences, every noun phrase repeated in full, and not one substitution anywhere.
**Rewrite it using *one*, *the former*, *the latter*, *do so* and *likewise*, and count the words
you saved.**

### 8C · Plan

> the plain sentence → the two-part sentence held together by *the former* and *the latter* →
> two short clauses the reader completes → the point, pointed back at with *as such*

### 8D · Write — 200 words
### 8E · Peer review — three criteria

> ☐ Can every ellipsis be rebuilt, and from something earlier in your own text?
> ☐ Is each substitution pointing at exactly one thing?
> ☐ Did you cut anything a reader needed, and how would you know?

### 8F · Write it again · 8G · Publish

Rewrite after review. **Publish both, and mark every place where something has been left out.**

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# What happens to the eight hundred words

**Option one:** nothing. The printed story is the story, every edition since 1911 has been an
edited version, and telling readers what they are not getting is a strange way to sell a newspaper.

**Option two:** publish the full filed version online and link to it from the print story.

**Option three:** publish the full version online **with every cut marked**, and say in the print
story that it is there.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u08_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 8.6.** Dennis Hoy, subeditor, 30 seconds: *"I have cut about four hundred thousand words
out of other people's stories. Most of them deserved it. The ones that did not were never the
beautiful sentences; writers fight for those. They were always seventy-three words of names and
dates that nobody fights for, because fighting for them sounds like vanity, and because they are
genuinely the dullest thing on the page."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and keep every turn under fifteen words.

> *I would ______ , because the reader cannot ______ . — So would I. / Neither would I.*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and the cut you would defend hardest.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. The full version alone is unread — six hundred words is what people have time for,
> and a link to fourteen hundred is a link nobody follows. Marking the cuts turns the long version
> into something you can skim for what is missing, which is a different and much shorter act of
> reading. The cost is that the desk's judgement becomes public, and the desk is right most of the
> time and will be visibly wrong occasionally.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald published the marked version. Readers of it rose over a year from
> almost nobody to about a fifth of the print readership — far more than the plain full version had
> ever drawn.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: reporters began filing shorter. Seeing, week after week, which
> of their paragraphs survived, they stopped writing the ones that did not. Average filed length
> fell by nineteen per cent in a year, and the number of paragraphs the desk cut for repetition
> fell by half.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> Nobody told the reporters to write less. What did marking the cuts actually show them?
