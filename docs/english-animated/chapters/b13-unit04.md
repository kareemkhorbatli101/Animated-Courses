## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Purpose, reason and result

### 3A · Notice

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b13_u04_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual · branch**

### 3C · Know

| | Takes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ***so that*** | a clause, usually with *can / will / would* | *The beds are small so that each student has one.* |
| ***in order to* / *so as to*** | an infinitive, same subject | *We grow twelve crops in order to show twelve cycles.* |
| ***so … that* / *such … that*** | adjective / noun + result | *The yield is so low that nobody notices.* |
| ***because* / *since* / *as*** | a clause, giving a reason | *Since the beds are small, the yield is low.* |
| ***owing to* / *due to*** | a noun phrase | *Owing to the bed size, the yield is low.* |

> **Purpose looks forward. Result looks back.** *So that* is the one that can do either, and
> the verb tells you which: *so that students can learn* is a purpose, *so that nobody noticed*
> is a result.

> ***In order to* needs the same subject in both halves.**
> ✗ *The beds are small in order to students can have one.*
> ✓ *The beds are small so that students can have one.*
> ✓ *We made the beds small in order to give each student one.*

> ***so* + adjective, *such* + noun.**
> *so low that…* · *such a low yield that…* — the article moves, and that is the whole
> difference.

> **Why it matters.** An explanation that gives the rule and not the reason cannot be argued
> with, improved, or applied anywhere else.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

An explainer, titled **"Why the beds are a metre square."**
**What do you expect the last paragraph to concede?** Two sentences.

### 5B · Read — two minutes and a quarter

---

**SCHOOL OF AGRICULTURE · TEACHING GARDEN · NOTE FOR NEW STAFF**
**Why the beds are a metre square**

Every bed in the teaching garden is one metre by one metre. New staff ask about this roughly
once a semester, usually in the form of a suggestion that we merge them.

The beds are a metre square so that one student can reach the centre of a bed from any edge
without stepping on the soil. That is the whole rationale, and it is not about yield. A student
who steps on a bed compacts it, and a compacted bed teaches the wrong lesson for the rest of the
year because everything planted in it does slightly worse for a reason the student cannot see.

Twelve crops are grown in order to show twelve growing cycles inside one academic year. Three
of the twelve are chosen specifically because they fail reliably in Melbourne. A student who has
only grown things that work has not learned to read a plant.

The consequence of both decisions is a yield so low that it is not worth measuring. Last year
the garden produced 94 kilograms of food. The campus kitchen uses that in about four days. We
know this and it has never been a problem, because the garden was never specified as a food
source.

It has become one. The kitchen started taking from the garden two years ago, without a decision
being made anywhere, in the way that these things start. It is now on a menu board. The kitchen
has asked whether we could grow "more of the things people actually eat", which is a reasonable
question with an unreasonable answer: we could, and the garden would stop doing what it was
built for within one season.

The honest position is this. At the scale of a teaching garden the two purposes are compatible
because the food is incidental. At any scale the kitchen would find useful, they are not. It
does one of them well.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# A classroom, or a market garden

The garden produced 94 kg last year across 40 metre-square beds. A merged plot on the same land
would produce about 480 kg. The kitchen uses 94 kg in four days and 480 kg in nineteen.

- **Keep it as a classroom.** Twelve crops, forty beds, twenty students a semester. The kitchen
  takes what there is and nobody calls it a food source.
- **Convert it.** Four crops, merged plots, five times the yield. Students visit it rather than
  run it, and three of the twelve growing cycles disappear from the curriculum.
- **Split it.** Half classroom, half production. Half the beds, half the yield, and a boundary
  that will move every year in whichever direction has the louder advocate.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b13_u04_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual**

1. **The brief and the harvest sheet** from Part 5E — and the line added by hand.
2. **The chart** — yield per square metre, and growing cycles taught, for each option.
3. **The voice.** A third-year, thirty seconds: *"I killed a bed of brassicas in second year by
   standing on it in March. Nobody told me I had. I worked it out in September from the yield,
   and I have never stood on soil since. You cannot teach that in a merged plot, because in a
   merged plot nobody would ever have known it was me."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In fours. Everybody speaks twice. Use the frame.

> *It was built so that ______ . In practice ______ , which is why ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *purpose · rationale · constraint · trade-off · specification ·
yield · plot · harvest · curriculum · by design*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**. Choose, say why, and name what it costs.

> I think the School should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .
> What this costs is ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> Keep it as a classroom and build two merged beds at the far end, as the model in 8A suggests.
> The merged beds are not a compromise between the two purposes; they are part of the first one.
> A student who has managed a metre square and then seen what the same soil does at scale has
> learned the trade-off rather than been told it. The kitchen gets about twelve extra kilos,
> which is nothing, and that is also part of the lesson.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The two merged beds went in. They produced 51 kilograms between them, which
> is more than half of what the other forty beds produced together, and the figure is now on the
> wall of the teaching shed where every student sees it in week one.
>
> The kitchen stopped taking from the garden the following year, not because anybody asked but
> because the head chef read the explainer and said she had not realised the garden was a
> classroom. The menu board was changed in an afternoon.
>
> Two years later the School was asked to double the garden for a food-security grant. They
> said no, and the sentence they used in the application was *it does one of them well*, which
> the funder quoted back approvingly in a rejection letter.

**The two beds taught the thing the garden could not say. The chef changed a menu board because
somebody finally wrote down what the garden was for.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say what the School should do about the grant.
