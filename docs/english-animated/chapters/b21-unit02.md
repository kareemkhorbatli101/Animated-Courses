## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### The perfect is a move, not a tense

### 3A · Notice

Look at the four sentences in Part 2 again.

1. Which sentence closes the event at a point in the past?
2. Which one connects a past event to the present moment, and why is that useful here?
3. What does the past perfect in sentence 3 do to the reader's timeline?
4. What does the future perfect in sentence 4 allow the writer to assert?

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u02_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **present perfect** | the event is relevant **now** | *We have always said this.* |
| **past perfect** | the event is relevant to a **past** point | *We had said the opposite, until 2019.* |
| **future perfect** | the event is complete by a **future** point | *By next year we will have said it for a decade.* |
| **the discourse function** | the perfect imports a past event into the present argument | and that is why institutions like it |

> **The perfect hides the date.** *We have always said* contains no year. The simple past
> requires one, which is why an archive is dangerous to a paper that prefers the perfect.

> **Inclusive and exclusive *we*.** English does not mark the difference and most languages that
> do mark it find English maddening here: *we have always said* can mean the institution, the
> editorial board, the staff, or the staff and the readers together, and the sentence does not
> say which.

| | |
|---|---|
| **institutional** | *this paper believes · the paper's view* |
| **impersonal** | *one might say · it is widely held · the general view* |
| **personal** | *speaking personally · the present writer · as a reader* |
| **generic** | *we all know* — the most expansive and least checkable |

> **Why it matters.** Every piece of persuasive writing places the writer somewhere relative to
> the reader, and the placement is done almost entirely by pronouns and perfects.

> One (5) ______ (might say) that nothing has changed, but the archive disagrees.

### 3E · Use — guided

1. We took this position in 2019.
2. We have always opposed the plan.
3. The paper had supported it until the inquiry.

### 3F · Use — open

**In pairs.** One of you writes an editorial sentence using *we*. The other asks, four times,

### 3G · Error autopsy

> ✗ *We have said this in 2017.*
> ✗ *The paper has been believing this since 1911.*
> ✗ *By the time we published, we have already corrected it.*

For each, name the rule and say what the writer was reaching for.

### 3H · Check — eight items

> 1. We ______ (say) this since 2019. *(continuity to now)*
> 2. We ______ (say) the opposite in 2017. *(a point, dated)*
> 3. The board ______ (not discuss) it before the piece ran. *(earlier than a past point)*
> 4. By March we ______ (hold) it for six years. *(complete by a future point)*
> 5. ✗ *We have said this in 2017.* → correct it.
> 6. ✗ *The paper has been believing this since 1911.* → correct it.
> 7. Rewrite *We have always said this* so that it can be checked.
> 8. Rewrite *This paper believes* so that a named person is responsible.

### 5B · Read — two minutes

**The ward that is always in the paper**

Ward 7 has appeared in the Herald eighty-one times in two years. The paper has a bureau four
kilometres away and has never had a correspondent who lives there.

Of the eighty-one pieces, sixty-two quote a police source, forty-one quote a city councillor,
and nine quote somebody who lives in the ward and is not an official. Two of those nine are the
same person, who runs a shop on the main street and has now been quoted in four pieces, in each
of which he is described as a local resident.

The paper's editorials have said, four times in those two years, that the ward has been
neglected. They have not said by whom.

Last month a reporter spent six days in Ward 7 and filed nine hundred words. The readership
figures for that piece were the lowest of the month. The eighty other pieces, filed from the
bureau in an afternoon, did better.

Nobody at the paper thinks that is an argument. Everybody at the paper knows what it will be
used as.

## Part 7 · Pronunciation & Fluency Lab ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u02_p07_v10` · V10 Phonetics Visual**

### A stressed pronoun is a contrast

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 2.3

> *WE have always said this.* — somebody else has not
> *We have ALWAYS said this.* — the continuity is the claim
> *We have always said THIS.* — not the other thing

### 7B · Say

Say each one three times. Then say this:

> *I didn't say it. WE said it.* — the stress moves responsibility from a person to a body, and
> that is the whole function of the editorial *we*.

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *We take the view that…* — unstressed *we*: the institution, routinely
> *WE take the view that…* — stressed: and somebody else takes a different one

**An unstressed pronoun is a reference. A stressed one is a contrast,** and the contrast always
implies a party who is not in the room.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **one position, stated four times — as the institution, impersonally, as
yourself, and as a reader.** Three times, faster.

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### A position, with its holders named · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> This paper opposed the Ward 7 plan in a leader on 14 March 2019. `[the institution, with a
> date — the perfect would have hidden it]`
>
> We had supported an earlier version of it in 2017, and we were wrong about the costs.
> `[the past perfect places the earlier position, and the admission is in the same sentence]`
>
> The present writer was not on the board in 2017 and has read the file. `[the person,
> distinguished from the institution, and a perfect doing proper work]`
>
> It is widely held in this building that the ward has been neglected. By whom is a question we
> have not answered, and by March we will have been asking it for six years. `[the impersonal,
> the challenge to it, and a future perfect that dates the failure]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The first sentence uses a simple past with a date. **What would *we have always opposed* have cost?**
2. *The present writer* appears once. **Why not *I*?**
3. The last sentence turns the paper's own phrase against it. **Which word does the work?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> We have always believed that Ward 7 deserves better. Our readers expect this paper to speak for the communities it serves. Mistakes were made in the past but we have learnt from them. This paper believes in accountability.

Four sentences, four unexaminable claims, and not one date or person in any of them.
**Rewrite it so that every claim has either a date or a name attached.**

### 8C · Plan

> the institution, with a date → the earlier position, placed → the person, separated →

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The byline on the leader page

The Herald's editorials have been unsigned since 1911.

**Option one:** keep it. An unsigned leader is the paper speaking, which is the point of a leader
page, and a byline makes it a column.

**Option two:** sign them. The person who wrote it is named, as everywhere else in the paper, and
*we have always said* becomes harder to write.

**Option three:** keep them unsigned and publish, once a year, the names of everybody who sat on
the board and the dates of every position change.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u02_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 2.6.** The columnist, 30 seconds: *"I have written under my own name for nineteen
years and I have been wrong in public about four things. The leader page has been wrong about
rather more and has never apologised, because there is nobody there to apologise. That is not a
bug in the system. It is the system."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and state the set behind every *we* you use.

> *I would ______ , because ______ , and the people affected are ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and who would be accountable under it.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Signing the leader turns it into a column and loses the one thing a leader page
> can do, which is commit an institution. Publishing the board and the dates keeps the
> institutional voice and makes *we have always said* checkable — which is the only part of the
> current arrangement that is doing damage.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald published the board list and the position changes. The leader page
> stayed unsigned.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: the phrase *we have always said* disappeared from the leader
> page within four months, without any instruction. Writers who knew a reader could check the
> date simply stopped reaching for the construction.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> Nobody banned the phrase. What did the list of dates actually change?
