### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b13_u08_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual · ladder**

### 3C · Know

| Form | Means | Did it happen? |
|---|---|---|
| ***should have* + pp** | it was the right thing and it did not happen | no |
| ***ought to have* + pp** | the same, slightly more formal, often about duty | no |
| ***could have* + pp** | it was possible and it did not happen | no |
| ***would have* + pp** | it was the consequence of a condition that did not hold | no |
| ***might have* + pp** | it was possible, and we do not know | unknown |
| ***needn't have* + pp** | it happened, and it was unnecessary | **yes** |

> **The one that catches everybody.** *Needn't have done* means it was done. *Did not need to
> do* means it was not. *She needn't have come in* — she came. *She did not need to come in* —
> and probably stayed at home.

> ***could have* is not a criticism by itself.** *I could have asked* says it was possible.
> Whether it is an accusation depends entirely on what follows: *I could have asked, but nothing
> told me to* is a defence.

> **Use *otherwise* to carry the condition.** *The threshold should have been four. Otherwise
> nothing fires until it is too late.*

> **Why it matters.** A post-mortem written in *should have* is a list of accusations. One
> written in *could have* and *would have* is a description of a system, and only the second
> kind changes anything.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

An opinion column, titled **"Stop asking who."**
**What do you expect it to propose instead?** Two sentences.

### 5B · Read — two minutes and a quarter

---

**STOP ASKING WHO**
*opinion*

Every institutional failure I have read about in twenty years ends the same way: a report, a
name, and a sentence beginning *should have*. And every one of them produces the same two
results, which are that one person's career is damaged and the thing happens again within four
years.

I want to be careful here, because there is a version of what I am saying that is an excuse for
everybody, and that is not it. People are responsible for what they do. The question is whether
*who* is the most useful question to ask first, and I think the evidence is that it is not.

Take the case everybody in this sector knows. A student missed four consecutive classes in week
three. An automatic alert fired, because the threshold is four, and it went to the unit
coordinator. In week six the coordinator changed, as coordinators do, and the handover document
had a section for assessment deadlines and no section for open student cases. In week nine the
student's personal tutor received a separate flag, from a different system, and emailed the
coordinator — the new one — who replied the same day asking for context she did not have.

Three people. Three emails. Each of them did what their role described. The student withdrew in
week eleven.

The report named the second coordinator. With hindsight, it said, she ought to have asked a
follow-up question. She ought to have. She also ought to have, in the same week, prepared two
hundred and ten exam scripts and run a staff recruitment panel, and if we are listing the things
that a reasonable person ought to have done in week nine we are going to be here for some time.

What nobody asked was why a handover template that runs to four pages has no line on it for
open cases. That is a fixable sentence in a Word document. It does not require anybody to be
braver, or more diligent, or to have more hours in the day, and it would have worked in week
six without anybody noticing it had.

Ask *who* second. Ask it properly, and name people when they have earned it. But ask *what*
first, because *what* is the only one of the two questions that has an answer you can act on
next Tuesday.

> Unfortunately there was a breakdown in communication and the student fell through the cracks.

Correct English, and nothing in it will stop this happening again. **Name the three things it
has turned into weather**, and say what *all staff are reminded* is a substitute for.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Three warnings, three people, one student

A student withdrew in week eleven after failing three units. Three warnings were generated.
Each reached one person. Each of those people followed their role description. The review
panel has to write a finding.

- **Name the second coordinator.** She received the week-nine email, asked for context and did
  not chase it. It is the only point in twelve weeks where one person held enough to act.
- **Find no individual fault.** Three people, three roles, three correct actions. Record it as a
  process failure and recommend a template change.
- **Name the process and the gap.** No individual fault, and a specific, named omission: nobody
  owns the sequence, because no role description contains it, and that is a decision somebody
  made in 2016.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b13_u08_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual**

1. **The three warnings and the handover template** from Part 5E — and the reply that asks for
   context.
2. **The chart** — how many students hit each attendance threshold, and how many withdrew.
3. **The voice.** The second coordinator, thirty seconds: *"I am not going to say I did nothing
   wrong, because a student withdrew. What I will say is that I asked for context and the system
   that sent me the flag could have attached it, and in the eleven weeks since, nobody has asked
   me what the email actually said."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In fours. Everybody speaks twice. Use the frame.

> *______ should have ______ , and nobody did. ______ could have ______ , but ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *threshold · handover · escalate · oversight · accountability ·
omission · with hindsight · in retrospect · nobody owned it · the duty of care*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**. Choose, say why, and name what it costs.

> I think the panel should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .
> What this costs is ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> Name the process and the gap, and date the gap. A finding of no individual fault reads as a
> cover unless it says precisely what was missing and when it went missing, and *nobody owned
> the sequence* is only useful if it is followed by *because the 2016 role review removed case
> continuity from the coordinator description and did not put it anywhere else*. That sentence
> has a year in it and a document behind it. It is also the only version of this finding that
> somebody can act on without waiting for anybody to be braver.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The panel found no individual fault and recommended a line be added to the
> handover template. The line was added in the following July: *Open student cases, with dates*.
>
> It fired for the first time in the second week of the new semester, when a coordinator
> changing mid-unit listed two open cases, one of which had been open since the previous
> October. Nobody had done anything wrong about that one either.
>
> The second coordinator was not named in the final report. She left the university eighteen
> months later and said in her exit interview that the thing which had stayed with her was not
> the review but the eleven weeks before it, during which four people asked her what had
> happened and none of them asked to see the email.

**One line in a Word document, added in July, found a case that had been open since October.
And the only person who was nearly named is the only person anybody remembers.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say what the panel owed the second coordinator.
