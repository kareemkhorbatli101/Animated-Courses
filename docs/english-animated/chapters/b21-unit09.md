### Signposts, and what each one promises

### 3A · Notice

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u09_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **contrast** | *however · nevertheless · by contrast · on the other hand* | the next clause pushes back |
| **concession** | *that said · having said that · in the first place* | you grant something, then continue |
| **result** | *accordingly · hence · thereby · therefore* | the next clause follows from the last |
| **sequence** | *to begin with · thereafter · meanwhile · which brings me to* | position in a list or a timeline |
| **dismissal** | *in any case · for one thing · as it happens* | the previous point is set aside |
| **legal and formal** | *whereby · thereby · thereafter* | written only, and mostly in contracts |

> **A marker is a promise about the next clause.** If you write *however* and the next clause does
> not contrast with anything, the reader has been misdirected, and they will not trust the next
> one either.

> **Position changes force, not meaning.** *However, the figures do not support that* is a formal
> announcement. *The figures, however, do not support that* is quieter and more confident. *The
> figures do not support that, however* sounds like an afterthought, which is sometimes exactly
> what you want.

| | |
|---|---|
| **managing a conversation** | *move on · come back to · go on to* |
| **holding the floor** | *the point being · which brings me to · for one thing* |
| **conceding and continuing** | *having said that · that said · in any case* |

> **Why it matters.** A reader who always knows what kind of sentence is coming next will follow
> you a long way. One who has been promised a contrast and given an example will stop reading, and
> will not be able to tell you why.

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

The title is **"The word *so*, and who is allowed to use it."**
**Why might a rule written in 1911 have trouble with a newsletter?**

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**The word *so*, and who is allowed to use it**

The Herald's style guide runs to two hundred and fourteen pages. It was written in 1911 and last
fully revised in 1998. It contains no entry for *newsletter*, *app*, or *push alert*.

Its entry for discourse markers is four lines long and says that they should be used sparingly.
Counted across a week, the paper averages nine per thousand words. The newsletter averages
thirty-one. The app sits between them at fourteen, not by policy but because the app version is
produced by cutting the print version.

The newsletter has sixty-one thousand subscribers, three quarters of whom open it. The paper
prints a hundred and twelve thousand copies. The app had three hundred and forty thousand readers
last month.

In the meeting the print desk's argument was that *so* at the start of a sentence is a verbal tic.
The newsletter writer's argument was that it is a signpost, that her readers are reading on a
phone between two other things, and that a reader who loses their place in a newsletter does not
come back.

Both are right, and the guide cannot say so, because it has one voice and the paper now has three
readers.

---

6. **Why does the writer point out that the app density is not a policy?**

## Part 7 · Pronunciation & Fluency Lab ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u09_p07_v10` · V10 Phonetics Visual**

### A marker has its own tune

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 9.3

> *However,* ↗ *the figures do not support that.* ↘ — the marker rises, then a pause
> *The figures, however,* → *do not support that.* ↘ — level, and almost swallowed
> *The figures do not support that, however.* ↘↘ — tacked on, and lower than everything

### 7B · Say

Say all three. Then say this:

> *That said — and I do take the point — the figures do not support it.* — two markers, three
> pauses, and the voice does not fall until *it*.

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *So, the guide was written in 1911.* — a signpost: here comes the background
> *SO the guide was written in 1911?* — a challenge: you are summarising to object

**An initial marker said on a level tone is a signpost; said with a jump, it is an argument.**
The word is identical; the tune decides which one the listener hears.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **one argument in three stages, each opened by a marker, each marker in a different
position.** Three times, faster.

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### A guided argument · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> To begin with, the guide was written for one reader, who bought a paper on the way to work in
> 1911. `[the opening marker sets up a list, and the reader now expects at least a second item]`
>
> It has been revised eleven times since; nevertheless, it still has one voice. `[nevertheless,
> not however: the contrast holds in spite of the eleven revisions, which is a different claim]`
>
> The newsletter, as it happens, is read by more people than buy the paper — which brings me to
> the question the guide cannot answer. `[an aside in mid-position, then a marker that hands the
> reader forward instead of back]`
>
> Accordingly, the proposal is not a second guide but one rule: each channel states its own marker
> density, and no marker may promise a relationship the text does not have. `[accordingly earns
> its place because what follows really does follow]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. *Nevertheless* rather than *however*. **What does the extra meaning buy?**
2. *As it happens* sits in the middle of its sentence. **Why not at the front?**
3. The last sentence uses *accordingly*. **Would the paragraph survive without it?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> So, the guide is old. However, it is still used. Moreover, it has been revised. Nevertheless, people ignore it. Furthermore, the newsletter is popular. Therefore, something should be done.

Six sentences, six markers, and not one of them describes the relationship it sits between.
**Rewrite it with three markers, each one true.**

### 8C · Plan

> an opening marker that starts a list → a concession that holds in spite of something → an aside
> in mid-position, then a marker that hands the reader forward → a result that really follows

### 8D · Write — 200 words
### 8E · Peer review — three criteria

> ☐ Does every marker describe the relationship it actually sits between?
> ☐ Is at least one marker somewhere other than the front of its sentence?
> ☐ Could a reader say, at any point, what kind of sentence is coming next?

### 8F · Write it again · 8G · Publish

Rewrite after review. **Publish both, and mark every broken promise in the first version.**

# STUDY FILE · The three markers you own

### 9A · And, but, so 🔊 Track 9.4

Most learners at this level run a whole argument on three markers, and the three are nearly always
*and*, *but* and *so*. Everything else is understood when heard and never produced. The result is
fluent and flat: the listener can follow the words and not the shape.

| | |
|---|---|
| **what you have** | *and · but · so* |
| **what it signals** | addition, contrast, result — the three coarsest relationships |
| **what is missing** | concession, sequence, aside, dismissal |
| **the smallest useful fix** | one marker from each missing group, used this week |

### 9B · Add four

**Write one true sentence with each, about your own week, and say them aloud.**

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# One voice or three

**Option one:** one house style, as now. The paper's voice is the paper's voice, and a reader who
meets it in three places should recognise it in all three.

**Option two:** three style guides, one for each channel, each written by the people who write that
channel.

**Option three:** one guide, with a single new rule: each channel states its own marker density,
and no marker anywhere may promise a relationship the text does not have.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u09_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 9.6.** Nkechi Adeyemi, newsletter, 30 seconds: *"I am not asking to write badly. I am
asking for permission to tell sixty-one thousand people, on a phone, on a bus, which bit is
coming next. On paper you can see the shape of the thing — you know there are four more
paragraphs because you can see them. On a phone there is no shape. The markers are the shape."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and open every turn with a marker you then keep.

> *To begin with, ______ . That said, ______ . Which brings me to ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and the thing it will not fix.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Three guides is three voices and, within two years, three papers. One guide with
> one voice is already failing, because the voice was designed for a reader who could see how long
> the article was. The density clause is administrative and will be argued about; the second
> clause is the one that matters, and it applies identically on paper and on a phone, because a
> broken promise costs a reader everywhere.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald adopted the single rule. The density bands were set at nine, fourteen
> and thirty-one, and were quietly ignored within six months.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: print marker density rose, from nine to seventeen. Writers who
> had assumed markers were forbidden discovered that what was forbidden was the false ones, and
> print comprehension scores rose with the count — on paper, where everybody had agreed the
> problem did not exist.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The rule was written to give the newsletter permission. Why did the newspaper change more?
