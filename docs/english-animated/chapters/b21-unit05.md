## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### A condition is a door, and you choose how wide

### 3A · Notice

Look at the six sentences in Part 2 again.

1. Which two could swap places with no change of meaning at all?
2. Which one narrows the door to a single crack, and which word does it?
3. Sentence 4 describes a world that did not happen. **What is it actually telling the other side?**
4. Sentence 6 has no *if* in it. **What is doing the work?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u05_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **provided · provided that** | formal, and the condition is a requirement | *provided the piece was disclosed* |
| **as long as** | the same meaning, spoken register | *as long as you tell us first* |
| **on condition that** | explicit, and usually written into a document | *on condition that the cap applies* |
| **only if** | exclusive — nothing else will do | *only if the paper was told* |
| **unless** | the negative condition, and it is not *if not* | *unless the paper was told* |
| **in the event that** | formal, about something that may happen | *in the event that a claim is made* |
| **supposing · assuming that** | a hypothesis, invited rather than imposed | *supposing nothing was disclosed* |
| **but for · were it not for** | a condition on something that already happened | *but for the disclosure rule* |
| **failing · absent** | formal, in the sense *if there is no* | *failing agreement tonight* |
| **otherwise** | the consequence of the condition not being met | *disclose it; otherwise the cap goes* |

> **The difference between provided and only if is the size of the opening.** *Provided* says: this
> is what I need. *Only if* says: this and nothing else, and I am telling you that on purpose.

> **Unless is not the same as if not.** *Unless you tell us, the cap goes* has one exception.
> *If you don't tell us, the cap goes* is a straight consequence. The first is an invitation; the
> second is a threat, and a negotiator who confuses them loses the room.

| | |
|---|---|
| **the formal connectors** | *notwithstanding · insofar as · to the extent that · given that* |
| **the hypothetical frame** | *supposing · assuming that · were it not for · all other things being equal* |
| **what you do under pressure** | *fall back on · hold out for · give in* |

> **Why it matters.** A condition stated loosely is a condition you will not be able to enforce,
> and one stated too tightly is a condition nobody will accept. The grammar is the negotiation.

### 3D · Use — controlled

Complete with the connector the context requires.

> We will cap the indemnity (1) ______ the piece was fully disclosed. *(a requirement)*
> (2) ______ agreement tonight, the old contract rolls over. *(formal, "if there is no")*
> (3) ______ the disclosure rule, we would not have offered a cap at all. *(a condition on the past)*
> (4) ______ nothing was disclosed — what happens then? *(an invited hypothesis)*
> Disclose it before publication; (5) ______ , the cap does not apply. *(the consequence of not)*

### 3E · Use — guided

Rewrite each one with the connector in brackets, keeping the meaning as close as you can.

1. If you tell us first, we will cap it. *(provided)*
2. If you don't tell us, the cap goes. *(unless)*
3. We will cap it, but only in cases where you told us. *(only if)*
4. If there is no agreement tonight, the old contract continues. *(failing)*
5. We offered a cap because the disclosure rule exists. *(but for)*

### 3F · Use — open

**In pairs.** One of you makes an offer with a condition. The other must restate the condition
more narrowly, then more widely. **Four rounds, and nobody may use *if* at any point.**

### 3G · Error autopsy

> ✗ *We will cap it unless you tell us first.*
> ✗ *Provided that you would tell us, we would have capped it.*
> ✗ *But for you disclose it, there is no cap.*

For each, name the rule and say what the writer was reaching for.

### 3H · Check — eight items

> 1. We will cap it ______ the piece was disclosed. *(a requirement, formal)*
> 2. ______ agreement tonight, the old contract rolls over. *(one word, formal)*
> 3. ______ ______ the disclosure rule, there would be no cap. *(two words, about the past)*
> 4. ______ nothing was disclosed — what then? *(one word, an invited hypothesis)*
> 5. Disclose it; ______ , the cap does not apply. *(one word)*
> 6. ✗ *We will cap it unless you tell us first.* → correct it.
> 7. Rewrite *If you don't tell us, the cap goes* so that it sounds like an invitation.
> 8. Rewrite *We'll cap it if you tell us* so that nothing else will satisfy the condition.

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**Four hundred and ten signatures and one envelope**

Four hundred and ten people write for the Herald without working for it. The new contributor
contract runs to nine pages, and eight of them were agreed by lunchtime.

Clause 7 says that a contributor will indemnify the paper, without limit, against any claim
arising from their work. The association's position is that the clause is unenforceable in
practice and corrosive in principle: nobody with four thousand three hundred dollars of annual
freelance income can meet a claim, so the clause does not transfer risk, it transfers fear.

The paper's position is that the clause is standard, that it has never been invoked, and that the
alternative is to stop commissioning anything difficult.

In nine years there have been three claims. All three arose from work by staff reporters. None
arose from a freelance piece.

At eight o'clock a side letter was placed face down on the table. It had been offered, three
months earlier, to eleven named contributors, capping their liability at five thousand dollars.
The association had not been told. Nobody in the room disputed that the eleven are the eleven who
file the hardest stories.

The negotiator read it twice and said one sentence: *That is not what we agreed.*

### A long conditional does not fall until the end

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 5.3

> *Provided the piece was fully disclosed before publication,* ↗ *we will cap it.* ↘
> *We will cap it,* ↘ *provided the piece was fully disclosed.* ↘
> *Unless you tell us first,* ↗ *the cap does not apply.* ↘

### 7B · Say

Say each one three times. Then say this twenty-two-word sentence without letting your voice fall
until the last three words:

> *Provided the piece was fully disclosed to a named editor before publication, and nothing was
> withheld, the cap applies.*

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *We will cap it, provided you tell us.* — the condition is an afterthought, and sounds routine
> *Provided you tell us, we will cap it.* — the condition is the news, and the room hears it

**Front the condition and it becomes the point; put it last and it becomes a detail.** Negotiators
front conditions they intend to enforce.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **one offer, three conditions, each fronted, and the voice level until the end of
each.** Three times, faster.

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### An offer with its conditions · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> The paper will cap a contributor's liability at the fee paid for the piece, provided the piece
> was disclosed in full to a named editor before publication. `[the offer, then the condition,
> fronted inside the sentence so it cannot be skimmed]`
>
> Unless that disclosure was made, the cap does not apply. `[unless, with one exception — not a
> threat, and the difference is deliberate]`
>
> In the event that a claim is made, the paper will conduct the defence and the contributor will
> cooperate. `[the formal frame for something that may happen, and both obligations in one
> sentence]`
>
> But for the disclosure rule, this cap would not be on the table; all other things being equal,
> the association would have preferred the clause deleted, and has said so. `[a condition on the
> past, and the other side's position stated by us before they state it]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The condition is fronted in sentence one. **What would moving it to the end have done?**
2. Sentence two uses *unless*. **What would *if you don't* have sounded like?**
3. The last sentence states the other side's preference. **Why give that away?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> We are pleased to offer a cap on liability. We would of course expect contributors to act responsibly and to keep us informed. Obviously if something goes wrong we will need to look at the circumstances. We hope this is acceptable.

Four sentences, three conditions, and not one of them stated in a form anybody could enforce.
**Rewrite it with every condition explicit and the consequence of each one named.**

### 8C · Plan

> the offer with its condition fronted → the exception → the formal frame for a future event →
> the condition on the past, and the other side's position

### 8D · Write — 200 words
### 8E · Peer review — three criteria

> ☐ Is every condition stated in a form somebody could check?
> ☐ Does each connector do the job you meant — requirement, exception or hypothesis?
> ☐ Is the consequence of each unmet condition stated, not implied?

### 8F · Write it again · 8G · Publish

Rewrite after review. **Publish both, and underline every conditional connector.**

Learners set conditions on their own study constantly, and almost never in a form that can be

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Clause seven

It is twenty past seven and eight of the nine pages are agreed.

**Option one:** keep clause 7 as drafted. It is standard across the industry, it has never been
invoked, and removing it will be read as an admission.

**Option two:** delete it. The paper commissions the work, the paper carries the risk, and the
claims log says the risk is not where the clause puts it.

**Option three:** cap liability at the fee paid for the piece, conditional on full disclosure to a
named editor before publication — and withdraw every side letter, publishing the fact that they
existed.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u05_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 5.6.** The association's negotiator, 30 seconds: *"I have been asked all evening what we
would accept. Here is what we will not accept: a clause that makes eleven people safe and three
hundred and ninety-nine people quiet. Cap it, delete it, I can argue either at a meeting. The side
letter I cannot argue at all, because it is not a term, it is a favour, and a favour can be
withdrawn by the person who gave it."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and front every condition you state.

> *I would ______ , provided ______ , and unless ______ , I would ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the condition it rests on, and what would make you
abandon it.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. Deletion is cleaner and I would sign it, but it removes the one thing the clause
> could usefully do, which is make a contributor tell an editor what they know before publication
> rather than after. A cap at the fee is small enough to be survivable and large enough to be felt,
> and it is conditional on the behaviour the paper actually wants. The side letters go in any case:
> whatever they bought, they cost more.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald capped liability at the fee, conditional on disclosure, and
> withdrew the eleven side letters. Two of the eleven resigned from the panel in protest at losing
> them. Nobody else did.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: pre-publication disclosure by freelancers rose from under a
> third to four fifths within a year — not because the cap was generous, but because a condition
> somebody can meet is a condition they think about, and an unlimited liability is one they can
> only try not to think about at all.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The uncapped clause asked for more and got less. Why?
