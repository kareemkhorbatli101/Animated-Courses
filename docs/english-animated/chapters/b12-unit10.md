## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Wishing about now, and about then

### 3A · Notice

Look at the four sentences in Part 2.

1. In sentence 1, does she know? In sentence 2?
2. **Why is the verb in the past in sentence 1 when the wish is about now?**
3. Sentence 3 uses *if only*. **Is it stronger or weaker than *I wish*?**
4. In sentence 4, *I'd rather somebody said it* — why a past tense after *would rather*?
5. Try *"I wish I know."* **Why does English refuse this?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b12_u10_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual · ladder**

### 3C · Know

| | Shape | About |
|---|---|---|
| **wish + past simple** | *I wish I **knew**.* | now — and I do not |
| **wish + past perfect** | *I wish I**'d known**.* | the past — and I did not |
| **wish + *would*** | *I wish he**'d stop** saying that.* | somebody else's behaviour, now — a complaint |
| **wish + could** | *I wish I **could** tell you.* | ability, now |
| **if only** | same shapes, more force | ***If only** I'd known.* |
| **would rather + past** | *I'd rather you **didn't**.* | what you want somebody else to do |
| **suppose / supposing** | + past, for a hypothesis | ***Suppose** we **asked** him.* |

> **The past tense is doing the same job as in the second conditional:** it marks distance from
> reality, not past time.

> ***wish + would* cannot be used about yourself.**
> ✗ *I wish I would stop.* ✓ *I wish I could stop.* ✓ *I wish he would stop.*
> It is a complaint about somebody else's choices, and you cannot complain about your own.

> **Watch out.** ✗ *I wish I know.* ✗ *I wish I would know.* ✓ *I wish I knew.*

## Part 5 · Reading ◆ **A · THE WORLD**

### 5A · Predict

**"The curriculum is not the problem."**
**What do you expect the report to say the problem is?** Two sentences.

### 5B · Read — two minutes and a half

---

**THE CURRICULUM IS NOT THE PROBLEM**
*Extract from a review of first-year clinical experience*

**4.1** We asked 211 doctors in their first year what they wished they had been taught. We
expected clinical answers. We received eleven.

**4.2** The remaining answers divided into three groups.

**4.3** The first group — about half of all answers — concerned **who to ask**. Not what to do:
who to ask. Several respondents described spending between twenty and fifty minutes locating a
person who could answer a question that took ninety seconds to answer. One wrote: *I wish
somebody had drawn me a map of who knows what.*

**4.4** The second group concerned **how to say that you do not know**. Respondents described
learning this by one of two routes: either a senior colleague said *I don't know* in front of
them in the first fortnight, in which case they could do it from then on, or nobody did, in which
case most of them reported still finding it difficult several years later. It appears to be
almost entirely a matter of modelling, and the modelling either happens in the first two weeks or
does not happen.

**4.5** The third group concerned **what is normal**. Respondents reported being unable to tell,
for about a year, whether a thing that felt wrong was wrong or was simply how hospitals are.
Several said they had stopped reporting things in their second year, not because they had stopped
noticing but because they had stopped being able to tell.

**4.6** None of these three is in any curriculum. Two of the three cannot obviously be put in
one. The first can, costs almost nothing, and is the one nobody does.

**4.7** We note, without comment, that 190 of the 211 responses described something the
institution could have provided and did not, and that the institution has asked us three times
whether the clinical content is adequate.

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Needed, or good

Matías has to choose a speciality. There are nine unfilled posts in the region in one and two in
the other. He is measurably better at the one with two.

- **Where he is needed.** Nine posts, a job anywhere, a shortage that is somebody's emergency,
  and work he is competent at and does not love.
- **Where he is good.** Two posts, a competitive application, and the thing three separate
  supervisors have independently said he has a feel for.
- **Neither yet.** A year in a general post, which delays the decision, costs a year of training,
  and is what about a fifth of his year group does.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b12_u10_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual**

1. **The curriculum and the survey** from Part 5E — and the hand annotation.
2. **The chart** — posts, applicants and attrition in each speciality over ten years.
3. **The voice.** Dr Herrera, thirty seconds: *"I chose the one I was needed in, in 2007, because
   of a train. I have been good at it for eighteen years and I would make the same decision now
   and I would like somebody to notice that those are two different sentences."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In fours. Everybody speaks twice. Use the frame.

> *I wish ______ . If only ______ . So I think ______ .*

**Words you can use:** *gap · insight · competence · confidence · mentorship · out of my depth ·
on the job · with hindsight · the hard way · a steep learning curve*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**. Choose, say why, and name what it costs.

> I think Matías should ______________________ ,
> because ______________________ .
> What this costs is ______________________ .

### 11D · Model decision

> He should take the one he is good at and should say out loud, now, that he is doing it, because
> the shortage speciality's nine vacancies are a real emergency and the only honest thing is to
> decline them in public rather than quietly. Shortages are solved by making the work better, not
> by recruiting people who will be adequate at it and leave in four years, and the attrition
> figures say exactly that. He owes the region a clear no, which is more than most people give it.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** He took the one he is good at. He wrote a four-line note to the regional
> training lead explaining why he had not applied to the shortage speciality, which he was told
> was unnecessary and which he sent anyway.
>
> The training lead quoted it, anonymously, in a paper to the regional board about why the
> shortage is not a recruitment problem. Two other students sent similar notes the following
> year.
>
> He is in his third year and has twice been close to moving. He says the thing that keeps him is
> not that he is good at it but that in his first fortnight somebody said *I don't know* in front
> of him in a corridor, and that he has been able to say it ever since, and that he does not
> think he would have been able to in the other department.

**He chose the thing he was good at. The reason he has stayed is something that happened in a
corridor in his second week.**

> **Was it the right decision?** Say why — and say what the four-line note was actually for.
