## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Moving the point to where the reader is looking

### 3A · Notice

Look at the five sentences in Part 2 again.

1. In sentence 1, which word carries the point, and how do you know?
2. Sentence 2 begins with four words that contain no information at all. **What are they for?**
3. Sentence 3 puts the fare box early. **What happens to everything after *that*?**
4. Sentences 4 and 5 both delay the point. **Which one delays it longer, and why might you want to?**

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u06_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **the it-cleft** | *It was X that…* — focus early, the rest presupposed | *It was the fare box that they borrowed against.* |
| **the pseudo-cleft** | *What… was X* — focus at the end, where English wants it | *What they did was borrow against the fare box.* |
| **the reversed pseudo-cleft** | *X is what…* — the focus first, emphatically | *The fare box is what they borrowed against.* |
| **the *what happened was* frame** | an empty opening that buys the reader a second | *What happened was that the city borrowed.* |
| **the *thing that* frame** | the most natural spoken version of all of them | *The thing that nobody mentioned was the fare box.* |

> **English puts new information at the end of a sentence.** Every cleft is a device for getting
> something to the end — or, in the it-cleft, for declaring that everything after it is already
> agreed.

> **The frame carries no information, and that is the point.** *What the city did was* contains
> nothing a reader did not already know. It costs five words and buys one position, and the
> position is worth more than the words.

| | |
|---|---|
| **narrowing to the item** | *namely · specifically · precisely* |
| **ranking it** | *chiefly · notably · crucially* |
| **going to the root** | *essentially · fundamentally* |
| **spoken focus frames** | *all I did was · where it goes wrong is · that is why · it was then that* |

> **Why it matters.** Readers do not read sentences; they read positions. A true sentence with the
> point in the wrong position is read as a different sentence, and the correction column cannot
> fix it, because nothing in it was wrong.

### 3D · Use — controlled

**Six hundred readers, four paragraphs, one story**

In 2016 the city raised money for the east-end line by selling the right to thirty years of ticket
income. The fare box was not the security for a loan. It was the thing that was sold.

The Herald reported this accurately, in a story of nine hundred words, whose first paragraph read:
*The city has finalised a financing package for the east-end line, involving a range of
instruments including a securitisation of future farebox revenue.*

Four years later the readers' editor surveyed six hundred readers who had read the story. Thirty-
eight per cent could state what the city had done. Of the rest, the largest group believed the
line had been paid for by a fare rise.

The cadet course now begins with that paragraph. The cadets rewrite it four ways. The version that
works, every year, is the one that begins *What the city did was*, which the subeditors hate,
because it uses five words to say nothing and is, by any measure they were trained on, worse
writing.

It is also the only version that six hundred readers could repeat back.

### The last stressed word is the point

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 6.3

> *The city borrowed against the FARE BOX.* — the fare box is the news
> *The CITY borrowed against the fare box.* — and not the province
> *The city BORROWED against the fare box.* — it did not sell it, it borrowed

### 7B · Say

Say each one three times. Then say this:

> *What the city did was SELL it.* — the frame is unstressed, every word of it, and the point
> lands on the last content word, where English wants it.

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *It was the FARE BOX that they sold.* — the focus is early, and everything after is agreed
> *What they sold was the FARE BOX.* — the focus is last, and the reader has been prepared

**A cleft moves the nuclear stress, and the nuclear stress is the sentence's actual meaning.**
Write the stress where you want it and the syntax will follow.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **one fact, said five ways, with the nuclear stress in a different place each
time.** Three times, faster.

## Part 8 · Writing ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

### An explanation that lands · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> What the city did in 2016 was sell the next thirty years of ticket income. `[the pseudo-cleft:
> five words of frame, and the point at the end where it is remembered]`
>
> It was not a loan secured against the fare box; it was the fare box itself. `[an it-cleft
> correcting the misconception the reader almost certainly arrived with]`
>
> The thing that nobody mentioned at the time was what happens in year thirty-one. `[the spoken
> frame, which delays the point for a full clause and is the most readable of all of them]`
>
> Essentially, the line was paid for by passengers who have not travelled yet; specifically, by
> everybody who will buy a ticket between now and 2046. `[root first, then the exact set — the two
> adverbs do different jobs and must come in that order]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. The first sentence spends five words saying nothing. **What did those five words buy?**
2. The second sentence corrects something the reader was never told. **Why is that allowed here?**
3. *Essentially* then *specifically*. **What happens if you swap them?**

### 8B · The anti-model

> The financing package, which involved a securitisation of future revenue streams in conjunction with conventional instruments, was finalised in 2016 following a procurement process that had commenced the previous year and was subject to the usual approvals.

One sentence, forty-two words, every fact true, and the point buried in the middle of a
subordinate clause.
**Rewrite it as four sentences, each with its point at the end.**

### 8C · Plan

> the pseudo-cleft with the main point → the it-cleft correcting the misconception → the spoken
> frame delaying the hardest idea → the root, then the exact set

### 8D · Write — 200 words
### 8E · Peer review — three criteria

> ☐ Does each sentence end on the word you want remembered?
> ☐ Is every frame earning its words, or is one of them just a throat-clearing?
> ☐ Could a reader state the point back to you after one reading?

### 8F · Write it again · 8G · Publish

Rewrite after review. **Publish both, and mark the last stressed word of every sentence.**

# STUDY FILE · Explaining something in a language you are still learning

### 9A · The point at the wrong end 🔊 Track 6.4

Learners explaining something in English tend to put the point first, because they are not sure
they will be able to finish the sentence. The result is accurate, complete and unmemorable: the
listener meets the hardest idea before they are ready for it.

| | |
|---|---|
| **what you do** | state the conclusion, then the background |
| **why** | insurance, in case the sentence collapses |
| **what it costs** | the listener has nothing to attach the conclusion to |
| **the fix** | one frame — *what happens is…* — which buys you four words of thinking time |

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# The first paragraph

Thirty-eight per cent of readers could state what a correct story said.

**Option one:** change nothing. The story was accurate, the words were right, and a newspaper
cannot write down to its readers without insulting them.

**Option two:** a house rule that the point of any complex story appears in the first seven words.

**Option three:** a one-sentence standfirst above every complex story, written in a focus frame —
*What the city did was…* — and written before the story, not after it.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u06_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 6.6.** Dennis Hoy, subeditor, 30 seconds: *"I have spent thirty-one years taking those
five words out of other people's copy. 'What the city did was.' It is flab, it is a throat-clear,
and I was right every time by the standard I was taught. The survey says I was also removing the
only part of the sentence the reader could hold on to. Both of those things are true and I have
not worked out what to do with that yet."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame, and put the point of each turn at the end of your sentence.

> *What I would do is ______ , because ______ , and the cost of it is ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and what you would be giving up.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. The seven-word rule is a blunt instrument that will produce seven bad words at the
> top of every difficult story. The standfirst keeps the story itself intact and gives the point
> one position where it cannot be missed. Writing it first is the part that matters: a writer who
> cannot put the point in one framed sentence does not yet know what the story is, and finding that
> out before writing nine hundred words is worth more than the standfirst.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald adopted the standfirst. Readers' comprehension on complex stories
> rose from thirty-eight per cent to seventy-one within a year.
>
> Then something nobody predicted: the standfirsts began to change the stories. Writers drafting
> the framed sentence first found, often, that they could not finish it — and the thing they could
> not finish was usually the thing they had not yet reported. The desk's query rate to reporters
> rose by a third, before the first word of copy was written.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The rule was about where a sentence puts its point. Why did it change what got reported?
