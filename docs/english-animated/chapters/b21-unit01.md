## Part 3 · Grammar Lab 1 ◆ **B · THE SYSTEM**

### Aspect is not about time

### 3A · Notice

Look at the four sentences in Part 2 again.

1. Which of the four presents the event as a whole, closed and countable?
2. Which presents it as in progress, and therefore incomplete and possibly reversible?
3. What does the perfect in sentence 3 add that sentence 1 does not have?
4. What does sentence 4 claim about something that did not happen?

### 3B · See

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u01_p03_v09` · V9 Grammar Visual**

### 3C · Know

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **simple** | the event as a complete unit | *The company cut four hundred jobs.* |
| **continuous** | the event as in progress, unfinished | *The company is cutting four hundred jobs.* |
| **perfect** | the event seen from a later point | *The company has cut four hundred jobs.* |
| **perfect continuous** | duration up to a later point | *The company has been cutting jobs since March.* |

> **The choice is a claim, not a decoration.** *Is cutting* says the process is live and the
> number may change. *Cut* says it is done and the number is final. A newspaper that writes
> *cuts* when it means *is cutting* has published a fact it does not have.

> **Four tenses, one distinction.** Aspect is independent of time: *was cutting*, *is cutting*,
> *will be cutting* make the same claim about completeness at three different times.

| | |
|---|---|
| **state verbs resist the continuous** | *owns*, *contains*, *consists of* — and when they take it, the meaning shifts |
| **the continuous softens** | *I'm hoping you can help* is less committed than *I hope* |
| **the perfect links** | the event is being presented **because of its consequence now** |
| **the future in the past** | *was going to*, *had been going to* — a plan that did not survive |

> **Why it matters.** Every sentence in a news report is a claim about how finished something is,
> and that is the claim most likely to be wrong.

### 5B · Read — two minutes

---

**Forty minutes behind**

At 15.40 on a Tuesday the Herald had a single source, a screenshot and a claim that four hundred
jobs were going at a plant in Thunder Bay. The market closed at 16.00. Everybody in the room knew
what the story was worth before the close and what it was worth after it.

The stringer who filed it has lived in Thunder Bay for eleven years and has never been wrong. That
is not the same as corroboration and everybody in the room knew that too.

At 15.52 a rival outlet published. The share price moved four per cent in the last eight minutes
of trading. The Herald was still asking a second plant for a second account, and did not publish
until 09.10 the next morning.

On Thursday the company confirmed three hundred and ten jobs, not four hundred, and said no
decision had been taken on the timing. The rival ran a correction on Friday, two column
centimetres, on page eleven.

Nobody in the Herald newsroom believes the paper made money on Tuesday. The argument in the room
since has been about a different question: whether being right on Wednesday is a product anybody
is buying.

### Prominence falls on what is new

### 7A · Hear 🔊 Track 1.3

> *We can't stand it UP.* — the problem is the verification
> *WE can't stand it up. (somebody else can)* — the problem is us
> *We can't stand IT up. (we can stand the other one up)* — the problem is this story

### 7B · Say

Say each one three times. Then say this:

> *The company is cutting jobs. It isn't cutting FOUR HUNDRED jobs.* — the second prominence is
> the whole correction.

### 7C · Make it mean something

Say these two:

> *The company IS cutting jobs.* — somebody has denied it
> *The company is CUTTING jobs.* — somebody expected something else

**English puts the main prominence on the information the listener does not already have.**
Moving it is not emphasis for its own sake; it is a claim about what the listener knows.

### 7D · Fluency 30 ⏺

Thirty seconds: **one claim, stated four times with four different aspects**, each followed by
the evidence it would require. Three times, faster.

### A story you can defend · 200 words

### 8A · Read the model

> **Thunder Bay plant: 400 jobs under review** `[the headline hedges with two words]`
>
> The Herald understands that the company is considering cuts of up to four hundred jobs at its
> Thunder Bay plant. `[*understands* and *is considering* — a claim with a source removed and a
> process presented as unfinished]`
>
> A single source, who was not authorised to speak, described a board paper circulated on Monday.
> The Herald has not seen the paper. `[what is not known, said in the second paragraph, not the
> ninth]`
>
> The company has been losing money at the plant since the third quarter of last year, which is a
> matter of public record. `[the perfect continuous, on the one fact that needs no source]`
>
> The company declined to comment. No decision is understood to have been taken on timing.
> `[the second hedge, and the thing the rival did not write]`

**Questions about the writer's choices:**

1. *is considering* rather than *is cutting*. **What exactly does the change in verb and aspect buy?**
2. *The Herald has not seen the paper.* **Why in paragraph two?**
3. The only perfect continuous in the piece is on the public-record fact. **Why there?**

**Rewrite it so that every claim is defensible against the evidence described in 5B.**

## Part 11 · The Decision ◆ **C · THE PERFORMANCE**

# Forty minutes

It is 15.40. One source, a screenshot, a claim that moves a share price, and twenty minutes of
trading left.

**Option one:** publish now with hedges. *Is understood to be considering.* Defensible, fast,
and a hedge in a headline is read by nobody.

**Option two:** hold until a second account. Costs the exclusive and about forty thousand
page views, and is what the paper's own standards policy requires.

**Option three:** publish the fact that you are holding it — a line saying the Herald has heard
the claim and has not been able to corroborate it.

### 11A · The evidence

> **FIGURE `fig_b21_u01_p11_v06` · V6 Data Visual · full page**

🔊 **Track 1.6.** The standards editor, 30 seconds: *"We ran four hundred corrections last year.
Nobody can tell me what one costs, because the cost is not on the day you correct it. It is six
months later, in a different story, when somebody decides on balance not to believe us."*

### 11B · Talk about it

In threes. Use the frame. Everybody takes one position and must state the evidence it rests on.

> *I would ______ , because ______ , and I would be wrong if ______ .*

### 11C · Decide

Write **three sentences**: the decision, the evidence, and the condition under which you would
reverse it.

### 11D · Model decision

> Option three. The first two are a choice between being wrong and being late, and both of those
> have been priced. Nobody has ever priced the third, which is the only one that tells the reader
> what the paper actually knows at 15.40 — and what a reader buys from a newspaper, if they buy
> anything, is the difference between those two states.

### 11E · The Reveal

> **What happened.** The Herald held. It published at 09.10 with *is understood to be
> considering*, and it has never published a line saying what it is holding.
>
> Then something nobody in the room predicted: the rival's correction, two column centimetres on
> page eleven, was screenshotted and shared about forty thousand times. The correction travelled
> further than the story it corrected, and it is still the first result for the company's name.

**One question. You do not have to answer it out loud.**

> The newsroom argued for two days about whether being right on Wednesday is a product. What did
> the forty thousand screenshots suggest about who was buying it?
