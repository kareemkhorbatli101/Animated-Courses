## B1.3 · Unit 1 · The Next Video

**0B** · Guess it
The poster showing a 22 per cent pass rate and the half-empty lecture theatre. One is the published figure, the other is the room it describes, and the two are eleven weeks apart.

**1B** · Sort — what you do, what it does, or what it counts?
> **what it does** — autoplay · notification · feed · prompt
> **what it counts** — engagement · retention · metric · dwell
> **what you do** — scroll
**The pair:** *engagement* and *dwell*. Dwell is the seconds a reader spends; engagement is the same seconds named from the platform's side, with approval built in. *(scroll* also belongs in two columns — it is a thing you do and a thing that is counted.)

**2A** · Attendance and the pass rate 🔊 Track 1.1
**1** c) 45 to 22 · **2** b) 11 · **3** a) at two in the morning · **4** b) the look of the room
**Table** — Dr Ahmadi: has measured attendance and outcomes; concludes the recordings do no harm; has not measured who the non-attenders are. The committee: has measured attendance; concludes engagement has fallen; has not measured attainment by group. Bec: has surveyed 41 students; concludes the gain is concentrated among students in paid work; has not measured anything representative (self-selected sample).

**Noticing** — Sentence 1 is defining: *some* of the students, only the ones who work. Sentence 2 is non-defining: *all* of them, and they happen to work. There is no way to say how many from sentence 1 alone; sentence 2 means the whole group.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 of · 2 which · 3 which · 4 who / that · 5 which · 6 of · 7 which

**3E** · Use — guided
1 Forty-one students, thirty-one of whom work, completed the unit. · 2 The recordings, which are watched at night, have been available since March. · 3 Attendance has halved, which nobody disputes. · 4 There are eleven lectures, four of which were not recorded. · 5 The pass rate rose eleven points, which is the figure in dispute.

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 of whom · 2 which · 3 which · 4 who / that · 5 of which · 6 all of whom · 7 *The students who work passed* (defining — no commas, no *that* after a comma) · 8 *41 students, most of whom work, passed* **or** *41 students passed; most of them work.*

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 That attendance in the two piloted units had fallen by about a third and that assessment outcomes were unchanged.  
2 That she would not support a measure which reduced attendance, attendance being itself a form of engagement. Asked for evidence that attendance *caused* attainment rather than merely accompanying it, she conceded that she did not think the question could be answered.  
3 That of eleven students consulted, nine were in paid employment, most of whom had missed a lecture for a shift. She asked the committee to record that none of them had chosen between a lecture and leisure.  
4 Because it pre-empts the inference the committee would otherwise draw — that non-attendance is a preference rather than a constraint.  
5 Recording for a two-year trial, subject to review, with attendance monitored.  
6 Attendance data and employment data.  
7 Attendance data has been collected continuously since 2019; employment data has never been collected.  
8 That recording was made permanent in 2022 by a decision of the Dean, without reference to the committee — and that the 2021 review never took place.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**piloted** — ran as a limited trial first · **accompanying it** — occurring alongside, without causing · **subject to review** — approved only until somebody looks again · **would be difficult** — a refusal with no reason attached · **without reference to the committee** — without asking the body that had authorised it

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Attendance 45 → 22 per cent over six years; the overall pass rate moves two points, inside the normal four-point range.  
2 The +11 points for students in paid work: 41 responses out of 214 enrolled, self-selected, from a student union survey the university did not commission.  
3 Four — 3, 10, 17 and 24 March, all recorded as hardware faults.  
4 Attendance at those four averaged 37 per cent against 22 for the rest: fifteen points higher, which is the only controlled comparison in the file.  
5 The footnote: the university does not collect employment data and has not done so since it was requested in 2019.

**6A** · Sort — adds, contrasts, names, or points back?
> **names** — namely · incidentally · respectively
> **adds** — likewise · moreover · additionally
> **points back** — at which point · the latter of which · of which
> **contrasts** — conversely
*Likewise* adds by analogy rather than by accumulation; accept it in either of the first two columns if the learner can say why. *Incidentally* names in the weak sense — it labels what follows as an aside.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 of · 2 of · 3 point · 4 of · 5 back · 6 refer

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
1 namely · 2 respectively · 3 Incidentally

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** Two cohorts, **the latter of which** had recordings from week one.
> **2** The pass rate rose, **which** is the disputed figure. *(sentential)*
> **3** The recordings, **of which there are eleven**, are watched at night. *(or: There are eleven recordings, all of which are watched at night.)*
> **4** Only the students **who** are in work did better. *(defining — no commas)*
> **5** Attendance halved, **which** nobody disputes. *(sentential)*
> **6** All the students, **who** are in work, did better. *(non-defining — the clause describes the whole group)*
> **7** 41 students, **31 of whom** work.
> **8** Eleven lectures, **four of which** were not recorded.

## B1.3 · Unit 2 · One Lift, Two Buildings

**0B** · Guess it
The worn path across the grass and the propped fire door. Both exist because the lift is out of order, and neither was made by anybody with authority — they were made by the people who had to get in.

**1B** · Sort — the building, the policy, or the person?
> **the building** — ramp · barrier · signage · access
> **the person** — disability
> **the policy** — accommodation · provision · exemption · inclusion
**The one that moves:** *disability*. It is filed under the person and is produced by the building: a step is a step for everybody, and it only becomes a disability where there is no second route. *access* also sits in two columns — a property of a building and a thing a person does or cannot do.

**2A** · The audit 🔊 Track 2.1
**1** b) a ramp · **2** c) neither · **3** c) A · **4** c) B
**Table** — Building A (Menzies): 2,100 people a week; a ramp at 1:11 with a 44 N door; the alternative route is the ramp itself. Building B (Harcourt): 400 a week; nothing; the rear entrance named in the access statement, which opens into a plant room.

**Noticing** — Sentence 3. *Taken by nobody* attaches to *the route*, while the main clause subject is also *the route*, so it is sound; but the opening participle in sentence 3 (*marked on the map*) is a reduced relative, not a hanging participle. The one to watch is sentence 3 against sentence 1: in 1 the subject of *having walked* is *I*, and in 3 there is no first-person subject anywhere.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 Having walked · 2 Given · 3 marked · 4 taken · 5 Lacking · 6 Taken · 7 having

**3E** · Use — guided
1 Lacking a ramp, B has no step-free route.  
2 Having measured both, I changed my mind.  
3 The route marked on the map is not the one people use.  
4 **Cannot be reduced safely.** The subjects differ — the lift was out, the lecture was moved — so *Being out of order, the lecture was moved* says the lecture was out of order.  
5 Taken together, the figures do not agree.  
*(Item 4 is the first of the two. The second is a trap only if a learner tries to reduce item 2 to* After measuring both*, which is fine, or to* Measuring both*, which loses the sequence.)*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 Having walked · 2 Given · 3 marked · 4 Lacking · 5 Taken · 6 *Lacking a ramp, the building has no route* — the route cannot lack a ramp · 7 Having · 8 Due / Owing

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Argue against her own union's submission.  
2 2,100 a week for Menzies against 400 for Harcourt. They supported the case for putting the lift in Menzies, which is the case the union made.  
3 The gradient is 1:11 where it should be 1:14, and it ends at a door heavier than the standard allows.  
4 It describes a step-free rear entrance. There is a rear entrance, it is step-free, and it opens into a plant room.  
5 In Menzies we would be improving access for people who have it badly; in Harcourt we would be creating it for people who do not have it at all.  
6 He concedes she is right about Harcourt, and says he did not know about the plant room.  
7 Lifts are funded from a capital line assessed on usage, so a lift serving 400 people a week scores below the threshold and will be queried every year for fifteen years. He raises it not as a counter-argument but because it will come up, and because he will not remember it in four years.  
8 Fund Harcourt, and write her paragraph into the capital submission.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**argue against our own submission** — contradict the case her own side made · **on every measure of volume** — by any count of how many people · **the standard allows** — the published maximum · **a capital line** — a budget for building work, separate from maintenance · **will be queried** — will be challenged every year by someone reading only the usage figure

**5E** · The counter-text
1 That all teaching buildings have a step-free entrance, and that where the principal entrance is stepped a step-free alternative is signed from it. Menzies: ramp at the principal entrance. Harcourt: rear entrance, signed. Both last reviewed 2014.  
2 (i) Menzies ramp gradient 1:11 against a standard of 1:14. (ii) Menzies door 44 N against a 30 N maximum. (iii) Harcourt signage from the principal entrance: none found, and the statement requires it. (iv) Harcourt rear entrance must reach a corridor and reaches a plant room.  
3 1:11; it should be 1:14.  
4 Since 2014 — eleven years, and it was true in 2014.  
5 The fire evacuation route, both buildings: the auditor lacked the authority to open the doors and says so.

**6A** · Sort — present participle, past participle, or preposition?
> **preposition** — given · due · considering
> **past participle** — based · followed · taken · surrounded · seen
> **present participle** — lacking · facing · having
*Given* and *considering* are past and present participles by form and prepositions by behaviour — they take no subject and cannot hang. That is the point of the column.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 said · 2 circumstances · 3 Due · 4 taken · 5 considered · 6 allow

**6C** · Three that are not participles any more
1 *If you take the numbers into account, A is the obvious choice.* Lost: the compression, and the sense that the numbers are agreed rather than being argued for.  
2 *If you consider the gradient, the ramp is unusable in the wet.* Lost: the impersonality — *considering* makes the gradient the reason, not the reader.  
3 *Because the lift was out, the class was moved.* Lost: nothing much, which is why *due to* survives in formal writing — it buys brevity at no cost.

**6D** · Two that need a preposition to finish
1 based on · 2 followed by · 3 based on

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Cannot be reduced.** The subjects differ — the lift was out, the class was moved. *Being out of order, the class was moved* makes the class out of order.
> **2** **Cannot be reduced.** The subject of the first clause is *nobody*, the subject of the second is *the statement*.
> **3** Having walked it, I knew the map was wrong.
> **4** The students waiting at the rear door cannot get in. *(reduced relative)*
> **5** Taken together, the figures disagree.
> **6** Having measured both, she changed her mind.
> **7** The route marked on the map adds 400 m. *(reduced relative)*
> **8** Lacking a ramp, the building excludes people.

**7C** · Make it mean something
The first is the concession: the pause after *that* closes the participle clause, and *four hundred is still four hundred* is the main clause. The second runs on, so *that four hundred is still four hundred* becomes the object of *said* — a report of what somebody said, with no concession in it at all.

**12A** · Recycle — twelve items
1 Having walked · 2 Given · 3 marked · 4 Lacking · 5 free · 6 order · 7 paper · 8 around · 9 of whom · 10 which · 11 is having · 12 which

## B1.3 · Unit 3 · The Best Voice in the Room

**0B** · Guess it
The hand half-raised and lowered again, and the notebook with a question written and crossed out. Both are Thanh. The first silence is a decision taken in the room and lost to timing; the second is taken before the room, and is the one nobody can see.

**1B** · Sort — the room, the argument, or the person?
> **the argument** — play devil's advocate · take a position · common ground · concede the point
> **the person** — defer
> **the room** — dominate · silence · the floor · talk over
**The two that move:** *silence* belongs to the room and to the person — a property of the discussion and a thing somebody is doing. *defer* is a move in the argument and a habit of the person, and the whole unit turns on telling those two apart.

**2A** · Fifty minutes, one voice 🔊 Track 3.1
**1** c) usually right · **2** a) not at all · **3** a) speaks once · **4** a) neither
**Table** — Marcus: 17 min 10 s; interrupted 0 times; interrupted others 6 times. Thanh: 11 seconds; interrupted once; interrupted nobody. The rest: 9 min 14 s between eight people, of whom three did not speak at all.

**Noticing** — Sentence 2. *Despite the quality of what he says* concedes the strongest thing the other side has, in the other side's own terms, and concedes it before saying anything else. Sentence 1 concedes the same fact more cheaply; 3 concedes and then withdraws; 4 concedes nothing — *whereas* sets two things side by side without granting either.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 Although · 2 Despite · 3 However / Nevertheless · 4 whereas · 5 In spite · 6 yet / but · 7 Admittedly / That said

**3E** · Use — guided
1 Although he is right, nobody else has spoken.  
2 Despite the quality of his argument, the room has gone quiet. *(word class: a noun phrase, not a clause)*  
3 Marcus speaks for nineteen minutes, whereas Thanh speaks for forty seconds.  
4 She knows it is a problem, yet she has not intervened.  
5 In spite of preparing properly, he takes the whole hour. *(word class: -ing, not a clause)*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 Although / Though · 2 Despite / In spite of · 3 However / Nevertheless / Nonetheless · 4 whereas · 5 In spite · 6 *Although he is right, he is loud* — one concession marker per sentence, so delete *but* · 7 yet · 8 though

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Sixty-one units; fifteen per cent of the mark.  
2 Nothing during the class. They reconstruct a figure in week twelve.  
3 The top speaker took a median of 17 minutes; the bottom four took a median of 40 seconds between them.  
4 It concedes that airtime is a crude measure and that nobody has proposed grading it directly. It says anyway that it is the only measure available of something carrying fifteen per cent of a mark.  
5 That the quiet students could speak and choose not to. It is answered by the fact that the gap widened between weeks three and nine — which is not what a free choice usually does.  
6 The gap between the top speaker and the rest widens. It is significant because a widening gap suggests the format is producing the silence rather than merely permitting it.  
7 Because intervention is a blunt instrument, and the tutors who intervene most do not have the best participation spread.  
8 She would mark the question asked rather than the number of times somebody speaks — and she immediately admits she knows exactly who would lose by that too.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**not defensible** — it could not be justified if anybody asked · **a crude measure** — it measures the right thing badly · **a blunt instrument** — it works by force and damages what it touches · **the participation spread** — how widely contribution is distributed across a group · **a capacity rather than a behaviour** — what a student is able to do, not what they happen to do

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Marcus 17 min 10 s; Aoife 5 min 40 s; Dilan 3 min 05 s; Thanh 11 seconds. (Four others: 29 seconds in total; three did not speak.)  
2 He interrupts six times and is interrupted none. In the transcript he interrupts Thanh at minute 31 — "It is the same sample. Sorry, it is, I checked."  
3 Thanh: "Can I just — the second table is a different sample, so it —". Marcus talks over him, apologises while keeping the floor, and Thanh's next turn is marked [inaudible].  
4 Aoife: "I take your point about the table. I still think he is doing something different with it in chapter four." She concedes and keeps the objection, and Marcus concedes back.  
5 Thanh's row in the tutor notes. The notes were written in week twelve, three weeks after anybody could remember — and he is the one student there was nothing to remember.

**6A** · Sort — how much does each one concede?
> **a little** — even so · all the same · that said · on the other hand
> **none at all** — and yet · nevertheless · still · nonetheless
> **a lot** — admittedly · granted · up to a point · to be fair
The first group names the other side's point and agrees with part of it. The second acknowledges it and moves on. The third marks a contrast without conceding anything — which is why a paragraph built only from the third group reads as if it has not listened.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 To · 2 point · 3 said · 4 so · 5 same · 6 other

**6C** · Two that are almost the same
Model answers. **Nevertheless:** *Airtime is a crude measure. Nevertheless, it is the only measure the report has.* **Nonetheless:** *The report declines to recommend intervention; it nonetheless recommends that the criterion be rewritten.* Either word works in either sentence — the difference is register, not meaning.

**6D** · Two verbs that concede
Model answers. *I allow that airtime is a crude measure.* · *I grant that nobody has proposed grading it directly.* · *I allow that the quiet students could speak and choose not to.* Both verbs take a that-clause and both concede a point the writer is about to limit.

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Wrong.** *However* starts a new sentence or follows a semicolon: *He is right. However, nobody agrees.* A comma leaves two main clauses spliced.
> **2** **Right.** *In spite of* takes a noun or -ing.
> **3** **Right.** *Whereas* joins two parallel clauses and concedes nothing.
> **4** **Wrong.** *Nevertheless* takes no object: *In spite of the evidence, she did nothing* or *The evidence was clear. Nevertheless, she did nothing.*
> **5** **Wrong.** One concession marker per sentence: *Although he is right, nobody agrees.*
> **6** **Wrong.** *Though* goes at the front of its own clause or at the end of the sentence, never alone before a comma: *Though he is right, nobody agrees* or *He is right, though.*
> **7** **Right.** End-position *though*, informal and very common.
> **8** **Wrong.** *Despite* takes a noun or -ing: *Despite being right* or *Despite the quality of his argument*.

**7C** · Make it mean something
The first is final: a falling tone on *though* closes the sentence and the concession is the last word. The second rises and holds, so *though* becomes a hinge — the speaker has more to say and the listener waits for it.

**12A** · Recycle — twelve items
1 Although / Though · 2 Despite · 3 However · 4 whereas · 5 In spite · 6 yet · 7 though · 8 take · 9 said · 10 came · 11 back · 12 ground

## B1.3 · Unit 4 · The Garden That Has Two Jobs

**0B** · Guess it
The stack of crates by the gate. They were bought for moving seedling trays between the beds and the greenhouse, and they are being used to carry harvested food out to the kitchen — you can tell because they are stacked at the gate rather than at the greenhouse door, and they are facing out.

**1B** · Sort — the garden, the document, or the decision?
> **the garden** — yield · plot · harvest
> **the document** — a design brief · curriculum
> **the decision** — in practice · the whole point · by design · at the expense of
**The two that move:** *curriculum* is a document and a decision — it is written down and it is the thing that fixes the bed size. *yield* is a fact about the garden and, once the brief declines to set a requirement on it, a decision as well.

**2A** · Two beds, two reasons 🔊 Track 4.1
**1** a) for teaching · **2** b) fewer crops · **3** c) compatible at a small scale · **4** a) scaled down
**Table** — Teaching garden: beds 1 m × 1 m; twelve crops; 94 kg a year. Food garden (merged): plots about 5 m × 1.2 m; four crops; about 480 kg on the same land.

**Noticing** — Sentence 3. *So low that the kitchen gets about four days of salad* is a result: it reports what followed. Sentences 1 and 2 give purposes, and sentence 4 gives a reason followed by a result, which is why it is the one learners most often misfile.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 so that · 2 in order to / so as to · 3 such · 4 that · 5 so · 6 that · 7 Owing / Due

**3E** · Use — guided
1 The beds are small so that each student can have one.  
2 We planted twelve crops in order to show twelve cycles.  
3 The yield is so low that the kitchen hardly notices it.  
4 It is such a low yield that the kitchen hardly notices it.  
5 Owing to the curriculum's requirement of twelve crops, the beds cannot be merged.  
*(The two needing a new subject are 2 — the infinitive takes* we *from the main clause — and 5, where the clause must become a noun phrase.)*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 so that · 2 in order to / so as to · 3 so … that · 4 such … that · 5 Owing / Due · 6 *The beds are small so that students can have one* **or** *We made the beds small in order to give each student one* · 7 so · 8 therefore / thus / hence / consequently

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Why the beds are a metre square — usually in the form of a suggestion that they be merged, about once a semester.  
2 So that one student can reach the centre of a bed from any edge without stepping on the soil. It is not about yield.  
3 It teaches the wrong lesson for the rest of the year: everything planted in it does slightly worse, for a reason the student cannot see.  
4 Because a student who has only grown things that work has not learned to read a plant.  
5 94 kilograms; the campus kitchen uses that in about four days.  
6 Two years ago, without a decision being made anywhere — in the way these things start. It is now on a menu board.  
7 Whether they could grow more of the things people actually eat. They could, and the garden would stop doing what it was built for within one season.  
8 At the scale of a teaching garden the two purposes are compatible because the food is incidental; at any scale the kitchen would find useful they are not. It does one of them well.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**compacts it** — presses the soil so air and water cannot move through it · **reliably** — every time, predictably — here a virtue rather than a fault · **incidental** — produced as a side effect, not aimed at · **never specified as** — never written into the brief as a requirement · **within one season** — inside a single growing year

**5E** · The counter-text
1 R1 one bed per student for 20 students; R2 a reachable centre from any edge; R3 twelve crops showing twelve cycles; R4 three crops that fail reliably; R5 no requirement on yield.  
2 R2. Meeting it fixes the bed at one metre square, which with R1 fixes the number at forty, which fixes the yield at about a fifth of what the soil can do — and nobody wrote that down, because nobody was asked to.  
3 2.35 kg per square metre, against 11–13 kg for a commercial bed in the same soil.  
4 Cauliflower, three beds, 0 kg. It is not a failure: R4 specifies three crops that fail reliably, so it is the brief working.  
5 "R6 — the kitchen takes what there is. Not a requirement. Nobody has agreed to this," added in a different pen, three years later, by D.O., the garden manager.

**6A** · Sort — purpose, reason, or result?
> **result** — consequently · such that · therefore · hence · thus
> **purpose** — so as to · so that · in order to
> **reason** — whereby · because · owing to · as · since
*So that* can give either a purpose or a result, and the verb decides: *so that students can learn* looks forward, *so that nobody noticed* looks back. *Whereby* introduces a mechanism rather than a cause, so accept it in a fourth column if a learner argues for one.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 view · 2 sake · 3 being · 4 as · 5 that · 6 order

**6C** · Three that mean *therefore*
Model answers. **Therefore:** *The beds are a metre square; the yield is therefore about a fifth of what the soil can do.* **Thus:** *No crop reaches a usable quantity, and the garden thus feeds nobody.* **Hence:** *Twelve cycles had to be shown in one year — hence the twelve beds.* Note that *hence* alone takes the noun phrase.

**6D** · The one that joins a rule to its reason
Model answers. *The brief set out a system whereby each student manages one bed for a semester.* · *R2 fixes an arrangement whereby no one ever has to stand on the soil.* *Whereby* names the mechanism; it does not say what the mechanism is for, which is why it cannot replace *so that* in the explainer.

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Right.** *Such* + article + adjective + noun.
> **2** **Right.** *Hence* takes a bare noun phrase.
> **3** **Wrong.** *Owing to* takes a noun phrase: *Owing to the low yield, the kitchen looked elsewhere.*
> **4** **Wrong.** *So* + adjective, *such* + noun: *The yield is so low that nobody notices.*
> **5** **Right.** *So that* + clause, with its own subject.
> **6** **Right.** A noun phrase follows *owing to*.
> **7** **Wrong.** *So as to* takes an infinitive: *so as to show twelve cycles*.
> **8** **Wrong.** *In order to* takes an infinitive and borrows the main subject, so it cannot introduce a new one: *so that students can reach the middle*, or *We made the beds small in order to let students reach the middle.*

**7C** · Make it mean something
The first stresses *so*, which marks the clause as a purpose: building it to fail was the plan. The second stresses *fail*, which puts the new information at the end of the purpose clause — the speaker is making a point about the failing itself, usually in answer to somebody who assumed success was the aim.

**12A** · Recycle — twelve items
1 so that · 2 in order to / so as to · 3 so … that · 4 such … that · 5 Owing / Due · 6 so · 7 therefore / thus · 8 whereby · 9 set · 10 ended · 11 scaled · 12 build

## B1.3 · Unit 5 · The Essay Nobody Heard

**0B** · Guess it
The mark sheet with one blank row and the essay with a comment in the margin. They are the same student, hours apart: the blank row is written before the essay is read, and the comment is written after. Nothing about Thanh changed between them.

**1B** · Sort — before, during, or after the reveal?
> **after** — the thing nobody saw · and that is when · it turns out
> **before** — foreshadow · withhold · a slow build · save it for
> **during** — focus · emphasis
**The two that move:** *withhold* is a decision taken before and sustained during — it is the only one of these a storyteller does continuously. *and that is when* marks the turn, so it sits on the boundary between during and after, which is exactly its job.

**2A** · The mark that is due on Friday 🔊 Track 5.1
**1** a) unenforceable · **2** c) hers · **3** b) has not asked · **4** c) Friday
**Table** — Thanh: spoke twice in eleven weeks; essay mark 84, the highest in the group; the rule gives him 4 out of 15. Group median: spoke in 7 of 11 weeks; essay 69; participation 11.

**Noticing** — 1 spotlights *the criterion* (an it-cleft, contrasted with *the student*); 2 spotlights the action *mark him on something he was never given access to* (a wh-cleft); 3 spotlights the smallness of what was done (*all* means *only that*); 4 spotlights *next year's* by first naming and then dismissing this one.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 It · 2 What · 3 The · 4 The · 5 All · 6 It was · 7 The

**3E** · Use — guided
1 It was the criterion that failed, not the student.  
2 All she did was read eleven essays.  
3 What she wants is the rule changed, not the mark.  
4 The reason why he said nothing is that the moment had always gone.  
5 The thing that worries her most is the audit.

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 It · 2 What · 3 All · 4 reason · 5 thing · 6 *It was the criterion that failed* — the slot after *it was* takes a noun phrase, not a verb · 7 not until · 8 one

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 In the same chair, third from the window, for eleven weeks.  
2 The gap between a question and an answer in that room. He timed it once with the stopwatch on his phone, under the table.  
3 Because that is how long it takes him to produce an answer. He measured it by writing an answer down and reading it aloud at the speed people talked, in a stairwell, twice.  
4 He puts the sheet in his bag at the end of each session and goes to the library, where the gap between a question and an answer is as long as he wants it to be.  
5 The tutor asks a question nobody answers. The silence runs six seconds, seven, nine. At eleven he opens his mouth; at eleven and a half Marguerite speaks.  
6 Something almost but not quite what he had been going to say. The *not quite* is the point: the room has not lost a repetition, it has lost a better version, and nobody in it will ever know.  
7 "This is the best piece of writing I have had from this group in four years. Why have I never heard any of this?"  
8 He squares the edges of the eleven sheets and puts them on her desk on his way out without saying anything — which, he realises on the stairs, is exactly the problem she has just asked him about.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**under the table** — out of sight, so nobody knew he was timing them · **at the speed people talked** — at conversational pace, not reading pace · **squared the edges** — tapped the stack straight, a gesture of care · **on his way out** — without stopping, so that no exchange could follow · **which was, he realised** — a sentential relative carrying the whole preceding action

**5E** · The counter-text
1 "Participation (15%). Students are assessed on the extent to which they contribute regularly to seminar discussion." Last amended 2011.  
2 The criterion gives him 4 out of 15. The essay gives him 84, the highest mark in the group.  
3 Seven across the faculty this year, out of 2,140 marks — five on medical grounds and two for "format".  
4 It is held on file for three years. None has ever been queried at audit.  
5 Thanh's row: 4 crossed out, 13 written beside it. Somebody applied the rule, looked at the essay, and changed their mind — and the sheet is the only record that the two numbers were ever both in play.

**6A** · Sort — what does each one spotlight?
> **a degree** — specifically · particularly · merely · precisely · exactly
> **a person or thing** — the one · the thing · it · the reason
> **an action** — what · all
*The reason* spotlights a cause, which is a thing rather than an action — accept a fourth column for it if a learner argues the case. *All* is the odd one: it spotlights an action and simultaneously limits it, which is why it always carries a defence.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 who · 2 mean · 3 did · 4 thing · 5 why · 6 one

**6C** · Four adverbs that narrow
*Merely* narrows to a degree — it says *no more than that*. The other three narrow to a point. Model sentences: *The reveal is precisely the number the reader already had.* · *Vinh needed exactly eleven seconds.* · *He timed one thing specifically: the gap.* · *The last paragraph is particularly short.* Swap *merely* for any of the others and the sentence stops limiting and starts pointing.

**6D** · Two verbs that point at something
Model answers. *The tutor's comment points up the gap between what Vinh wrote and what the room heard.* · *On the stairs he zeroed in on the one thing the comment had actually asked.* **Point up** makes something visible; **zero in on** means everything else has stopped mattering.

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Wrong.** A wh-cleft takes a bare infinitive after *was*: *What I did was read them in order.*
> **2** **Right.** Bare infinitive after *was*, and the *all* carries the defence.
> **3** **Wrong.** The slot after *it was* takes a noun phrase: *It was the criterion that failed.*
> **4** **Right.** *The reason why* + clause + *is*.
> **5** **Wrong.** The cleft needs its second half: *It was not until then **that** she saw it.*
> **6** **Right.** A standard it-cleft.
> **7** **Right.** Bare infinitive, correctly.
> **8** **Wrong.** Bare infinitive, not a past form: *All I did was ask a question.*

**7C** · Make it mean something
The first stresses *ask*: the speaker is minimising the action and inviting you to look at the consequence — *and look what happened*. The second stresses *all*: the speaker is minimising the extent and defending themselves — *and nothing else*. Same words, and only the first one is pointing away from itself.

1 It · 2 What · 3 All · 4 reason · 5 thing · 6 until · 7 one · 8 zeroed · 9 points · 10 give · 11 held · 12 land

**6A** · Sort — which preposition?
> **on** — insist · congratulate somebody
> **against** — advise somebody · warn somebody
> **of** — accuse somebody · remind somebody
> **for** — blame somebody

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 of · 2 to · 3 for · 4 on · 5 against · 6 on

**6C** · Two that change meaning with the pattern
**He reminded her to read it.** — she was going to forget. **He reminded her that she had read it.** — she had forgotten that she already knew. **She warned them against describing it as standard.** — an instruction not to. **She warned them that it was not standard.** — information, with no instruction in it.

**6D** · The two that persuade
Model answers. *They talked the Dean into taking it to the committee.* · *She talked them out of calling the clause standard.* Both take -ing, both imply the other person did not want to, and both are warmer and less formal than *persuade* and *dissuade*.

**6E** · Deny, admit, and the one that has no object
Model answers. *He denied writing the paragraph.* · *He admitted approving it.* · *He insisted on seeing the final text before it went out.* Note that *insist* takes no object: not *he insisted them*.

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Right.** *Accuse* + object + *of* + -ing.
> **2** **Right.** *Warn* + object + *against* + -ing.
> **3** **Wrong.** *Congratulate* takes *on*: *She congratulated them on the gift.*
> **4** **Wrong.** *Insist* takes *on* + -ing or a that-clause: *He insisted on seeing the draft.*
> **5** **Wrong.** *Deny* takes a bare -ing or a that-clause: *He denied writing it.*
> **6** **Right.** *Remind* + object + *to* + infinitive.
> **7** **Wrong.** *To* + infinitive after a reporting verb means somebody wanted the action: *They accused them of writing a veto.*
> **8** **Right.** *Insist on* + -ing.

**7C** · Make it mean something
The first stresses *admitted*: the admission itself is the news, and the implication is that it was not expected. The second stresses *writing*: the admission is old news and the contrast is with the other things he might have done — approving it, circulating it, signing it off.

## B1.3 · Unit 7 · Somebody Told Somebody

**0B** · Guess it
The form headed *Concern — Stage 1*. It is the only one of the twelve that the procedure put there. The other eleven — the photocopier conversation, the group chat, the blind down, the greyed-out timetable slot — are what happens in the absence of the procedure, and the form is blank.

**6A** · Sort — how far away does each one hold it?
> **neutral** — reportedly · apparently · allegedly
> **openly sceptical** — ostensibly · supposedly · purportedly
> **doubtful** — seemingly · presumably · arguably

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 thought · 2 said · 3 believed · 4 understood · 5 alleged · 6 According

**6C** · Three that are not neutral at all
Model answers. **Supposedly:** *The play is supposedly built entirely from documents.* **Ostensibly:** *He was moved to a non-teaching role, ostensibly for a period of review.* **Purportedly:** *The second act purportedly speaks for the town.* Each one takes a position, which is exactly what *reportedly* and *allegedly* do not.

**6D** · Two verbs for what a claim does
Model answers. *A story was put about that he had been asked to leave his last post.* · *None of the three versions stood up.* **Put about** names a deliberate act with no named actor; **stand up** is what a claim does under checking, and it is almost always used in the negative.

**6E** · The fixed expressions
Model answer. *It is said that he was asked to leave his last post. He is thought to have been interviewed by two people rather than four. According to sources, a complaint was made in 2019. None of which has been established.*

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Right.** Subject + passive + *to* + infinitive.
> **2** **Wrong.** The *it* shape takes a that-clause: *It is thought that he is fifty* — or use the other shape, *He is thought to be fifty.*
> **3** **Wrong.** Half of one shape and half of the other: *It is said that he left* or *He is said to have left.*
> **4** **Wrong.** *None* takes a singular verb in careful written English: *none of which **has** been established.*
> **5** **Right.** Dummy *it* + passive + that-clause.
> **6** **Right.** The past is carried by *to have*, and the reporting stays in the present.
> **7** **Right.** A passive inside a passive report, which is where the weak forms pile up.
> **8** **Right.** No inversion, no that-clause — *according to* is a preposition phrase.

**7C** · Make it mean something
*He is said to **be** fifty* reports a claim about now: he is fifty today. *He is said to have **been** fifty* moves the claim backwards — he was fifty at some past point, and is older now. In both the *is said* stays present, because the saying is happening now.

**6A** · Sort — how certain is each one?
> **certain** — clearly · demonstrably
> **probable** — should have · foreseeably · ought to have
> **possible** — conceivably · could have · might well have
*Clearly* claims certainty and often supplies none, which is why 6C asks which of the two should survive an edit. *Should have* and *ought to have* are certain about the duty and silent about the outcome — they belong in the middle column for that reason.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 should · 2 could · 3 ought · 4 needn't · 5 well · 6 which

**6C** · Two that sound like evidence
*Demonstrably* should survive: it says somebody has shown it, and in a post-mortem that is a claim the reader can check. *Clearly* claims only that the writer found it obvious. Model sentences: *The handover template demonstrably contains no line for open cases.* · *Clearly, somebody should have noticed* — which is the sentence the column is arguing against.

**6D** · Two that carry a consequence
Model answers. *The threshold should have been two. Otherwise nothing fires until a third of the semester has gone.* · *A handover in week six is hard. With two hundred and ten scripts to mark, all the more so.*

**6E** · The fixed expressions
**Naming a person:** *The second coordinator should have asked a follow-up question, and nobody did.* — this does not work; *nobody* contradicts the named subject. **Not naming one:** *Somebody should have put the three warnings together, and nobody did.* — this works, and it is a finding rather than an accusation, because the missing subject is the fault being described.

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Right.** The second clause is a plain negative.
> **2** **Right.** *Needn't have* means it was done and was unnecessary, and *but she did* agrees with that.
> **3** **Wrong.** *Needn't have come in* says she came. Use *She didn't need to come in, so she stayed at home.*
> **4** **Wrong** — not grammatically but logically. *Could have* states a possibility; the second clause removes the ground for blame, so the conclusion does not follow.
> **5** **Right.** Possible, and nobody has established it.
> **6** **Right.** *Ought* keeps its *to*.
> **7** **Wrong.** *Ought* is the one modal that keeps *to*: *ought to have included*.
> **8** **Wrong.** A modal takes *have* + past participle for the hypothetical past: *He should have asked.*

**7C** · Make it mean something
*She needn't have come in* says she came in and the journey was wasted. *She didn't need to come in* says there was no obligation and, by implication, that she stayed at home. Two different Thursdays, and in a post-mortem the difference is whether somebody's time was spent.

## B1.3 · Unit 9 · Perfectly Clear

**0B** · Guess it
The sign saying *Please do not leave glassware in the sink* and the handwritten note saying *don't put glass in here*. The handwritten one works better where it is — taped to the sink, read in two seconds by somebody holding a beaker. The printed one is on a wall and is addressed to an inspector.

**1B** · Sort — the writing, the marking, or the speaker?

> **the writing** — the house style · plain English · in your own words
> **the marking** — penalty · marking · mark down
**The two that move:** *vocabulary* is a property of the speaker and a line on a marking sheet, and the unit is about what happens when the second is used to measure the first. *in your own words* is an instruction about writing and a judgement about the speaker — it assumes there is a set of words that are yours.

2 Nowhere. Only *structure and length* (3 marks) touches the writing at all, and it is about shape, not idiom — and the deduction was four, which is more than that criterion carries.  
3 Brackets 1, 2 and 4 mark unidiomatic but correct English (*permits us to affirm*; *augments*; *it exists a relation*). Bracket 3 marks *in function of temperature*.  
4 "Prefer short Anglo-Saxon verbs where they exist: use rather than utilise, allow rather than permit, increase rather than augment. This is a preference, not a rule."  
5 Bracket 3: *in function of temperature* should be *as a function of temperature*. It is the one real error and it is not the one that was noticed.

**6A** · Sort — what does each particle add?
> **away from** — down · off · away
> **towards** — back · over · into
> **continuing** — on · around
> **completely** — up · through · out
Every one of these has other senses — *up* is also upward and *out* also outward — so the column is about the metaphorical sense that produces phrasal verbs: *drink up*, *see through*, *run out*. Expect arguments, and accept any placement a learner can illustrate.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 up with · 2 away with · 3 forward to · 4 out of · 5 along with · 6 up on

**6C** · Two that do the same job in different registers
Model answers. *The faculty should do away with the criterion until it is written down.* · *The faculty should abolish the criterion until it is written down.* Identical in meaning; the one-word version is usually right in a committee paper, because it is shorter in a document people skim — not because it is more correct.

**6D** · Two that are separable, and one trap
**Only *She took it over* and *He brought it up*.** A pronoun object must come between the verb and the particle in a separable phrasal verb. That is the only rule here that is absolute: with a noun object all three positions work, so there is nothing to remember until a pronoun arrives.

**6E** · The fixed expression
Model exchange. — *It reads as though it was translated. I could not point at a sentence and tell you it was wrong.* — **Say that again in writing.** — *…* The move works because an observation that cannot survive being written down is not a criterion, and the chair is making exactly that point without saying so.

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Wrong.** A three-part verb never splits: *He could not put up with it.*
> **2** **Right.** Separable, with a pronoun object — so it must split.
> **3** **Right.** Object after both particles.
> **4** **Wrong.** *Get across* is separable and takes its object before or after the particle with a noun: *She got the idea across* / *She got across the idea*. With a pronoun it must split: *She got it across.*
> **5** **Right.** A noun object may follow the particle.
> **6** **Wrong.** A pronoun object must split a separable verb: *She marked her down.*
> **7** **Wrong.** *Look forward to* is three-part and never splits: *We are looking forward to it.*
> **8** **Right.** Object after all three parts.

**7C** · Make it mean something
*She **TOOK** over* stresses the verb, which makes it a handover: somebody else had it and now she does. *She took **OVER*** stresses the particle, which makes it total: she took the whole thing, and nobody else has a say. In a phrasal verb the particle normally carries the stress, so the first version is the marked one.

## B1.3 · Unit 10 · Never Have I Been So

**0B** · Guess it
The staff photograph from 2016 with one person at the edge, and the lanyard being handed over. Nine years apart, same person. What has changed is that in the first she is at the edge because nobody arranged the photograph around the night shift, and in the second she is at the centre of a room arranged entirely around her — and neither picture was her idea.

**1B** · Sort — before, during, or after?
> **after** — acknowledgement · tribute
> **during** — on this occasion · take the floor · in all my years
> **before** — promotion · occasion · a step up · a few words
**The two that move:** *tribute* is the thing being given during the address and the thing the room remembers afterwards. *a step up* is said before, during and after, by different people meaning different things — eleven times in a fortnight, as the interview has it.

**2A** · A step up 🔊 Track 10.1

**Noticing** — Sentence 4. *At no point was there a conversation* becomes, in ordinary order, *There was no conversation at any point* — which is a plain sentence. **6A** · Sort — which take *do / did*?
> **uses an existing auxiliary** — scarcely · no sooner · never · hardly · rarely · seldom
> **uses be** — nor · neither
> **needs do / did** — little · only · nowhere
The real rule is one rule: whatever a question would do, the inversion does. If the sentence already has an auxiliary or *be*, that word moves; if it has neither, English supplies *do*. So every item in the first column can land in the second whenever the sentence it fronts happens to carry an auxiliary — which is why the column to argue over is the third.

**6C** · Two that are not negative at all
Model answers. **Only:** *Only after eleven days did she get the two answers in writing.* **Not until:** *Not until the email went out did anybody ask her.* Neither word is negative; both restrict, and restriction is what triggers the inversion.

**6D** · Three phrasal verbs for a career
Model answers. *When she set out, nine years ago, the night rota had eleven people on it.* · *Looking back, the lockouts were the job and the patrols were the job description.* · *She rose to it, and the only record that she did is a number nobody collected until it became a cost.*

**6E** · The fixed expressions
Model paragraph. *Not once has anybody asked what happens in the building between eleven and six. Not for one moment do I think that was deliberate. It is simply that the people who set the times are never the people who do the waiting.*

**6F** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **Wrong.** *Only* + a fronted element inverts: *Only then did she answer.*
> **2** **Right.** *Nor* inverts and joins a previous negative.
> **3** **Wrong.** With no auxiliary present, English supplies *did*: *Not until Friday did anybody ask.*
> **4** **Wrong.** *Sooner* is a comparative, so it takes *than*: *No sooner had it been signed than my name was on it.* (*Hardly* and *scarcely* take *when*.)
> **5** **Wrong.** The auxiliary moves in front of the subject: *Never have I been so sure.*
> **6** **Right.** *Did* supplied, subject after it.
> **7** **Right.** Auxiliary before subject.
> **8** **Right.** The comparative takes *than*.

**7C** · Make it mean something
*I did not ask for **THIS*** contrasts the thing with some other thing: she asked for something and was given this instead. *I did not **ASK** for this* contrasts the asking with its absence: she had no part in it at all, which is the complaint the whole unit is built on.
