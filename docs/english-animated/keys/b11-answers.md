## B1.1 · Unit 1 · Ground Floor

**0B** · Guess it
**Open.** Better reasons use evidence in the drawing: the planning notice on the hoarding (which names demolition of the single-storey structures), the two-metre frontage with no lease visible, the scaffold already up next door. Worse reasons are predictions about the kind of street it is, with nothing in the picture behind them. Mark the reason, not the choice.

**1B** · Sort — how much is destroyed?
**Least to most destroyed, as most groups order them:** do up · repaint · refit · convert · extend · redevelop · demolish.  
*Do up* and *repaint* are regularly swapped, and the swap is the useful disagreement: *repaint* changes less of the fabric, *do up* changes more but is cheaper and shorter. *Extend* destroys almost nothing and takes longest. The question to put back to the class is **what are you measuring** — fabric, cost, or how long people are out.

**2A** · Three people on the same street 🔊 Track 1.1
**1** A · **2** C · **3** C · **4** A · **5** B · **6** B
**Noticing** — the six sentences are keyed above, by letter.

**Gist** — Tom. He is the only one with somewhere to go and the only one who talks about the street as a view rather than a living.

**Detail** — Grace: was workshops and people she knew; is scaffolding and a lift that breaks; will be flats she cannot afford to clean in. Mr Kamau: was quiet and poor; is busy and expensive; will be finished, and he says *it's progress*. Tom: was nothing to look at; is noisy; will be worth more than he paid.

**Inference** — "I've been here twenty-two years and nobody has written my name down" and "they put the notice up in English." Neither sentence contains a worry; both report a position she has no standing in.

**Attitude** — the vowel in *progress*: lengthened and falling, which carries resignation. The word itself is approving; the sound is not.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 opened · 2 was · 3 were repairing · 4 rented · 5 died · 6 went · 7 had … raised · 8 worked *(or* was working*)* · 9 blocked *(or* was blocking*)* · 10 did not complain · 11 bought

**3E** · Use — guided
**Order:** Mwangi rented the frontage from Otieno → Otieno died → the first tower was finished → Mwangi was working by lamp for eleven days → the second tower took the open ground → the drivers moved three streets away.  
**Model connecting text:** *Mwangi had rented the frontage from Otieno for fifteen years before Otieno died in 2009. By the time the first tower was finished in 2014, the rent had already risen twice. For eleven days afterwards he was working by lamp, because the new building blocked the sun until ten. The second tower then took the open ground, and the drivers moved three streets away.*

**5C** · Close reading
1 Open ground, where matatu drivers parked and argued.  
2 Because the first tower blocked the sun until after ten in the morning for eleven days after it was finished.  
3 Before, he repaired work clothes; now he takes in suits. And his customers are young people who have just started earning and bought the wrong size online — a different trade, not a smaller one.  
4 That he raised the rent twice and then forgot about it. The detail does two things: it explains why Mwangi is still there at all, and it makes the displacement of the other thirteen businesses a matter of attention rather than malice.  
5 Their rent went up, they moved somewhere cheaper, and then that place got expensive too. The sentence refuses the word *failure* and describes a process instead.  
6 That the new buildings brought him the customers he has now — and that the lane had been dangerous after dark for years before anyone built anything.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**frontage** — the width of street a stall or shop occupies · **stretch** — a length of one street, treated as a unit · **sentimental** — attached to the past in a way that gets in the way of judgement · **trading** — still open and doing business · **take in** — alter a garment to make it narrower · **the lane had been dangerous** — past perfect: dangerous before the period the sentence is about, and the point is that it was dangerous *first*

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Demolition of the existing single-storey structures and erection of a nine-storey building: commercial floorspace at ground and first floor, residential above, with access, servicing and landscaping.  
2 25 February 2026 — twenty-one days from publication on 4 February.  
3 Development Control, PO Box 30075, in writing.  
4 One: the applicant, Westrand Holdings (Nominee) Ltd — and *Nominee* means that is not a person either. No individual is named anywhere on the notice.  
5 Nothing, through this route: representations received after the closing date will not be taken into account. They can inspect the application at the public counter, 09:00–15:00 Monday to Thursday — which is when a person with a job is at work.

**6A** · Register sort
> **both** — by the time · at first · in the meantime
> **speech** — back then · ever since · up to that point · from then on · before long · later on
> **the planning notice** — thereafter · subsequently · previously
The three in both columns are the ones with no register marking at all: *by the time*, *at first*, *in the meantime*. The notice column is Latinate and the speech column is Germanic, which is the whole pattern and is worth saying out loud.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 By · 2 Until · 3 ever · 4 before · 5 At / Later

**6C** · Semantic map
**EARLIER:** previously · up to that point · back then · at first · before long *(relative to a later point)*  
**AT THE SAME TIME:** in the meantime · ever since *(a span running up to now)*  
**LATER:** thereafter · subsequently · from then on · later on · before long  
**A BOUNDARY:** by the time · from then on · up to that point · at first  
*Three sit in two zones:* **from then on** *(later, and a boundary),* **up to that point** *(earlier, and a boundary),* **before long** *(earlier or later, depending on the anchor). Accept any placing the learner can justify with a sentence.*

**6D** · Contrast Clinic — past simple against past perfect
> **1** closed — a dated event. *(Pairs with 2.)*
> **2** had already closed — before the notice. **The difference:** in the first the period ran its course; in the second the notice went up after it was over.
> **3** had left — before that year. *(Pairs with 4.)*
> **4** left — during that year. **The difference:** four by 2016 is a running total; four in 2016 is one year's worth, and the two can describe completely different streets.
> **5** had expired — before she read it. *(Pairs with 6.)*
> **6** expired — the same day. **The difference:** in the first she was a day too late; in the second she still had hours.

**7C** · Make it mean something
The elided version — /aɪ dəʊnəʊ wɒt ðeɪ juːstə duː/ — sounds like a person. The fully articulated one sounds like a document read aloud. You would want the second when you are being quoted, when you are speaking to somebody who is straining to follow, or when the precise words matter more than the relationship — a dictation, a legal statement, a number read back.

## B1.1 · Unit 2 · What It Costs To Make

**0B** · Guess it
**Open — keep the number.** The cloth is 1,302 of a 4,800 jacket, which is about 27 per cent. Most classes guess much higher, usually between a half and two thirds. The point in Part 8 is that the guess is wrong in a predictable direction: people price the material they can see and not the hours they cannot.

**1B** · Sort — who carries the risk?
**Paid first to paid last:** the cloth merchant *(cash on collection)* · the trim supplier *(30 days)* · the machinists *(weekly)* · the workshop's landlord *(monthly)* · Mariam *(on invoice, 60 days)* · the buyer's own company *(on sale)*.  
**Who carries the risk:** Mariam. She has paid for cloth, trims and labour before anybody has bought a jacket, and her invoice is settled after the buyer has sold them. If nobody buys, the buyer records no loss and she has forty jackets and an empty account.

**2A** · Forty jackets 🔊 Track 2.1
**1** A · **2** B · **3** A · **4** B · **5** A · **6** B
**Noticing** — the six sentences are keyed above.

**Gist** — No. He leaves without placing it; what he places is a sample order.

**Detail** — Price: he says *we're working to a number*; she hears that the number is already fixed and is not hers. Quantity: he says *forty to start*; she hears that forty is the whole order. Timing: he says *fairly soon*; she hears a date she will be held to and he will not.

**Inference** — *We've been looking at a few workshops* is the continuous, which points at the activity rather than a result. If he had been to three workshops he would say *we've spoken to three* — a countable result. The continuous is doing the work of a number he does not have.

**Attitude** — the second *I can do that* has a falling-rising tone on *can* and a longer vowel. The first is an offer; the second is a concession with a cost in it.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 has been working · 2 has made · 3 has trained · 4 has not been sleeping · 5 has gone *(or* has been going*)* · 6 have been doing · 7 have never had · 8 have been hearing

**3E** · Use — guided
1 I've been writing emails to them. **Changes:** the three disappear and the effort appears — it now sounds like a complaint rather than a report.  
2 We've looked at four suppliers. **Changes:** a countable result replaces an activity, and the speaker becomes answerable for the number.  
3 She's cut the cloth for forty. **Changes:** the morning disappears and the work is finished.  
4 They've been paying in instalments. **Changes:** two becomes a pattern, and the sentence stops saying how much is left.

**5C** · Close reading
1 The price on the invoice, and nothing else.  
2 Four days of rework when the first batch comes back with the seams wrong; two weeks of delay while a replacement is cut; the senior fitter's time on a problem the cheaper workshop was never equipped to avoid.  
3 The buyer records no loss at all. The cost shows up as a sourcing problem eighteen months later, and nobody connects the two.  
4 A single figure with a name attached — the invoice — as against a figure that has to be built from four departments, three quarters and one person's weekend. The first always wins an argument, whatever it leaves out.  
5 That the workshops which survive are the ones that learned to put the assembled number on the page before anybody asked for it.  
6 Because the obvious reading is that buyers are being mean, and the piece is arguing that they are hitting the targets they were given. Naming that early stops the reader dismissing the argument as a complaint about people.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**procurement** — the function that buys, measured on price · **rework** — doing again what was done wrong, and the cost falls on the other side · **underpricing** — quoting below the real cost, usually without knowing it · **deferred** — put off, and still owed — here maintenance · **assembled** (the assembled number) — built from parts held by different people · **subsidising** — paying part of somebody else's cost out of your own margin

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Cloth 1,302 + lining 118 + trims 186 + labels 124 = **1,730**.  
2 Eleven hours — 1.5 cutting, 8.0 making, 1.5 finishing — at 150 an hour, the workshop rate rather than the machinist rate. **1,650** in all.  
3 Sampling: eleven hours across three rounds, costed at zero. It is on the sheet because the work was really done; it is at zero because it is carried by the workshop and charged to nobody. Putting it on the page at zero is the one honest move available.  
4 1,080 on 4,800 — **22.5 per cent**.  
5 The sampling line. Eleven hours at 150 would be 1,650, which is more than the margin on the whole batch of forty — and it is the assembled number the article says nobody puts on the page.

**6A** · Sort — which group?
> **this points at a result** — finalised · outstanding · incomplete · twice already · in one go · provisional · cumulative · so far
> **this points at an activity** — steadily · repeatedly · keep at · ongoing · all morning · gradually · for the last six months · intermittent · up to now · bit by bit · I've been meaning to… · get through · throughout
**The three that go in both:** *so far* (a running total, and a period still open), *up to now* (the same), and *cumulative* (a result built out of an activity). *get through* is arguable as a fourth — *I got through it* is a result, *I've been getting through it* an activity.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 bit / bit · 2 in / go · 3 outstanding · 4 final / finalised · 5 been meaning

**6C** · Collocation grid
**ongoing:** costs ✓ · work ✓ · progress ✓ — *ongoing payment* ✗  
**outstanding:** costs ✓ · payment ✓ · work ✓ — *outstanding progress* ✓ *(but it means "excellent", not "unfinished", which is the trap)*  
**cumulative:** costs ✓ · progress ✓ — *cumulative payment* ✓ · *cumulative work* ✗  
**provisional:** costs ✓ · payment ✓ · progress ✓ — *provisional work* ✗  
**The one nobody says:** *ongoing payment*. *Ongoing costs* and *outstanding payment* are both ordinary; the two words simply do not combine.

**6D** · Contrast Clinic — simple against continuous
> **1** has been cutting — *since seven* fixes a span, so the activity is the point.
> **2** has been chasing — *for a month* is a span, and the sentence is a complaint about effort.
> **3** has cut — forty is a countable result.
> **4** have been speaking — *all week* is a span, and no number is offered.
> **5** have spoken — three is a result the speaker can be held to.
> **6** has sent — *twice* counts completed actions.

**7C** · Make it mean something
The natural, elided version sounds like somebody defending their work: the reductions carry fluency, and fluency reads as confidence. The fully pronounced version sounds like a witness statement — every syllable separable, because somebody may need to write it down.
