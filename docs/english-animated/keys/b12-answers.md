## B1.2 · Unit 1 · What the Canteen Buys

**0B** · Guess it
Costing the hospital almost nothing: beans and rice, and tinned fruit — both bought in bulk, stored dry, and priced close to what they cost. Costing it a great deal: the fish of the day and the subsidised staff meal. **No** — the board does not tell you. The staff meal is priced at 45 per cent of a number that is itself below cost, and the only sentence on either menu that mentions what anything costs the hospital is the note at the foot of the staff copy.

**1B** · Sort — the food, the money, or the habit?
> **the habit** — generation · household
> **the money** — budget · portion
> **the food** — canteen · processed · staple · seasonal · recipe
**The one that moves:** *seasonal*. With money it is a description of the food — what is good this month. With less of it, it becomes a budget word: *seasonal* starts meaning *whatever is cheap*, and the menu stops naming the fruit.

**2A** · The canteen budget 🔊 Track 1.1
**1** b) 400 · **2** c) subsidised · **3** b) 11 · **4** a) dignity
**Table** — Rosa wants the cheaper menu; her reason is that the budget has fallen 19 per cent in real terms; what she is not saying is that the saving falls almost entirely on the staff meal. Dr Herrera wants the fish kept; his reason is nutrition; what he is not saying is that he eats at lunch and the night shift does not. Ignacio wants the night service kept as it is; his reason is that people on nights have nowhere else; what he is not saying is that he has eaten at three in the morning for nineteen years and is describing his own life.

**Noticing** — Sentences 1 and 2 are the stopped habits: *used to* for a past state and *would* for a repeated past action. Sentence 3 (*I'm used to*) describes a present state of familiarity; sentence 4 (*get used to*) describes the process of becoming familiar. Neither of the last two is about the past at all.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 used to cook · 2 would come · 3 would leave · 4 used to be · 5 was not used to · 6 got used to

**3E** · Use — guided
1 There used to be a bakery in the basement. *(only* used to *— a state)*  
2 Every Saturday he would bake bread. *(or* used to bake*)*  
3 I am used to the night shift now.  
4 I got used to it.  
5 The hospital used to have its own garden. *(only* used to *— a state)*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 used to be · 2 would · 3 used to · 4 get used to · 5 Did … use · 6 use · 7 working · 8 used to have

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Eleven times a month in 1990; three now.  
2 Households buying tinned fish at least weekly rose from eighteen per cent to sixty-one.  
3 Because fresh fish has risen about forty per cent in real terms and tinned thirty-four — a gap of six points, which cannot account for a fall of eight purchases a month.  
4 In 1990, forty-one per cent of households had somebody at home between two and four in the afternoon. That figure is now eleven per cent.  
5 That the cost of fresh fish is not money but timing: it has to be bought in the morning, cooked the same day, and eaten by people who are in the house.  
6 Over sixty: young people cannot cook. Under thirty: fish is expensive.  
7 A schedule — both are a way of describing a change in the shape of the day without mentioning it.  
8 Because the canteen buys for four hundred people whose shifts are the reason the region's afternoons are empty: whatever it serves, it is serving the thing that caused the change.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**in real terms** — adjusted for inflation, so the comparison is honest · **account for** — explain the whole of · **the shape of the day** — when people are at home, awake and free · **a way of describing** — a description that does not name what it describes · **whatever it serves** — the menu is beside the point; the institution is the cause

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Six in 1994; four now.  
2 In money it has gone from half price to a subsidy of 55 per cent of a much larger number. In real terms the subsidy is worth less: the catering budget funding it has fallen 19 per cent in real terms since 2019, and the subsidy is reviewed each March.  
3 Fish of the day, cazuela, and beans and rice. *(Tinned fruit also appears on both.)*  
4 The salad bar, priced per 100 g — a category that did not exist in 1994.  
5 "A subsidised staff meal costs the hospital 2,180. The subsidy is funded from the catering budget, which has fallen 19 % in real terms since 2019. The subsidy is reviewed each March."

**6A** · Sort — a habit, a change, or a speed?
> **a speed** — overnight · gradually · steadily · constantly
> **a habit** — routine · practice · typically · tradition · custom
> **a change** — increasingly
*Increasingly* is the only one that names a direction without naming a rate, which is why it survives in writing where *gradually* would have to be defended. *Constantly* is a speed in form and a habit in use — accept it in either column with a reason.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 time · 2 rule · 3 more · 4 up · 5 into · 6 days

**6C** · Three adverbs that are not interchangeable
1 **gradually** (or *steadily*) — twenty years with stops and starts. *Overnight* would be false. 2 **overnight** — in March, with no notice; and here it is literal rather than an exaggeration. *Gradually* would contradict "with no notice". 3 **steadily** — the afternoon figure fell from 41 to 11 per cent without reversing. *Overnight* would be absurd; *gradually* would allow for stops the data does not show.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** used to *(a past state or repeated job — and she does not now)*
> **2** used to *(a state; *would* is impossible with* be*)*
> **3** used to *(present familiarity, + -ing)*
> **4** would *(or* used to *— a repeated action)*
> **5** used to *(a state; not* would*)*
> **6** 'm used to *(present familiarity)*
> **7** Did … use *(question form — no* d *on* use*)*
> **8** get used to *(the process of becoming familiar)*

**12A** · Recycle — twelve items
1 used to be · 2 would · 3 used to · 4 get used to · 5 down · 6 staple · 7 up · 8 time · 9 have been waiting · 10 which · 11 used · 12 to replace

## B1.2 · Unit 2 · The Form Nobody Signs

**0B** · Guess it
The barcode scanner, which is mounted on the wall as a doorstop because it has been broken since March, and the drug cupboard, whose shelf labels have been written over by hand. Somebody has worked around a scanner that does not scan and a label that does not match the formulary — and both work-arounds held for eleven weeks.

**1B** · Sort — the event, the response, or the system?
> **the event** — incident · harm · error
> **the response** — report · disclosure · anonymous
> **the system** — blame · culture · prevent
**The one argued about in the wrong column:** *blame*. It is a property of the system — what the system does with a report — and it is always discussed as though it were a response one person chooses. That is why the signature field changed the filing rate and no policy did.

**2A** · Two milligrams 🔊 Track 2.1
**1** c) at the bedside · **2** a) porter · **3** b) not at all · **4** b) filed
**Table** — Carla: prepared the dose and did not catch it; would have checked the formulary against the label rather than the shelf; is afraid of being the person who gets named. Dr Benítez: signed the prescription; would have written the dose in words as well as figures; is afraid the investigation stops at Carla. Ignacio: read the wristband and stopped it; would have done nothing differently; is afraid of being asked why a porter was reading a wristband.

**Noticing** — Least to most formal: **2** (*If only I'd said something*) · **1** (*If I hadn't looked…*) · **3** (*But for the scanner being broken…*) · **4** (*Had it not been for Ignacio…*).

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 had not checked · 2 would have received · 3 had been · 4 been working · 5 would not have reached · 6 had acted · 7 would have been · 8 would not be

**3E** · Use — guided
1 If the scanner had not been broken, the error would not have reached the bedside.  
2 Had Ignacio not been passing, he would not have noticed. *(inverted)*  
3 If anybody had acted on the March report, the cupboard would have been relabelled.  
4 Had she not reported it, the cupboard would not have been changed. *(inverted)*  
5 If he had signed the form, it would have been investigated.

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 had not looked / would have had · 2 Had … been · 3 for · 4 had said · 5 had acted / would have been · 6 Had · would not have prepared · 7 had not signed / would have happened · 8 had studied / would be *(mixed — a past condition with a present result)*

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 In March, and she signed it because that is what you are told to do and because she had been in the job eight weeks and had not yet learned which instructions are real.  
2 It was relabelled in April, then relabelled back in May by somebody in pharmacy who had not been told why it had been changed and who correctly observed that the new labels did not match the formulary.  
3 She has the automatic reply: two lines long, timed 04.11.  
4 She was forty minutes past the end of a shift and thought *if I write this one, I will be the person who writes these*.  
5 That no policy needs to contain a sentence about being the person who writes these for the fear to work. The deterrent is social, and it is stronger for not being written down.  
6 A bag went up at ten times the dose and was caught at the bedside by a porter.  
7 Because thanking him would mean writing down what he was doing — reading a wristband, which is not his job.  
8 That the third form would have gone to the same inbox, arrived at four in the morning, and changed nothing. The reason to file it was never that it would have worked.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**which instructions are real** — which written rules are actually enforced · **the formulary** — the official list of drugs and doses · **the same inbox** — an address nobody is rostered to read · **past the end of a shift** — unpaid, and the point at which reporting costs something · **it would have changed nothing** — the honest admission that makes the argument stronger, not weaker

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Eight fields on the form (eleven lines in all); four are compulsory — date and time, location, what happened, what stopped it.  
2 An anonymous report is accepted and logged but cannot be followed up, because there is nobody to ask. A named report may be followed up, and the reporter is protected under the disclosure policy — in force since 2019 and never tested.  
3 55 filed; 17 anonymous.  
4 Signed: 23 of 38, 61 per cent. Anonymous: 2 of 17, 9 per cent. Overall 25 of 55, 45 per cent.  
5 Two were added this year: *Contributing factors* and *Has this happened before?* — both system questions, and both optional.

**6A** · Sort — how close, and how lucky?
> **how lucky** — fortunately · luckily · thankfully
> **how close** — nearly · almost · barely · narrowly
> **how it looks afterwards** — as it turned out · hindsight
*Barely* and *narrowly* both mean the margin was small; *barely* adds that it was nearly not enough. The second column is the one to watch in a report: all three of those words describe the writer's relief rather than the event.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 whisker · 2 minute · 3 out · 4 worse · 5 out · 6 out

**6C** · Three that look identical
1 **Luckily** — his being there was chance. *Fortunately* would claim it was a good outcome rather than an accident; *thankfully* would make it about the speaker.  
2 **Fortunately** — this is about the outcome. *Luckily* would imply the catch was chance, which insults the porter; *thankfully* is again about feelings.  
3 **Thankfully** — this sentence is openly about relief, which is the one place the word belongs. The other two would understate a family not being told.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** If anybody had acted in March, the cupboard would be right now. *(mixed — past condition, present result)*
> **2** If Ignacio had not been passing, he would not have caught it.
> **3** Had Ignacio not been there, nothing would have been caught. *(inverted)*
> **4** But for his reading the wristband, she would not be alive. *(or: Had he not read the wristband, she would not be alive.)*
> **5** If only she had filed the third form. *(regret only — no result clause)*
> **6** Had I known, I would not have prepared it. *(inverted)*
> **7** If she had not signed the first two, they would not have been investigated.
> **8** But for the broken scanner, the error would not have reached the bedside.

**7C** · Make it mean something
The first — a contrastive stress on *would* — belongs in speech, and only when somebody has just denied it; written down it reads as an argument with an absent person. The second is the neutral form and the one for a report: it states the consequence without implying anybody disputes it.

**12A** · Recycle — twelve items
1 had not looked / would have had · 2 Had … been · 3 for · 4 had said · 5 cause · 6 faith · 7 out · 8 worse · 9 used to be · 10 used to · 11 had already started · 12 must have broken

## B1.2 · Unit 3 · The Post That Ends on Friday

**0B** · Guess it
The locum, who is covering a consultant post he was not appointed to and is carrying an agency lanyard rather than a hospital one; and the administrator running the recruitment round, who has a finance badge and is holding the vacancy notice — the department has no HR post, so the person who costs the budget is also the person writing the advertisement.

**1B** · Sort — a thing you apply for, a thing you are given, or a thing you become?
> **a thing you apply for** — shortlist · post · vacancy
> **a thing you are given** — appointment · reference
> **a thing you become** — supervisor · trajectory · speciality · mentor
**The one that switches:** *vacancy*. For the person it is a thing to apply for; for the institution it is a thing it is given — an unfilled post arrives, it is not sought — and the whole cost argument in this unit turns on which way round the hospital is reading the word.

**2A** · Three weeks and a Friday 🔊 Track 3.1
**1** b) eleven months · **2** b) 2.0 · **3** a) three months · **4** b) the rota changed
**Table** — Dr Herrera wants Dr Benítez renewed; it costs twice the permanent salary; what she is not saying is that four rounds have produced four applicants and nobody. The finance office wants the locum stopped; it costs three months short-staffed; what it is not saying is that the surcharge on every booking is avoidable. Dr Benítez wants a permanent post with a different rota; it costs him the agency rate; what he is not saying is that nobody has asked him.

**Noticing** — Sentences 1 and 3. Each has a past condition (*had advertised*, *hadn't come*) with a present result (*would have somebody by now*, *would be three doctors short tonight*). The tell is the time adverbial in the result clause: *by now*, *tonight*.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 had been advertised · 2 would have · 3 had not come · 4 would be · 5 were · 6 would have said · 7 advertise · 8 will be

**3E** · Use — guided
1 If we had advertised in March, we would have somebody now. *(mixed — past condition, present result)*  
2 If he were permanent, he would have said something in March. *(mixed — present condition, past result)*  
3 If she had not trained in paediatrics, she would not be where she is now. *(mixed — past condition, present result)*  
4 If I were a confident speaker, I would have applied in 2019. *(mixed — present condition, past result)*  
5 If they had not cut the training budget in 2021, there would be five supervisors now. *(mixed — past condition, present result)*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 had advertised / would have · 2 were / would have said · 3 had not come / would be · 4 had not taken / would be working · 5 were / would have applied · 6 had not cut / would be · 7 advertise / will be · 8 had spoken / would have got

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Paediatrics, since she was nineteen — she had arranged her entire fifth year around it, including an elective she paid for herself.  
2 The one train to the Temuco interviews was cancelled. Standing on the platform at six in the morning she decided that arriving forty minutes late was worse than not arriving, and did not take the replacement bus.  
3 Because she thinks they would have seen her. She was twenty-four, had never been late to anything, and did not know that lateness is survivable.  
4 She took the emergency rotation that was still open, because it was the only one still open.  
5 That emergency medicine changes what a person is like — she has watched it happen to eleven others — so the person she now is was produced by the cancellation, not chosen.  
6 A candidate arrived thirty-five minutes late, apologised once, and was excellent; two of the three panellists wanted to mark her down for it.  
7 She told them about the train, and they appointed her. She has not told the candidate any of it.  
8 Open — but the strongest reading is that telling her would make the appointment a story about the writer rather than about the candidate, and would hand her a debt she did not incur. *(Accept: because the writer is not sure the story is a lesson rather than a regret.)*

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**an elective** — a placement a student arranges and often pays for · **the replacement bus** — the option that existed and was refused · **lateness is survivable** — being late does not end things, which a twenty-four-year-old did not know · **still open** — not chosen, merely left · **mark her down** — deduct for the lateness rather than judge the interview

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Consultant, emergency medicine; 4,160,000 a month.  
2 One month of locum cover is 8,341,600 — a ratio of 2.0 to the permanent post. Eleven months at that rate is 91.7 million.  
3 Eleven months; advertised four times, four applicants in all, nobody appointed.  
4 It says they are looking for "the right fit for a busy and supportive department". It does not state the rota, the number of unfilled posts, or that the department has been running short since January.  
5 The out-of-hours surcharge, 1,120,000 — applied where a booking is made with less than 72 hours' notice. All bookings in the period were.

**6A** · Sort — how formal, and how certain?
> **written** — consequently · therefore · as a result
> **very formal** — hence · accordingly · thereby
> **spoken** — so · which meant that
**The one usually got wrong:** *thereby*. It takes an *-ing* clause and attaches to the same subject — *The post was left unadvertised, thereby costing 91 million* — and learners use it as a sentence adverb meaning *therefore*, where it is simply wrong.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 result · 2 meant · 3 led · 4 off · 5 about · 6 on

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
1 **consequence** — it follows directly from the vacancy. 2 **knock-on** — nobody was looking at supervision when the budget was cut. 3 **outcome** — where the whole process ended, after four rounds.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** If they had not cut the budget, there would be five supervisors now. **Mixed A** — past condition, present result.
> **2** If he spoke Spanish, he would have got the job. **Mixed B** — present condition, past result.
> **3** If we had advertised, we would have somebody now. **Mixed A.**
> **4** If the train had run, her life would be different now. **Mixed A.**
> **5** If she had taken the bus, she would be a paediatrician now. **Mixed A.**
> **6** If I were permanent, I would have said something in March. **Mixed B.**
> **7** If I were permanent, I would say something. **Second conditional** — both halves present.
> **8** If we had advertised, we would have had somebody in June. **Third conditional** — both halves past.

**7C** · Make it mean something
What follows. *We'd have* is followed by a bare infinitive (*have somebody*), so the next word is an infinitive; *we'd advertised* is followed by a past participle. The contraction is identical and the verb after it carries the whole tense.

**12A** · Recycle — twelve items
1 had advertised / would have · 2 were / would have said · 3 for · 4 up · 5 paper · 6 result · 7 led · 8 off · 9 had not looked / would have had · 10 used to be · 11 will be living · 12 who / that

## B1.2 · Unit 4 · One Bed, Two Scores

**0B** · Guess it
The two sets of used gloves, the third pair still sealed, and the unused dressing pack. Two sets of gloves say two people were working; the sealed third pair says there was no third; the opened-but-unused pack says the work stopped before it began. Together they rule out a resuscitation — three or more people and an opened crash trolley — and they rule out a completed procedure.

**1B** · Sort — a thing, a relation, or a conclusion?
> **a relation** — sequence · inconsistent · consistent · angle
> **a thing** — residue · evidence · trace · timeline
> **a conclusion** — reconstruct
**The one that needs two of something else:** *inconsistent*. Nothing is inconsistent on its own — it takes two observations to make the relation, which is why a single trace is never evidence against anything. *(Accept* consistent *for the same reason.)*

**2A** · Reading the room 🔊 Track 4.1
**1** c) at least two · **2** b) started and stopped · **3** b) forty minutes · **4** a) door wedge
**Table** — Two sets of gloves: at least two people were working; rules out one person alone. The unused dressing pack: somebody started something and stopped; rules out a completed procedure, and rules out nothing having been begun. The trolley angle: it was moved from the wall and not put back; rules out an unhurried departure.

**Noticing** — Most to least certain: **2** *can't have been* (excludes absolutely) · **1** *must have been* (near certain) · **4** *unlikely to have been* (improbable, not impossible) · **3** *might well have been* (possible, strengthened).

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 must have been · 2 can't have been · 3 must mean · 4 might well have been · 5 is unlikely · 6 was

**3E** · Use — guided
1 There must have been two people.  
2 He might well have been called away.  
3 It is unlikely to have been an arrest.  
4 The coffee can't have been made after the monitor was switched on.  
5 Somebody might have come back for the phone.

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 must have been · 2 can't have been · 3 might well have been · 4 is / have · 5 must have been · 6 can't have been · 7 must be *(present — her coat is here now)* · 8 probability

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Eleven variables; a number between 0 and 40.  
2 Above 28, intensive care; below 12, ward. Between the two, clinical judgement.  
3 That by handing the middle band back to a person, the system is conceding it sorts patients rather than deciding about them — it is a tool, not an oracle.  
4 About four points in either direction. So two patients both scoring 31 are indistinguishable to the instrument.  
5 Because 29 and 33 differ by four, which is inside the interval — so the instrument cannot separate them either.  
6 That the decision be made on clinical grounds and documented.  
7 That in practice the tie is broken by who arrived first, and that nobody writes that down.  
8 Not from the instrument but from the page of the manual that should exist and does not — the page saying what to do when the instrument has told you nothing.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**well validated** — tested against outcomes and shown to work · **a higher bar than it sounds** — beating most people most of the time is genuinely hard · **an oracle** — something that answers rather than sorts · **a confidence interval** — the range within which the true value probably sits · **independently** — without having discussed it with each other, which is what makes three of four significant

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Both 31. They differ on four of the eleven variables.  
2 Respiratory rate (B worse, 5 against 3); oxygen saturation (B worse, 5 against 4); systolic BP (A worse, 5 against 3); conscious level (A worse, 3 against 2). Two in each direction.  
3 "The score carries a confidence interval of +/- 4 points. Two patients whose scores differ by less than this should be regarded as indistinguishable by this instrument, and the decision made on clinical grounds and documented."  
4 Patient B: "Resp rate climbing. Score does not reflect this." Patient A: nothing.  
5 Patient B's sheet carries a time of assessment; Patient A's box was left blank. Arrival times are 03.54 (A) and 04.05 (B) — eleven minutes apart, which is the unwritten tiebreak.

**6A** · Sort — raising, lowering, or reporting?
> **reporting** — apparently · presumably · seemingly
> **lowering** — plausibly · implausibly · conceivably · arguably
> **raising** — doubtless · certainly · undoubtedly · definitely
**The two misused:** *apparently* and *seemingly*. Both report — one reports what somebody else said, the other reports an appearance — and both are routinely used as if they raised certainty. *Doubtless* is the third trap: it raises in form and weakens in use.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 suggests · 2 evidence · 3 probability · 4 well · 5 stand · 6 add

**6C** · Three that weaken what they attach to
1 *The patient was stable at four.* **Gained:** a claim somebody can check. **Lost:** the fact that this is an appearance rather than a measurement.  
2 *Nobody was called.* **Gained:** directness. **Lost:** the attribution — *apparently* was reporting somebody else, and without it the writer owns the claim.  
3 *This is the most important variable.* **Gained:** a position. **Lost:** the admission that it is contestable — and *arguably* was conceding that the writer is the one arguing.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** The monitor must have been switched on before the gloves were used.
> **2** He might well have been called away.
> **3** It is unlikely to have been an arrest.
> **4** She might have made the coffee.
> **5** There can't have been anybody else here.
> **6** There must have been two people here.
> **7** The decision was arguably made on arrival order.
> **8** The door was apparently wedged by the porter.

**7C** · Make it mean something
The contrastive *MUST* belongs in the argument about the reconstruction — it answers somebody who has just said one. The weak *must've* belongs in the reconstruction itself: it states what the evidence gives you, with nobody disagreeing in the room.

**12A** · Recycle — twelve items
1 must have been · 2 can't have been · 3 might · 4 unlikely · 5 sight · 6 for · 7 stand · 8 probability · 9 had advertised / would have · 10 had not looked / would have had · 11 must have broken · 12 is grown

## B1.2 · Unit 5 · Four Per Cent

**0B** · Guess it
The new ward and the corridor refurbishment. The ward: the hoarding is up, the sign gives a date in the past, and the window frames are still stacked outside. The corridor: the trolleys are parked along a wall that was supposed to have been cleared, and the floor tape marking the works boundary has been walked through and never relaid.

**1B** · Sort — a number, a date, or a belief?
> **a number** — throughput · baseline · variance · demand
> **a date** — target · milestone · deadline
> **a belief** — forecast · assumption
**The belief treated as a number:** *assumption*. The four per cent sits in the model in the same typeface as the baseline, is multiplied like a measurement, and has not been reviewed since 2019. What it costs is twelve hundred and sixty-three people by year three, and no document anywhere recording the gap.

**2A** · The three-year model 🔊 Track 5.1
**1** b) 11,000 · **2** c) 16,000 · **3** c) returned for revision · **4** c) 2019
**Table** — The finance lead says the plan is affordable; assumes four per cent; if wrong, the plan is met and the service fails. Dr Herrera says the number is wrong; assumes nine; if wrong, four months are lost to a revision. Matías says both should be submitted; assumes nothing, which is the point; if he is wrong, a one-page annex is wasted.

**Noticing** — Completed: **1** (*will have passed*) and **3** (*will have doubled*). Still running: **2** (*will be running*) and **4** (*will have had*, which is a state continuing to that point — accept either placing if the learner can say why).

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 will have passed · 2 will be running · 3 will have been doing · 4 opens · 5 will have doubled · 6 will have had · 7 will still be assuming · 8 will have run out

**3E** · Use — guided
1 will be sitting — **future continuous**: at a point inside the meeting. *Will sit* would make it a decision; *will have sat* would put it after the meeting.  
2 will have finished — **future perfect**: completed before six. *Will be finishing* would put the finishing at six.  
3 will have left — **future perfect**: before the reading. *Will leave* would make it simultaneous.  
4 will have been working — **future perfect continuous**: a duration up to June. *Will have worked* loses the continuity the *for two years* needs.  
5 will know — **future simple**: a state beginning at that point. *Will have known* would imply the knowing was over.

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 will have passed · 2 will be running · 3 opens / will have doubled · 4 will have been working · 5 will know · 6 will have finished by · 7 will be presenting · 8 will have run out

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 That a department's three-year plan contains a demand assumption chosen not because anybody believes it but because it produces a plan that can be approved — and that nobody writes this down, because writing it down would end it.  
2 Because the real number produces a plan asking for money that does not exist.  
3 It is returned for revision, which costs four months.  
4 They notice that the department has been running on an approved plan for two years with no document saying what it actually needs. They do **not** notice that the assumption was wrong — everybody already knew that.  
5 Not financial: that at the end of three years there will be no record of the gap.  
6 A plan that was met, a service that is failing, and no piece of paper connecting the two.  
7 Submit the four per cent plan **and** a one-page annex describing the nine per cent world, every year.  
8 Because in four years somebody will ask when this started, and somebody ought to be able to answer.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context

**6A** · Sort — bare infinitive, *to*-infinitive, or past participle?
> **to-infinitive** — require · allow · permit · entitle · cause · oblige
> **past participle** — have · get
> **bare infinitive** — make · let

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 done · 2 sent · 3 to · 4 entitled / required · 5 behalf · 6 to

**6C** · Three that look like permission and are not
1 **entitled** — a right. *Obliged* or *required* would turn her right into her duty, which is the opposite.  
2 **required** (or *obliged*) — a duty imposed by the policy. *Entitled* would make the record optional.  
3 **obliged** — the moral flavour is the point: there is no duty, and no rule, and that is the finding. *Required* would be factually the same and would lose the reproach.

**6D** · The fixed expression
The words do not change; the stress and the context do. Spoken as a decision, the stress falls on *out* and the sentence is about an arrangement the speaker made. Spoken as a misfortune, the stress falls on *had* and the sentence is about something done to them. English leaves the two identical on the page, which is why a clinical record has to say who decided.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** It can be done under local. **Modal passive.**
> **2** She was made to wait. **Passive** — and *make* takes *to* in the passive.
> **3** I got my notes sent to the clinic. **Causative with *get*** — informal.
> **4** She should have been seen in March. **Modal perfect passive.**
> **5** She is having it done on Thursday. **Causative with *have*.**
> **6** Nothing was explained to her. **Passive.**
> **7** She was referred by her own doctor. **Passive**, agent kept.
> **8** Her daughter is not permitted to interpret. **Passive.**

**7C** · Make it mean something
Neutral stress — *She had her hair **cut*** — is the arrangement: she went to a hairdresser. Stress on *HAD* is the misfortune: somebody made her, and the stress is the only thing marking it. The ambiguity is real in English and is why the causative needs an agent in writing whenever the difference matters.

What makes it honest in the second case is adding the basis — *about a third, give or take; I have not counted, that is from the shape of the list* — which costs one clause and is the whole difference between a hedge and a guess wearing one.

**6A** · Sort — a wish, a preference, or a hypothesis?
> **a hypothesis** — supposedly · suppose · hypothetically · admittedly
> **a preference** — frankly · ideally · rather · preferably
> **a wish** — regrettably · wish · if only
**The two that are not about the future:** *supposedly* and *admittedly*. Both look like hypothesis markers and both point backwards — one at somebody else's claim, the other at a concession the speaker has already made. *frankly* is the third trap: it announces a manner, not a preference.

**6C** · Three that are not hedges
1 **Supposedly** — the sentence reports the institution's claim and doubts it. *Admittedly* would concede it, which is the opposite; *frankly* would be rude about something nobody disputes.  
2 **Admittedly** — a concession against the speaker. *Supposedly* would be absurd about one's own actions; *frankly* would make an admission sound like an attack.  
3 **Frankly** — the sentence is impolite about the review and announces it. *Supposedly* would attribute the claim elsewhere; *admittedly* would make it a concession, which it is not.

**6D** · The fixed expression
**Would have listened:** *If only somebody had told me. I spent fourteen months deciding that the thing I noticed in week three was normal.* **Would not have listened:** *If only somebody had told me. They did, in the first fortnight, and I remember thinking it was the kind of thing people say.* The second is the honest version, and it is the one almost nobody writes.

**7C** · Make it mean something
*I **WISH** I'd known* is the defence: the stress answers an implication that you did know, and the sentence is about the speaker's state rather than the missing information. *I wish I'd **KNOWN*** puts the news on the knowing, which is the version that is actually about the gap.
