## B2.1 · Unit 1 · Forty Minutes Behind

**0B** · Guess it
The second screen showing the rival's site and the lawyer's number on the note. Both exist only because of the market ticker: a claim that moves a share price creates a race (somebody else may publish first) and an exposure (if it is wrong, the damage is measurable in money). The chain runs ticker → price-sensitive claim → rival screen and lawyer. Accept the wall clock in place of the ticker if the learner says why the two are the same object here.

**1B** · Sort — before publication, after publication, or both?
> **after publication** — correction · retraction
> **before publication** — source · embargo · corroboration · provenance · verification · evidence
> **both** — claim · attribution
**The two that move:** *claim* and *attribution*. A claim exists before publication and is what a correction is about afterwards; an attribution is decided before and defended after. *Embargo* is arguably both — accept it if the learner can describe a post-publication embargo.

**1D** · Collocation Lab
1 stand a story up · 2 run a correction · 3 off the record · 4 on the record · 5 hold the line  
**Not about publishing:** *stand a story up*. It describes work done on the evidence, and it is done whether or not anything is ever printed. Accept *off the record* with an argument: it describes the terms on which something was said, not what the paper does with it.

**1E** · The phrasal verbs, and the three fixed expressions
***Who else has it?*** It is not a request for information; everybody in the room already knows the answer. It is a request for permission. If a rival has the story, being wrong is shared and being late is not — so the question converts a judgement about evidence into a judgement about competitors, which is a different and much easier question.

**2A** · Fifteen forty 🔊 Track 1.1
**1** T · **2** F · **3** T · **4** F
**Table** — *the job cuts*: claimed, 400; corroborated, a screenshot and one caller; assumed, that the screenshot is of a genuine board paper. *the timing*: claimed, this week; corroborated, nothing; assumed, that a decision taken is a decision announced. *the board meeting*: claimed, Tuesday; corroborated, the date only; assumed, that the cuts were on the agenda.

**Noticing** — Sentence 2, *The company cuts four hundred jobs*, makes the strongest factual claim: the simple aspect presents the event as whole, closed and known. Sentence 3, *has been cutting jobs since March*, commits the paper to least: it asserts a process, not a number, and a process cannot be wrong by four hundred minus three hundred and ten.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 is cutting · 2 is considering · 3 has been moving · 4 has been losing · 5 was going to announce

**3E** · Use — guided
1 The company is closing the Thunder Bay plant. *(a process under way, not a completed act)* · 2 The regulator is rejecting the application. *(no final decision asserted)* · 3 The minister is resigning. *(the resignation is happening, not finished)* · 4 Three directors were selling shares in the week before the announcement. *(activity, with no total claimed)* · 5 The union is accepting the offer. *(under way, and still reversible)*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 has cut · 2 has been cutting · 3 has been changing · 4 had been going to announce · 5 *The report contains three errors.* *(contain is stative)* · 6 *We have known this since Tuesday.* *(know is stative; the duration goes on the perfect)* · 7 *Was cutting* presents the number as a process still running, and a running process has no final figure. The sentence asserts both that the number may still move and that it cannot. · 8 *The minister is resigning.* **or** *The minister is understood to be resigning.*

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 A single source, a screenshot, and a claim that four hundred jobs were going at a plant in Thunder Bay.  
2 16.00.  
3 Eleven years.  
4 15.52 — eight minutes before the close.  
5 Four per cent, in the last eight minutes of trading.  
6 Three hundred and ten, ninety fewer than the four hundred that was published.  
7 Because a record of being right is a fact about a person, and corroboration is a fact about a claim. The stringer's eleven years tell you how likely the claim is; they tell you nothing about this one. Treating the first as the second is the commonest failure in the room.  
8 The new question is whether being right a day late is a product anybody is buying. Nobody can answer it because the paper has never measured it: it knows what Tuesday's traffic was worth and has no figure at all for what its accuracy on Wednesday is worth.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**going at a plant** — being cut, in the informal sense of jobs disappearing · **filed it** — sent the story in to the desk · **a second account** — an independent version from somebody with no connection to the first · **no decision had been taken** — the passive with the decider removed, which leaves open that one was about to be · **two column centimetres** — the physical size of the correction, and the real measure of how seriously it was meant

**5E** · The counter-text
1 That the figure of four hundred was incorrect and the company has confirmed three hundred and ten. It is on page eleven, two column centimetres, below the fold.  
2 The original used *is cutting* — a process asserted as under way, with a number attached. The correction uses *had been reported as* — a past perfect passive, which places the error in the reporting rather than in the company, and attributes it to nobody.  
3 The original claim is nineteen words; the correction is thirty-one. The correction is longer and reaches a twentieth of the readers.  
4 It does not say who was wrong, when the paper knew, what the source was, or that the share price moved. It corrects a number and nothing else, which is the only part that cannot be argued with.

**6A** · Sort — closed, running, or unsettled?
> **closed** — settled
> **running** — ongoing · iterative · habitual · unfolding
> **unsettled** — emergent · provisional · momentary
*Habitual* is the awkward one: it describes something that recurs rather than something that runs, and a learner who puts it in a column of its own has seen the point. *Momentary* is unsettled in the sense that matters here — a momentary figure is one you cannot publish.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 stands · 2 writing · 3 treated · 4 understood · 5 going · 6 thought

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
*is understood to* — attributes to an unnamed source the writer has actually spoken to. *is thought to have* — attributes to nobody at all; the thinking has no thinker. *is being treated as* — attributes to an institution's procedure: somebody has classified it and the writer is reporting the classification, not endorsing it.

**6D** · The phrasal verbs and the fixed expressions
***The situation is developing*** and ***we understand that***. The first asserts only that time is passing; the second attributes without naming, and can be written the moment anybody says anything. ***It now appears that*** requires a previous position to have been held and abandoned — it is a claim about the paper, not about the world, and a reader is entitled to ask what it appeared to be before.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** The board **had been going to announce** it on Thursday.
> **2** The company **has been losing** money since March.
> **3** The company **has cut** four hundred jobs.
> **4** The plant **closed** last March.
> **5** The company **has been restructuring** since 2019.
> **6** The number **was changing** all day on Monday.
> **7** The regulator **has rejected** the application.
> **8** The company **is understood to be cutting** jobs. *(no aspect alone will carry this one — it needs the attribution frame)*

## B2.1 · Unit 2 · We Have Always Said

**0B** · Guess it
The style guide, open at page 9, is the reason the leader-page proof carries no byline. The causation runs from the rule to the page, and not the other way: the rule was written first and the page is produced by it daily. The bound archive is what makes the rule expensive, because an unsigned institution can say *always* and an archive can date it.

**1B** · Sort — a person, an institution, or a group of readers?
> **an institution** — editorial · outlet · bureau · voice · masthead
> **a person** — columnist · correspondent
> **a group of readers** — constituency · audience · readership
**The two that sit in two columns:** *voice* — a columnist has one and so does the paper, and the unit is about the second borrowing the first; and *constituency* — a group of readers, and also a group somebody claims to speak for, which is the political sense and the reason the word is in this list at all.

**1D** · Collocation Lab
1 an unsigned piece · 2 parachute journalism · 3 local to the story · 4 a named writer · 5 whose paper is it  
**The one that could be checked:** *local to the story*. Where somebody lives is a fact with an address. The other four are judgements, and two of them are judgements about judgements.

**1E** · The phrasal verbs, and the three fixed expressions
***We have always said*** and ***this paper believes*** assert. ***Who is the we here?*** interrogates. What the third one does to the other two is make them answerable: both assert a position on behalf of a set, and neither states the set. The question does not dispute the position; it asks for the membership list, which is the one thing neither sentence can supply.

**2A** · The leader conference 🔊 Track 2.1
**1** F · **2** F · **3** T · **4** T
**Table** — *2017*: the claim, the plan deserves a hearing; the archive, a leader of 14 March 2017 supporting it; the *we*, an editorial board of nine, two of whom are still in post. *2021*: the claim, no position stated; the archive, silence; the *we*, nobody. *today*: the claim, we have always opposed it; the archive, the opposite in 2017; the *we*, the institution since 1911, unspecifiable.

**Noticing** — Sentence 1, *We have always said this*, makes the claim about continuity: the present perfect with *always* asserts an unbroken state and supplies no date. Sentence 2, *We said this in 2017*, makes a claim about a point: the simple past requires the date, which is exactly why an institution with an archive prefers the first.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 has said · 2 said · 3 will have held · 4 had not yet discussed · 5 might say

**3E** · Use — guided
1 *We have held this position since 2019.* **(point → continuity)** · 2 *We opposed the plan in a leader on 14 March 2019.* **(continuity → point)** · 3 *The paper supported it in 2018 and changed position at the inquiry.* **(past perfect → two dated points)** · 4 *By next spring we will have held this view for a decade.* **(the future perfect, correctly formed)** · 5 *The board has held that decision since March.* **(point → continuity)**

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 have said · 2 said · 3 had not discussed · 4 will have held · 5 *We said this in 2017.* *(a dated point takes the simple past; a present perfect cannot carry a finished-time adverbial)* · 6 *The paper has believed this since 1911.* *(believe is stative; the duration goes on the simple perfect)* · 7 *We have said this since March 2019* — or, better, *We said this in a leader on 14 March 2019 and have not changed position since.* · 8 *The editorial board decided on 14 March 2019 that…* — a person, or a body with a membership, and a date.

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Eighty-one times in two years.  
2 Four kilometres.  
3 Nine.  
4 Two of the nine are the same person.  
5 That the ward has been neglected.  
6 Because *neglected* is a passive with the agent removed, and the paper is one of the candidates for that agent. The sentence is the whole argument of the piece, made by noticing an absence rather than by asserting anything.  
7 *Has now been quoted in four pieces* imports a past series into the present: it is not reporting four events, it is reporting a state the shopkeeper is now in, which is what makes the phrase *a local resident* read as a job description.  
8 Because the gap between what people think and what will happen to the figure is the subject. Ending on it leaves the reader holding the distinction rather than the conclusion, and the piece has no conclusion to offer.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**a bureau four kilometres away** — a staffed office near the ward but not in it · **described as a local resident** — labelled that way by the paper, repeatedly, without the label being checked · **neglected** — a passive with no agent, and the agent is the point · **filed from the bureau** — written without going to the place · **what it will be used as** — the readership figure will be cited as an argument against sending anybody again

**5E** · The counter-text
1 2017: the plan deserves a hearing. Today: we have always opposed it.  
2 2017 — *our readers in the east end* (a set that could be counted), *we will be asking* (the newsroom, about nine people). Today — *we have always opposed* (the institution, since 1911), *we have said so throughout* (unspecifiable; no set exists that makes it true).  
3 2017: none. Today: four. Each perfect imports a past position into the present argument without dating it, which is how a change of position becomes a continuity.  
4 A byline would make the sentence *we have always opposed* either false or absurd, because a named person who joined in 2020 cannot have always said anything. The byline does not improve the argument; it makes one sentence unwritable.

**6A** · Sort — includes the reader, excludes the reader, or avoids the question?
> **excludes the reader** — institutional · exclusive · corporate
> **includes the reader** — collective · inclusive · generic
> **avoids the question** — anonymous · impersonal
*Collective* is the arguable one: it says the group acts as one and says nothing about who is in it. Accept it in the third column if the learner makes that argument.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 royal · 2 might · 3 held · 4 writer · 5 personally · 6 view

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
*in this paper* claims institutional authority — the paper is speaking. *as a reader* claims none — it puts the writer on the other side of the page. *the general view* claims a consensus, and is the only one of the three that could be checked and never is.

**6D** · The phrasal verbs and the fixed expressions
***We take the view that***. The plural commits an institution to a position, so only somebody entitled to speak for it can say it. You know because of what happens if the wrong person says it: *that is not our position* is the sentence that follows, and it can only be said by somebody further up.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** **The business desk**, which made it, regrets the error.
> **2** **Dr Sollis**, who compiled them, says the figures are unreliable.
> **3** **Three residents** of the six streets were asked, by telephone, on one afternoon.
> **4** **I think** the plan will fail.
> **5** **The news desk** made two errors in the 14 March edition.
> **6** The board **decided on 14 March 2019** to oppose it.
> **7** The **editorial board** adopted a transparency policy on **9 May 2021**.
> **8** **Sixty-one per cent of 480 readers surveyed in March** said they expected better.

**12A** · Recycle — fifteen items
1 have said · 2 said · 3 had not discussed · 4 will have held · 5 royal · 6 One · 7 held · 8 writer · 9 Speaking · 10 believes · 11 Who is the we here · 12 our position · 13 stand in · 14 own up · 15 written up

## B2.1 · Unit 3 · A Doubling of Risk

**0B** · Guess it
The whiteboard with the base rate on it — four in ten thousand. It is the one figure that makes the headline decidable, and nobody has opened it because the release arrived at nine with a ratio already in its first line, and a ratio reads like a finding. Accept the preprint on screen if the learner argues that page eleven carries the same number and is forty pages in.

**1B** · Sort — counted in people, a relation between numbers, or a measure of doubt?
> **a measure of doubt** — confidence · significance · interval
> **a relation between numbers** — risk · hazard · correlation · causation
> **counted in people** — incidence · prevalence · baseline · exposure · cohort
**The two that are swapped daily:** *correlation* and *causation*. Both are relations between two numbers; only one of them licenses a sentence with *because* in it. *Baseline* is arguable — it is a count, and it exists only in order to be divided into, which is the second column's business. Accept either with the argument.

**1D** · Collocation Lab
1 a press release · 2 a preprint · 3 peer reviewed · 4 a causal link · 5 the margin of error  
**Checked by somebody with no stake in it:** *peer reviewed* — and the point of item 3 is that nothing here has been. Everything else on the sheet has been produced by people who benefit from it being interesting.

**1E** · The phrasal verbs, and the three fixed expressions
***The evidence does not support that*** closes the door. The two that leave it open are *it may well be* and *we cannot rule it out*. Of those, *it may well be* is the more honest: it places the claim somewhere on the scale, low but real. *We cannot rule it out* says nothing about probability at all — it reports the absence of an exclusion, which is true of almost every claim ever made, and reads as support.

**2A** · The four o'clock conference 🔊 Track 3.1
**1** F · **2** F · **3** T · **4** T
**Table** — *the release*: the number, a doubling; what it is out of, not stated; how sure, highly significant and demonstrably linked. *the preprint*: the number, four to eight per ten thousand per year; what it is out of, ten thousand person-years; how sure, statistically significant, no causal claim, interval 1.2 to 3.3. *the epidemiologist*: the number, the same; what it is out of, the same; how sure, it may well be real and we cannot rule it out.

**Noticing** — The paper could defend sentence 3, *It may well be a real effect*, in court: it asserts possibility and the interval supports it. Sentence 1, *It must be a real effect*, is the bar sentence: it is the strongest inference on the scale and the data at an interval of 1.2 to 3.3 cannot carry it.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 may / might / could · 2 must · 3 cannot have / could not have · 4 should · 5 may … or may not

**3E** · Use — guided
1 *Night shifts may be associated with the condition; this cohort cannot show that they cause it.* · 2 *The release must have been written by somebody who had not read the paper.* · 3 *It could hardly have been chance, at that interval.* · 4 *A replication could settle it, if anybody funds one.* *(a hope, not a forecast)* · 5 *The authors are said to disagree with the release.* *(sourced elsewhere, because you have not asked them)*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 may / might / could · 2 must · 3 cannot have / could not have · 4 could hardly have · 5 may or may not · 6 *It must be a real effect.* *(a modal is followed by the bare infinitive)* · 7 *Night shifts are associated with the condition in this cohort; no causal claim is made.* · 8 *About four extra cases per ten thousand people a year, on a baseline of four.*

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Sixty-one thousand Danish adults, followed for nine years.  
2 Four cases per ten thousand people per year.  
3 From 1.2 to 3.3.  
4 Three times.  
5 That they cannot establish a causal link, and that the cohort was not designed to.  
6 Because three hundred and ten words is enough room to give the base rate four times over. The length turns an omission into a choice.  
7 Both describe the same data. The ratio says how much the risk changed; the absolute figure says how much risk there was to change. Neither is wrong, and only the second lets a reader decide what to do.  
8 The constraint is not space — a headline can hold either figure. It is that only one of them will be read, and the desk is choosing which fact the reader walks away with.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**consistent with an effect of almost nothing** — the lower end of the interval is close to no effect at all · **just** — barely; the result scrapes the threshold · **designed to** — set up in advance to answer that question, which this one was not · **what it is out of** — the denominator; the base rate · **room for one of them** — the headline will carry one figure, and the choice is editorial

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Nowhere. The release contains no base rate and no absolute figure at all.  
2 *demonstrably* — claims a causal link the authors refuse; *highly significant* — uses *significant* in the lay sense of large, which is not what it means; *is said to be* — attributes to an unnamed somebody; *is bound to prompt* — a forecast at the top of the scale; *has yet to be peer reviewed* — true, accurate, and in the last line.  
3 The release leaves out the base rate, the absolute figures and the interval. The preprint leaves out nothing of substance; what it does not supply is a sentence a newsroom can use.  
4 A press office, which is measured on coverage obtained. Nobody in that building is measured on whether a reader could act on the number.

**6A** · Sort — the writer is unsure, somebody else said it, or the writer is committed?
> **the writer is unsure** — arguably · conceivably · presumably
> **the writer is committed** — demonstrably · unequivocally
> **somebody else said it** — reportedly · allegedly · ostensibly
*Ostensibly* is the one worth arguing about: it reports an appearance and quietly disputes it, so it is somebody else's claim plus the writer's doubt. *Presumably* is the other: it is an inference the writer is making, not a hedge about somebody else's.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 should well / is likely to · 2 must surely be · 3 might well have · 4 could hardly have · 5 must surely be · 6 may or may not · 7 is said to · 8 has yet to be  
*(1 takes* is likely to *; 5 takes* must surely be *— accept* there is bound to be *for 5 if the learner keeps the word count.)*

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
*is likely to* gives a probability — it places the claim on the scale. *is said to* gives a source, and no probability at all. *has yet to be* gives a date that has not arrived: it says nothing about whether the thing is true, only that the step which would settle it has not happened.

**6D** · The phrasal verbs and the fixed expressions
*on the balance of probability* is the legal standard — more likely than not, and the civil test. *to the best of our knowledge* is the institutional limit — true as far as this organisation has looked. *that is overstating it* is the correction. A scientist would use the second about their own work, because it is the only one of the three that reports the boundary of what was checked.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** Night shifts **are associated with** the condition in this cohort; no causal claim can be made from it.
> **2** The release **must have been written** by the press office.
> **3** Night work **is demonstrably associated** with four extra cases per ten thousand a year in one cohort.
> **4** The effect **could hardly have been** chance, at that interval.
> **5** The study **may be wrong**; I have read only the abstract.
> **6** The effect **may or may not** be real: the interval runs from almost nothing to three times the reported size.
> **7** It **could be replicated** next year, if anybody funds it.
> **8** The authors **are said to have approved** the release.

**12A** · Recycle — fifteen items
1 may / might / could · 2 must · 3 could hardly have · 4 may or may not · 5 has yet to be · 6 Presumably · 7 necessarily *(accept* demonstrably *with an argument)* · 8 The base rate · 9 a preprint · 10 On the balance of probability · 11 To the best of our knowledge · 12 That is overstating it · 13 scale up · 14 come down on · 15 hedge about

## B2.1 · Unit 4 · It Was Going to Open in 2019

**0B** · Guess it
The framed 2014 announcement and the budget board. Both were made on the same day in October 2014; the announcement still says $2.4 billion and spring 2019, and the board has been crossed out four times. The board is the one that has been quietly replaced — four times — which is why the two no longer agree and why only one of them is framed.

**1B** · Sort — made beforehand, the shape of the thing, or counted afterwards?
> **made beforehand** — forecast · scenario · projection · contingency · assumption
> **the shape and the distance** — trajectory · horizon · foresight
> **counted afterwards** — revision · hindsight · overrun · shortfall
**The pair looking in opposite directions:** *foresight* and *hindsight*. Same faculty, same person, and only one of them is ever claimed in advance. *Trajectory* is arguable in the first column — accept it if the learner says that a trajectory is drawn before anything has happened.

**1D** · Collocation Lab
1 The plan was to · 2 As things turned out · 3 With hindsight · 4 The gap between · 5 A missed deadline  
**Writable in 2014:** *the plan was to* — and only just, because the past tense already implies the plan has been superseded. The other four all require an ending the writer has already seen.

**1E** · The phrasal verbs, and the three fixed expressions
***Nobody saw it coming.*** It is almost always false when it is said, because somebody usually did and wrote it down — here, on page eleven of the 2016 contingency documents. What it does for the speaker is convert a failure of attention into a property of the world: if the thing was unforeseeable, nobody is answerable for not having foreseen it.

**2A** · The forecast audit 🔊 Track 4.1
**1** T · **2** F · **3** T · **4** F
**Table** — *the line*: it was going to open in spring 2019; it has been revised to 2023, 2027 and 2031; nothing has opened. *the budget*: $2.4 billion; revised downwards once in 2016 and upwards three times; the central estimate is now $6.1 billion. *the paper's forecast*: forty forecasts in five years; eleven came off; none of the thirty-one dated pieces mentioned the forecast it replaced.

**Noticing** — Sentence 3, *It would have opened in 2019, but for the harbour*, blames something: *but for* names the obstacle. Sentence 4, *It was to have opened in 2019*, is the most formal, and carries a reproach the writer never has to state. Sentence 2, *It was due to open in 2019*, is the plain report: a schedule, stated, with nobody blamed.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 was due to · 2 was to have been · 3 was about to · 4 would have · 5 would later

**3E** · Use — guided
1 *The line was going to open in 2019.* · 2 *They were about to sign in March.* · 3 *The budget would later reach six point one billion.* · 4 *The paper would get one forecast in four right.* · 5 *The station model was to have been moved into the finished concourse.*

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 was due to · 2 was to have been · 3 were about to · 4 would have · 5 would later · 6 *It was going to open in 2019.* *(going to takes the bare infinitive)* · 7 *They were about to sign when the survey came back.* · 8 *The line would not open until 2031.* — or, standing in 2014, *It was to open in 2019 and would in fact open in 2031.*

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 October 2014; it was due to open in the spring of 2019.  
2 Two point four billion dollars; the central estimate is now six point one billion.  
3 Thirty-one.  
4 None.  
5 The harbour problem is modelled on page eleven, with a worst case of 2029.  
6 Because the model is the only part of the 2014 announcement that was actually delivered. It is a photograph of a thing that exists standing in for a thing that does not, and it is in a lobby where it will be seen daily.  
7 The writer sets a 2023 quotation against a 2016 document and lets the dates do the work. Saying it outright would be a judgement the reader could argue with; the two dates are facts the reader draws the judgement from, which is harder to dispute and impossible to forget.  
8 *Revised four times* is a count of events and sounds like diligence. *Downwards once and upwards three times* is a direction, and the direction is the finding.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**it was to have been** — formal future in the past, carrying a reproach the writer never states · **has since been revised** — changed more than once, with the revisers unnamed · **released under access law** — obtained by legal request, not volunteered · **of the second delay** — the quotation is about one delay out of several · **model the harbour problem** — set it out as a scenario, with numbers

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Read from today, every main verb in it is a future in the past: *will open* → was going to open; *is to be* → was to have been; *is to begin* → was due to begin; *will fall* → was going to fall.  
2 Spring 2019, March 2015, forty years, 11.3 km. Only the tunnel length still stands.  
3 *Subject to the completion of the harbour survey.* Eleven words, at the end of the second quotation. It was protecting the construction start date, and it is the one clause that has done its job.  
4 Four signatories. None of them is still in post, which is why the announcement can be quoted against the city and not against anybody in it.

**6A** · Sort — at the start, somewhere along the way, or looking from one end?
> **looking from one end** — retrospectively · prospectively
> **somewhere along the way** — eventually · ultimately · latterly · subsequently
> **at the start** — originally · formerly
*Eventually* and *ultimately* are arguably a column of their own — both name the end rather than a point along the way, and *ultimately* carries a judgement that *eventually* does not. Accept the split if the learner can state the difference.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 was going to · 2 was due to · 3 were about to · 4 would have been · 5 had intended to · 6 was to have been · 7 would later · 8 As it would turn out

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
*was going to* reports a plan — somebody intended it. *was due to* reports a commitment — a schedule existed, often contractual, and somebody can be held to it. *was to have been* carries a complaint: it is formal, written, and says the thing did not happen without ever having to say so.

**6D** · The phrasal verbs and the fixed expressions
*back out* and *pull out* describe leaving an agreement; *call off* describes ending an event. Between the first two: *back out* implies a commitment already made and suggests fault; *pull out* is neutral about fault and is the word a company uses about itself.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** Nobody **was expecting** a second delay, although the 2016 documents **had modelled** one.
> **2** **Retrospectively**, the paper got one forecast in four right.
> **3** It **was not going to open** for another seventeen years.
> **4** The line **was to have opened** before the harbour survey was completed.
> **5** The budget **would later reach** six point one billion.
> **6** The line **was due to open** in 2019.
> **7** The contractor **had intended to** stay on the project.

*do so* belongs in a report — formal, written, and it points at a verb phrase with no ambiguity. *do it* belongs in conversation, and in between in print: it is neutral and slightly more concrete. Bare *did* is the spoken British form — *he asked me to file and I did* — and is the one advanced learners avoid, which is what marks their register.

**6D** · The phrasal verbs and the fixed expressions
***I will if you will.*** It looks like a condition and is an offer: the speaker has already decided and is asking for cover. What they want is not the other person's action but their co-signature, so that the decision has two names on it.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** She would not cut it, **and neither would I**.
> **2** **The former** carries the argument; **the latter** merely agrees with it.
> **3** I have cut **both**.
> **4** **If so**, send it to the stone.
> **5** The second quote can go, **and so can the third**.
> **6** The paragraph is unreadable and, **as such**, goes first.
> **7** He asked me to file the story **and I did**.
> **8** The long version is better than **the short one**.

**12A** · Recycle — fifteen items
1 has · 2 have · 3 So · 4 Neither · 5 so · 6 done so · 7 the former · 8 The latter · 9 as such · 10 The first pass · 11 It can go · 12 We are over by forty · 13 cut it down · 14 chopped out · 15 pared back

## B2.1 · Unit 9 · Three Readers, One Style Guide

**0B** · Guess it
The app preview, held up. The app had three hundred and forty thousand readers last month against a print run of a hundred and twelve thousand. The style guide on the table — two hundred and fourteen pages — has no entry for *app*, *newsletter* or *push alert*, so the channel with the most readers is the one with no written rules at all.

**1B** · Sort — a person or a group, a place or a time, or a document?
> **a place or a time** — galley · splash · spike · slot · deadline
> **a person or a group** — desk · subeditor · copytaster · conference
> **a document** — wire · house style · style guide
**The one that is also a verb:** *spike* — to spike a story is to kill it, and the object on the desk is where killed stories used to go. *Desk* and *conference* are in the first column because in a newsroom both are groups of people before they are furniture; accept either in the second with that argument. *Wire* is a source rather than a document in the strict sense — accept a fourth column.

**1D** · Collocation Lab
1 The news list · 2 a running order · 3 hold the front page · 4 A late change · 5 filed on time  
**A measure of a person:** *filed on time* — every day for nine years. The other four are properties of the day's paper.

**1E** · The phrasal verbs, and the three fixed expressions
***That is a splash*** is the judgement; the other two are decisions. The news editor makes it, and only the news editor: it says a story is strong enough for page one before anybody has decided to run it there, and it commits nothing. The decisions — *we are going with it* and *it will not make the paper* — can be reversed; the judgement is quoted back at you for a fortnight.

**2A** · The style guide meeting 🔊 Track 9.1
**1** T · **2** F · **3** F · **4** T
**Table** — *the paper*: nine markers per thousand words; the reader is somebody holding a page and able to see how long the article is; the desk says more markers cost precision. *the newsletter*: thirty-one; the reader is on a phone between two other things; the writer says fewer markers cost the reader their place. *the app*: fourteen; the reader is the same as the newsletter's; nobody has decided anything, because the app version is made by cutting the print version.

**Noticing** — Sentences 2 and 4, *that said* and *having said that*, concede: both grant the previous point before continuing. Sentence 5, *by contrast*, announces a comparison. Sentence 6, *in any case*, says the argument no longer matters — it sets the previous point aside rather than answering it.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 However · 2 Accordingly · 3 To begin with · 4 That said · 5 In any case

**3E** · Use — guided
1 *The figures, however, do not support that* — quieter, more confident. *The figures do not support that, however* — an afterthought. · 2 *The newsletter is, nevertheless, read to the end* / *… read to the end, nevertheless.* · 3 *The guide will, accordingly, be rewritten* / *… be rewritten accordingly.* *(at the end,* accordingly *stops being a marker and becomes a manner adverb)* · 4 *The app version, meanwhile, went out unchanged* / *… went out unchanged meanwhile.* *(the end position is barely possible, which tells you something about* meanwhile*)* · 5 *The print version, by contrast, carries nine markers* / *… carries nine markers, by contrast.* Force falls from front to middle to end in every case.

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 However · 2 Accordingly · 3 To begin with · 4 That said · 5 In any case · 6 *The guide was revised in 1998, whereby the newsletter entry was added* — or simply *The guide was revised in 1998.* *Whereby* cannot open a sentence; it joins a clause to a noun phrase before it. · 7 Front: *However, the figures do not support that* — most force, a formal announcement. Middle: *The figures, however, do not support that* — less force, more confidence. End: *The figures do not support that, however* — least force, an afterthought. · 8 Any sentence where the second clause does not hold in spite of the first — e.g. *The guide is long; nevertheless, it is detailed.* Length and detail do not oppose each other, so *nevertheless* promises a contrast that never arrives.

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Two hundred and fourteen pages; last fully revised in 1998.  
2 Four lines, saying that discourse markers should be used sparingly.  
3 Nine per thousand words in print, thirty-one in the newsletter, fourteen in the app.  
4 Because the app version is produced by cutting the print version, so its density is whatever survived the cut.  
5 Three quarters of sixty-one thousand — about forty-six thousand.  
6 Because a number that looks like a decision and is not is the most misleading thing on the page. Fourteen reads as a compromise between nine and thirty-one; it is an accident, and the paper has been citing it as evidence of a middle way.  
7 One document with one voice cannot hold two true statements that contradict each other about different readers. The guide is not wrong; it is a single-valued answer to a question that has three values.  
8 That the guide should state one rule and let each channel set its own figure under it — which is option three, proposed by the structure of the sentence rather than by the writer.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**used sparingly** — rarely, with no figure attached, which is why the entry has lasted · **not by policy but because** — the result of a process, not a decision · **reading on a phone between two other things** — interrupted, with no sense of how much is left · **does not come back** — a reader who loses the thread abandons the piece permanently · **has one voice** — the guide describes a single register, for a single reader

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Print: none. Newsletter: five — *so*, *here is the thing*, *however*, *and the guide?*, *which brings me to*. App: one — *however*.  
2 *Which brings me to the awkward bit*, which promises a next item and is followed by nothing. The paragraph ends there.  
3 It kept whichever markers happened to survive the cut, which is why *however* is doing work in the app version that it was not doing in print: it now joins two facts that were separate sentences.  
4 Open. Most readers choose the newsletter version on a phone and the print version on paper — which is the finding, because they are not the same version and the guide says they should be.

**6A** · Sort — contrast, result, time, or formal only?
> **contrast** — however · nevertheless
> **time** — meanwhile · thereafter
> **result** — thereby · accordingly · hence
> **formal only** — whereby
*Thereby* is the one to argue about: it marks a result and it is also formal-only, so accept it in either column with the argument. *Hence* is formal but not formal-only — it survives in speech, where *whereby* does not.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 In the first place · 2 That said · 3 As it happens · 4 For one thing · 5 On the other hand · 6 By contrast · 7 In any case · 8 To begin with

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
*however* simply contrasts: the next clause pushes back. *nevertheless* contrasts in spite of something: it concedes the first clause and holds the second anyway. *that said* grants the other person a point first, which is why it is the one that keeps a negotiation open.

**6D** · The phrasal verbs and the fixed expressions
***The point being.*** It is left unfinished on purpose: the speaker states the relationship and lets the listener supply the clause, which keeps the floor without committing to a form of words. Finish it and you have made a claim somebody can answer; leave it and you have made one they have to complete for you.

**6E** · Contrast Clinic
> **1** You may dispute the count. **In any case**, the survey is separate.
> **2** The guide was revised in 1998; **accordingly**, it says nothing about newsletters. *(or* hence*)*
> **3** The rule was agreed, **thereby** requiring every channel to state its own density.
> **4** The print desk objected; **meanwhile**, the newsletter went out unchanged.
> **5** The guide has one voice, **whereas** the paper has three readers. *(formal written contrast; accept* by contrast *at the front of the second clause)*
> **6** The newsletter is informal. **That said**, it is the most read thing the paper makes.
> **7** **To begin with**, the markers were counted; **thereafter**, the readers were surveyed.
> **8** The app has more readers; **however**, it has no style guide.

**12A** · Recycle — fifteen items
1 However · 2 Accordingly *(accept* hence*)* · 3 Meanwhile · 4 Nevertheless · 5 Thereafter · 6 In the first place · 7 That said · 8 As it happens · 9 In any case · 10 Which brings me to the point · 11 Having said that · 12 The point being · 13 send through · 14 turn around · 15 sit on

## B2.1 · Unit 10 · Never Once in the Culture Pages

**0B** · Guess it
Tuesday's front page and the culture page from the same week. Both are about the six streets around the Lake and Seventh junction; only the front page names the ward, because the Tuesday story is about a crime and the Friday one is about a hall reopening. Nothing in the style guide requires that, and the difference has been consistent since at least 1994.

**1B** · Sort — the place and its edges, who lives there, or what the paper adds?
> **what the paper adds** — framing · displacement · headline · stereotype · gentrification
> **the place and its edges** — neighbourhood · zoning · boundary · ward
> **who lives there** — resident · tenure · demographic
**The two residents never use about themselves:** *demographic* and *gentrification*. Both are words for a place said from outside it. *Gentrification* and *displacement* are processes happening in the ward rather than things the paper adds — accept them in a column of their own, which is how the cutaway in 1A sets them out.

**1D** · Collocation Lab
1 named in the headline · 2 A postcode in a headline · 3 who is quoted / the first paragraph · 4 The first paragraph · 5 The local paper  
**What decides what a reader remembers:** the headline — and that is the point of item 4. The first paragraph repeats it, which is why it so rarely corrects it.

**1E** · The phrasal verbs, and the three fixed expressions
***That is not what it is called*** is the correction; the other two are accusations. It is the hardest to answer because it is a fact rather than a judgement: there is a name residents use and a name the paper uses, and the paper cannot defend its own name without arguing that it knows the place better than the people in it.

**2A** · The naming rule 🔊 Track 10.1
**1** T · **2** F · **3** F · **4** T
**Table** — *crime*: the ward is named in seventy-one per cent of headlines; a resident is quoted in eleven per cent; thirty-four pieces. *planning*: eighteen per cent named, thirty-four per cent quoting a resident; twenty-two pieces. *culture*: nought per cent named, fifty-two per cent quoting a resident; eleven pieces.

**Ordinary order** — 1 *Nobody had ever counted them before.* 2 *The culture pages did not name it once.* 3 *We only use the ward when the story is about crime.* 4 *As soon as the letter arrived, the editor asked for the figures.* 5 *The ward appears in good news so rarely that readers assume there is none.* 6 *We did not know the rule had never been written down.* What is lost each time is the emphasis: the fronted word is the one the reader repeats, and in ordinary order nothing is.

**3D** · Use — controlled
1 *Never before had anybody counted them.* · 2 *Not once did the culture pages name it.* · 3 *Only when the story is about crime do we use the ward.* · 4 *No sooner had the letter arrived than the editor asked for the figures.* · 5 *So rarely does the ward appear in good news that readers assume there is none.*

**3E** · Use — guided
1 *Nobody had counted them before.* — the count becomes background. · 2 *The culture pages did not name it once.* — *once* now reads as a quantity, not as a refusal. · 3 *The editor only then asked for the figures.* — the sequence survives and the turning point is gone. · 4 *A resident was not consulted at any point.* — the absolute becomes an ordinary negative. · 5 *The reaction was so great that the rule was rewritten.* — the sentence is now about the rule rather than about the reaction. In every case the information is identical and the sentence is no longer the one the reader repeats.

**3H** · Check — eight items
1 Never before had · 2 Not once did · 3 Only when … do · 4 No sooner had · 5 So rarely does · 6 *Never before had anybody counted them.* *(the auxiliary must come before the subject)* · 7 *At no point was a resident asked.* · 8 Open. A defensible example: *Not once in two years was my street named in anything but a crime report.*

**5C** · Comprehension — eight questions
1 Eighty-one, over two years.  
2 Crime seventy-one per cent, planning eighteen, business six, culture nought.  
3 Eleven.  
4 Specificity — a crime happens somewhere and readers want to know where.  
5 Forty thousand.  
6 Because it removes the defence that the paper has no interest in the ward. The paper campaigned for the hall, won, and then reported the reopening without saying where it was.  
7 That the specificity argument, if it is sound, applies to every event; a concert has a location in exactly the way a crime does. Six words are enough because the reader supplies the rest.  
8 It converts a table into a person. Everything before it is a pattern; the last sentence is somebody who lives four streets from the junction and has been written about eighty-one times without once being asked anything.

**5D** · Vocabulary in context
**sorted them by section** — grouped the eighty-one pieces by which part of the paper they appeared in · **all three were about specificity** — all three reasons were versions of the same argument · **account for sixty-two of the eighty-one** — make up that many of the total · **four streets away** — close enough to be inside every one of the stories and outside all of them · **has never been quoted in any of them** — the paper has written about her street eighty-one times and never asked her anything

**5E** · The counter-text
1 Thirty-one of the eighty-one: twenty-four of thirty-four crime pieces, four of twenty-two planning, one of fourteen business, and none of eleven culture.  
2 *POLICE SEEK WITNESSES IN WARD 7* (news, Tuesday) and *HALL REOPENS AFTER FOUR YEARS* (culture, Friday). The first names the place.  
3 A postcode names a smaller area than a ward and sounds administrative rather than communal. It locates a reader's own address inside the story, which a ward name does not, and it is the one form residents object to most.  
4 Open. A consistent set: *HALL REOPENS IN WARD 7 AFTER FOUR YEARS*; *WARD 7 FIRM WINS CONTRACT*; *POLICE SEEK WITNESSES AFTER WARD 7 ASSAULT*. The test is that no section can be identified from the naming alone.

**6A** · Sort — how often, how nearly not, where, or how restricted?
> **how restricted** — only
> **how nearly not** — little · hardly · scarcely
> **how often** — rarely · seldom · never
> **where** — nowhere
*Little* is the one to argue about: in *little did we know* it is a degree word doing the work of a negative, and it survives almost nowhere else. *Never* is a frequency word that behaves as an absolute — accept a fourth column for it with that argument.

**6B** · Chunk completion
1 Never before · 2 Not once · 3 Only when · 4 No sooner · 5 At no point · 6 Under no circumstances · 7 So rarely does · 8 Such was the

**6C** · Three that are not interchangeable
*rarely* is about frequency — how many times out of how many. *hardly* is about degree — how nearly something did not happen at all, and it says nothing about how often. *only* restricts the whole clause: it does not describe the event, it rules out every case except the one named.

**6D** · The phrasal verbs and the fixed expressions
***Single out.*** The letter accuses the paper of singling the ward out — naming it when the news is bad and not otherwise. The paper would say it was *setting out* the facts: giving the location because a location is a fact. The disagreement is not about any one headline; it is about whether a practice applied in one section only is specificity or selection.
