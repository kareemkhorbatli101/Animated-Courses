# 14 · Specimen Texts

The grading spine in `03` asserts lengths and complexity. This document **demonstrates** them:
real texts at six points on the shelf, written to band, with their counts verified and printed.
Its job is to let you judge the gradient by reading it rather than by trusting a table.

Every count below was measured, not estimated. The verification table is §7.

## 1 · Reading

### 1.1 · A1.1 Unit 9 · *How do you find your way?* — main text (5A)
> **Band:** 80–120 words · mean sentence 8–10 · 1.1–1.3 clauses/sentence · genre: a notice
> **Grammar in use:** imperatives, prepositions of movement · **Returning:** *there is/there are*

**The card with the cats on it**

Ana works at the language school on Rua do Monte. Every day, new students ask her the same
question. "How do I get to the school?" So Ana writes a card, and now she gives it to everybody.

*From the station, walk up the hill. It is a long hill! Pass the blue church on your left. Then
turn right at the café with the red chairs. We are the big yellow building on the corner. There are
two cats on the step, and the cats are always there.*

The card works and nobody is late now. But everybody is very tired.

### 1.2 · A2.2 Unit 3 · *What happened before that?* — main text (5A)
> **Band:** 180–240 words · mean sentence 11–13 · 1.4–1.6 clauses · genre: first-person account
> **Grammar in use:** past perfect · **Returning:** past simple and continuous

**The night the water came**

Eimear still has the photograph. It shows her kitchen table, and the water is twelve centimetres up
the legs.

The storm had been on the news for three days before it arrived. Everyone in the town had heard the
warnings. Her neighbour Donal had moved his car to the top road on the Thursday. People had laughed at him
a little. By Saturday night nobody was laughing.

Eimear was asleep when the water came in. She woke up because the dog was barking, and when she put
her feet down, the floor was wet. She had left her boots by the back door, which was the worst place
she could have left them. It took her four minutes to get her mother out of the downstairs bedroom.

What surprised her afterwards was not the water. It was how fast the town organised itself. By ten
on Sunday morning, someone had opened the community hall, someone else had made two hundred
sandwiches, and a boy she did not know was carrying her furniture upstairs.

"We had always said we were a close town," she says now. "I think we only found out it was true that
weekend."

The water went down on the Monday. The photograph stays on the fridge.

### 1.3 · B1.1 Unit 1 · *Who is a city built for?* — main text (5A)
> **Band:** 250–320 words · mean sentence 13–15 · 1.6–1.9 clauses · genre: feature article
> **Grammar in use:** narrative tenses contrasted · **Returning:** past perfect
> *(This is the text specified in `12-specimen-unit.md`, written.)*

**The last shop on the corner**

Mwangi Njoroge has been taking in trousers on the same corner of Ngong Lane for thirty-one years. In
that time three buildings have gone up around him and two have come down. His stall has not moved
by a metre.

When he started, the lane was mostly workshops. People repaired things here — radios, shoes,
bicycles, trousers. The building that now houses Jengo House was a timber store, and the plot next
to it was open ground where matatu drivers parked and argued. Mwangi rented his two metres of
frontage from a man called Otieno, who died in 2009, and whose son raised the rent twice and then
forgot about it.

The first tower went up in 2014. Mwangi remembers the week it was finished, because for eleven days
he could not see the sun until after ten in the morning, and he was working by lamp. The second one
took the open ground. The drivers moved three streets down and most of them never came back.

"People ask me if business is worse," he says. "It is not worse. It is different. Before, I repaired
work clothes. Now I take in suits for young people who have just started earning and bought the
wrong size online."

He is not sentimental about it. He points out that the new buildings brought him the customers he
has now, and that the lane had been dangerous after dark for years before anyone built anything.

But he also notes that of the fourteen businesses that were on this stretch when he arrived, his is
the only one still trading. The others did not fail. Their rent went up, and they moved somewhere
cheaper, and then that place got expensive too.

### 1.4 · B2.2 Unit 3 · *How much can one noun carry?* — main text (5A), opening 620 words
> **Band:** 500–650 words · mean sentence 17–20 · 2.1–2.5 clauses · genre: report extract
> **Grammar in use:** complex noun phrases · **Returning:** nominalisation

**What the port does not see**

The container terminal at the western end of the Maasvlakte is the most automated in Europe, and on
a Tuesday morning in February there is almost nobody in it. Thirty-eight automated stacking cranes
move along their rails without a cab, without a driver and, for most of the shift, without anyone
watching them. The ships are unloaded by quay cranes operated from a building two kilometres away,
where eleven people sit in front of screens in a room that is quieter than a library.

This is the image of the modern port that gets photographed. It is accurate, and it is also about a
fifth of the story.

The automation covers the part of the journey that is easiest to automate: the movement of a
standardised steel box between a ship, a stack and a truck. A container is a container. It is the
same size every time, it has a number on the side, and the number can be read by a camera. Almost
everything that happens to it inside the terminal fence has been engineered down to a repeatable
operation. The result is a facility that runs at night, in fog, and through most of a storm.

What has not been automated is everything that touches the outside world. A cargo-insurance
discrepancy on a shipment of refrigerated pharmaceuticals arriving from Mumbai with a temperature
log showing a four-hour excursion is not a repeatable operation. Nor is a customs hold on a
consignment whose declared commodity code does not match the weight. Nor is a haulier arriving
ninety minutes late because of an accident on the A15, with a driver whose working-time hours run
out at four.

These are the cases that reach the operations floor, and the operations floor is a room full of
people on telephones.

Marieke de Vries, who has coordinated the import desk for nine years, puts the proportion at
somewhere between eight and twelve per cent. "Nine out of ten boxes, nobody thinks about," she says.
"They arrive, they get stacked, they leave. The tenth one is the job. And the tenth one is never the
same problem twice, which is exactly why there is still a desk."

The pattern repeats across the chain. The warehouse management system that allocates a pallet to a
location is automated; the decision about what to do with the pallet whose shrink wrap has failed is
not. The system that books a slot for a truck is automated; the conversation with the driver who has
missed it is not. Each piece of automation has, in effect, filtered out the ordinary cases and
concentrated the difficult ones into fewer, denser hours of human work.

That concentration is the part the productivity figures tend to miss. The terminal's throughput per
employee has roughly tripled since 2008. The share of each remaining employee's day that is spent on
non-routine problem-solving has risen at least as fast. The training that would support it has not. A coordinator hired in 2008 learned the difficult cases slowly, in between easy ones. A
coordinator hired this year meets nothing but difficult cases from the second week, and the person
who would have explained the first one is on a call about the third.

None of this is an argument against automating the terminal. The Maasvlakte handles volumes that
would be unmanageable by any other method, at an accident rate that has fallen by more than half.
It is an argument about where the remaining work went. A port that has successfully removed the
need for a thousand routine decisions has not noticed that it created a hundred difficult ones in
the same movement, and staffed them with the people it had left over.

### 1.5 · C1.2 Unit 5 · *Did they mean the opposite?* — main text (5A), opening extract
> **Band:** 800–1,000 words (extract below: 270) · mean sentence 20–24 · 2.5–3.1 clauses
> **Genre:** architectural criticism, unedited authentic register
> **Grammar in use:** irony, understatement, litotes

**A building of considerable confidence**

It would be unfair to say that the new cultural centre at Mapo-gu fails to make an impression. It
makes several, in rapid succession, none of them the one intended.

The first is scale, which the architects have described in their own literature as "generous". The
word is doing a great deal of work. Standing on the plaza at its northern edge, one has the
sensation, not uncommon in buildings of this kind, of having been reduced to a unit of measurement —
a figure placed in the rendering to establish that the roof is, in fact, very high. The second
impression is of material seriousness: board-formed concrete, a great deal of it, treated with the
reverence normally reserved for stone, and detailed with a precision that it would be churlish not
to admire. The third, arriving somewhat later and rather harder to shake, is that nobody has been
told what the building is for.

This is not quite a criticism of the architects, who were given a brief that asked for a library, a
performance space, a café, a "flexible community zone", and a civic presence appropriate to the
district's ambitions. They have delivered all five with a competence that is beyond question.
Whether five things delivered competently in one envelope add up to a building is a different
matter, and one the brief did not raise.

The library is the best of it. Shelved on three sides and lit from a clerestory that runs the full
length of the north elevation, it is the only room in the complex that appears to know what time of
day it is…

### 1.6 · C2.2 Unit 7 · *Can you hold a claim for 2,000 words?* — main text (5A), opening extract
> **Band:** 1,200–1,600 words (extract below: 263) · mean sentence 22–28 · 2.7–3.6 clauses
> **Genre:** long-form investigative essay, unedited

**The paperwork was in order**

Every document in the case was correct. That is the part that took longest to understand, and it is
the part that matters.

There is a familiar shape to stories of institutional failure, and it involves a missing file, a
forged signature, a instruction given verbally so that it would leave no trace. Investigators look
for the gap, because the gap is where the wrongdoing is assumed to live, and because a gap can be
photographed and put on a front page. In the eleven months I spent with the inspection records of
the Thornbury site, I did not find a gap. I found four hundred and six inspection reports, every one
of them signed, dated, countersigned and filed within the period required, describing a facility
that was at all material times in compliance with the standard against which it was being inspected.

The facility was also, throughout that period, discharging into the river.

Both of these things are true, and the relationship between them is the subject of this piece. It is
not a story about a cover-up, because nothing was covered up; the discharge figures were published
quarterly and were available to anyone who asked for them, which for six years nobody did. It is not
a story about a rogue operator, because the operator did, so far as I can establish, exactly what it
was told. It is a story about what happens when a standard is written in such a way that a facility
can satisfy every clause of it while producing the outcome the standard exists to prevent…

## 3 · Model writing — Part 8

Every model is annotated in the book for the writer's **choices**. The annotation callouts are shown
here in square brackets.

### 3.1 · A1.1 Unit 9 — *Directions to a place you know* (band 40–60 w)
> 45 words

> My aunt's house is easy. **[starts with the easy part — tells the reader it will be fine]** From
> the bus stop, walk to the big supermarket. Turn left. Walk past two cafés. Her street is the small
> one after the second café. The house is number 14. It is green. There is a bicycle outside.

### 3.2 · A2.2 Unit 3 — *A night something changed* (band 80–110 w)
> 88 words

> The shop closed in March, but I had known for a long time. **[past perfect in sentence one —
> fixes the order before the story starts]** My mother had told me in January that the owner was
> ill, and after that I noticed things. The bread came later. Some shelves were empty.
> On the last day I went in to buy milk and there was a sign on the door. I did not read it. I bought
> the milk and I said goodbye normally, because I did not want to make it a goodbye. **[the reason
> comes last — it is the point]**
> I still walk past it.

### 3.3 · B1.1 Unit 1 — *How a street changed* (band 120–150 w)
> 143 words — the model in `12-specimen-unit.md`

> The road I grew up on is called Station Road, and there has not been a station on it since 1968.
> **[opens in the present, then goes back — the reader needs the now first]**
> When we moved there in 2004 it was mostly houses, with a post office at one end and a garage at the
> other. The garage had already been there for forty years, and the man who ran it had learned the
> trade from his father. **[past perfect — the only one in the text, and it is doing real work]**
> Then the bypass opened. Traffic that had come through the village went around it instead, and
> within three years the post office had closed, the garage had closed, and a developer had bought
> both sites.
> There are eleven new houses now, and they are good houses. The people in them drive to the
> supermarket. Nobody walks up Station Road any more, because there is nothing on it to walk to.
> **[who gained and who lost — stated, not implied]**

### 3.4 · B2.2 Unit 3 — *A system and what it pushed elsewhere* (band 220–280 w)
> 254 words

> Our ticketing system was replaced eighteen months ago, and by every measure the department
> reports, it has been a success. Average first-response time has fallen from nine hours to
> forty minutes. The backlog, which used to be the first slide in every monthly review, no longer
> appears in the deck at all. **[the concession goes first, and it is a genuine one — not a
> rhetorical throat-clear]**
> What the measures do not capture is where the difficulty moved.
> The system resolves the routine cases automatically, which is what it was bought to do. A password
> reset, a licence reallocation, a request for access to a shared drive: these now close without a
> human reading them, and they used to be roughly seven of every ten tickets. The remaining three
> have not changed in number, but they are now the entirety of what a support officer sees.
> This has had two effects that nobody planned for. The first is that the job has become
> considerably harder without becoming more senior; the grade and the pay are what they were when
> the job was seven-tenths routine. **[the sentence that carries the argument — long, and the
> weight is at the end]** The second is that new staff no longer have the easy cases to learn on.
> A colleague who joined in 2019 spent two months answering simple tickets and absorbing, almost
> incidentally, how the estate was put together. A colleague who joined in March had none of that
> and was expected to be useful in a fortnight.
> I am not arguing that we should go back. I am arguing that we costed the system and did not cost
> the training it quietly made necessary.

### 3.5 · C1.2 Unit 10 — *An editorial judgement* (band 350–450 w), opening
> Extract: 204 words

> The paragraph you have asked me to cut is the one I would defend longest, and I want to set out
> why before I cut it anyway.
> Its function in the piece is not informational. Everything it states is stated more efficiently
> elsewhere, and a reader in a hurry loses nothing by skipping it. Its function is tonal: it is the
> only place where the narrator is permitted to be uncertain, and that uncertainty is what makes the
> confidence of the final section bearable. Remove it and the piece becomes an argument delivered by
> someone who was never in any doubt, which is both less true and, I think, less persuasive. Readers
> are considerably better at detecting unearned certainty than we generally allow for.
> Against that: the piece is nine hundred words over, the paragraph is the most cuttable thing in
> it on any purely structural reading, and I have been wrong about this exact instinct before — the
> Harrow piece was better for the cut and I said at the time that it would not be.
> So: cut it, but let me move two clauses of it into the opening, where the hesitation will cost
> less and do nearly as much…

## 4 · Model speaking answers

These are **transcribed learner-level performances**, not written prose. They include the
hesitation, self-repair and chunking that real speech at each level contains, because a model that
is too polished teaches learners to aim at the wrong thing. They appear in the Teacher's Edition and
on the platform as audio with a transcript; the Student's Book prints only the phrase bank.

### 4.1 · A1.1 Unit 9 — *Direct a visitor* (band 20–30 s)
> 43 words / 29 s

> "Okay. So… from the station you walk up. Up the hill. It's long! Then — the church is blue, pass
> the church. Then right. At the café. And then the yellow building. There are cats. Two cats. You
> see them."

### 4.2 · A2.2 Unit 3 — *Something that changed in your town* (band 60–75 s)
> 130 words / 70 s

> "I want to talk about the market in my town. Before, it was every day, and there were maybe
> thirty people selling — vegetables, fish, clothes, everything. My grandmother went every morning.
> Every morning, not sometimes.
> Then two things happened. A supermarket opened near the station — that was 2018 I think — and
> after that, in the same year, they changed the parking. You couldn't park near the market any
> more. So people with cars stopped coming.
> Now the market is Saturday only. There are maybe eight people. My grandmother still goes, and she
> complains, but she goes.
> I think the parking was the bigger problem. The supermarket, people still want the fresh things.
> But if you can't park, you don't come. That's… yes. That's what I think."

### 4.3 · B1.1 Unit 1 — *Tell the story of a street that changed* (band 60–90 s, the Outcome Task)
> 167 words / 81 s

> "Right, so the street I'm going to talk about is where my school was, and the thing you need to
> know first is that it's completely different now, so — I'll start before.
> When I was at school there were three shops on it. There was a bakery, there was a place that fixed
> phones, and there was a shop that sold, honestly, everything — batteries, school things, sweets.
> We went there every single day.
> What happened was, they built a new road. Before that, all the traffic came through our street,
> which was horrible, but it meant people stopped. After the new road, nobody came through. And the
> everything-shop closed first, then the phone place.
> The bakery's still there, which surprises everyone.
> So I suppose — the street is quieter and it's safer, and my mum says it's much better. But the
> people who had the shops, they didn't get anything from that. They just lost the customers. So it
> depends who you ask, really."

### 4.4 · B2.2 Unit 3 — *Chair a five-minute decision* (band 3 min), opening
> Extract: 152 words / 60 s

> "Okay — before we start I want to be clear about what we're deciding, because I think we've been
> having two arguments at once. We're not deciding whether the system was a good purchase. That's
> done, and honestly I think it was. We're deciding whether we resource the training gap it created,
> and if so from whose budget. So let's park the first one.
> Sanne, you've got the numbers, so can you give us the two-minute version — and specifically I want
> the figure for how long a new coordinator takes to get to independent now, versus before.
> And Joost, I know you disagree with the framing, so rather than cutting across I'd like you to
> come in straight after her with why. Properly, not in one line.
> Then we'll see where we are. If we're not close by twenty past, I'd rather take it away than force
> it."

## 5 · What these texts are doing that the corpus's are not

| | Corpus | English Animated |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose of the text | Carries the grammar target; sometimes *explains* it | Worth reading; the grammar is in it because the content needs it |
| Voice | One neutral narrator throughout | Different genre, register and voice at every level |
| Position | Resolved; the right answer is implied | Unresolved; B1.3 and above take a position the learner may reject |
| People | Named, with a job title | Named, with a stake and a contradiction |
| Register | Uniformly careful written English | Includes false starts, self-repair, overlapping speech, bureaucratic prose and irony |
| What the learner does with it | Comprehension | Comprehension, then: who wrote this, what did they leave out, do you believe them |

Note particularly the **B2.2 and C1.2 texts**. Neither tells the learner what to think, both contain
a concession the writer genuinely means, and the C1.2 text's central device — sustained
understatement — is a reading skill with no comprehension question that can test it. That is the
point at which a course either goes to C1 or stops pretending to.
