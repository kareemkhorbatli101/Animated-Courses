**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 9.12 · At, on and in: a point, a line and a space.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **at** | + a point | a spot | Wait at the junction. |
| **on** | + a line | a street | He lives on Alder Street. |
| **in** | + a space | a town | The market is in the square. |
| **along / towards** | + a path | movement | Walk along the lane. |
| **above / below** | + a thing | position | His flat is above the shop. |

**Remember:** *at* is for a point, *on* for a line, *in* for a space: *at the junction, on Alder Street, in the square*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *The health centre is behind of the square.* → ✓ *The health centre is behind the square.* (no *of* after *behind*). ✗ *Walk at the bridge.* → ✓ *Walk to the bridge.* (*to* for where you end up).

**Part 5: Why Some People Cannot Read a Map**

*Figure 9.23 · One walk, two ways of knowing it.*

Before you read: do you find your way by map or by landmark?

> **Gloss:** landmark = a thing you can see and remember · route = the line you walk from one place to another · survey = a picture of the whole place · facing = looking in that direction
>
> Two people walk the same way home every day for a year. Ask them to draw it. One draws a line with four turns, in the right order. The other draws the whole area, river included. Both of them get home. Only the second one finds a new way when the road is closed. The first kind of knowing is the route: the line you walk. The second is the survey: the picture of the whole place. Most of us stay with the first, because it works. A map helps only when the reader already knows which way they face. That is why a traveller can hold a map, turn it round twice, and walk the wrong way out of a station. The map is not the problem. The direction is.

**Part 5: How Three Cities Tell You Where You Are**

*Figure 9.25 · Three cities, three ways of saying where you are.*

Before you read: how does your own city tell you where you are?

> **Gloss:** grid = streets that cross each other in straight lines · avenue = a wide street with trees · police box = a very small police station on a corner
>
> Cities answer that very differently. In Tokyo most streets have no name. An address names the area, then the block, then the building, and the buildings run by age, not by position. So people find their way by landmarks, and there is a police box on many corners. In New York the streets run one way in numbers and the avenues run the other way. Two numbers fix a place exactly, and nobody there offers a landmark. In Venice almost nothing carries a useful number. Instead small yellow signs sit high on the walls, and every one points to the Rialto or to San Marco. They never find a stranger’s own street, but nobody stays lost for long.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Numbers on the Dutch Signs**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 9.35 · A sign with a place name, and a sign with a number.*

Before you read: can a sign tell you the way without naming a single place?

> **Gloss:** network = many paths joined together · pole = a tall thin post · stranger = somebody who does not know the place

> In the Netherlands the bike paths do not name where you are going. They give a number. At every junction of the network there is a small green and white sign on a pole. It carries a number of two figures, and the next numbers in each direction. A rider plans the day as a string of numbers: sixty-one, fifty-eight, forty-four, twelve. Then the ride goes from one to the next, and no place name comes into it at all. The idea came from old working paths in Belgium and moved north. The clever part is what it does to a stranger. A place name only helps a rider who already knows the map. A number helps anybody able to count. Children use it. Visitors use it. So do the local families, every week of the year.

1. What else is on the sign besides the number?

3. Why does a number help a stranger more than a place name?
