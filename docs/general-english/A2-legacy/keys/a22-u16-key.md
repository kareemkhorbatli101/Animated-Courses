**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 16.1)

The point is a negative one, and it is the reason this unit is hard to hear.

*-ed* is one sound: /d/ after a voiced sound, /t/ after an unvoiced one, /ɪd/ only after /t/ or /d/. The unit has *lived* /lɪvd/ and *changed* /tʃeɪndʒd/, both one sound. Nothing is added to the verb when the present perfect arrives. *I lived* and *I have lived* end identically.

So the tense is carried entirely by *have* / *has*, which is unstressed, early in the sentence, and often contracted. A learner listening at the end of the verb for the difference between the two tenses is listening in the wrong place, and will keep hearing nothing there, because there is nothing there. Say that out loud. It is the single most useful sentence in the unit.

The same was true of *can* in Unit 8 of volume one and *will* in Unit 12 of this one: English puts the grammar in the little word and then says the little word quietly.

**Part 2 · Grammar — Present Perfect and Past Simple**

**Part 2: Notice**

A finished time you can name: **Amina opened the shop eleven years ago** · **Tomas started at twenty-four** · **The market moved in the spring**
A line that reaches now: **She has not changed the shelves since** · **He has worked nights ever since** · **It has been quiet ever since**

The teaching point: this is not a choice about how long ago something was. It is a choice about whether the sentence names the time. *Eleven years ago* names it, so the verb is past simple, however recent it feels. *Since* does not name it, so the verb is present perfect, however old it is. Learners from languages that use the perfect for any past event will produce *I have opened the shop eleven years ago* for weeks. Correct it every time and explain it once.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. opened *(given)* · 1. has not changed · 2. started · 3. has worked · 4. moved

**Part 2: Finished Time or Up To Now?**

0. finished *(given)* · 1. up to now · 2. finished · 3. up to now · 4. finished

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. I have lived here **for** four years. *(*for* takes a length)*
2. She **opened** the shop in March. *(a named month takes the past simple)*
3. He **has worked** nights since he was twenty-four. *(a line reaching now)*
4. The market **moved** in the spring. *(a named season is a finished time)*

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One finished time, one that runs to now.

> Sample: *The baker on the corner shut in 2019. I have walked past the empty window about four thousand times since and I still look in.*

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: What Has Changed**

Marking points: ☐ one *has changed since…* ☐ one *there used to be…* ☐ one thing that has not changed ☐ no named date inside a present perfect.

> Sample: *Two things have changed since I arrived. There used to be a post office and it has been a phone shop for about three years. The bus stop has moved twenty metres, which sounds like nothing and has cost me two buses. Nothing about the park has changed at all, and I am glad, because I did not choose any of the rest of it.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *What was there before?* ☐ *How long has it been there?* ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **the market** (moved / still there) · **coffee shops** (four / one) · and the pair must find that **the shoe shop** and **the school** match. A pair that reports four has counted the school, which is the same on both cards; a pair that reports two has missed the number of coffee shops, because they asked *is there a coffee shop* and both answered yes.

> Sample exchange: *A: Has the market moved? — B: No, it is still there. — A: Mine moved. One. What about the shoe shop? — B: Closed. — A: Closed as well. The school? — B: The same. — A: The same. How many coffee shops? — B: One. — A: Four. Two. — B: That is only two. — A: The market and the coffee shops. Two.*

**Part 4: Describing a Change**

Marking points: ☐ A asks what was there before ☐ A asks again, further back ☐ B gives one finished date ☐ B gives one thing still running ☐ B does not say whether it is better.

Card A must produce: the *before* question, a second ask going further back, one more question, an opinion of their own at the end.
Card B must produce: the name of the thing, one finished date, one line that reaches now, and no verdict.

The commonest fault is B volunteering *and it is much worse now* in the second turn. The card forbids it on purpose: a factual account of a change is a separate skill from an opinion about it, and this unit teaches the first one. Card A gets the opinion, and only at the end.

***Stretch:*** the change was good for you and bad for them. Accept *The market moving was the best thing that has happened to my morning, and I know it took your Saturday away. I am not going to pretend otherwise.* The marker is naming both halves in one turn without taking either back.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ as it was when you arrived ☐ as it is now ☐ one finished time and one that runs to now.

> Sample (52 words): *When I arrived there were two food shops and a launderette. The launderette closed in my first winter. One of the food shops has been there the whole time and has not changed its window once. There are now three places to buy coffee and nowhere to buy a potato after six.*

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: A Street, Then and Now (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ one finished time ☐ one that runs to now ☐ one thing unchanged ☐ no named date inside a perfect ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (60 words): *The fish shop shut in 2014 and the window has been papered over ever since. There was a bench outside it until somebody took the bench, and nobody has ever found out who. The bus still stops in the same place and still comes every twelve minutes. Four things have opened in the fish shop and all four have closed.*

**Part 6: Was Life Better Before? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *No, and I would say that even on a bad day. Two things have got better that are hard to argue with: teeth and maps. One thing has got worse and it is the one everybody means: there is no longer any hour of the day when nobody can reach you, and that hour was useful.*

**Part 6: Something That Has Not Changed (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what has not changed ☐ how long ☐ why it has lasted ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (57 words): *My mother has had the same three cups since before I was born and has broken none of them. Everything else in that kitchen has been replaced twice. The cups have lasted because they are not good enough to be careful with, so nobody has ever put them anywhere safe, and safe is where things get broken.*

**Part 6: Reflection — A Photograph of Myself (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what the photograph shows ☐ how it has changed for you ☐ why ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (53 words): *There is a photograph of me on my first day at a job I have now left. I have looked at it twice. The first time I saw somebody pretending, and the second time I saw somebody about nineteen. Nothing moved in the photograph. I stopped being the person it was embarrassing for.*

**Part 7 · Real-World File — Talking About How Things Were**

**7A: Phrase Bank**

Item 3 is the one to teach. *It was better, and I would say that anyway* is the sentence the Part 5 reading is about, compressed into eleven words. A learner who can say it has learned something that is not grammar.

**7B: The Shop That Everybody Remembers Differently** (Track 16.5)

0. A bread shop. *(given)*
1. A tool shop.
2. Eleven years.
3. The end of a shop.

**7C: Controlled Practice — Describing a Change Fairly**

0. Say what is there now — **1** *(given)*
Say how long you have been watching — **4**
Say what was there before — **2**
Say what you think, and that you would think it anyway — **5**
Say when it changed — **3**

> Step 4 comes before step 5 for a reason worth saying out loud: how long you have been watching is what decides how much your opinion is worth, so it goes in before the opinion and not after it as a defence.

**7D: Role-Play — Two People, One Street**

Marking points: ☐ A says what they remember ☐ A says how long they have been there ☐ A agrees out loud ☐ B corrects the fact and not the person ☐ B gives the longer stretch ☐ B does not win.

> Sample: *A: It was a launderette. — B: It was a launderette for about eighteen months. — A: I have been here six years. — B: I have been here twenty-nine. Before the launderette it was a bank for forty years. — A: A bank. — B: Everybody gets the last one. — A: I have been telling people launderette for six years. — B: So had I, about the bank, until somebody showed me a photograph.*

***Stretch:*** you have been there longest and you are the one who is wrong. Accept *Hold on. I am doing the thing I accuse everybody else of. You are right and I have been wrong about this for years, out loud, to people.* The marker is noticing it mid-conversation and not at the end, and not apologising twice.

**7E: Writing — A Note for Somebody Who Has Been Away (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ two things that have changed ☐ one finished date ☐ one thing that has not changed ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (74 words): *Three things, so you are not surprised. The post office closed in 2021 and the nearest one is now by the station. The big tree on the green came down in a storm last February and they have not planted anything in the gap. Your bus is the same number, the same stop and, as far as I can tell, the same driver. The flat has had four tenants and none of them stayed.*

**Part 8 · Global Story — The City That Was Rebuilt From Paintings**

0. Several hundred years old. *(given)*
1. Photographs, drawings and a set of paintings.
2. Because the city paid him by the detail.
3. They went back to the painting, and not to the memory of anybody living.

> Item 2 is the detail that makes the story work and it is easy to read past. The paintings are accurate not because anybody was thinking about a war a century and a half away, but because a man was paid per window. The accuracy was a side effect of a pay arrangement.

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because the old market hall was put back after a fire and most people do not know it is new. This is different from where I live because nobody here had a painting to work from, so they guessed, and the guesses are visible if you look at the windows. I think the most important thing is that a copy everybody agrees to stop calling a copy becomes the real thing after about two generations, which is uncomfortable and is probably true.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Shop That Was Four Shops**

0. Four. *(given)*
1. Because it was a tool shop for twenty of the thirty years, and he bought a hinge there in his first week.
2. Because it was a bread shop for the two years before she opened her own shop, and she went in every morning.
3. She asked all five of the others.

> Every one of them is right about the two years they were watching and wrong about the thirty. That is the Part 5 reading again, in a street instead of a photograph, and it is worth saying so.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) write down what it was** — the only one that survives you, and the only defence against the thing this unit keeps showing: that in ten years five people will remember five different places. It also takes an afternoon and nobody will read it for twenty years.
- **(b) say nothing and let it go** — places change and a person who records every change is making a museum of their own street. It is also the choice that guarantees the Mr Okonkwo argument happens, and he is the only one in the building who can settle it.
- **(c) tell everybody who will listen** — the only option that might actually stop the change, because enough noise occasionally does. It is also the one that costs other people something, and most of them have not asked to mind.

> Sample (63 words): *I think I should write down what it was, because I have watched it for eleven years and I am the only person who has. Also, I have already been wrong once about what was here before. This is better than telling everybody, because I have been the person who tells everybody, and it changed nothing except how often I was invited out.*
