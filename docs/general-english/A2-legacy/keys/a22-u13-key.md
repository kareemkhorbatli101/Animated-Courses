**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 13.1)

The sound work here is short; the meaning work behind it is the most useful ten minutes in the unit.

*Must* keeps a soft **t** before a consonant. *Mustn’t* loses the middle **t** entirely: /ˈmʌsn̩t/, not /ˈmʌstnt/.
*Have to* is /ˈhæftə/ in ordinary speech, with an **f**, not a **v**. Learners who have only seen it written say /hæv tuː/ and are understood, and sound careful.

The point that matters more than the sound: *mustn’t* and *don’t have to* are opposites. One forbids, one frees. Most learners who confuse them were never told; they assumed the negative of *must* is *don’t have to*, which is a reasonable guess and wrong. Say it aloud, write both on the board, and come back to it in Part 2.

**Part 1: Four Places, Four Rules**

|---|---|---|

The night-bus row is the one to draw out: it is the clearest example in the unit of a rule with no notice, no guard and no fine that is kept almost perfectly.

**Part 2 · Grammar — Must, Have To and Mustn’t**

**Part 2: Notice**

Necessary: **You must show your card** · **You have to be a member**
Forbidden: **You mustn’t touch the paintings** · **You mustn’t smoke anywhere inside**
Not necessary: **You don’t have to pay a fee** · **You don’t have to be quiet in the garden**

The teaching point is the middle column against the right one. Learners read *mustn’t* and *don’t have to* as near neighbours because both contain a negative. They are at opposite ends: one closes a door, the other opens it.

The second point, worth a minute: *must* often sounds like the rule speaking and *have to* like a person reporting it. *You must show your card* is the notice. *You have to be a member* is the person at the desk, who did not make the rule and says so with their voice.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. must *(given)* · 1. mustn’t · 2. have to · 3. don’t have to · 4. mustn’t

**Part 2: Necessary, Forbidden or Not Necessary?**

0. necessary *(given)* · 1. forbidden · 2. not necessary · 3. necessary · 4. not necessary

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. You **mustn’t** touch the paintings. *(*must* makes its own negative)*
2. You **don’t have to** pay, the gallery is free. *(*mustn’t pay* would forbid paying)*
3. You have to **show** your card at the desk. *(plain verb after* have to*)*
4. You must **be** a member. *(no* to *after* must*)*

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

> Sample: *You mustn’t take a bicycle through the hall, because the paint is new and the handlebars are exactly the height of it. You don’t have to sign anything to use the room; just put the light out.*

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: Rules Where You Live**

Marking points: ☐ one *mustn’t* ☐ one *have to* ☐ one *don’t have to* ☐ a reason in the third answer.

> Sample: *You mustn’t put anything in the hall, not even for an hour, because of the pushchairs. People think you have to be in by eleven and that has never been a rule; the door is just heavy. The rule I think is wrong is that children can’t use the lift alone, because the stairs are worse.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *Do I have to…?* questions ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **the cap** (have to / don’t have to) · **bags** (allowed in / go in a locker) · **the fee** (none / two pounds). **Showering first is the same** at both pools. Pairs who report four have guessed; the shower row is the trap.

> Sample exchange: *A: Do I have to shower first? — B: Yes. — A: Same. A cap? — B: No. — A: We have to. One. Can I take a bag in? — B: It goes in a locker. — A: Ours come in. Two. Is there a fee? — B: Two pounds. — A: Ours is free. Three.*

**Part 4: Asking What the Rules Are**

Marking points: ☐ A asks one thing at a time ☐ A asks about the fee ☐ A checks what is forbidden ☐ B separates necessary from free ☐ B names the thing people forget.

Card A must produce: single questions, the money question, a check, thanks.
Card B must produce: the necessary, the free, the forgotten one, no argument.

The commonest fault is B defending the rule. The card says do not explain it, and that is deliberate: somebody on a desk did not write it, and learners need to hear what it sounds like when a person passes on a rule they did not make.

***Stretch:*** the rule makes no sense and the person telling you did not make it. Accept *I’ll put it in the locker, of course. It does seem a strange rule, and I know that is not yours to answer for.* The marker is compliance plus an opinion that does not land on the person in front of you.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ three rules ☐ at least one *mustn’t* and one *don’t have to* ☐ one rule explained, not only stated.

> Sample (56 words): *At the pool you must shower before you go in, which everybody does. You mustn’t swim in the fast lane unless you really are fast, and that one is broken every Saturday by one man. You don’t have to wear a cap, although people think you do, and so about half the swimmers wear one anyway.*

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: Rules for a Shared Space (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ one *must* ☐ one *mustn’t* with a reason ☐ one *don’t have to* ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (54 words): *You must take your shoes off at the door, because the floor is old and we have all agreed to care about it. You mustn’t use the big table after ten at night; the room below is a bedroom. You don’t have to ask before you make tea, and you never will have to.*

**Part 6: Is a Rule Better Than a Sign? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (53 words): *Do not write it down. A sign turns a shared place into somebody’s property, and the moment one goes up, three more follow. People learn a rule faster from a person than from a wall. The real reason anybody wants a sign is so they never have to say the thing out loud.*

**Part 6: A Rule You Had to Learn (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what you did ☐ what happened ☐ what you do now ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (54 words): *I took a seat on a train with a small card on the back of it, in a language I could not read. A woman arrived twenty minutes later holding a ticket and looking at me the way you look at furniture in the wrong room. Nobody was unkind. I read the cards now.*

**Part 6: Reflection — A Rule I Would Change (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ which rule ☐ why ☐ what you would put in its place ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (46 words): *I would change the rule that you must not eat on the underground. It exists because of smell and mess, which is fair. I would allow anything cold and dry, and ban anything hot, which is the real problem and is never what the notice says.*

**7C: Controlled Practice — Explaining a Rule Well**

0. Say the rule in one sentence — **1** *(given)*
Say what happens if nobody keeps it — **3**

Say who it does not apply to — **4**
Ask whether that makes sense — **5**

**7D: Role-Play — Asking Why the Rule Exists**

Marking points: ☐ A asks why, not whether ☐ A says they were not told ☐ B gives the reason in one sentence ☐ B admits what they do not know ☐ B does not defend the system.

> Sample: *A: Why must the bikes go in the yard? — B: The hall is the fire way out. — A: Nobody said. — B: Nobody ever does. — A: Then how is anybody meant to know? — B: They are not, and I have never once seen that fixed.*

***Stretch:*** the rule has no reason left and you both know it. Accept *Nobody can remember why, and we both think it is nothing. Shall we keep it anyway for a year and see if anybody misses it?* The marker is the two of them deciding, rather than one of them being right.

**7E: Writing — A Notice for a Shared Door (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ the rule in the first sentence ☐ the reason ☐ one thing the reader does not have to do ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (62 words): *Please do not prop this door open, even for two minutes. It is the fire door for the whole of this floor and it only works shut. You don’t have to carry everything in one trip; press the green button inside and it holds for thirty seconds. If it is sticking again, tell flat 2 and it gets fixed the same week.*

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because the same law came in two years later and the same people said the same things. This is different from where I live because ours is kept in the cities and ignored completely in small towns, so it is really two laws. I think the most important thing is that it came in everywhere on one day, because a rule with exceptions is an argument and a rule without them is just the weather.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Rules Nobody Wrote Down**

0. About eleven. *(given)*
1. Because of his nights.
2. By getting every one of them wrong first, then hearing about it kindly from whoever was standing there.
3. That she does not need them.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) write them all on a notice** — the only one that reaches somebody who moves in while everybody is out. It also makes a home look like a waiting room, and a list of eleven rules reads as a complaint about the last tenant.
- **(b) tell each new person yourself** — kind, and it is a way of meeting them. It depends entirely on one person remembering, and it fails the week that person is away.
- **(c) leave it as it is** — what every building in the world actually does, and it works for everybody except the newest person, who is the only one it matters for.

> Sample (57 words): *I think I should write them on a notice, because the person who needs them most is the one who has nobody to ask yet. Also, a list can be short and friendly. This is better than telling each person myself, because I am not always here and the rules do not stop when I am away.*
