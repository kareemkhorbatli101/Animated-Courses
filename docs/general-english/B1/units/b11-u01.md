**Part 2 · Grammar — Past Continuous vs Past Simple**

***[CORE] Core track · pre-class input***

**Part 2: Notice**

*Figure 1.11 · Lines from the notice, with the target form ringed in each.*

> **Read the two sentences. What is the difference between them?**
>
> The lorry **left** the skip on the pavement, and then the van **arrived**.
>
> The van **was arriving** when the lorry **left** the skip on the pavement.
>
> In the first sentence the two events happen one after the other. The lorry put the skip down, and then the van arrived, so the driver could still turn round at the corner. In the second sentence one event happens inside the other. The van was already coming down the street when the skip went down in front of it, so the driver could not turn round. The words are the same in both sentences, and the time is the same. The only difference is whether the action was finished or still going on, and in English you show that difference in the verb.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 1.12 · Finished, and still going on: two pasts, two jobs.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **past simple** | verb + -ed, or irregular | the thing that happened, and finished | The card reader stopped at 2.10. |
| **past continuous** | was/were + -ing | the longer action, in the middle of happening | She was serving a queue. |
| **when** | + past simple | introduces the short, finished action | I was crossing town when it died. |
| **while** | + past continuous | introduces the longer background action | While I was crossing town, it died. |
| **negative** | was/were not + -ing | the background that was not happening | He was not watching the car park. |

**Remember:** some verbs are almost never used in the continuous, however long the action lasted — *know*, *believe*, *understand*, *belong*, *own*, *need*, *want*, *hear*, *see*, *like*, *hate*. *I was knowing the answer* is not English, and the past simple is what you want instead.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *While the queue was waiting, the card reader were stopping.* → ✓ *…the card reader stopped.* (the thing that interrupts is past simple). ✗ *I was understanding the problem.* → ✓ *I understood the problem.*

**Part 5: Why Two Things Always Happen at Once**

*Figure 1.23 · One Saturday afternoon, hour by hour, across a single street.*

> **Before you read:** think about which hour of your week contains the largest number of other people’s arrangements.
>
> **Gloss:** *overlap* — the part of two periods of time that covers the same hours · *capacity* — the largest amount a service or a place can take · *slot* — a fixed period of time booked for one job · *backlog* — work that has built up because it was not done when it should have been

> Saturday afternoon is the busiest four hours of the week on an ordinary street. Deliveries, viewings, repairs and family arrangements all happen between one o’clock and five. They overlap, and the overlap is not bad luck. This text gives three reasons why one Saturday afternoon carries so much at once.
>
> The first reason is that delivery companies sell the same hours to everybody. A company works to a fixed capacity and can make only so many drops in a day. Most people are at home on a Saturday to take a parcel in, so a company sells more slots on a Saturday than on any other day. Each slot is two hours wide. A street of eleven households can take nine deliveries between one and five, and two of those vans will need the same six metres of kerb. This is why the companies put all of their Saturday work on the same few hours.
>
> The second reason is that the services behind those companies run a smaller shift at the weekend. Banks, offices and help lines all run a small Saturday shift. A card reader that stops at ten past two must wait for the same engineer as every other card reader in the city. There is also a backlog of work left over from Friday evening. So a fault that takes forty minutes to repair on a Tuesday can take a whole Saturday afternoon, because the shift is smaller.
>
> The third reason is that everybody is at home at the same hour. A building is almost empty between nine and five on a Thursday. At three o’clock on a Saturday it holds all of its people, their visitors, their children and their cars. So a problem at that hour happens in front of more people than the same problem on a Thursday morning. It is also understood by fewer of them, because each person sees only the two or three minutes they happen to be looking at. Everybody is at home, and everybody sees a different part of the same hour.

**Part 5: Three Streets, Three Saturdays**

*Figure 1.25 · Three streets, and how much of each afternoon went to waiting.*

> **Before you read:** the same four hours look entirely different depending on which street you happen to live on.
>
> **Gloss:** *peak* — the hour in which demand is at its highest · *dispute* — an argument between two sides that has not been settled · *kerb* — the edge of the pavement, where a vehicle can pull in

> **Alder Street has one lane.** There is a kerb on one side and a line of parked cars on the other, so one vehicle that stops stops everything behind it. The skip arrived at half past one, the peak hour for deliveries. The stock van reached the corner eleven minutes later and could not get past. The driver waited six minutes, rang the depot and took the whole load back. Nothing on Alder Street was broken that day, and one shop lost a day’s trade because the street has one lane.
>
> **Pell Road has a loading bay at each end.** The road was rebuilt four years ago, two miles from Alder Street. But the same thing happened on Pell Road on the same afternoon: a skip, a delivery and an argument about the booking. It cost Pell Road nine minutes, because the van pulled into the loading bay and the lane stayed open. Nobody on Pell Road remembers that afternoon, and the reason is the loading bay.
>
> **Carrow Street has a list of telephone numbers.** The list covers all eighteen households, and it is three years old and still correct. So when a connection failed in one house on Saturday, somebody rang the house next door within the minute. For example, that is how Carrow Street learned that the fault was in the exchange and not in anybody’s own flat. Carrow Street lost the afternoon as well, but the list is the reason it knew why.

*Figure 1.26 · The shape of the message you are about to write.*

**Part 6: Reflection — The Afternoon I Lost (80–100 words)**

**Model — read this first:**

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Day Our Street Became a Film Set**

*Figure 1.35 · The street at eight in the morning, and the same street at noon.*

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

> **Before you read:** a film company closes a residential street for a day. Think about which of your own arrangements would survive that, and which would not.
>
> **Gloss:** *location* — a real place used for filming instead of a studio · *permit* — written permission from an authority to do something · *diversion* — a route that traffic is sent along when a road is closed · *compensation* — money paid to somebody for trouble or loss they did not cause

> The letter came on the Thursday evening, and most people on Calle Sarmiento in Rosario, Argentina, read it some time on Friday. It said that the city had chosen the street as a location, and that the permit covered the Saturday. It ended by asking residents for their understanding. It named the street and it named the day. It did not name the hours, and that was the problem.
>
> Julia Benítez was putting the letter on the fridge when her sister rang about the Saturday. Julia expected no trouble at all, because the word location made her think of one camera. She read the word location and pictured one camera at the end of the road. At six the next morning two lorries were parking across the entrance. A young man with a list was explaining, very politely, that nobody could drive in or out until the evening.
>
> The next morning the diversion affected two kinds of people in two very different ways. First, the people who had planned nothing had a remarkable day. They sat on their steps and watched the same scene eleven times, and a production company with orders to keep everybody friendly brought them coffee. The people who planned something had a different day. A man two doors up was starting a night shift and could not get his car out at five. A woman on the corner had a hospital appointment at eleven in a district forty minutes away.
>
> But the production company had thought about this in advance. There was a telephone number on the letter, and somebody did answer it. The young man with the list walked the woman to a taxi at the far end, and she reached the clinic on time. The production company offered the man on the night shift compensation for a taxi, and he took it. He still says the money was not what mattered to him. He had to ask a stranger for permission to leave his own street.
>
> By Monday the lorries were gone and the street looked exactly as it always did. Several residents wrote to the city about the permit, and none of them asked for the filming to be stopped. What they asked for was a line on the letter saying when. The city changed the form eight months later, and the new one has three boxes on it: the street, the date and the hours. Julia keeps the old letter on the fridge, underneath the new one.

> **Answer the questions. (0. is done for you.)**

0. What did the letter fail to say? — **The hours the closure would cover.**

1. Why did Julia think the closure would not affect her?

2. What two things did the production company do for people who had arrangements?

3. What change did the residents actually ask the city for?

**Part 9 · Close to Home — Three Weeks Later at Number 14**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / decide***

*Figure 1.37 · The close-to-home reading, drawn along one street.*

> **Before you read:** one cracked window, two hundred and ten pounds, and six people who were all somewhere else.

> The residents’ meeting at 14 Alder Street ran for an hour and a half. About fifteen minutes of it were about the window. The building fund holds two hundred and ten pounds, and the glass on the half landing will cost a hundred and forty. The rules of the building put communal glass on the residents, so there is nobody to send the bill to.
>
> Mr Okonkwo thinks the fund should pay and the matter end there. He was in the car park for three hours that afternoon looking for his cat, and he is the only person who was. His view is that a building which spends an evening arguing about a hundred and forty pounds will spend the next one arguing about eighty.
>
> Another view came from Dani. He wants the fund to pay, and a note in the car park asking people to play at the far end. He was out there twice and saw nothing, and he says that is why a note is better than a rule. A note asks people to be reasonable. A rule tries to catch somebody.
>
> Amina does not want to spend the fund at all. She lost a day’s trade to the skip that afternoon. Her view is that the building keeps paying for damage and never for the thing that prevents it. She would rather put the money towards a bollard that keeps the kerb clear.
>
> Yuki missed the afternoon and the first half of the meeting. She asked the one question all five people who were there had missed. She wanted to know whether the crack was there before Saturday. Three people could not swear that it was not.

> **Answer the questions. (0. is done for you.)**

0. What do the rules of the building say about communal glass? — **That the residents pay for it.**

1. Why does Mr Okonkwo want the matter to end quickly?

2. What does Amina want to spend the money on instead?

3. What did Yuki ask, and how did people answer?
