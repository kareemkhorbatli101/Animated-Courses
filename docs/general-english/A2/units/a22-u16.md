**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 16.12 · A finished point, and a line that reaches now.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **past simple** | opened, started, moved | a finished time you can name | She opened the shop eleven years ago. |
| **present perfect** | has opened, has started | from then until now | She has not changed it since. |
| **since** | since + a point | where the line starts | He has worked nights since March. |
| **for** | for + a length | how long the line is | He has worked nights for four years. |

**Remember:** *since* takes a point, *for* takes a length: *since March*, *for four years*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *I have lived here since four years.* → ✓ *I have lived here for four years.* (*for* takes a length). ✗ *She has opened the shop in March.* → ✓ *She opened the shop in March.* (a finished month takes the past simple).

**Part 5: Why Nothing Feels as Old as the Recent Past**

*Figure 16.23 · One street, in four pictures.*

Before you read: which photograph of your own life looks oldest to you?

> **Gloss:** fashion = what most people wear or like at one time · dated = looking old in a way nobody planned · taste = what a person finds good
>
> A photograph from a hundred years ago looks like history. There is a gas lamp in it, and nobody argues with the lamp. A photograph from twenty years ago looks wrong. The same goes for a ten-year-old computer. This is not about the clothes, or not only. Anything old enough stops arguing with the present and becomes a picture. Anything recent is still close enough to judge against now, and it loses. Anyone who designs things knows this already. A thing looks dated for about thirty years, and then it stops and becomes a period. The same happens to taste, to buildings and to how a street talks about an old shop. Nothing anybody says about a change is honest until the change is older than the person complaining.

**Part 5: Three Things That Came Back**

*Figure 16.25 · Three things people threw away and then wanted again.*

Before you read: has anything you threw away come back into fashion?

> **Gloss:** vinyl = the material old records are made of · tram = a bus on rails in a street · bicycle lane = a part of the road kept for bikes
>
> Some things go away and then return, and the return is never for the first reason. Records went away when the CD arrived. They came back thirty years later, and not because they sound better. They came back because they are slow, and because you have to choose. Trams left the British cities, and several are putting the rails down again. **Part 8 · Global Story — The City That Was Rebuilt From Paintings**

> The corner at the end of Alder Street has been four things in thirty years and everybody at number 14 names a different one. Mr Okonkwo says tools, because it was tools for twenty of those years and he bought a hinge there in his first week. Amina says bread shop. It was a bread shop for the two years before she opened her own shop, and she went in every morning. Maya says it has always been the phone shop. Dani has never thought about it at all. Yuki has been here least of all. She asked all five. She now knows more about that corner than anybody except Mr Okonkwo, and he still has the hinge.
