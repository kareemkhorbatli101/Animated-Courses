**🔊 Audio Track 12.1**

> **Listen to *will* and *won’t*. Say each pair aloud.**
>
> It will rain — in speech *will* drops to one sound: *it’ll rain*
>

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 12.12 · Sure and possible: two words, two jobs.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **will** | will + plain verb | sure about the future | It will rain this afternoon. |
| **won’t** | will not + plain verb | sure it does not happen | It won’t be warm until April. |
| **might** | might + plain verb | possible, not sure | It might snow on Thursday. |
| **will** (deciding) | the same form | a decision made as you speak | Fine — I will take a coat. |

**Remember:** *will* and *might* never take *to*: *it might rain*, not *it might to rain*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *It will to rain.* → ✓ *It will rain.* (no *to* after *will*). ✗ *Maybe it will perhaps rain.* → ✓ *It might rain.* (one word for possible is enough).

**Part 5: Why Nobody Believes the Forecast**

*Figure 12.23 · One forecast, from the measurement to the morning.*

Before you read: do you believe the forecast where you live?

> **Gloss:** forecast = what somebody says the weather will do · percentage = how many out of a hundred · wrong = not right
>
> A forecast that says *sixty in a hundred for rain* is right far more often than people think. People still call it no use at all. The reason is simple and it is about us. Sixty in a hundred means that on six mornings out of ten like this one, it rains. Today may be one of the four dry ones, and the forecast was still not wrong. It never promised anything. But a person does not remember six wet mornings. A person remembers the one Saturday they called off, and the sun that came out at eleven. Forecasters know this, and the good ones say *probably* and *might* as often as the language allows. The audience then says they never give a straight answer, which is the first complaint again.

**Part 5: Three Places and Their Weather**

*Figure 12.25 · Three places, and the one thing the weather does to each.*

>
> Weather builds houses, and not only in the sunny half of the world. In the south of Spain the windows are small and the shutters are thick, because for four months the sun is what you keep out. **Part 8 · Global Story — The Day the Rain Is Due**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 12.35 · A year of dust, and the week the rain arrives.*

Before you read: can a whole country wait for one day of weather?

> **Gloss:** monsoon = the season of heavy rain that comes once a year · harvest = the crops people cut and bring in · dam = a wall that holds water back

> In India the rain has a date. The monsoon arrives on the south coast around the first of June and moves north over six weeks. The day it reaches each place goes round the whole country. People stand outside for it. Parents send the children out into it. The radio carries the day it reaches each city, the way it carries a result. The whole year of work turns on whether it is early, late or thin. A late monsoon means a small harvest, and a small harvest means a hard winter in villages a thousand miles from any newspaper. Nothing in Europe compares. The nearest thing is a bad winter, and a bad winter only costs you a week.
