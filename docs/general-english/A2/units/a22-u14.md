**Part 1: Pronunciation**

**🔊 Audio Track 14.1**

> **Listen to *should* and *shouldn’t*. Say each pair aloud.**
>
> You should rest — the **l** says nothing: /ʃʊd/, never /ʃʊld/
>
> You shouldn’t wait — the **l** says nothing here either: /ˈʃʊdn̩t/

>

>

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 14.12 · Advice, advice against, and asking for it.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **should** | should + plain verb | advice for doing it | You should drink more water. |
| **shouldn’t** | should not + plain verb | advice against doing it | You shouldn’t go to work. |
| **Should I…?** | a question | asking for advice | Should I see a doctor? |
| **I should / I shouldn’t** | about yourself | advice you give yourself | I shouldn’t have another coffee. |

**Remember:** *should* never takes *to*: *you should rest*, not *you should to rest*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *You should to rest.* → ✓ *You should rest.* (no *to* after *should*). ✗ *You don’t should go.* → ✓ *You shouldn’t go.* (*should* makes its own negative).

**Part 5: Why We Ask the Wrong People**

*Figure 14.23 · One small worry, from Tuesday to Friday.*

Before you read: who do you ask first, and is that the right person?

> **Gloss:** symptom = a sign that something is wrong with your body · harmless = not able to hurt you · reassure = to say something that stops a person worrying
>
> Almost nobody asks a doctor first. They ask a friend, a sister, a shop, and most of all they ask a screen, usually at night and usually alone. The screen is very good at listing everything a symptom could mean. It gives the terrible things the same calm face as the harmless ones. Somebody already a little afraid gets to the end of that list no wiser at all. They only feel worse. What a friend does, and a screen cannot, is reassure badly and ask one good question: how long is it now? That question sorts almost everything. It is the first thing the chemist asks and the second thing the doctor asks, and the list never asks it at all.

**Part 5: Three Things People Get Right Without Knowing Why**

*Figure 14.25 · Three old habits, and what each one turns out to do.*

Before you read: which old remedy in your family actually works?

> **Gloss:** remedy = an old answer to a small illness · steam = hot water turning into air · honey = the sweet thing bees make · cough = the sharp noise a bad chest makes
>
> Some old remedies survive because they work, and not for the reason anybody gave. Chicken soup was said to feed the blood. What it really does is get warm water and salt into somebody who stopped drinking, and that is most of the problem with a cold. Steam over a bowl was said to drive the illness out. It does nothing to the illness and a great deal to a blocked nose for twenty minutes, which is enough. Honey for a cough is the strangest of the three, because it beats most of what you can buy. The reason seems to be that it coats a throat that hurts. It also tastes good enough that a child will take it. Three remedies, three wrong reasons, three results.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Country That Asks First**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 14.35 · A waiting room, and a telephone line.*

Before you read: should the first person you see about your health be a doctor?

> **Gloss:** triage = sorting people by how urgent they are · pharmacist = the person who knows what is in every box · minor = small, and not dangerous

> In New Zealand a great deal of the first conversation happens before anybody sees a doctor. A nurse on a telephone line takes the call, asks how long and how bad, and sends most people somewhere other than a waiting room. A pharmacist handles a long list of minor things without an appointment at all. The idea behind it is simple. The hardest part of medicine is not treating people, it is sorting them. Sorting does not need the most expensive person in the building. It upsets people at first. Nobody enjoys hearing that their problem is small, even when it is. A year in, the same people are the ones who argue hardest for it.

> It went through number 14 in nine days and it took them in order, from the top down. Yuki first, then Maya, then Dani, and then Amina, who shut the shop for the only two days she has shut it in eleven years. Tomas did not get it at all, which he said was the hospital and everybody else said was simply unfair. Mr Okonkwo did not get it either, and at seventy-one he was the one carrying bread up four flights to people half his age. On the ninth day Amina opened again and found five people waiting, every one of them buying soup. Nobody said one word about it, which at number 14 is how a thing gets marked and remembered.
