> She asked, “Where is it?” — the voice goes up at the end

> She asked where it was — the voice comes down, because it is no longer a question

***[CORE] Core track · pre-class input***

**Part 2: Notice**

*Figure 20.11 · Lines from the notice, with the target form ringed in each.*

> **Read these pairs. What changes in the reported half?**

> “The van is outside.” → She said the van was outside. “I have not seen it.” → He said he had not seen it. “Where is the key?” → She asked where the key was.
>
> Three things move: the tense goes back a step, the person changes, and a question loses its order.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 20.12 · One step back: what each tense becomes.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **present → past** | was, were | for words said in the present | She said the van was outside. |
| **past → past perfect** | had seen, had been | for words said in the past | He said he had not seen it. |
| **will → would** | would ring | for a promise or a plan | She said she would ring. |
| **question** | where it was | no mark, and no question order | She asked where it was. |

**Remember:** *said* takes no person after it; *told* must have one. *She said the van was outside.* *She told me the van was outside.*

> **Watch out!** ✗ *She said me the van was outside.* → ✓ *She told me the van was outside.* (*said* takes no person). ✗ *She asked where was the key.* → ✓ *She asked where the key was.* (a reported question is not a question).

**Part 5: Why a Story Gets Shorter Every Time It Is Told**

*Figure 20.23 · One sentence, passed along five people.*

Before you read: what happens to a story on the fourth telling?

> **Gloss:** version = one person’s account of what happened · detail = one small part of something · witness = a person who saw it happen · certainty = being sure, with no doubt left
>
> Pass a story along five people and it comes out shorter and surer. This has been tested many times and always goes the same way. The details that go first are the ones nobody can use: the colour, the hour, the name of the second person. What survives is the shape — somebody did something and somebody minded. And what grows is certainty. The first witness says he thinks it was about twenty minutes. The last person says it was twenty minutes. Nobody lied anywhere along that line. Each of them simply dropped the part of the sentence doing the least work. *I think* is always that part.

**Part 5: Three Ways News Used to Travel**

*Figure 20.25 · Three old ways of carrying news, and how fast each one was.*

Before you read: how long should news take?

> **Gloss:** messenger = a person whose work is carrying messages · signal = a sound or light that carries a short message a long way · telegraph = an old machine that sent words down a wire
>
> Before anybody had a telephone, news moved at the speed of the fastest thing going that way. A messenger on a horse managed about a hundred and sixty kilometres a day. Nothing went faster for three thousand years. Then came signals: towers on hilltops, with arms that could be read from the next hill. They carried a short sentence across France in two hours. They could not work at night, or in fog. Then the telegraph, the real break: it was the first time a message could move without anything moving. What it cost was the detail. A telegraph message was paid for by the word, so people learned to say things in nine words. A hundred years of news reads strangely short.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Street That Reads the News Out Loud**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 20.35 · A loudspeaker on a pole and a street that has stopped hearing it.*

Before you read: would you like the news read to your street at six?

> **Gloss:** loudspeaker = a machine that makes a voice loud enough for a street · ward = one small part of a city, with its own office · announcement = a thing said out loud to everybody at once

> In Hanoi, in Vietnam, most wards still have loudspeakers up on posts. In many of them a voice reads the local news at about six in the morning. It is the oldest working news system in the city and has never been turned off. People will tell you it gave out the end of a war and a water cut in the same week, in the same voice. Younger people mostly say they do not hear it any more. That is not quite the same as saying it is not heard. A survey asked people what the voice had said that morning, and a third of them could say. The city has twice discussed turning the speakers off and twice decided not to. Before you read: would you write down what your neighbours said?

> Yuki has a notebook and six of the pages have names on them. She started it in her first month, because she could not keep the names and faces together. She has never stopped. It now holds what each of them said about the van, with the day beside it. Amina knows about the notebook and says so, loudly and often. Maya knows and does not like it and has not said so. **Part 9: Decision Task**

*Figure 20.38 · Three ways to decide, and what each one costs.*

> **You keep notes about what people near you said. One of them has found out and does not like it. Should you (a) stop, (b) show them their page, or (c) keep it and say nothing? Decide and explain. Use the frame.**
>
> Answer frame: “I think I should ___ because ___. Also, ___. This is better than ___ because ___.”

**Model — read this first:**

> I think I should show her the page. What she is imagining is worse than what is written, and I can end that in a minute. Also, if the page cannot be shown then she is right and I should stop. This is better than stopping quietly, because stopping without showing her leaves her believing I wrote something I could not show. (62 words)
