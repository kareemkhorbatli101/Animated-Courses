**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 10.12 · An order, a sequence and a manner: three jobs, three places in the sentence.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **imperative** | the verb alone, no subject | telling somebody to do it | Mix it well. |
| **negative** | *do not* + the verb alone | telling them not to | Do not press too hard. |
| **order words** | first, then, after that, finally | the order of the steps | First, warm the teapot. |
| **adverbs** | adjective + *-ly* | how to do it | Pour it slowly. |

**Remember:** an instruction has no *you*: *Mix it well*, not *You mix it well*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *Don’t to press it.* → ✓ *Do not press it.* (no *to* after *do not*). ✗ *Pour it careful.* → ✓ *Pour it carefully.* (an adverb, not an adjective, after the verb).

**Part 5: Why Instructions Are So Hard to Write**

*Figure 10.23 · One job, written badly and written well.*

Before you read: have you ever followed instructions that left a step out?

> **Gloss:** expert = somebody with years of practice · obvious = so clear that nobody says it · assume = to think something is true without checking · draft = an early try, before the last one
>
> Ask an expert to write down how they do a thing, and watch them leave steps out. They are not being lazy. The missing steps are the ones they stopped seeing years ago, because the hand does them without asking. A baker writes *knead the dough* and assumes everybody knows how hard. A carpenter writes *fix the shelf to the wall* and never says which goes in first. The step that is obvious to the writer is the one the reader needs most. The best instructions in the world are usually written by somebody who learned the job last year and still remembers not knowing. Give the first draft to a beginner and watch their hands, not their face. The place where the hands stop is the missing step.

**Part 5: Three Ways of Writing an Instruction**

*Figure 10.25 · Three kinds of instruction, and who each one is for.*

Before you read: can an instruction work with no words at all?

> **Gloss:** diagram = a drawing that explains · translate = to put into another language · symbol = a small picture that stands for a word · recipe = written steps for cooking one thing
>
> People build an instruction in very different ways. A flat-pack company in Sweden decided years ago to use no words at all, only diagrams. The same box goes to fifty countries, and nobody wants to translate it fifty times. A recipe does the opposite. It names every thing first, in a list, and only then tells you what to do. Nothing is missing by the time your hands are too dirty to turn a page. The safety card in the seat in front of you uses symbols and almost no reading. It has to work for a frightened person in a hurry. Each one chose its form from its reader. That is the whole art of it.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Street Laid by Hand**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 10.35 · A street laid by machine, and a street laid by hand.*

Before you read: is anything near you still made by hand?

> **Gloss:** craft = a skill of making things with your hands · trade = a job you learn by doing it beside somebody · design = the shape or pattern somebody planned

> Most of a city goes down by machine in an afternoon. In Portugal the famous black and white streets go down one small stone at a time, by hand, on somebody’s knees. The worker cuts each stone to shape with a hammer, sets it into the bed, taps it level, and moves on. A good one lays perhaps six square metres in a day. The design is a wave, or a ship, or a star, and the worker reads it from a drawing the way a player reads music. It is slower than a machine and it costs more, and the cities keep doing it, because a machine street is the same street everywhere. They teach the trade the old way.
