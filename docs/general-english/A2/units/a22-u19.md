**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 19.12 · One word joins two halves, and tells you what kind of thing you are talking about.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **who** | the woman who runs the shop | a person | Amina is the woman who runs the shop. |
| **which** | the bus which goes there | a thing | The bus which goes there is the 14. |
| **that** | the shop that sells bread | a person or a thing | The shop that sells bread opens at seven. |
| **where** | the street where I live | a place | This is the street where I live. |

**Remember:** *that* works for people and things both, and it is what people actually say. *Which* is for things only, and *who* is for people only.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *The woman which runs the shop.* → ✓ *The woman who runs the shop.* (*which* cannot take a person). ✗ *The shop that it sells bread.* → ✓ *The shop that sells bread.* (the joining word is the subject; do not add another one).

**Part 5: Why Streets Describe People Instead of Naming Them**

*Figure 19.23 · One person, described five ways by five neighbours.*

Before you read: how many of your neighbours could you name?

> **Gloss:** neighbour = a person who lives near you · nickname = a name people give you that is not your name · landmark = a thing people give directions by
>
> In a street of forty people, most people know about six names. The rest are held as descriptions: the woman who runs the shop, the man with the two dogs, the one who parks badly. This looks lazy and it is not. A description carries what you need and a name carries nothing. A name has to be learned twice: once as a sound, once as a person. A description is already the person. It is also why a street can hold hundreds of people it has never been introduced to. A new face is described long before it is named. The cost is that a description sticks. A man who parked badly once, years ago, is still the one who parks badly. Nobody on that street has driven with him since.

**Part 5: Three Places Named After One Person**

*Figure 19.25 · Three places, and the person each one is named after.*

Before you read: is anything near you named after a person?

> **Gloss:** monument = a thing built to make people remember somebody · plaque = a small flat sign on a wall, with a name on it · lane = a small street · mill = a building where grain was once ground into flour
>
> A street name says more about who chose it than about the person chosen. Mill Lane in this city has no mill and never had one. It is named after a family called Mill, who paid for the road. Half the roads in one part of Birmingham carry the names of the daughters of the man who built them. There were eleven daughters. He ran out, and then he started on cousins. And there are about forty streets in England named after one woman. She ran a school for children who could not hear. Every one of the forty was chosen by a town office, and every office had read the same small book about her. A plaque is a decision somebody made in an afternoon, and it then lasts longer than everybody who argued about it.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Town Where Every Cloth Has a Name**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 19.35 · A pattern on a loom and the name it carries.*

Before you read: does a pattern mean anything where you live?

> **Gloss:** loom = the frame a cloth is made on · weave = to make cloth by crossing threads · pattern = lines or shapes that repeat · strip = a long thin piece of something · thread = one long thin line of cotton or wool

> Bonwire, in Ghana, is the town kente cloth comes from, and every pattern in it has a name. The names are not just for show. A pattern called *obaakofoo mmu man* means roughly that one person does not rule a nation, and a chief who wore it was saying something. Another is put on only after a parent has died. The cloth is made in thin strips, on a loom a man sits inside. The strips are then sewn together, side by side. So the pattern has to be planned before the first thread goes on. A man who can name two hundred patterns is not showing off. The names are the job. A cloth given to the wrong person is a sentence said by mistake.
