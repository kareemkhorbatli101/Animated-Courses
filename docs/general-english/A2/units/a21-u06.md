**Part 2 · Grammar — Past Simple: Irregular Verbs**

***[CORE] Core track · pre-class input***

**Part 2: Notice**

> **Read these lines about Maya’s Thursday. Underline the past verbs. Which ones do not end in *-ed*?**

> Maya left the flat at seven and forgot her bag. She went back for it and missed the bus. She caught the next one and fell asleep. She woke up two stops late and found that it was raining.
>
> *Missed* ends in *-ed*. The others do not: they change in the middle instead, and there is no rule for which way. English has about two hundred of these, and a learner meets the same forty again and again.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 6.12 · Regular and irregular: two ways back into the past.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Regular** | verb + -ed | the ordinary way | He missed the coach. |
| **Irregular** | the middle sound changes | about forty common verbs | He caught the next one. |
| **Negative** | did not + plain verb | the same for both kinds | He did not catch it. |
| **Time phrases** | last week · two days ago · in the spring | say when, once, near the start | Last summer I took the coach. |

**Remember:** after *did* and *did not*, every verb goes back to its plain form: *did he go?*, not *did he went?*

> **Watch out!** ✗ *He goed back for it.* → ✓ *He went back for it.* (*go* is irregular). ✗ *Did he went?* → ✓ *Did he go?* (*did* carries the past).

**Part 5: Why Journeys Go Wrong**

*Figure 6.23 · One journey, five places it can break.*

Before you read: is a long journey more likely to go wrong than a short one?

> **Gloss:** connection = a second train or bus you need to catch · margin = the spare time between two things · chain = a line of things, each one holding the next · link = one piece of a chain
>
> A journey is a chain, and a chain breaks at its weakest link. The flight is not often the problem. The problem is the forty minutes between the flight and the last train. Or the ten minutes between two buses in a town where the buses do not wait. Travellers usually go wrong at a connection. The reason is almost always the same: there was no margin. Somebody planned the journey as if nothing could be late. Then one thing was. Experienced travellers do the opposite. They build in the boring hour, carry the charger, and leave a spare key with a neighbour. It looks like pessimism. It is really a kind of respect for how much has to go right.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Train That Climbs**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 6.35 · Sea level, and four and a half thousand metres up.*

Before you read: what happens to a train when the air gets thin?

> **Gloss:** zigzag = a line that turns sharply left and right to climb · switchback = a place where a train goes forward, stops, and goes back up · carriage = one part of a train, with seats in it

> The train line from Lima into the Andes is very old, and it is still one of the highest in the world. It leaves the coast at sea level and reaches four and a half thousand metres. The air up there is thin, and the staff carry a little oxygen for anybody who needs it. The line could not climb that fast straight, so the engineers cut zigzags into the mountain. The train runs forward into a dead end, stops, and goes back up the next section. There are many of these switchbacks. Passengers who do not know about them think something is wrong the first time the train starts to go back. It takes eleven hours to cover a distance a car does in five. Almost everybody who travels it says the eleven hours were the point.

Before you read: who has a key to your home, apart from you?

> Amina has four spare keys in a box under the till, and each one has a small paper with a flat number on it. Maya left hers there three years ago after she spent a night on the stairs. She says it was the coldest night of her life. Dani left his after he locked himself out twice in one month. Amina says she was not surprised either time, and that she expects a third. Mr Okonkwo left his before anybody asked, and said only that somebody needs one. Yuki left no key. Amina never suggested it, because there is a right moment to ask and it is not the first month. The box is not a security system. Twice a year somebody comes in at eight with the face of a person locked out. Amina reaches under the till and says nothing.

> **Answer the questions. (0. is done.)**

0. Where does Amina keep the spare keys? — In a box under the till.

1. Why did Maya leave a key?

2. What did Mr Okonkwo say when he left his?

3. Why did Amina not suggest it to Yuki?

**Part 9: Decision Task**

> **A neighbour wants to keep a spare key to your home. Should you (a) say yes, (b) say no and keep a key somewhere outside, or (c) say no and change nothing? Decide and explain. Use the frame.**
>
> Answer frame: “I think I should ___ because ___. Also, ___. This is better than ___ because ___.”

**Model — read this first:**

> I think I should say yes, because a locked door at midnight is a worse problem than a neighbour with a key. Also, the question means something, and saying no says something back. This is better than hiding a key outside. A key outside is a key anybody can find, and I would never quite stop thinking about it. (59 words)
