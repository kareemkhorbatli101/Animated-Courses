> **Listen to *have* and *has* in the present perfect. Say each one aloud.**

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 15.12 · A whole life up to now, and a finished moment.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **have / has + past participle** | I have been, she has gone | experience, with no date | I have been to four countries. |
| **ever** | in questions | at any time in your life | Have you ever slept outside? |
| **never** | in statements | not once, in your whole life | She has never flown. |
| **already / yet** | already in statements, yet in questions and negatives | sooner than expected, or up to now | He has already eaten. They have not arrived yet. |

**Remember:** the present perfect never takes a finished date: *I have been to Peru*, not *I have been to Peru last year*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *I have been there last year.* → ✓ *I went there last year.* (a finished date takes the past simple). ✗ *Have you ever went?* → ✓ *Have you ever been?* (the third form, not the past).

**Part 5: Why We Remember the Difficult Ones**

*Figure 15.23 · One holiday, and the three days anybody still talks about.*

Before you read: which of your holidays do you actually tell people about?

> **Gloss:** smooth = with nothing going wrong · peak = the strongest moment of something · ending = how a thing finishes
>
> Ask somebody about a holiday and they tell you about the boat that broke down. Nobody tells you about the nine smooth days. This is not only a story habit. People remember a long experience by two moments: the strongest one, and the last one. The nine quiet days leave almost nothing behind, and the two bad hours leave a shape. That is why a difficult trip with a good ending stays in the head as a good trip. A smooth week that ends in an argument at an airport does not. It also explains why people go back to the places where something went wrong. They are not going back for the trouble. They are going back for the only days their memory bothered to keep.

**Part 5: Three Places People Travel a Long Way to See**

*Figure 15.25 · Three places, and what people go there for.*

Before you read: is there a place you would cross a continent to see?

> **Gloss:** northern lights = coloured light in a very cold night sky · migration = animals moving a long way together, every year · ruins = what is left of an old building
>
> Some places people travel for days to reach, and the reason is never only the view. In the far north of Norway people wait three nights in the cold for the northern lights. About a third of them see nothing at all and come back anyway. In Kenya the migration crosses the same river every year, and the animals do not know anybody is watching, which is most of the point. In Peru there are ruins at the end of a train ride and a long walk. Almost nobody gets there without hearing, twice, that it is not worth the climb. All three have something in common. You cannot be sure of any of them, and that is why people go.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Visitors Who Came Before You**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 15.35 · A wall of names, and the people who left them.*

Before you read: how old do you think the habit of visiting an old place is?

> **Gloss:** ancient = very, very old · temple = a building people built for a god · visitor = somebody who comes to see a place

> In Egypt there are temples with names cut into the stone by visitors, and the names are two thousand years old. Greek and Roman travellers came up the river to look at buildings that were already ancient when they arrived. They paid a guide, they walked round, and then a good many of them wrote their own name on a wall. One of them added that he had seen it and had not thought much of it. The buildings those visitors came to see were older to them than they are to us. Nothing about the trip has changed much either. There is the boat, the guide, the long walk in the heat, and the name on the wall, which we now call a photograph.
