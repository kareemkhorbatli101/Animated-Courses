**Part 2 · Grammar — Past Simple: Was, Were and Regular Verbs**

***[CORE] Core track · pre-class input***

**Part 2: Notice**

> **Read these lines about last Sunday. Underline the past verbs. Which are *was* or *were*?**

> Last Sunday was warm. Amina opened the shop at eight. Mr Okonkwo carried a chair outside and stayed there all afternoon. The children were noisy by the lake. Dani cooked for four and three people arrived. It was a quiet day.
>
> *Was* and *were* say what something was like. The other verbs say what somebody did, and they all end in *-ed*.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 5.12 · Then and now: the same verbs, one step back.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **was / were** | I/he/she/it was · you/we/they were | how something was | Sunday was warm. |
| **Regular past** | verb + -ed, every person | what somebody did | She opened the shop. |
| **Negative** | did not + the plain verb | what did not happen | She did not open. |
| **Question** | did + person + plain verb | asking about then | Did she open? |

**Remember:** after *did* and *did not*, the verb loses its *-ed*: *did you walk?*, not *did you walked?*

> **Watch out!** ✗ *I was walk to the lake.* → ✓ *I walked to the lake.* (one past, not two). ✗ *Did you walked?* → ✓ *Did you walk?* (*did* has the past already).

**Part 5: Who Invented the Weekend**

*Figure 5.23 · One Saturday, from morning to night.*

Before you read: has there always been a weekend?

> **Gloss:** factory = a big building where people make things · campaign = a long effort to change something · agreement = when people decide something together · refuse = to say no
>
> The weekend is not very old. For most of history people rested on one day, and in many places they worked on the other six. The two-day weekend arrived with factories, and it arrived slowly. Workers campaigned for it and owners said no. Then one or two factories tried it. They found something surprising: two rested days produced as much work in five as tired days produced in six. After that the argument changed. In time most of Europe and North America settled on five days and two. Almost nobody now remembers that it was ever a fight. Tomas works nights and sleeps through Saturday. He is a reminder: the weekend was always an agreement, never a fact about the world.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Country That Tried a Shorter Week**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 5.35 · Five days, and then four.*

Before you read: would you work four days for the same money?

> **Gloss:** trial = a test over a period of time · productivity = how much work gets done in an hour · burnout = being so tired from work that you cannot work well · union = a group that speaks for workers

> Over four years Iceland ran one of the largest trials of a shorter working week. About one worker in every one hundred took part: nurses, office staff, people in schools and in city offices. They moved from forty hours to thirty-five or thirty-six, and they kept the same pay. One nurse said she got her Thursdays back. The question everybody asked was obvious. Would the work still get done? It did. Productivity stayed the same or improved in almost every place of work, and the reports showed a large drop in worry and burnout. The trial ended, and then something more interesting happened. The unions used the results, and in a few years most workers in the country won shorter hours. Iceland did not prove that four days suits everybody. It proved that the five-day week was a choice, and that a country can change its mind.

Mr Okonkwo was outside in his chair. In forty years of teaching, he said, he never once worked a Saturday. He does not know how anybody manages it. Tomas said he manages it by not counting. Then he asked what time the shop closed and went to buy milk. That was his weekend.
