>

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 13.12 · Necessary, forbidden and not necessary: three jobs, four forms.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **must** | must + plain verb | necessary; often the rule itself speaking | You must show your card. |
| **have to** | have to + plain verb | necessary; often somebody else’s rule | You have to be a member. |
| **mustn’t** | must not + plain verb | forbidden | You mustn’t touch the paintings. |
| **don’t have to** | do not have to + verb | not necessary; you may if you like | You don’t have to pay. |

**Remember:** *mustn’t* and *don’t have to* are opposites, not the same. One forbids; one frees.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *You don’t must touch it.* → ✓ *You mustn’t touch it.* (*must* makes its own negative). ✗ *You mustn’t pay, it is free.* → ✓ *You don’t have to pay, it is free.* (*mustn’t pay* means paying is forbidden).

**Part 5: Why Some Rules Work and Others Do Not**

*Figure 13.23 · One rule, from the notice to the habit.*

Before you read: which rule where you live does everybody ignore?

> **Gloss:** obey = to do what a rule says · enforce = to make people keep a rule · reason = why something is so
>
> A rule that people keep has three things, and a rule that nobody keeps is usually missing one of them. The first is a reason you can see. People put their bags in a locker because they can see why a gallery might ask. Nobody keeps a rule whose reason died years before the notice did. The second is fairness. A rule that one group keeps and another does not is not a rule. It only costs the people with no excuse. The third is somebody who will mention it. Not a guard necessarily, not a fine; somebody willing to say, quietly, that you are standing in the way. Take away any one of the three and the notice stays on the wall and means nothing, which is the state of most notices.

**Part 5: Three Places and Their One Rule**

*Figure 13.25 · Three places, and the one rule each of them lives by.*

Before you read: which place near you has exactly one rule that matters?

> **Gloss:** lane = the part of a pool you swim up and down · aisle = the walkway between seats or shelves · carriage = one part of a train
>
> Some places have one rule and it carries everything. In a swimming pool it is the lane: fast on one side, slow on the other. One swimmer in the wrong lane ruins an hour for twelve people without touching anybody. In a cinema it is the aisle seat. Does the person in it stand up, or turn their knees sideways? That question divides countries and families. On a night train it is the carriage at the end, the quiet one. Nobody wrote that rule down, and everybody keeps it more carefully than the written ones. None of the three needs a guard. All three fall apart the moment one person decides the rule is for other people.

Before you read: can a whole country change a habit in one night?

> **Gloss:** indoors = inside a building · workplace = anywhere people work · custom = what people have always done

> Ireland was the first country in the world to stop smoking in every indoor workplace, and almost nobody thought it would hold. The bar was the heart of the thing, and the custom was older than anybody arguing about it. Smoking customers said they would go on smoking. Owners said they would lose everything. The rule came in on a Monday. By the end of that week almost everybody was keeping it, with no guards and almost no fines. What surprised the loudest arguers was how fast the new habit became the normal one. Thirty years of smoke left a room in a weekend. *Figure 13.37 · The close-to-home reading, drawn along one street.*

Before you read: how does a new person learn the rules of a building?

> There is no list of rules at number 14 and there are about eleven of them. The bins go out after six. You must pull the front door shut, not push it to. Nobody parks in front of the shop before nine. You do not knock on Tomas’s door before two in the afternoon, because of his nights. Nobody uses the side door after nine and nobody now alive knows why. Yuki learned nine of the eleven in her first month. She got every one of them wrong first, then heard about it, kindly, from whoever was standing there. The other two she still does not know, and the building decided long ago that she does not need them.
