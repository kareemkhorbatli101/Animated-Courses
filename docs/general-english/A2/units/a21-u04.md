**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 4.12 · Countable or uncountable: which words go with which.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Countable** | a tin · three tins | things you can count one by one | There are three tins. |
| **Uncountable** | milk · bread · rice | things you measure, never *a* | There is milk. |
| **a few / a little** | a few + countable, a little + uncountable | a small amount | a few eggs · a little cheese |
| **a / an / the** | *a* first time, *the* after that | which one we mean | a bakery → the bakery |

**Remember:** say *a bottle of milk*, not *a milk*. The container is countable.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *I want a bread.* → ✓ *I want some bread.* (bread is uncountable). ✗ *There are a little tins.* → ✓ *There are a few tins.* (tins are countable).

**Part 5: What a Small Shop Is For**

*Figure 4.23 · From the farm to the basket, step by step.*

Before you read: why do small shops stay open when big ones are cheaper?

> **Gloss:** supermarket = a very big food shop · stock = the things a shop has to sell · credit = buying now and paying later
>
> A small shop cannot win on price. A supermarket buys ten thousand bottles of milk and a corner shop buys thirty. So the corner shop pays more for each one. It cannot win on choice either: there is one kind of rice, not eleven. What a small shop has is everything else. It is open when you need one thing at seven in the morning. It knows your name, and sometimes it knows you are good for it until Friday. That is a kind of credit no supermarket offers. It carries the stock the street actually eats. The street says what that is, slowly, by buying and not buying. Amina says the shop is not really a shop. It is a list of what four hundred people want, written down in boxes.

**Part 5: Breakfast Around the World**

*Figure 4.25 · The first meal of the day in three places.*

Before you read: what do you eat in the first hour of the day?

> **Gloss:** savoury = salty, not sweet · staple = the food people eat most days
>
> Breakfast is the meal that travels worst. In much of Europe it is sweet: bread, butter, something with sugar in it, and coffee. In Japan it is often savoury, and rice is as normal at eight in the morning as at eight at night. In Egypt a common breakfast is beans with oil and bread. It is a staple, it costs very little, and it keeps you going until the afternoon. Visitors find other people’s breakfasts stranger than other people’s dinners. The first meal is the one we learn first, and we never quite stop expecting it.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Shop With No Prices**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 4.35 · The old market and the new one.*

Before you read: can a shop work without a price on anything?

> **Gloss:** haggle = to argue about a price until both people agree · fixed price = a price that does not change · label = a small paper with the price on it

> In an old market in Fez, in Morocco, almost nothing has a price on it. You ask, the seller says a number, you say another number, and somewhere between the two you both stop. Visitors hate this. They want a label. But the system is older than labels and it does something a label cannot: it sets a different price for a different buyer. A neighbour pays less than a stranger. A family buying for a wedding pays less for more. The fixed price arrived with big shops, because a shop with four hundred products and six assistants cannot haggle four hundred times a day. It was faster. It was fairer in one way and less fair in another, and almost nobody noticed what we gave up. In Fez, they say the number is not the point. The talking is.

> There is a notebook under Amina’s till, and it is older than the till. In it there are names and small numbers. Nobody calls it credit. A man with no money until Friday takes bread and milk. Amina writes two eighty next to his name, and on Friday she crosses the line out. It works because the street is small and because almost everybody comes back. Almost. Twice in eleven years somebody moved away without paying. Both times Amina closed the page and said nothing to anybody. Her son thinks the book is a bad idea and wants her to stop. He is probably right about the money. He is wrong about what the book is for, and she cannot find the words to tell him.

> **Answer the questions. (0. is done.)**

0. Where is the notebook? — Under Amina’s till.

1. What does Amina write in the notebook?

2. What happened twice in eleven years?

3. What does Amina’s son want?
