**Part 2 · Grammar — Present Simple vs Present Continuous**

***[CORE] Core track · pre-class input***

**Part 2: Notice**

> **Read these lines from Alder Street. Underline the verbs. Which are routines, and which are happening now?**
>
> Maya works in a bookshop. She usually starts at nine. Right now, she is waiting for the bus. Tomas works at the hospital every week. This week, he is working nights. Dani studies at the college. He usually cooks on Tuesdays, but today he is eating at his mother’s. Mr Okonkwo reads every afternoon. At the moment he is reading about birds.
>
> Five of these sentences are about a routine, and three are about now. The time words tell you which is which. *Usually*, *every week* and *every afternoon* point one way. *Right now*, *this week*, *today* and *at the moment* point the other.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 1.12 · Present simple and present continuous: again and again, or now.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Present simple** | I/you work; he works | routines, facts | Maya works in a bookshop. |
| **Present continuous** | am/is/are + -ing | now, this week | Tomas is working nights. |

**Remember:** every day, usually, often → present simple. now, right now, at the moment, this week → present continuous.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *She work in a shop.* → ✓ *She works in a shop.* (add -s for he/she/it). ✗ *I waiting now.* → ✓ *I am waiting now.* (don’t drop am/is/are).

**Part 5: What Is a Routine?**

*Figure 1.23 · One morning, six people, in order.*

Before you read: what three things do you do every single morning?

> **Gloss:** routine = the same things you do every day · commute = the journey between home and work · flexible = easy to change · habit = something you do without thinking
>
> A routine is the set of things a person does again and again. Most people have a morning routine, a work routine, and a weekend routine. Maya gets up at half past six, makes coffee, and listens to the radio. Then she catches the bus. Her commute takes twenty minutes. At the bookshop she opens the till, checks the new books, and makes a list. This is the same almost every weekday. Right now, however, Maya is doing something different: she is studying for an exam, so on Tuesdays she is leaving work early. Routines are useful because they save thinking. A good routine is also flexible. When Tomas works nights, his whole routine changes, and he sleeps while the street is busy. Some people love a fixed routine; others find it boring. Most of us need a little of both: the same breakfast, and a different evening.
>

**Part 5: Routines Around the World**

*Figure 1.25 · The same day in three places.*

Before you read: in your country, what time do people usually eat the evening meal?

> **Gloss:** siesta = a short sleep in the early afternoon · rush hour = the time when many people travel to or from work
>
> Routines are not the same everywhere. In some countries people eat the main meal at midday and rest after it; in others, nobody stops until the evening. In parts of Spain and Latin America, some shops still close for a siesta. In Japan, the morning rush hour is so busy that station staff help people onto the trains. In much of the north of Europe, the working day finishes at four or five, and the evening is long. The clock is the same everywhere. What people do with it is not.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Man Who Changed His Mornings**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 1.35 · Jun’s mornings before and after one small change.*

Before you read: can a small change in the morning change a whole day?

> **Gloss:** alarm = the clock sound that wakes you · swap = to change one thing for another · habit = something you do without thinking about it
>
> In Seoul, a man called Jun worked in an office and hated his mornings. Every day he stopped the alarm four times, then ran for the train, and arrived angry. One winter he made one small change: he put his phone in the kitchen instead of beside his bed. Now, to stop the alarm, he had to stand up and walk. It was a very small thing, and it changed everything. He was awake five minutes earlier, so he had time for tea. With tea, he sat down. Sitting down, he read ten pages. After a year, he had thirty books behind him and he was not angry on the train. Now people ask him: “What is your secret?” He tells them: “I swapped one habit, not twenty.” His brother tried the same thing and hated it. Jun says that is also part of the answer: a routine has to be yours.
>

Before you read: when somebody new moves into your building, who speaks to them first?

> Yuki moved into the top flat at 14 Alder Street on a Saturday in March. She did not know anybody in the city. For the first four days she spoke to nobody except Amina in the shop, and only about bread. She worked from home, so she did not meet people at an office. In the evening she heard the building: doors, a television, somebody laughing on the stairs. She did not know any of the voices. After four days, Maya knocked on her door with a short note about the post and the bus. It took two minutes. Yuki says now that those two minutes were the difference between living in a building and living in a street. Three weeks later she knew four of the six people at number 14, and she had started leaving her door open when she cooked.
