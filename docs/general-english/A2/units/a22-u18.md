**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 18.12 · One half waits, the other half answers.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **if + present** | if it rains | it may happen | If it rains, I will stay in. |
| **will + verb** | I will stay in | what you will then do | If it rains, I will stay in. |
| **when + present** | when it rains | it will happen, time unknown | When it rains, the road floods. |
| **unless + present** | unless it rains | the one thing that would stop it | I will walk unless it rains. |

**Remember:** no *will* after *if*, *when* or *unless*. The present does the work there: *if it rains*, never *if it will rain*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *If it will rain, I will stay in.* → ✓ *If it rains, I will stay in.* (no *will* after *if*). ✗ *Unless it does not rain, I will walk.* → ✓ *Unless it rains, I will walk.* (*unless* is a negative already).

**Part 5: Why Nobody Believes a Warning the First Time**

*Figure 18.23 · One warning, and what people do in each minute.*

Before you read: have you ever ignored a warning and been right?

> **Gloss:** warning = a word or sound that tells you something bad is coming · alarm = a loud sound made on purpose, to warn · drill = a practice for something bad, done when nothing is wrong
>
> If an alarm goes off in a building, almost nobody moves. They look up, then at each other, and the first person to stand decides it for the rest. The same thing has been counted everywhere: people wait to be told twice. The reason is not that people are slow. It is that a first warning costs nothing, and being wrong in front of others is expensive. So a good alarm does two things. It says what is happening, not that something is, and it names what to do. *Fire on the third floor. Use the back stairs.* Nobody waits for a second opinion about that.

**Part 5: Three Warnings That Come Too Late to Be Useful**

*Figure 18.25 · Three warnings, and how long each one gives you.*

Before you read: how much warning would be enough?

> **Gloss:** flood = water covering land that is usually dry · sensor = a small machine that notices a change · siren = a very loud sound that carries a long way
>
> A warning is only worth the time it buys. One four hours early empties a street: four hours is enough to move a car and a freezer. One eleven minutes early does almost nothing. In two cities it made things worse: people went back indoors for their papers. An earthquake warning is the strangest. It cannot be early, because nothing happens first. A sensor can feel the first shake and send a siren faster than the ground moves. That buys about a minute. A minute sounds like nothing. It is enough to stop a train and get a surgeon’s hands out of somebody.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The City That Gets a Minute**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 18.35 · A shake under the ground and a sound above it.*

Before you read: what would you do with one minute?

> **Gloss:** earthquake = a shaking of the ground · loudspeaker = a machine that makes a voice loud enough for a street · rehearse = to practise a thing before it is needed

> Mexico City sits a long way from the coast where its earthquakes start. That distance is the whole of its luck. Sensors on the coast feel the first small shake. The shake travels through rock at about three kilometres a second; a message down a telephone line travels far faster. So the loudspeakers in the city can sound about sixty seconds before the ground moves. A minute is not enough to leave a building and nobody is told to. It is enough to stop the trains, open the fire station doors and get out of a lift. The city rehearses it every September. If the siren sounds on another day, people do what they did in the drill. That part took thirty years, and it was not the sensors.

> **Answer the questions. (0. is done.)**

0. Where do Mexico City’s earthquakes start? — A long way off, near the coast.

1. How fast does the shake travel through rock?

2. Name two things a minute is enough for.

3. What took thirty years?

> There is a bag by the front door of number 14 and nobody voted for it. Yuki put it there in April, after the water came up in the spring and four of them lost something. She did it on a Saturday and she told nobody first. It holds a lamp, a list of six telephone numbers, two sets of spare keys and a bar of chocolate nobody is allowed to eat. Amina has added nothing and is glad it is there. Tomas has looked inside it twice, professionally. Dani has eaten the chocolate once and replaced it.
