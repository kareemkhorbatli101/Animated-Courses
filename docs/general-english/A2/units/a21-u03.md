**Part 2 · Grammar — Present Continuous and Present Simple**

***[CORE] Core track · pre-class input***

**Part 2: Notice**

> **Read these lines about the city. Underline the verbs. Which are always true, and which are happening now?**
>
> The market opens at six every morning. Right now it is opening, and the first van is arriving. Buses stop at this corner. At the moment three buses are standing in a line. Mr Okonkwo reads on that bench every afternoon. Today he is reading there in the rain.
>
> Three sentences are facts; three are about this minute. The time words tell you which.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 3.5 · Always true, or happening now: the same verb, two jobs.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Present simple** | it opens · they stop | a fact, a timetable, every day | The market opens at six. |
| **Present continuous** | is / are + -ing | this minute, in front of you | It is opening now. |
| **Time words** | every day · at the moment | they decide the tense | Today he is reading in the rain. |
| **Stative verbs** | know, want, like | no -ing form | I want a ticket. |

**Remember:** a timetable takes the present simple: *the bus leaves at six*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *The market is opening at six every day.* → ✓ *The market opens at six.* (a fact). ✗ *I am wanting a ticket.* → ✓ *I want a ticket.* (no -ing).

**Part 5: The City Has Two Clocks**

*Figure 3.9 · One day in the city, hour by hour.*

Before you read: is your city a different place at different hours?

> **Gloss:** rush hour = the time when many people travel to or from work · queue = a line of people waiting · deliver = to bring things to a place
>
> A city runs on two clocks at once. The first is the timetable: the market opens at six, the library at nine, the last train leaves at half past eleven. Somebody writes that clock down, and it almost never changes. The second clock is what people are actually doing, and it is different every day. At six the market opens, but today one van is late and half the tables are empty. At nine the library opens. This morning there is already a line of people outside, because it is cold. The woman at the door says it is never empty at that hour. Most of us live by the first clock and complain about the second. Night workers see a third city. The rest of us never meet it. A cleaner is washing the floor of an empty station. Somebody is delivering bread to a shop. It opens in four hours.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Map That Was Wrong on Purpose**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 3.13 · The old map and the new one.*

Before you read: can a map of a city be wrong and useful at the same time?

> **Gloss:** underground = the train system under a city · diagram = a simple drawing that shows how things connect · scale = how a map’s size compares to the real size · cancelled = not running today

> In Tokyo, like every big city, the underground map is not a picture of the city. It is a diagram. The lines are straight and the corners are all the same shape. The distance between two stations on paper has little to do with the real distance. The first map of this kind came from London, a long time ago. Its maker had an unusual job. He drew electric lines for a living. He drew the city the same way. People complained that it was wrong. It was. It was also the first map anybody could read on a moving train. Today almost every big city uses the same idea. The map is wrong about scale and right about the only three things a passenger needs: which line, which direction, how many stops.

Before you read: what happens to a street when its bus stop moves?

> For eleven years the 7 stopped at the corner of Alder Street, outside Amina’s shop. In March the bus company moved the stop two hundred metres, to the main road. The reason was the traffic: the corner is too small and the bus was blocking it twice an hour. Nobody argued with the reason. But two hundred metres is a long way with shopping, and Mr Okonkwo stopped going into town on Tuesdays. Amina noticed first. People waiting for the bus used to buy bread. Now they do not. She counted: eleven fewer customers a day, all of them between half past eight and nine. The bus is faster. The corner is clearer. And a shop with a bus stop outside it is now only a shop.

> **Answer the questions. (0. is done.)**

0. Where did the 7 stop for eleven years? — At the corner of Alder Street, outside Amina’s shop.

1. Why did the bus company move the stop?

2. What did Mr Okonkwo stop doing?

3. How many fewer customers does Amina count, and when?
