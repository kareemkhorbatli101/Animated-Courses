*Figure 8.6 · Where the stress falls in 0 words of this unit.*

**🔊 Audio Track 8.1**

> **Listen to *can* and *can’t*. Say each pair aloud.**
>
> I can swim — **can** is short and weak: /kən/
>
> I can’t swim — **can’t** is long and clear: /kɑːnt/

>
> This is the hardest sound in the unit, and it matters. In the middle of a sentence *can* almost disappears, and *can’t* is the one you hear. If a learner says *can* loudly, an English ear hears *can’t*, and the answer comes out wrong.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

*Figure 8.12 · Can and could: what they do, and how polite they are.*

| | **Form** | **Use** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **can / can’t** | can + plain verb, every person | ability now | She can swim. He can’t drive. |
| **could** | could + plain verb | ability in the past | He could float at eight. |
| **Can you…?** | a question, plain | asking a friend | Can you lend me a pen? |
| **Could you…?** | a question, softer | asking anybody | Could you watch the shop? |

**Remember:** *can* and *could* never take *to*: *I can swim*, not *I can to swim*.

> **Watch out!** ✗ *He cans swim.* → ✓ *He can swim.* (*can* never takes -s). ✗ *Can you to help me?* → ✓ *Can you help me?* (no *to* after *can*).

**Part 5: Why Adults Think They Cannot Learn**

*Figure 8.23 · One skill, from the first week to the first year.*

Before you read: is it harder to learn something new at forty than at four?

> **Gloss:** adult = a grown-up person · beginner = somebody starting to learn · progress = getting better over time
>
> Adults often say they cannot learn a new skill, and they are almost always wrong. What they mean is that they cannot be a beginner in front of other people. A child of four falls over in a swimming pool and nobody thinks anything of it. An adult of forty falls over and feels it in the back of the neck for an hour. So adults avoid the first six weeks. Those six weeks are the only really unpleasant part. Then they decide they were too old. The research says something different. Adults learn some things faster than children, because they can understand an explanation and a child cannot. What adults cannot do is practise badly in public for long enough. The skill is not the problem. The audience is.

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Library That Lends Everything**

***[PLUS] Plus track · pre-read / discuss***

*Figure 8.35 · What a library used to lend, and what it lends now.*

Before you read: should a library lend you a drill?

> **Gloss:** drill = a tool that makes holes · tool = a thing you use to make or mend something · membership = the right to use something, usually for a year · ladder = steps you carry, for reaching high things · storage = space where things sit and wait

> There is a number that people in libraries like to repeat. The average drill in a house does thirteen minutes of work in its whole life. Everything else is storage. Some years ago a library in Toronto took that seriously and started lending tools as well as books. There are now hundreds of these libraries across Canada and beyond. You pay a small membership, or in many cities nothing at all, and you borrow a drill, a bike, a sewing machine, a ladder. The arguments against it were obvious and mostly wrong: people would break things, nobody would bring them back, it would cost a fortune. In practice tools come back, because the next job usually needs the same tool. The libraries say the surprising part is not the lending. It is how many people arrive to borrow a drill and leave knowing how to use one.

1. What did a library in Toronto start doing?

2. What were the arguments against it?

3. What do the libraries say is the surprising part?
