**Part 1 · Vocabulary and Terminology**

**Part 1: Words for Chance and Care**

1. c · 2. e · 3. a · 4. g · 5. f · 6. d · 7. b
Not needed: **h)** *the hair of a sheep, turned into cloth*

> Item 0 (*Unless*) is a standalone worked example, as in the source: the word is not in Column A and its meaning is not in Column B, so it uses up no letter. Seven items, eight letters, one spare.

> Items 1 and 2 are the pair worth a minute. *Chance* is how likely a thing is, good or bad; *risk* is the chance of something bad only. A learner who treats them as the same word will later write *there is a risk of sun*.

**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 18.1)

The first conditional is two halves and the voice tells you which one you are in.

The *if* half ends on a rise and hangs there: *if it rains* ↗. The *will* half falls and closes: *I will stay in* ↘. Said in that order there is a clear break between them, and in writing a comma marks it. Said the other way round — *I will stay in if it rains* — there is no break at all and nothing is written between the halves.

Two faults follow, and they are opposite faults. A learner who says both halves flat leaves the listener waiting for a sentence that has already finished. A learner who has been taught the comma reads it out when it is not there, and produces *I will stay in, if it rains*, which sounds like an afterthought or a condition being added grudgingly.

Drill it as a shape and not as a rule: hand up on the *if* half, hand down on the *will* half, and no hands at all when the order reverses.

**Part 1: Four Things and What You Will Do**

0. It rains all day — I will stay in and be glad I have nothing to post *(given)*

Open; there is no expected set, because the right-hand column is the learner’s own week. Mark the form, not the plan: the left column is already present tense and the right column must take *will*. The commonest error appears here first and it is *I will stay in if it will rain*, with *will* in both halves.

**Part 1: If, When or Unless?**

1. a · 2. b · 3. c · 4. d · 5. e
Not needed: **f)** *a hundred years*

> This is the whole unit in five lines. *If* and *when* are the pair learners confuse, and the difference is certainty and nothing else: *when the rain stops* says it will stop, *if the rain stops* says it may not. English will not let you choose freely, and a learner who says *when I win* about a lottery has said something about themselves.

**Part 1: Write About a Plan (short)**

Open. Two or three sentences about something that might happen this week. The unit prints a model; this is a second one.

> Sample (46 words): *If the lift is broken again I will take the stairs, which is six floors and good for me, and I will hate it. There is a chance it is already mended. Nobody has told me, and nobody will, and I will find out at eight.*

**Part 2 · Grammar — The First Conditional**

**Part 2: Notice**

*Will* in the second half: **I will stay in** · **Amina will move the freezer** · **Dani will sleep through it** · **the hospital will ring Tomas**
Present tense in the first half: **if it rains** · **when the water comes up** · **unless somebody wakes him** · **if the roads are bad**

The teaching point: this is not a rule about *if*. It is a rule about every word that opens a time or a condition — *if*, *when*, *unless*, *as soon as*, *after*, *before*, *until*. None of them takes *will*. English marks the future once per sentence and marks it in the main half.

Say it that way round and the rule stops being a list to memorise. A learner who has learned "no *will* after *if*" will still write *when he will arrive* and *as soon as it will stop*, because *if* was not the point.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. rains *(given)* · 1. will move · 2. will ring · 3. wakes · 4. stops

> Items 3 and 4 are the ones to check. Both have the conditional half second, so the present tense is at the end of the sentence and *will* is nowhere near it. Learners who have been filling in the second gap all exercise will put *will* there out of rhythm.

**Part 2: Will It or Might It?**

0. will happen *(given)* · 1. might happen · 2. will happen · 3. might happen · 4. will happen

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. If it **rains**, I will stay in. *(no *will* after *if*)*
2. **Unless it rains**, I will walk. *(*unless* is a negative already)*
3. When he **gets** home, he will ring you. *(no *will* after *when* either)*
4. If the water comes up, Amina **will move** the freezer. *(the main half needs *will*)*

> Item 4 is the mirror of item 1 and it is there on purpose. A learner who has learned to strip *will* out will strip it out of both halves and be left with a sentence about a habit.

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One *if*, one *unless*.

> Sample: *If the shop is shut I will go to the one by the station, which is worse and nearer. Unless it rains I will walk both ways and pretend that was the plan.*

**Part 2: Grammar Review**

1. **D)** If it rains, I will stay in. · 2. **C)** Unless it rains.

**Part 3 · Listening**

**Part 3: What We Will Do If the Water Comes Up** (Track 18.2)

0. True *(given)*
1. **True** — “There is a list in my head and it has four things on it.”
2. **False** — “If you write it down, nobody will read it.”
3. **Not Given** — the water is not dated in this conversation.

Item 2 is the one that teaches the format. Amina states the opposite of the statement rather than simply not stating it, and that is the whole line between False and Not Given. Item 3 is the other side of it: the water did come up in the spring, and the class knows that from the warm-up two pages earlier — but this conversation does not say so, and a True/False/Not Given task is answered from the text in front of you and from nothing else. Learners who answer item 3 True have read it correctly and answered the wrong question.

**Part 3: The Call That Comes When the Roads Are Bad** (Track 18.3)

0. The hospital rings Tomas. *(given)*
1. They ring everybody.
2. They ring the next name.
3. Because somebody is coming in whatever he does, and it may as well be somebody who slept.

> Item 3 is the best sentence in the unit for the grammar. *If the roads are bad, somebody is coming in whatever I do* has a conditional half and a main half with no *will* in it at all, because he is not predicting — he is stating what is already arranged. Point it out only to a strong class; it is a complication, and it is also real English.

**Part 3: Six People, Six Plans** (Track 18.4)

Not needed: **e)** *will write a list and read it out*

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: What Will You Do If…**

Marking points: ☐ one *if … I will* ☐ one *in case* ☐ one *as soon as* ☐ no *will* in the conditional half.

> Sample: *If it rains all weekend I will finally do the cupboard, which has been waiting since March. I will take an umbrella in case, and I will not use it, because I never do. As soon as I get home today I am going to sit down for ten minutes before anybody asks me anything.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *What will you do if…?* ☐ *And if that does not work?* ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **who they ring** (sister / brother) · **the late bus** (walk / wait) · and the pair must find that **the long coat** and **staying home if it floods** match. A pair that reports four has counted the coat twice, once as a thing taken and once as a thing worn.

> Sample exchange: *A: Who will you ring? — B: My brother. — A: My sister. One. What will you take? — B: The long coat. — A: Same. If the bus is late? — B: I will wait. — A: I will walk. Two. And if it floods? — B: Stay at home. — A: Stay at home. So two. — B: Two, and the bus was the real one.*

**Part 4: Agreeing on One Plan**

Marking points: ☐ A asks what will happen ☐ A asks about the no-time case ☐ B names one thing they will do ☐ B gives one thing up out loud ☐ B says what they will do alone.

Card A must produce: the opening question, a harder case, a question about order, a question about their own absence.
Card B must produce: one action, one thing abandoned, a first priority, and a commitment for when they are alone.

The commonest fault is B refusing to give anything up. The card asks for it on purpose: a plan that keeps everything is not a plan, and the moment that decides whether two people can act together is the moment one of them says *then the freezer stays*.

***Stretch:*** your partner’s plan is better and you spoke first. Accept *Mine was worse. We will do yours, and I am glad one of us thought about it properly.* The marker is handing it over in one sentence and not taking it back in the next.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ one ordinary thing that might go wrong ☐ two *if* sentences ☐ what you will do first.

> Sample (51 words): *If the nursery rings before ten it is nothing, and if they ring after two I am leaving. The first thing I will do is text my sister, because she is four minutes away and I am forty. If she cannot go, I will go, and work will have to manage.*

**Part 5 · Reading**

**Part 5: Why Nobody Believes a Warning the First Time**

0. Move. *(given)*
1. **A)** The first person to stand
2. **D)** Because being wrong in front of others is expensive
3. **B)** What to do

> Item 2 is the one to discuss. The text says the delay is not slowness, it is cost: a first warning is free and standing up alone in a room of seated people is not. A class that has sat through a fire alarm at work will recognise it, and the honest follow-up question is what their own building’s alarm actually says.

**Part 5: Vocabulary in Context**

1. c · 2. d · 3. a · 4. b
Not needed: **e)** *a long line of cars that is not moving*

**Part 5: Three Warnings That Come Too Late to Be Useful**

0. Only the time it buys. *(given)*
1. It empties a street, because four hours is enough to move a car and a freezer.
2. Because people went back indoors for their papers.
3. Stopping a train, and getting a surgeon’s hands out of somebody.

> Item 2 is a hard fact and worth stating plainly: a short warning can cost lives that no warning would not have cost, because it sends people back for their things. That is the argument for a warning naming what to do rather than what is coming, which is the Part 5 reading again.

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: A Plan for a Bad Day (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ one *if* sentence ☐ one *unless* sentence ☐ no *will* after *if* ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (55 words): *If the car does not start I will leave it and get the 9.14, which means standing all the way. Unless it is freezing I will not ring the garage, because they will say bring it in and I cannot bring it in if it does not start. I will deal with it on Saturday.*

**Part 6: Should You Plan for Everything? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *You should, for about four things, and then stop. The four are fire, water, money and who has your keys. Everything past that is a hobby. The danger is not that you will forget the fifth plan; it is that having forty makes you feel ready, and feeling ready is what stops people checking the four.*

**Part 6: A Time You Were Lucky (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what went wrong ☐ why you were lucky ☐ what you do now ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (57 words): *I put a pan on and went to answer the door and stayed at the door for twenty minutes. The pan was empty, which is the only reason there is still a kitchen. If there had been oil in it, there would not be. I now carry the pan to the door with me, which looks mad.*

**Part 6: Reflection — The Warning You Ignore (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ which warning ☐ why you ignore it ☐ what would change it ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (50 words): *The smoke alarm in the hall goes off when anybody makes toast, so now when it goes off, somebody says toast. If it is ever not toast, somebody will say toast and go on reading. A warning that is wrong four times a week is not a warning any more.*

**Part 7 · Real-World File — Saying What You Will Do**

**7A: Phrase Bank**

1. c · 2. a · 3. d · 4. e
Not needed: **b)** *how much it will cost*

Item 3 is the one to teach and the one to be careful with. *Unless I hear from you, I will come* turns the other person’s silence into a yes, which is enormously useful and occasionally unfair. Ask the class when they would mind receiving it.

**7B: The Plan Nobody Agreed To** (Track 18.5)

0. Nobody; Yuki put it there. *(given)*
1. Until there is an emergency.
2. Thirty years.
3. He asks for his keys to be put in it.

> The last line is the whole exchange. Mr Okonkwo spends the conversation questioning the bag and then joins it, and he does it without conceding anything out loud. A strong class can be asked whether he has changed his mind or simply decided it costs him nothing.

**7C: Controlled Practice — Making a Plan With Somebody**

0. Name the thing that might happen — **1** *(given)*
Say what you will do if there is no time — **4**
Say what you will do first — **2**
Agree who rings who — **3**
Say what you will give up — **5**

> *What you will give up* goes last, after the ringing is agreed, and that order is the point. You cannot usefully decide what to abandon until you know who is doing what; a plan that starts with sacrifices gets abandoned in the first minute.

**7D: Role-Play — One Bag, Two Opinions**

Marking points: ☐ A says what they have done ☐ A names what to add ☐ A gives one hard case ☐ A asks for a decision ☐ B gives a reason once ☐ B agrees halfway and says which half.

> Sample: *A: There is a lamp and a list in the bag now. — B: I saw it. — A: Your keys should be in it. — B: I know where my keys are. — A: Not at four in the morning with the lights off. — B: No. Not then. — A: So shall I? — B: Put the spare set in. I am not giving you the ones I use.*

***Stretch:*** you are the planner and you have gone too far. Accept *The chocolate was too much and I know it. Keep the lamp and the keys in there and I will take the rest back out.* The marker is naming which part survives, not a general apology.

**7E: Writing — A Note About What to Do If (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ two *if* sentences ☐ one *unless* sentence ☐ who to ring ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (67 words): *If the post comes and it looks like a bill, leave it on the table. If the tap in the bathroom starts again, turn the small one under the sink and it stops. The window in the back room does not shut properly and it does not matter. Ring Amina downstairs before you ring me. Unless something is actually wet, it can wait until I am back.*

**Part 8 · Global Story — The City That Gets a Minute**

0. A long way off, near the coast. *(given)*
1. About three kilometres a second.
2. Stopping the trains, and opening the fire station doors.
3. Teaching the city what to do when the siren sounds.

> Item 3 is the point of the whole story and it is easy to read past. The sensors were the cheap part. What took thirty years was the September rehearsal, repeated until a siren on an ordinary Tuesday produces the same behaviour. A warning system is a habit with some wiring attached, and this is the Part 5 reading proved on a city.

**Part 8: Vocabulary in Context**

1. b · 2. d · 3. c · 4. a
Not needed: **e)** *water covering land that is usually dry*

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because we have a siren for the dam and it is tested on the first Monday of the month, so everybody knows the sound. This is different from where I live because nobody has ever told us what to do when it goes, so we all know the sound and none of us knows the plan. I think the most important thing is that the sound without the plan is worse than nothing, because it uses up the fright.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Bag by the Door**

0. Yuki. *(given)*
1. A lamp, a list of six telephone numbers, two sets of spare keys and a bar of chocolate. (Any three.)
2. He ate the chocolate once and replaced it.
3. He put his keys in it.

> The last two lines of the reading are a first conditional in both directions, and they are worth reading out: if nothing happens, the bag was a wasted Saturday; if something does, it was the best Saturday of the year. The same facts, two outcomes, and no way to know which until afterwards. That is what the grammar is for.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) join in** — it costs a spare set of keys and buys a share in something that might matter. It also means the plan becomes partly yours, and you will be asked about the next one.
- **(b) say nothing and leave it** — the bag harms nobody and the person who made it did not ask for a vote. It is also free riding, and the free rider knows it, which is a small cost paid daily.
- **(c) say it is too much** — somebody should say it, because a bag becomes a cupboard and a cupboard becomes a hobby. It is also the one option that makes a person who did something kind feel foolish.

> Sample (58 words): *I think I should join in, because a spare key costs me nothing and I keep losing the ones I use. Also, the water did come up in the spring and four people lost something, so this is not imaginary. This is better than saying nothing, because I would be using a plan I had not paid into.*
