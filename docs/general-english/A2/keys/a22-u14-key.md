**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 14.1)

Short, and almost nobody has been told it.

The **l** in *should* says nothing: /ʃʊd/. The same letter does the same nothing in *could* and *would*. Three very common words, one rule.
*Shouldn’t* is /ˈʃʊdn̩t/ — no **l**, and the middle **t** almost gone as well.
At the front of a question *should* weakens to /ʃəd/; at the end of an answer it goes strong.

The failure to listen for: learners who pronounce the **l** because it is in the spelling. They are understood every time, and they never sound like somebody who has heard the word said.

**Part 1: Four Problems, Four Answers**

0. A cold — rest, drink, wait — if it is still there after two weeks *(given)*

Accept any sensible pair, and expect the class to disagree about the last column, which is the point. The expected answers:

| The problem | What you should do | When to see a doctor |
|---|---|---|
| A headache that keeps coming back | drink more, sleep, write down when it comes | if the pattern does not change in two weeks |
| A tooth that hurts at night | see somebody; nothing else works | now, and not next month |
| A cut that is not healing | keep it clean and covered | if the edges go red or it is no better in a week |

The tooth row is the one to say out loud: the right answer is that there is no home answer, and a learner who writes *take a pill* has given the advice most people actually give.

**Part 1: Should, Shouldn’t or Had Better Not?**

1. d · 2. a · 3. b · 4. c · 5. e
Not needed: **f)** *somebody did it and felt worse*

Item 4 is the one to stop on: *you must see somebody today* is not advice at all, and the ladder in this exercise runs from *could* through *should* to *must*. Learners who treat all three as politeness levels have missed that the last one has left advice behind.

**Part 2 · Grammar — Should and Shouldn’t**

**Part 2: Notice**

Advice: **You should drink more water** · **You should see somebody if it gets worse** · **You shouldn’t go to work today** · **I shouldn’t have another coffee**
Asking for advice: **Should I see a doctor?** · **What should I take for it?**

The teaching point: *should* is the whole of English advice, and it does not change for politeness. Learners arriving from languages with a conditional for this often reach for *would* and sound strange. The fourth line is worth naming too: *I shouldn’t* is advice a person gives themselves, out loud, usually while doing the thing anyway.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

**Part 2: Advice, Advice Against, or Asking?**

0. advice *(given)* · 1. against · 2. asking · 3. advice · 4. asking

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. You **should rest** for two days. *(no* to *after* should*)*
2. You **shouldn’t** go to work. *(*should* makes its own negative)*
3. **Should I see** a doctor? *(no* to *in the question either)*
4. He **should** drink more water. *(*should* never takes* -s*)*

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One *should*, one *shouldn’t*.

> Sample: *You should put the telephone in another room an hour before you go to bed. You shouldn’t tell yourself you will catch up at the weekend, because you have been saying that since March.*

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: Advice You Give and Take**

Marking points: ☐ two *should* ☐ one *shouldn’t* ☐ a reason in the third answer.

> Sample: *Where I come from you should drink something hot with lemon and go to bed, and nobody has ever checked whether the lemon does anything. The advice I think is wrong is that you should sweat it out under four blankets. I ask my sister first, because she is a pharmacist and because she tells me when it is nothing.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *Should I…?* questions ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **what to drink** (water / warm water with salt) · **bed or moving** (stay in bed / get up and move) · and the pair must find that **taking nothing** and **five days** are the same on both. Pairs who report four have guessed; the trap is that two of the four rows match.

> Sample exchange: *A: What should I drink? — B: Warm water with salt. — A: Just water. One. Should I stay in bed? — B: Get up and move. — A: Stay in bed. Two. Should I take anything? — B: No. — A: Same. When should I see somebody? — B: Five days. — A: Same. So three.*

**Part 4: Asking for Advice**

Marking points: ☐ A says the problem in four words ☐ A asks what to take ☐ A asks when to worry ☐ B asks two questions before answering ☐ B gives the cheap answer too.

Card A must produce: a short problem, plain answers, two questions.
Card B must produce: two questions first, a cheap answer, a threshold, no alarm.

The commonest fault is B answering before asking. Stop them. Everything useful in this exchange is in the two questions, and a learner who gives advice without them is doing what the screen does in the Part 5 reading.

***Stretch:*** they should see a doctor today and do not want to. Accept *I would go today rather than Monday, and not because I think it is serious. It is easier to be told it is nothing on a Tuesday than to spend the weekend wondering.* The marker is urgency without fear.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ what people do where they grew up ☐ one *should* and one *shouldn’t* ☐ an opinion about which part works.

> Sample (51 words): *At home you should drink chicken soup and stay out of a draught, and the draught part is nonsense. The soup is warm salty water, which is most of what anybody with a cold actually needs. You shouldn’t go out with wet hair, I was told, every winter, for eighteen years.*

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: Advice for Somebody Ill (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ two *should* ☐ one *shouldn’t* ☐ when to see a doctor ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *You should do nothing at all tomorrow, and I mean nothing, not working quietly from bed. You shouldn’t answer anybody who writes to you about Thursday. Drink more than you want to. If the fever is still there on Sunday, or if it goes and comes back, ring the surgery on Monday morning rather than waiting.*

**Part 6: Should People Work When They Are Ill? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (53 words): *Nobody should, and almost everybody does. The real reason is not that people are brave; it is that staying at home costs money in most jobs and costs standing in all of them. Until the person at the top of an office stays at home with a cold, nobody below them ever will.*

**Part 6: Advice You Were Given (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ who gave the advice ☐ what it was ☐ whether you took it ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *A teacher told me I should never read in a moving car and I would ruin my eyes. My eyes are fine and the advice was wrong. I did stop reading in cars, for about twenty years, because she said it with such certainty that I did not think to check until I was nearly thirty.*

**Part 6: Reflection — Advice I Give and Do Not Take (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what you tell people ☐ what you do ☐ why ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (46 words): *I tell everybody they should see somebody about a thing rather than wait. I waited eleven months about a shoulder. The difference is that when it is somebody else, the question is simple, and when it is me, it is a week I do not have.*

**Part 7 · Real-World File — At the Chemist and the Surgery**

**7A: Phrase Bank**

1. d · 2. b · 3. a · 4. e
Not needed: **c)** *how much it will cost*

Item 1 is the one to teach hardest. English medicine opens with length, every time: *it started on Tuesday*. A learner who opens with how bad it feels instead will be asked for the length anyway, and has spent their first sentence.

**7B: The Appointment You Nearly Did Not Make** (Track 14.5)

0. That it is probably nothing. *(given)*
1. That she said it twice in one minute.
2. That anybody who truly thinks it is nothing does not mention it twice.
3. Make the appointment and be wrong; being wrong takes twenty minutes.

**7C: Controlled Practice — Describing How You Feel**

0. Say what is wrong, in one short sentence — **1** *(given)*
Say what you have already tried — **4**
Say how long it has been there — **2**
Say why you came today — **5**
Say what makes it better or worse — **3**

**7D: Role-Play — Asking Whether It Is Worth Asking**

Marking points: ☐ A says it is probably nothing ☐ A repeats it without noticing ☐ A pushes back once ☐ B does not guess what is wrong ☐ B gives one reason, not four ☐ B lets A decide.

> Sample: *A: It is nothing, really. — B: All right. — A: I mean, it is probably nothing. — B: That is the second time. — A: Is it? — B: It is. — A: Fine. I will ring them in the morning.*

***Stretch:*** they decide not to go and you think they should. Accept *I think you should, and I have said so, and it is your call. The offer to come with you stays open.* The marker is one more sentence and then a real stop.

**7E: Writing — A Note to a Doctor’s Desk (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what is wrong in one sentence ☐ how long ☐ what you have tried ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (66 words): *I would like an appointment in the next week or two, nothing urgent. My left knee gives way about once a fortnight, with no pain and no warning, and it started in the spring. I have changed my shoes and it made no difference. I am not in any discomfort; I would just like somebody to look at it before the winter. Any day except Wednesday.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Week Everybody Had It**

0. Nine days. *(given)*
1. Yuki.
2. She shut the shop, for the only two days in eleven years.
3. Carrying bread up four flights to people half his age.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) say nothing** — the only one that takes them at their word, which is what an adult is owed. It is also what everybody does when they would rather not be involved, and the two are indistinguishable from outside.
- **(b) ask once and leave it** — honest, brief, and it puts the decision where it belongs. It also asks a person who feels terrible to do the work of answering.
- **(c) bring something round without asking** — needs no answer and can be ignored. It can also be a way of deciding for somebody that they are worse than they say.

> Sample (56 words): *I think I should ask once and leave it, because an adult who says they are fine has told me what they want. Also, one question is easy to answer and easy to ignore. This is better than bringing soup round, because turning up at a door makes somebody get out of bed to take it.*
