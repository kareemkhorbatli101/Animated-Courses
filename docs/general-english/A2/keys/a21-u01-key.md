**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 1.1)

Stress as printed: rou-**TINE** · com-**MUTE** · ap-**POINT**-ment · **COL**-league · **U**-su-al-ly · **NEIGH**-bour

Note that *routine* and *commute* take the stress on the second syllable, while *colleague*, *usually* and *neighbour* take it on the first. Learners who stress *ROU-tine* are following their first language, not English.

The *-s* ending, three sounds:
/s/ after /p t k f θ/ — works, starts, gets, waits, stops
/z/ after a vowel or a voiced sound — lives, opens, reads, knows, goes
/ɪz/ after /s z ʃ tʃ dʒ/ — finishes, closes, watches, washes, changes

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: Discussing Routines**

Listen for present simple with *usually / every day* and present continuous with *this week / at the moment*. The second question is the one that forces the continuous.

> Sample: *I usually get up at half past six, but not at the weekend. This week I am travelling to another city for work, so I am getting up at five. I see my flatmate every day and the woman in the bakery every morning.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ questions in the present simple ☐ one question in the present continuous for *this week* ☐ exactly three differences reported.

Three differences: **workplace** (bookshop / hospital) · **transport** (bus / bike) · **this week** (evening class / working nights).
The get-up time (6.30) is the same in both — learners who report four differences have guessed.

> Sample exchange: *A: What time do you get up? — B: At half past six. — A: Me too. Where do you work? — B: I work in a hospital. — A: I work in a bookshop. How do you travel? — B: By bike. — A: I take the bus. And what are you doing this week? — B: I’m working nights.*

**Part 4: New Neighbour Role Play**

Marking points: ☐ both partners speak ☐ present simple for work and home ☐ at least one present continuous ☐ the exchange closes politely.

Card A must produce: a greeting, a name and floor, a workplace, two questions.
Card B must produce: a greeting, a statement that they are new, a job, two questions.

> Sample: *A: Hello — you’re new here, aren’t you? — B: Yes, this is my first week. I’m Yuki. — A: I’m Maya, flat 2. I work in the bookshop on the corner. Do you work near here? — B: I work from home, actually. Is the shop downstairs open on Sundays? — A: No, that’s the one day Amina closes.*

***Stretch:*** accept *I’m not sure, actually — I’ll ask Amina and let you know* or similar. The marker is a polite admission plus an offer, not an invented answer.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ at least one present-simple routine sentence ☐ at least one present-continuous *at the moment* sentence ☐ the person is identified before anything is said about them.

> Sample (51 words): *I want to talk about Mr Okonkwo, my neighbour on the fourth floor. He is a retired teacher, and he is seventy. He reads his newspaper every afternoon by the window, and he watches the whole street. At the moment he is waiting for an appointment at the clinic on Thursday.*

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: Describe a Person’s Day (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ at least one present simple routine sentence ☐ at least one present continuous *at the moment* sentence ☐ two time expressions ☐ 50–70 words ☐ capitals and full stops.

> Sample (53 words): *My brother gets up at five, because he drives a bus. First he makes tea, then he walks to the depot. He usually starts at six and finishes at two, so he is at home in the afternoon. At the moment he is doing a training course, so on Thursdays he finishes late.*

**Part 6: Why Routines Matter (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion in the first sentence ☐ two or three reasons, each with *because* or a linker ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *I do not think a fixed routine is always good. It helps in the morning, because nobody wants to think before coffee. But a routine can make a week disappear, and you remember nothing. I prefer a routine for small things and no routine for the evening. That way the days are not all the same.*

**Part 6: A Message to a New Neighbour (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ greeting and close ☐ two useful facts about the place ☐ an offer of help ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (53 words): *Hi Yuki, Welcome to the building. I’m Dani, flat 3. Two things that help: the hot water is slow before eight, so wait a few minutes, and the number 7 bus stops right outside. Amina downstairs will change a note for you if you need coins for the machine. Knock any time. Dani*

**Part 6: Reflection — My Perfect Weekday (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ three or four times of day named ☐ an activity at each ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (48 words): *My perfect weekday is quiet. At seven I drink coffee and nobody talks to me. At nine I start work, and I finish at three. In the afternoon I swim. In the evening I cook something that takes an hour, and I do not look at my phone.*

**7D: Role-Play — Welcoming a New Arrival**

Marking points: ☐ an opener that does not assume ☐ two facts a newcomer could not know ☐ an offer with a time attached ☐ the new arrival asks, not only answers.

Card A must produce: an opener, a name and floor, two local facts, an offer of help.
Card B must produce: a move-in date, a job, two questions about the area, thanks.

> Sample: *A: You must be the new neighbour. — B: Yes, I moved in on Saturday. I’m Yuki. — A: I’m Maya, second floor. Two things: the post comes before nine, and Amina’s shop is closed on Sundays. — B: That’s useful. Is the hot water always slow? — A: In the morning, yes. Give it five minutes. Knock any time after seven.*

***Stretch:*** the honest-but-kind answer. Accept *The flat above is noisy on Fridays, but they stop by eleven and they’re fine if you ask* — a real fact plus something that makes it liveable. Do not accept *Everything is perfect.*

**7E: Writing — A Welcome Note (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a warm opening ☐ exactly three facts, clearly separated ☐ an offer of help ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (59 words): *Dear Yuki, Welcome to Alder Street. Three things for this week. First, the post: it comes before nine, so listen for the door. Second, the front door sticks — lift it a little and it opens. Third, Amina’s shop downstairs is closed on Sundays, so buy bread on Saturday. I’m on the second floor if you need anything. Maya*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — A New Arrival on Your Street**

0. The top flat at 14 Alder Street. *(given)*
1. She worked from home, so she did not meet people at an office, and she knew nobody in the city.
2. She knocked on Yuki’s door with a short note about the post and the bus.
3. She knew four of the six people at number 14, and she had started leaving her door open when she cooked.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (59 words): *I think I should put a message in the group chat, because then everybody knows at the same time and nobody is the one person who did nothing. Also, a message is easy to answer later. This is better than knocking on the door, because some people do not want a stranger at the door in their first week.*

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) a note through the door** — works for a shy person or somebody who works from home; it asks nothing of them immediately.
- **(b) wait for the stairs** — no pressure, nothing forced; but it can take weeks, and may never happen at all.
- **(c) the group chat** — reaches everybody at once and is easy; but a new person may not be in the chat yet, and a message to a group is not a welcome to a person.
