**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 9.1)

Directions are the clearest place in the course to hear English rhythm, because the content words and the function words sit next to each other in a fixed pattern.

The big words carry the sense and take the beat: *left*, *junction*, *ahead*, *bridge*, *square*.
*at the* collapses to one quick sound: /ətðə/. *to the* does the same: /təðə/.
The *a* of *ahead* is a schwa, not a full vowel: /əˈhed/, never /eɪˈhed/.

The failure to listen for: a learner who gives every word the same weight. It sounds polite and careful, and it is very hard to follow, because the listener has no way of hearing which words are the instruction. Drill it as rhythm first, with the class tapping the beats, and only then as words.

**Part 1: Four Kinds of Place**

0. A junction — four roads and no sign — *at* the junction *(given)*

Accept any sensible pair. The expected answers:

| Place | What you see there | How you say where it is |
|---|---|---|
| A square | an open space, benches, buildings round the edge | *in* the square |
| A roundabout | a circle of road, cars going one way | *at* the roundabout |
| A lane | a narrow road, no markings, often no sign | *in* or *down* the lane |

The preposition is the point of the exercise, not the description. *At* a junction and *at* a roundabout because each is one point you arrive at; *in* a square because a square has sides and you stand inside it.

**Part 2 · Grammar — Prepositions of Place, Time and Movement**

**Part 2: Notice**

Where: **on** the corner · **behind** the square · **in** the square
When: **at** seven · **On** Saturday
How somebody moves: **along** Alder Street and **over** the bridge · **towards** the junction · **round** the roundabout

The teaching point: *at*, *on* and *in* all do both jobs. The same three words tell you where something is and when something happens, and only what follows them decides which.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. at *(given)* · 1. on · 2. along · 3. in · 4. towards

**Part 2: Where, When or How?**

0. where *(given)* · 1. when · 2. how · 3. where · 4. when

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. The health centre is **behind the square**. *(no* of *after* behind*)*
2. Walk **to** the bridge and then turn left. *(*to* for where you end up;* at *is where you already are)*
3. Amina opens the shop **at** seven. *(*at* for a clock time)*
4. Yuki lives **in the top flat at number 14**. *(*in* a flat,* at *a number)*

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One sentence of place, one of movement.

> Sample: *I live on the second floor of a house between a baker and a launderette. From the bus stop you walk towards the church, past the postbox, and we are the blue door on the left.*

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: How You Find Your Way**

Marking points: ☐ at least two prepositions of place ☐ one preposition of movement ☐ a reason in the third answer.

> Sample: *I go by the shops, always. I cannot hold a map in my head at all, but I remember a red door and a bridge. The hardest place I ever tried to find was a flat above a shop with no number, in a lane behind a square, and I walked past it four times.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *Where is…?* and *Is it…?* questions ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **the shop** (on the corner / opposite the square) · **the bench** (in the square / by the junction) · **the health centre** (behind the square / next to the bridge). Pairs who report four have guessed.

> Sample exchange: *A: Where is the shop? — B: Opposite the square. — A: Mine is on the corner. Is the bench in the square? — B: No, by the junction. — A: Different. And the health centre? — B: Next to the bridge. — A: Behind the square on mine. Three.*

**Part 4: Asking the Way**

Marking points: ☐ A opens with *excuse me* ☐ A asks how far ☐ A repeats the last step back ☐ B asks where they came from ☐ B gives no more than two steps ☐ B names one landmark.

Card A must produce: an opening, the place, the distance, a check.
Card B must produce: a question first, two steps, a landmark, a time.

The commonest fault is B giving six steps. Stop the pair and make them do it in two, then let them carry on. Real directions are short because the listener cannot hold more.

***Stretch:*** you do not know the way either. Accept *I’m sorry, I don’t know this street at all — but the post office at the junction is open and they do know.* The marker is an honest no plus one useful fact, not a guess.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ four steps, in order ☐ at least one landmark ☐ one preposition of movement in each step.

> Sample (51 words): *Out of my door and left, along the lane to the end. Across the main road at the lights, not before them, because the traffic comes round the bend fast. Then right, towards the river, and the shop is the second door after the postbox. Four minutes if nobody stops me.*

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: Directions to Your Door (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ where to get off ☐ no more than three steps ☐ one landmark ☐ what too far looks like ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (58 words): *Get off at the second stop after the bridge, by the postbox. Cross the road and walk towards the church, about two minutes. We are in the lane that runs behind it, third gate along, and the gate is green. If you come out at the roundabout you have gone past us. Ring twice; the bell is quiet.*

**Part 6: A Map or a Person? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (52 words): *A map is better, because a map does not round the numbers or forget a turn. People are kind and people are wrong. The best answer I ever had was from a man who walked me two streets and left me facing the right way, and no map can do that part.*

**Part 6: A Place That Is Hard to Find (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ where it is, in prepositions ☐ why people walk past ☐ one preposition of place in each sentence ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (57 words): *The office I work in sits above a shop and behind a car park, and the door is between two other doors that look exactly the same. There is no sign at street level. Visitors ring the wrong bell, then the other wrong bell, then ours. We put a note on the gate and somebody took it.*

**Part 6: Reflection — The Time I Got Lost (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ where you were ☐ what went wrong ☐ what you did ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (46 words): *I got lost between two platforms in a station I use every week. I came up the wrong stairs and the whole place was the wrong way round. I stood still for a minute, which felt stupid, and then everything turned itself the right way again.*

**7C: Controlled Practice — Giving Directions Well**

0. Ask where the person is standing now — **1** *(given)*
Say how long it takes on foot — **4**
Give two steps, and no more — **2**
Tell them what to do if they go too far — **5**

**7D: Role-Play — Asking the Way**

Marking points: ☐ B asks where they came from before answering ☐ two steps only ☐ one landmark named ☐ a time given ☐ A repeats the last step back.

> Sample: *A: Excuse me, am I near the square? — B: Which way did you come? — A: Up from the bridge. — B: Then you are two minutes off. Straight ahead, past the green shop. — A: Past the green shop, and then the square? — B: Yes. — A: Thank you.*

***Stretch:*** the place closed last year. Accept *That closed last spring, I’m afraid. The new one is on the far side of the square, behind the library.* The marker is the bad news first and the alternative second, in that order.

**7E: Writing — Directions for a Driver (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ where the driver is now ☐ which way to come ☐ one landmark ☐ something about parking or the door ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (67 words): *You are at the lights by the market, I think. Turn away from the market and come along the lane for about two hundred metres. Ours is the only building with a yard in front of it, and there is a green van in the yard most days. Park behind the van. The door is below the level of the road, so go down the three steps.*

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because our bus stops have letters, and everybody says meet me at stop C. **Part 9 · Close to Home — The House With No Number**

0. No, it does not. *(given)*
1. Because the old houses at the top came down and nobody numbered what was left.
2. GREEN SHOP.
3. A piece of wood with a 14 painted on it.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) ask the city to number it again** — the only one that fixes it for everybody, for ever, including the people who move in next year. It is also the one nobody alive on that street expects to see finished.
- **(b) put up your own sign at the junction** — cheap, quick, and placed exactly where every stranger already stops. It has no standing, so anybody can take it down, and somebody usually does.
- **(c) tell everybody the landmark instead** — what the street already does, and it works. It only works for people who were told, which leaves out every driver, every visitor and every emergency.

> Sample (57 words): *I think I should ask the city to number it again, because a landmark is no use to an ambulance at two in the morning. Also, our own sign lasts until the first windy week. This is better than telling everybody the green shop, because the people who most need the number are the ones nobody told.*
