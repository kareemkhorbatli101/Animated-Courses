**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 19.1)

The joining word is the quietest thing in the sentence, and English often leaves it out altogether.

*Who* and *that* reduce to almost nothing in the middle of a line: *the man who mends shoes* runs together, and *the shop that sells bread* comes out /ðə ʃɒp ðət/ with the vowel gone. Then there is the harder case: *the shop I use sells bread*. The joining word is not quiet there, it is absent, and nothing marks the seam.

That is where learners come unstuck, and it is not a grammar problem. They have learned *that*, they listen for it, they do not hear it, and so they cannot tell where the first half of the sentence ends. They parse *the shop I use* as a complete clause and then have a spare verb.

Drill it by taking the word out rather than putting it in. Say *the shop that I use*, then *the shop I use*, and have them tell you which one you said. A class that can hear the gap can read English sentences twice as long as before.

**Part 1: Write About a Person (short)**

Open. Two or three sentences about a person near you, using *who* or *that* at least once. The unit prints a model; this is a second one.

**Part 2 · Grammar — Defining Relative Clauses**

**Part 2: Notice**

Joining a person: **the woman who runs the shop** · **the one who works nights** · **the one who carries books**
Joining a thing: **the shop that sells bread** · **the bus which goes to the hospital**

The one that could drop its joining word: none of these five, and that is worth saying. In every one of them the joining word is the subject of its own clause — *who runs*, *that sells* — and a subject cannot be dropped. It is only droppable when something else is the subject: *the shop I use*, *the bus he takes*. The Notice task asks the question so the class discovers the limit rather than being handed it.

The teaching point: this clause is not a decoration on a noun, it is how English says *which one*. *The woman* is not enough in a street of forty people; *the woman who runs the shop* is. Learners who meet the relative clause as extra information will produce it and then not use it, and go on saying *the woman* and being asked *which woman*.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. who *(given)* · 1. which (or that) · 2. where · 3. who (or that) · 4. that (or which)

> Accept *that* in items 1 and 4 and *that* or *who* in item 3, and say so. A key that insists on *which* for things teaches a distinction English does not keep.

**Part 2: Person, Thing or Place?**

0. person *(given)* · 1. thing · 2. place · 3. person · 4. thing

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. The woman **who** runs the shop opens at seven. *(*which* cannot take a person)*
2. The shop **that sells** bread is on the corner. *(the joining word is the subject; do not add *it*)*
3. This is the street **where** I live. *(a place takes *where*)*
4. Tomas is the one **who** works nights. *(*which* cannot take a person)*

> Item 2 is the error that outlives the others. *The shop that it sells bread* feels right to speakers of languages that keep the pronoun, and it will come back in free writing for months after this unit.

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One *who*, one *where*.

> Sample: *The woman who has the flat above mine plays one song on a Sunday and I have never minded it. The corner where the two roads meet is where everybody waits, because nobody trusts the shelter.*

**Part 3 · Listening**

**Part 3: The Woman Who Knows Which Bus Is Late** (Track 19.2)

0. True *(given)*
1. **Not Given** — nothing is said about a timetable.
2. **True** — “It’s nine years of it.”
3. **False** — Yuki says guessing is not knowing, and Amina does not move her.

Item 3 is the one to go through. Yuki says *which is knowing* near the end, which looks like agreement and is the opposite: she is pressing the point, and Amina answers *which is nine years*. Nobody concedes anything. A learner who takes the last thing somebody says as their view will answer True.

**Part 3: The Man Who Mends Shoes** (Track 19.3)

0. The heels of shoes. *(given)*
1. Forty years.
2. That a shoe mended at the heel will last three more winters.
3. Because everybody on that street walks in the same strange way.

> Item 3 is a joke and it is also evidence, and a class should be allowed to notice both. Maya’s reason for believing him is that his customers are identifiable by their walk, which is either proof that the mending works or proof that it does not.

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: Describing People Without Names**

Marking points: ☐ one *the person who…* ☐ one *the shop that…* ☐ one *the street where…* ☐ no names used.

> Sample: *The person I see most often and cannot name is the woman who opens the café at half past six, and I have been nodding at her for two years. The shop everybody uses is the one that stays open on Sundays, which is not the good one. I grew up on a street where every house had the same door, and the doors are all different now.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *Who is the one who…?* ☐ *Whose is the…?* ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **the man on the corner** (shoes / clocks) · **the singer** (nobody / number 12) · and the pair must find that **the woman who runs the shop** and **the tailor at number 9** match. A pair that reports two has asked *is there somebody who mends things* and got a yes from both; the question has to be what he mends.

> Sample exchange: *A: Who is the man on the corner? — B: The one who mends clocks. — A: Mine mends shoes. One. Is there a tailor? — B: At number 9. — A: Number 9. Same. Anybody who sings? — B: Number 12. — A: Nobody. Two. And the shop? — B: The woman who runs it. — A: Same. So two? — B: Two, unless the tailor is a different tailor.*

**Part 4: Describing Somebody to Somebody Else**

Marking points: ☐ A asks who it was ☐ A says they know the one ☐ B names the person by what they do ☐ B narrows it with one more detail ☐ B gives the reason last.

Card A must produce: the opening question, a question about place, a recognition, and a question about themselves.
Card B must produce: a description instead of a name, one narrowing detail, a pause for recognition, and the reason held back to the end.

The commonest fault is B giving the reason in the second turn. The card holds it to the end on purpose: *he asked after you* is the point of the conversation, and the point of a conversation goes last or there is nothing to have it about.

***Stretch:*** your partner dislikes the person you are describing. Accept *He is the one who argued with your brother. He is also the only person on that street who mends anything, and I am going to say both.* The marker is both halves in one turn, with no apology for either.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ three people ☐ no names at all ☐ each one described by what they do.

> Sample (51 words): *There is a man who parks across my gate about once a week. There is a woman who has lived in the end house longer than anybody and who speaks to nobody. And there is a boy who delivers the papers at six and has never delivered mine to my door.*

**Part 5 · Reading**

**Part 5: Why Streets Describe People Instead of Naming Them**

0. About six. *(given)*
1. **C)** A sound and a person
2. **D)** Because descriptions do not need an introduction
3. **B)** It sticks

> Item 3 is the one to sit with. The reading argues that descriptions are efficient and then shows the bill: a man who parked badly once is still the one who parks badly, years later, and nobody has driven with him since. Ask the class what their street’s description of them would be and whether it is current.

**Part 5: Three Places Named After One Person**

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: A Person in Your Street (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ *who* or *that* twice ☐ no name used ☐ one thing they did ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (61 words): *There is a woman who waters the plants in the stairwell, and they are not her plants. Nobody knows whose they are. She is the person who moved them off the landing when the carpet came, and she moved them back the same evening. I have thanked her once. She looked at me as though I had accused her of something.*

**Part 6: Is It Rude to Describe People by What They Do? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (61 words): *It depends entirely on whether they chose it. The woman who runs the shop chose the shop. The man who parks badly chose nothing; he was in a hurry once. The test is easy and nobody applies it: would the person use the description about themselves? If they would, it is a fact. If they would not, it is a verdict.*

**Part 6: A Place You Know Well (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ *where* at least once ☐ what happens there ☐ one thing that changed ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (52 words): *The bench where the path turns is where everybody in my family stops, and the view from it is of a car park. There was a field. The field went before I was born. We still stop, we still look, and nobody in four generations has said anything about the car park.*

**Part 6: Reflection — How Others Describe You (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ the description others use ☐ whether it is fair ☐ how it started ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (50 words): *I am the one who is always on the telephone outside. It is fair. It started because the walls here are thin and it continued because the street is more interesting. Three people have told me they thought I worked in sales. I do not, and I have stopped explaining.*

**Part 7 · Real-World File — Pointing Somebody Out**

**7A: Phrase Bank**

1. b · 2. d · 3. e · 4. c
Not needed: **a)** *how much it will cost*

Item 3 is the one to teach, and it is the one learners cannot use. *You know the one* asks the listener to do the work, and it only lands if you are right that they know. Said to somebody who does not, it is a small social failure with no way out of it except the detail you should have given first.

**7B: The Man Everybody Has a Different Name For** (Track 19.5)

0. He cleans the lane. *(given)*
1. The caretaker.
2. From the name on the letters she takes in for him.
3. Because Amina has called him the caretaker for nine years.

> Item 3 is the whole exchange and it is not laziness. Maya knows the man’s name, knows Amina is wrong, and has decided that nine years of a habit is not hers to correct. Ask the class whether she is being kind or cowardly. Both answers are defensible and the conversation is better than either.

**7C: Controlled Practice — Pointing Somebody Out**

0. Say what they are wearing or carrying — **1** *(given)*
Say what they do, if you know it — **2**
Say where they were last — **3**
Accept the correction if you were wrong — **5**
Say which one they are not — **4**

> The order runs from what the listener can check now to what only you know, and the correction comes last because it can only come after a wrong guess. A learner who starts with the job has started with the thing the listener cannot see.

**7D: Role-Play — Two Names for One Person**

Marking points: ☐ A uses their own name for him ☐ A says how long ☐ A does not apologise ☐ B gives the real name ☐ B says how they know ☐ B lets the other one keep theirs, out loud.

> Sample: *A: The caretaker left a note. — B: Mr Hall. — A: Who is Mr Hall? — B: The man you call the caretaker. — A: Is that his name? — B: It is on everything that comes for him. — A: Nine years I have been saying the caretaker. — B: And you can go on saying it. He answers to both.*

***Stretch:*** the name you have used for years is one he dislikes. Accept *Nobody told me, and that is not an excuse, because I never asked. I will use Mr Hall from now on and I will say so to the two people I taught it to.* The marker is naming what they will do about the people they passed it on to.

**7E: Writing — A Note Describing Somebody to Expect (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ two things you can see ☐ one thing they do ☐ what to do ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (69 words): *A woman is coming about the roof, probably before nine. She is short, she has a yellow van, and she is the one who did the house opposite in the spring, so Mr Okonkwo will vouch for her. She will want to go up through the top flat. Let her. If a man comes instead, he is her son and he is not the one to ask about money.*

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Town Where Every Cloth Has a Name**

0. A name for every pattern. *(given)*
1. Roughly that one person does not rule a nation.
2. In thin strips, on a loom a man sits inside, and then sewn together side by side.
3. Because the strips are sewn together afterwards, so the pattern has to line up across all of them.

> Item 1 is the one to give time to. A chief who wore that pattern was making a political statement without saying a word, in a room where everybody could read it. Ask the class what in their own clothing is read that way, and the answers are usually about football or about money.

**Part 8: Vocabulary in Context**

1. b · 2. d · 3. c · 4. a
Not needed: **e)** *a small flat sign on a wall, with a name on it*

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because the scarf you wear to a match says which half of the city you grew up in, and people read it before they look at your face. This is different from where I live because ours says one thing and theirs says two hundred, and ours cannot be worn wrongly by accident. I think the most important thing is that a pattern with a name in it can be a mistake, and a pattern with no name cannot, which makes the first kind worth more and much harder to give as a present.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Woman in Flat 1 Nobody Has Met**

0. Four years. *(given)*
1. She painted the staircase, both flights, and left no note.
2. That she pays for her paper by the month, says four words a year, and has never said one of them first.
3. As permission, and she may be wrong about that.

> Item 3 is the honest line in the reading and the reason the decision task works. Yuki has read a silence as a yes because that is the reading she wants, and the text says so about her rather than leaving the class to notice. A strong group can be asked what Mr Okonkwo’s silence more likely means.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) knock** — four years is long enough that nobody else ever will, and somebody who paints a shared staircase is not hostile. It also puts her on the spot in her own doorway, where the only polite answer is one she may not want to give.
- **(b) write a note** — it asks nothing immediately and can be ignored without anybody losing face, which is exactly what a person who says four words a year might want. It is also the thing Yuki has already done twice and not posted, so it may be the choice that feels like action and is not.
- **(c) leave her alone** — the staircase was a message and the message was not *come and talk to me*. It is also the option that lets a building of six people keep congratulating itself on respecting somebody it has simply never bothered with.

> Sample (59 words): *I think I should write a note, because it lets her answer or not answer and neither is awkward. Also, I would rather she heard it from one person than found six of us discussing her. This is better than knocking, because a knock makes her decide in a doorway, and four years says she would rather decide slowly.*
