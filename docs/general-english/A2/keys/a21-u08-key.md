**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 8.1)

This is the hardest drill in the book so far, and it is worth ten minutes.

In the middle of a sentence, *can* is weak: /kən/, almost swallowed. *I can swim* sounds like *I kn swim*.
*Can’t* is long and clear: /kɑːnt/. The vowel is the signal, not the **t**, which often disappears before a consonant.
At the end of a sentence, *can* goes strong: *Yes, I **can**.* /kæn/

The failure to listen for: a learner who says *can* loudly in the middle of a sentence is heard as saying *can’t*, and the conversation goes backwards from there. Drill the minimal pair until the class can tell them apart with their eyes shut.

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: Talking About What You Can Do**

Marking points: ☐ *can* and *can’t* both used ☐ one *could* about childhood ☐ a reason in the third answer.

> Sample: *I can read a map, which seems to be unusual now. I could stand on my hands when I was twelve and I certainly cannot now. I want to learn to swim properly, and what stops me is that the pool is full of eight-year-olds who are better than me.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *Can you…?* questions ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **driving** (cannot / can) · **the instrument** (can / cannot) · **cooking** (well / a little). **Swimming is the same** — both can. Pairs who report four have guessed.

> Sample exchange: *A: Can you swim? — B: Yes. — A: Me too. Can you drive? — B: Yes. — A: I can’t. How well can you cook? — B: A little. — A: I cook well. Can you play an instrument? — B: No. — A: I can.*

**Part 4: Asking for a Favour**

Marking points: ☐ A asks before explaining ☐ the request is specific, with a time ☐ B does not say yes immediately ☐ B names one thing they cannot do ☐ thanks.

Card A must produce: a careful opening, the detail, the duration, thanks.
Card B must produce: a question first, a limit, a condition, agreement.

***Stretch:*** you have to say no. Accept *I am sorry, I really can’t this week — ask me again next week and I mean that.* The marker is a refusal with a door left open, not an excuse.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ what they could not do at first ☐ what they can do now ☐ one honest limit.

> Sample (56 words): *I started learning to swim at thirty-four. In the first lesson I could not put my face in the water, and I was the only adult in a pool full of children. Now I can swim half a kilometre without stopping. I still cannot jump in from the side and I do not intend to learn.*

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: Something I Can Do (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ two *can* sentences ☐ one *cannot* ☐ one *could* about the past ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (58 words): *I can sew well enough to mend anything I own. My grandmother could make a coat from nothing, and I cannot do that, but she sat with me for a winter when I was nine. I can take up a hem on a bus. I cannot cut a pattern, and I have stopped pretending that I will learn.*

**Part 6: Is It Harder to Learn Later? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (53 words): *It is harder, but not for the reason people give. A child has nothing else to do and no dignity to protect. An adult has a job, a tired evening and a strong memory of being bad at things. The brain is fine. It is the hour and the pride that are missing.*

**Part 6: Asking for Help in Writing (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ the favour in one sentence ☐ the time ☐ an easy way out for the other person ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *Amina, could you do me a favour on Saturday morning? I have to be at the hospital at seven and the box is coming at nine. It is one small box. If the shop is busy, please just say so — I can ask them to leave it with somebody else. Thank you either way. Tomas*

**Part 6: Reflection — Something I Want to Learn (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what you cannot do ☐ why you want it ☐ what stops you ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (47 words): *I cannot swim, and I am forty-one. I grew up two hours from any water and nobody thought about it. I want to be able to go into the sea without calculating. What stops me is the idea of a lesson with my own students in it.*

**7C: Controlled Practice — Asking Well**

0. Ask whether it is possible at all — **1** *(given)*
Thank them, whatever they say — **5**
Say exactly what you need and when — **2**
Say what happens if they cannot — **4**
Wait for an honest answer — **3**

**7D: Role-Play — Asking and Refusing**

Marking points: ☐ B asks a question before answering ☐ the no is clear, not buried ☐ a real reason given ☐ an alternative offered ☐ A accepts without pressing.

> Sample: *A: Could you take a box on Thursday? — B: What time? — A: Between nine and one. — B: I’m sorry, I can’t, I’d be asleep. — A: Of course, I didn’t think. — B: Ask Amina, she’s open. — A: I will. Thank you.*

***Stretch:*** the person is in real trouble and you still cannot help. Accept *I can’t do this, and I’m not going to pretend I might. What I can do is telephone Amina for you now.* The marker is a plain no plus one concrete thing.

**7E: Writing — Asking Somebody You Do Not Know Well (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ who you are ☐ the favour in one sentence ☐ an explicit way out ☐ a tone that does not over-apologise ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (68 words): *Hello, this is Dani from flat 3 — we met on the stairs last week. I am sorry to ask so soon. My bicycle lock has broken and I am waiting for a new one. Would it be possible to leave the bike inside the entrance for two nights? If that is awkward for any reason, please say so and I will find another answer. Thank you. Dani*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — What Mr Okonkwo Cannot Do**

0. Mend a chair, reline a coat and teach a child to read. *(given)*
1. Carry a full shopping bag up four floors without stopping twice.
2. She started putting his Friday shopping into two light bags instead of one heavy one.
3. He noticed all three and said nothing, which is a kind of thank you.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) offer directly** — the only honest option, and the only one that treats the person as an adult who can say no. It also forces them to admit something out loud, which is the hard part.
- **(b) help without saying anything** — costs them nothing and preserves the thing they are protecting. It can also be a way of deciding for somebody what they need.
- **(c) wait until they ask** — respects them completely, and is the right answer with people who do ask. The people who most need help are often the ones who never will.

> Sample (53 words): *I think I should offer directly, because helping in secret decides for somebody what they can manage. Also, an offer can be refused, and a refusal is information. This is better than waiting until they ask, because somebody who has not asked in four months is not about to start on the fifth.*
