**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 20.1)

Reported speech is the one grammar point in this volume with a pronunciation rule that is purely about stopping.

Direct speech has a seam in it. *He said, “I am tired”* has a real break before *I*, and two separate stress patterns either side of it. Reported speech has no seam at all: *he said he was tired* is one run, evenly stressed, with nothing marking where the reporting stops and the reported starts.

The bigger fault is the question. *She asked, “Where is it?”* rises at the end, because it is a question. *She asked where it was* does not, because it is not one any more — it is a statement about a question. Learners keep the rise, out of habit and because the word *where* feels interrogative, and the listener then hears a question and answers it. That misfire is worth staging once in class: ask a learner to report a question with the rise left on, answer it, and let the room see what happened.

Drill it by having them finish low and flat, on a falling hand. *She asked where it was.* Full stop, heard.

**Part 1: Four Things Somebody Said**

0. “The van is outside.” — She said the van was outside *(given)*

Expected: *He said he had not seen it* · *She asked where the key was* · *He told me to ring him that night*. Accept *she* for *he* throughout, and accept *that* after *said*.

Mark three things, in this order of importance: the tense has moved back, there is no question mark on item 3, and item 4 uses *told* with a person. An answer that keeps the quotation marks has not done the task at all.

**Part 1: Said, Told or Asked?**

1. b · 2. c · 3. e · 4. f · 5. d
Not needed: **a)** *a hundred years*

> Items 1 and 2 are the whole of the unit’s most common error. *Said* takes the words straight after it; *told* takes a person first. There is no reason for this, it is simply what those two verbs do, and no amount of explaining will fix it faster than drilling the pair.

> Item 2 is a joke with a grammar point inside it. *There is no news, which Amina says is the best kind* works because *news* is uncountable and singular in English — *the news is*, never *the news are* — and a learner translating from a language where it is plural will write *there are no news* here and nowhere else.

**Part 2 · Grammar — Reported Speech**

**Part 2: Notice**

What changes, in the three pairs: *is* becomes *was*; *I have not seen* becomes *he had not seen*, so both the person and the tense move; and *Where is the key?* becomes *where the key was*, losing its question mark and putting the subject back in front of the verb.

The teaching point: there is one rule and it is mechanical. Everything moves back one step — present to past, past to past perfect, *will* to *would* — and the pronouns move to match the new speaker. Learners find reported speech hard not because the rule is subtle but because three things change at once and they can only hold two.

So teach it as three separate passes over the same sentence. Tense first, then person, then word order if it was a question. A learner who does all three at once gets two right and one wrong, every time, and cannot see which.

**Part 2: Grammar Focus Box**

The fourth row is the one to dwell on. *Where is it?* → *where it was* reverses the subject and the verb, and that reversal is invisible to a learner who is concentrating on the tense. It is also the row that produces the error that survives longest into free writing.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. was *(given)* · 1. had not · 2. was · 3. would · 4. told

> Item 4 is the only one that needs a verb rather than an auxiliary, and it needs *told* because a person follows. A learner who writes *said* has produced *she said me to ring her*, which is the error Part 1’s matching task was built to prevent and which will appear here anyway.

**Part 2: Said or Told?**

0. said *(given)* · 1. told · 2. said · 3. told · 4. said

> Item 4 — *Mr Okonkwo said one sentence* — is the odd one, and it is correct. *Said* can take a direct object that is not a person: *said one sentence*, *said nothing*, *said four words*. What it cannot take is a person. Worth naming if somebody asks, and not worth raising if nobody does.

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. She **told me** the van was outside. *(*said* takes no person)*
2. He asked where **the key was**. *(a reported question keeps statement order)*
3. He said he **had** not seen it. *(one step back, not the present perfect)*
4. He **said** that he would ring. *(or **told me** that he would ring — *told* needs a person)*

> Item 4 has two correct answers and both should be accepted. *He told that he would ring* is wrong in exactly one way, and the learner may fix it by changing the verb or by adding a person. Marking only one of those right teaches the wrong lesson.

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One *said*, one *told*.

> Sample: *My sister said she would be here by six and she was here by nine. The man at the garage told me it was the battery, and he told everybody it was the battery, and twice a year he is right.*

**Part 2: Grammar Review**

1. **C)** He asked where the key was. · 2. **D)** She told me the van was outside.

> Option D in item 1 is the near-miss worth pointing at: *He asked where the key was?* has the grammar right and the punctuation wrong. It is the most common thing learners actually write, because they have learned the word order and kept the mark.

**Part 3 · Listening**

**Part 3: What Amina Said About the Van** (Track 20.2)

0. True *(given)*
1. **True** — “Tomas said Monday.”
2. **False** — “Tomas wasn’t here on Monday.”
3. **Not Given** — Maya says *you are, apparently*, but nothing says she has done it before.

Items 1 and 2 sit next to each other and have to be kept apart. Tomas *said* Monday — that is true, he said it. He *was not* there on Monday — that is also true, so the statement that Maya thinks he was is false. A learner who answers item 1 False has decided that a false statement cannot have been said, which is the exact confusion reported speech exists to clear up: *he said he had been* reports the claim without endorsing it, and Maya says so in the script.

**Part 3: The Message Nobody Replied To** (Track 20.3)

0. On Thursday. *(given)*
1. Whether she was free on Saturday.
2. He told her what they were doing on Saturday.
3. She will reply properly.

> Item 2 is the whole exchange. Dani asked a question and then answered it himself in the next line, which left Maya with nothing to reply to, and four days of silence that looked like rudeness. Worth a minute of class time: everybody present has sent that message.

**Part 3: Six People, Six Versions** (Track 20.4)

0. Amina → she said twenty minutes *(given)*
1. c · 2. d · 3. a · 4. b
Not needed: **e)** *wrote down what everybody said*

> The spare letter is Yuki’s, and this is the fourth and last time the course uses her as the distractor. She is never in Column A in these tasks because what she does is record the others, which is also why the unit can exist.

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: Passing On What Somebody Said**

Marking points: ☐ one *she said* or *he said* ☐ one *told me* with a person ☐ one *I asked whether* ☐ the tense one step back.

> Sample: *My neighbour told me the water would be off on Tuesday. I said nobody had put a note through, and she said the note was on the main door, where nobody looks. I asked whether it was all day and she said she had not read that far.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *What did they say?* ☐ *Who told you that?* ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **the vehicle** (a van / a bigger van) · **how long** (twenty minutes / an hour) · **the driver** (not mentioned / asked for directions) · and the pair must find that **Tuesday** matches. A pair that reports two has let the vehicle pass, because both cards have something van-shaped on them and the difference is the size.

> Sample exchange: *A: What did they say it was? — B: A bigger van. — A: Mine said a van. One. How long? — B: An hour. — A: Twenty minutes. Two. Which day? — B: Tuesday. — A: Tuesday. Did anybody mention the driver? — B: He asked for directions. — A: Nobody mentioned a driver at all. Three. — B: Who told you yours? — A: Amina. — B: That explains the twenty minutes.*

**Part 4: Telling Somebody What Was Said**

Marking points: ☐ A asks what was said ☐ A offers a different version ☐ B reports one person’s words ☐ B reports the other’s exactly ☐ B answers the hard question in one word ☐ B says what to write.

Card A must produce: the opening question, a competing version, a question about truth, and a question about what to record.
Card B must produce: two reports, one of them exact, a one-word answer, and a decision about the record.

The commonest fault is B flattening the difference — *they both said about half an hour* — which is the behaviour the Part 5 reading describes and the thing the task is built to catch. The right answer is the awkward one: write both, and write who said which.

***Stretch:*** the version you reported first was your own and it was wrong. Accept *Stop. That was me, not Amina. I have been saying twenty minutes because I thought it was twenty minutes.* The marker is correcting the attribution mid-conversation, which is harder than correcting the fact.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ three different people reported ☐ one event ☐ said or told used correctly each time.

> Sample (58 words): *My mother said the house had been empty for two years. My aunt said it had been eighteen months and that my mother always rounds up. The man next door told me a family had been in it the whole time and had simply never opened the curtains. I have not worked out which of them is wrong.*

**Part 5 · Reading**

**Part 5: Why a Story Gets Shorter Every Time It Is Told**

0. It gets shorter and surer. *(given)*
1. **B)** The ones nobody can use
2. **A)** Certainty
3. **C)** Lied

> Item 3 is the line the reading is built around, and it should be read out. Nobody lied anywhere along the line. The story still arrived false, because *I think* was the least useful two words in the sentence and every teller dropped them. That is a more uncomfortable account of how rumours work than the one most people hold, and it is the right note to end a course on.

**Part 5: Vocabulary in Context**

1. c · 2. d · 3. b · 4. a
Not needed: **e)** *the paper cover a letter goes in*

**Part 5: Three Ways News Used to Travel**

0. As fast as the fastest thing going that way. *(given)*
1. About a hundred and sixty kilometres a day.
2. Because they could not work at night, or in fog.
3. Because a telegraph message was paid for by the word.

> Item 3 has a nice consequence a class can be asked to work out before reading it: a hundred years of news reads strangely short, because it was written by people being charged by the word. The medium set the prose style, and the prose style outlived the medium.

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: Reporting What Somebody Said (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ *said* once ☐ *told* once, with a person ☐ the tense one step back ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (59 words): *The woman in the post office told me the parcel had gone back on Friday. I said nobody had left a card. She said the driver had said he had left a card. I said I had been in all day. She said she knew, and that he says that about everybody, and that she had written it down.*

**Part 6: Should You Pass On What You Hear? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (61 words): *Pass it on with the name attached or not at all. The name is the only thing that lets anybody check it, and a story that cannot be checked is the only kind that travels far. I have twice been the person who repeated something without a name on it, and both times it came back to me as a fact.*

**Part 6: A Message You Wish You Had Not Sent (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what you sent ☐ what came back ☐ what you do now ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (58 words): *I sent a long message at midnight to somebody who was asleep, and then I sent a second one apologising for the first. She replied to neither and rang me at nine and was perfectly normal. I had told her a great deal about myself and nothing had happened. I now write at midnight and send at nine.*

**Part 6: Reflection — The Story Your Family Tells About You (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ which story ☐ whether it is true ☐ how you know ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (52 words): *My family says I learned to read from a shop sign and would not be told otherwise. I was three. I remember the sign and not the learning. My brother says it was his sign and his shop, and he was six, and he may well be right about all of it.*

**Part 7 · Real-World File — Passing On a Message**

**7A: Phrase Bank**

1. c · 2. a · 3. d · 4. e
Not needed: **b)** *how much it will cost*

Item 2 is the one to teach. *Apparently* hands the information over and keeps the responsibility, and it is doing a great deal of work for one word. A learner who has it can repeat anything safely; a learner without it either refuses to repeat things or ends up owning them.

**7B: The Sentence Mr Okonkwo Said a Week Later** (Track 20.5)

0. One. *(given)*
1. Six days.
2. The driver.
3. Until his sentence was the only one left.

> The last two lines are a disagreement about one word and they are worth reading twice. Yuki says he waited until his sentence was the only one left; he says he waited until it was worth saying. Those are the same timing and different motives, and he says so: *it is not, and you know it is not*. A class that can say which of them is right has understood something that is not grammar.

**7C: Controlled Practice — Passing On a Message**

0. Say who the message is from — **1** *(given)*
Say what you do not know — **4**
Say what they said, in their words — **2**
Say when they said it — **3**
Offer to go back and ask — **5**

> *What you do not know* goes fourth, after the message and its date, and before the offer to find out. Put it first and you have undermined the message before delivering it; leave it out and the listener acts on a gap they could not see.

**7D: Role-Play — Two Accounts of One Afternoon**

Marking points: ☐ A gives a version with its source ☐ A questions the other source ☐ A notices the difference in the wording ☐ B reports the exact words ☐ B agrees the wording is not the same ☐ B proposes writing both.

> Sample: *A: Amina said twenty minutes, and she was looking at it. — B: Tomas said an hour. — A: When was Tomas there? — B: He said he had been there. — A: He said he had been. That is not the same as being there. — B: No, it is not. — A: So we write twenty. — B: We write that Amina said twenty and Tomas said an hour, and we put their names on it, and whoever reads it can decide.*

***Stretch:*** your source was wrong and you have told four people. Accept *I have passed that on to four people and I am going back to all four. It was my mistake, not his — he said he had been there and I turned that into he was there.* The marker is naming the four and naming what they got wrong, which is the correction the unit has been building towards.

**7E: Writing — A Note Passing On a Message (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ who the message is from ☐ what they said ☐ what they asked ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (64 words): *The woman from upstairs came down at about seven. She said the water would be off from nine until about two on Thursday, and that it was on a card on the main door. She asked whether you would be in, because the man needs to get to the tap in your kitchen. I said I would ask you. She is in all evening.*

**Part 8 · Global Story — The Street That Reads the News Out Loud**

0. About six in the morning. *(given)*
1. That they do not hear it any more.
2. A third of them could say what the voice had said that morning.
3. That in a flood nobody checks a telephone.

> Item 1 against item 2 is the point of the story and it is a lesson about reported speech as much as about loudspeakers. What people say about themselves and what they turn out to know are two different pieces of evidence, and the passage reports the first and then tests it. A strong class can be asked what else people say about themselves that a survey would contradict.

**Part 8: Vocabulary in Context**

1. c · 2. b · 3. d · 4. a
Not needed: **e)** *a person whose work is carrying messages*

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because the church bell still rings for a death and everybody under forty thinks it is for the hour. This is different from where I live because theirs says words and ours says only that something has happened, so ours cannot be ignored and cannot be understood either. I think the most important thing is that a system nobody listens to is still worth keeping if it works on the one day the telephones do not, and that this is impossible to argue for in any year when nothing goes wrong.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Notebook Yuki Keeps**

0. In her first month. *(given)*
1. Because she could not keep the names and faces together.
2. She does not like it, and she has not said so.
3. As permission, and she may be wrong about that.

> Item 2 and item 3 are the same failure from two sides. Maya has not said what she thinks, so Yuki does not know it; Mr Okonkwo has said nothing at all, and Yuki has read that silence as a yes. The unit has spent ten sections on the difference between what was said and what was meant, and the last reading is about what happens when nothing is said at all.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) stop** — the notebook was never agreed to by any of the people in it, and one of them objecting is enough. It also throws away the only record that has settled four arguments, and the next argument will be settled by whoever is loudest.
- **(b) show them their page** — the fastest way to end the worst version, which is the one she is imagining. It also shows her the others’ pages exist, and she may object to those on their behalf.
- **(c) keep it and say nothing** — it harms nobody, it has helped four times, and a private note about a conversation you were in is not surveillance. It is also the choice that requires her to go on not knowing, which is the thing she has objected to.

> Sample (61 words): *I think I should show her the page, because the version in her head is certainly worse than the one in the notebook. Also, if I cannot show it to her then she is right and I should stop. This is better than stopping quietly, because stopping without showing her leaves her sure I had written something I could not show.*

**End of A2.2, and end of the course.**

Twenty units, two volumes, two hundred glossary words, and one street. The six people at number 14 have been described, reported, disagreed about and written down, and the last unit is about the only one of them who kept a record. That is not an accident of ordering: a course that spends twenty units teaching learners to say what happened should end by asking who gets to say it.

For the teacher taking a class through the last unit: the Part 5 reading and Part 9 belong together. One says a story gets shorter and surer with every telling and that nobody lied; the other puts a notebook against that, and makes the notebook uncomfortable. There is no answer in the material and there should not be one.
