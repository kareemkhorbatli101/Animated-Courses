**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 12.1)

This is the same shape as the *can* and *can’t* drill in Unit 8 of volume one, and it is worth saying so to the class: the pattern repeats across English, and a learner who has met it once is halfway there.

*Will* is weak and usually contracted: *it’ll rain*, *I’ll bring one*. In the middle of a sentence it is almost nothing.
*Won’t* never contracts and never weakens. The vowel is long and unmistakable: /wəʊnt/.

The failure to listen for: a learner stressing *will*. An English ear hears a strong auxiliary and begins waiting for a *not*. The sentence is then heard as hesitant or as a contradiction, neither of which was meant.

**Part 1: Four Kinds of Weather**

0. Heavy rain all day — a coat and dry shoes — nothing; you go anyway *(given)*

Accept any sensible pair, and expect disagreement, which is the point. The expected answers:

| The weather | What you take | What you cancel |
|---|---|---|
| Ice on the roads | flat shoes, more time | the car, and anything with a hill in it |
| Fog in the morning | lights, and patience | nothing; it lifts by ten |
| Thirty degrees | water, and a hat | anything at two in the afternoon |

A class from one country will agree about the ice row and argue about the thirty-degree row, and a mixed class will do the opposite. Let the argument run; it is the most useful two minutes in the part.

**Part 1: How Sure Is It?**

1. c · 2. b · 3. d · 4. a · 5. e
Not needed: **f)** *it happened and it was heavy*

Items 2 and 5 are the pair to work on. *It might rain* and *it might not rain* describe the same uncertainty from two ends, and learners who think the second is more negative have not yet heard that *might* carries the doubt, not the verb.

**Part 2 · Grammar — Will and Might**

**Part 2: Notice**

Sure: **It will rain this afternoon** · **It won’t be warm again until April** · **the buses won’t run**
Possible: **It might snow on Thursday** · **I might take the bus**
A decision made while speaking: **I will take a coat**

The teaching point is the fourth one, and it is the one coursebooks skip. *I will take a coat* said in response to a forecast is not a prediction about the future; it is a decision being made aloud at the moment of speaking. Learners who only meet *will* as a prediction never learn to use it to agree, to offer or to decide.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. will *(given)* · 1. might · 2. won’t · 3. will · 4. won’t

**Part 2: Sure or Possible?**

0. sure *(given)* · 1. possible · 2. sure · 3. possible · 4. sure

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. It **will rain** this afternoon. *(no* to *after* will*)*
2. It might **snow** on Thursday. *(plain verb after* might*)*
3. It **might** rain later. *(one word for possible is enough)*
4. The buses **won’t** run in ice. *(*not* goes inside the contraction)*

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One sure, one possible.

> Sample: *The shop will be shut tomorrow, because it is shut every Sunday and always has been. It might be the day I finally clean the windows, although I said that last Sunday too.*

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: What the Weather Does to You**

Marking points: ☐ one *will* ☐ one *might* ☐ one *won’t* ☐ a real answer to the third question.

> Sample: *It will rain tomorrow, because it rained today and the sky has not changed. Nothing stops everything here except wind, and then the bridge shuts and the whole town is twenty miles apart. I carry an umbrella I have never once opened, and I will keep carrying it.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ future questions, not present ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **Wednesday** (sun / showers) · **Friday** (nine degrees / two degrees) · and the pair should find that **Monday and Saturday match**. The Friday row is the one they miss, because both say a number and learners stop comparing once they hear one.

> Sample exchange: *A: What will it do on Monday? — B: Rain. — A: Same. Wednesday? — B: Showers. — A: Sun. One. Friday? — B: Two degrees. — A: Nine. Two. Saturday? — B: Fog. — A: Fog as well. So three? — B: Two so far, and Friday makes three.*

**Part 4: Deciding What to Do About the Weather**

Marking points: ☐ A says what is coming and when ☐ A says what it will stop ☐ A does not give orders ☐ B states a plan ☐ B changes one thing and says the new plan aloud.

Card A must produce: a fact, a time, a consequence, silence.
Card B must produce: a plan, a question, a change, a restatement.

The commonest fault is A telling B what to do. Stop them: the card says say what will stop, not say what to do. The difference is the whole exercise, and it is a difference learners feel in their own language too.

***Stretch:*** they will not change the plan and the weather is genuinely dangerous. Accept *I have said it once and I am going to say it once more, and then I will stop: the hill will be ice at six. That is all.* The marker is one repetition and a visible end to it.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ all four seasons ☐ at least two *will* and one *might* ☐ what people do, not only what the weather does.

> Sample (55 words): *Where I grew up the summer will be hot and dry and everybody moves indoors at two. Autumn is short and it might rain for a fortnight without stopping. The winter will not go below zero, which surprises people. And in spring the wind comes from the east for three weeks and everybody is unbearable.*

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: A Weather Warning (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what is coming and when ☐ what it will stop ☐ one piece of advice ☐ what you are doing yourself ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *Wind on Sunday night, and it will be worse here than in town because of the gap between the flats. Anything in a yard will move, so put the bins and the chairs inside before dark. The power might go; it did in March. I have candles and a kettle on gas if anybody needs either.*

**Part 6: Does the Weather Change People? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (52 words): *No, and I think the idea is lazy. People in cold places are not colder people; they are people with better coats and a plan. What the weather changes is where you meet, not who you are. Give a northern street three warm evenings and it behaves exactly like a southern one.*

**Part 6: A Day the Weather Changed Everything (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ the plan ☐ what the weather did ☐ what you did ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (57 words): *We were going to drive across on the Saturday and the snow came in on the Friday night, four inches of it, in a town that owns one plough. We stayed where we were for three days with a family we had met twice. It is the only part of that whole year anybody still talks about.*

**Part 6: Reflection — The Weather I Would Choose (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what you would choose ☐ why ☐ what you would give up ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (49 words): *I would take rain, and plenty of it, over heat I cannot get away from. You can dress for rain and you cannot undress for thirty degrees. What I would be giving up is the evening light, and I know people who would not give that up for anything.*

**Part 7 · Real-World File — Warnings and Forecasts**

**7A: Phrase Bank**

1. d · 2. e · 3. b · 4. a
Not needed: **c)** *how much it will cost*

Item 4 is the one worth teaching. *Conditions may become difficult overnight* is how an official warning says *this could be bad* without saying it, and learners who read only the words miss the register entirely.

**7B: A Warning Nobody Took** (Track 12.5)

0. The river might come up. *(given)*
1. It came into the shop and Amina lost two days.
2. That nobody tells anybody anything until it is a story.
3. Move her books off the floor.

**7C: Controlled Practice — Giving a Warning Well**

0. Say what is coming and when — **1** *(given)*
Say what you are doing yourself — **5**
Say how sure you are — **2**
Give one thing they can do — **4**
Say what it will stop — **3**

**7D: Role-Play — Warning Somebody Who Is Not Worried**

Marking points: ☐ A says it once, plainly ☐ A says how sure ☐ A gives one fact, not five ☐ A says what they are doing themselves ☐ B gives a real reason for not worrying.

> Sample: *A: They said the wind might take the scaffolding on Sunday. — B: They always say that. — A: It went down two streets away in March. — B: Did it? — A: It did. I am moving the car tonight. — B: I might move mine as well.*

***Stretch:*** you warned them, they did nothing, and it happened. Accept *Is everything all right? I have a spare pump in the shed if the cellar is wet.* The marker is help offered and the sentence *I told you* never appearing in any form.

**7E: Writing — A Warning for a Whole Street (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what might happen and when ☐ how sure you are ☐ one thing to do ☐ what you are doing yourself ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (67 words): *The gas is off on Tuesday between nine and four, and that is certain, not a maybe. No heating and no hot water in any of the flats on this side. If you are at home all day, fill a flask in the morning and you will be fine. Mr Okonkwo is on the fourth floor with no lift; somebody should knock. I am out from eleven.*

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because the whole town watches the first frost and the farmers talk about nothing else for a week. This is different from where I live because nothing we grow decides whether anybody eats. I think the most important thing is that the date is known and the arrival is not, because waiting for a known date is a different kind of waiting.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Tuesday the River Came Up**

0. On a Tuesday two winters ago. *(given)*
1. Two days of trade and a freezer.
2. Foolish on Monday evening, and clever by Wednesday. *(Accept* silly *for* foolish*.)*
3. He went down and stood at the shop door with Amina for an hour, saying nothing.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) do nothing until you are sure** — the only option that costs nothing when the warning is wrong, which it usually is. It is also the option that is never ready in time when the warning is right.
- **(b) do the small cheap thing now** — ten minutes against a week of not thinking about it. The hidden cost is that a person who prepares for everything prepares for nothing in particular.
- **(c) warn everybody else as well** — the one that helps the people who did not hear it. It also spends something you cannot get back: if you are wrong twice, nobody listens the third time.

> Sample (56 words): *I think I should warn everybody as well, because the people who will be hurt by this are exactly the ones who do not read forecasts. Also, it costs me one message. This is better than quietly moving my own things, because being the only dry flat in a wet building is not something I want.*
