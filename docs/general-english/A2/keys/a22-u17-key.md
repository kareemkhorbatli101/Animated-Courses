**Part 1: Pronunciation** (Track 17.1)

The passive has a pronunciation problem no other tense has: the grammar is entirely in a word nobody says loudly.

*is*, *are*, *was* and *were* reduce hard in the middle of a sentence. *It is made* comes out /ɪts ˈmeɪd/, and *the buttons were changed* comes out /ðə ˈbʌtnz wə ˈtʃeɪndʒd/, with the auxiliary at a schwa. The participle takes the stress, every time.

So a learner listening for the difference between *they changed it* and *it was changed* is listening for the loudest thing in each line, and the loudest thing is the same in both: *changed*. The difference is the quiet word, and often the difference is only that the quiet word is there at all.

Drill it the other way round. Say the pair and ask which one has an extra small word in it, not which verb is different. A class that can hear the extra word can hear the passive; a class listening at the verb never will.

This is the third auxiliary the course has done it with: *can* in Unit 8 of volume one, *will* in Unit 12, *have* in Unit 15. Say that out loud. English hides every auxiliary, and a learner who learns to listen in the gap stops needing it taught again.

**Part 1: Four Things and What They Are Made Of**

0. A bottle — glass — in a factory, and then again in another one *(given)*

**Part 2 · Grammar — Active and Passive**

**Part 2: Notice**

The maker goes first: **Maya made this book** · **Somebody changed the buttons** · **One factory produces forty thousand bowls**
The thing goes first: **This book was made by hand** · **The buttons were changed** · **Forty thousand bowls are produced in one factory**

The teaching point, and it is not the one most courses give: the passive is not for being vague and it is not for being formal. It is for when the thing is what the sentence is about. English puts the topic in front of the verb, and if the topic is the cup rather than whoever made the cup, the verb has to change shape to let the cup stand there.

That is why *the buttons were changed* is good English and *somebody changed the buttons* is a different sentence rather than a better one. Learners taught the passive as a transformation drill will produce it correctly and use it wrongly. Teach the choice, then drill the form.

**Part 2: Controlled Practice**

0. made *(given)* · 1. were changed · 2. are produced · 3. broke · 4. is lined

> Item 3 is the trap and it is deliberate: *somebody* is in the sentence, so the verb stays active. A learner who has decided the exercise is about producing passives will write *was broken* and leave *somebody* stranded in front of it.

**Part 2: Active or Passive?**

0. active *(given)* · 1. passive · 2. active · 3. passive · 4. active

**Part 2: Correct the Sentence**

1. This book **was made** by hand. *(the third form, not the first)*
2. The bowl **was made** in a factory. *(*be* is not optional)*
3. Forty thousand bowls **are** produced every week. *(a plural subject takes *are*)*
4. The window **was broken** last winter. *(*broke* is the past, *broken* is the third form)*

**Part 2: Freer Practice**

Open. One active, one passive, about one thing.

> Sample: *My grandmother made this blanket in about 1970. It was washed roughly for fifty years and it has not lost a stitch.*

**Part 3 · Listening**

**Part 3: The Four Bowls That Were Thrown Away** (Track 17.2)

0. True *(given)*
1. **True** — “It was thrown away because I could see how it was made.”
2. **False** — “The new one isn’t finished.”
3. **Not Given** — no college and no teacher is mentioned.

Item 1 is the one learners get wrong, and not because the English is hard. *It was thrown away* sounds like a fault was found, so they look for the fault and find none, and answer False. The script says the bowl was thrown away for showing how it was made — which is not a fault anywhere except in a bowl. The passive is doing the work: *I could see how it was made* is a sentence about the bowl, not about Yuki, and that is exactly why it condemns the bowl.

**Part 4 · Speaking**

All four tasks are productive. Accept any answer that meets the brief; correct form, not content.

**Part 4: How It Is Made**

Marking points: ☐ one *it was made in…* ☐ one *it is made of…* ☐ one thing mended and one thrown away ☐ the auxiliary present every time.

> Sample: *The oldest thing I own is a knife and it was made in Sheffield, which I know because it says so on it. Nothing in my kitchen was made by hand. I mend clothes, badly, and I throw away anything with a motor in it, because those are not made to be opened.*

**Part 4: Find the Difference (pairwork)**

Marking points: ☐ *What is it made of?* ☐ *Where was it made?* ☐ exactly three differences.

Three differences: **the cloth** (bought in / made there) · **the people** (four / twelve) · and the pair must find that **the bowls** and **the waste** match. A pair that reports two has asked *do you make the cloth* and got a yes from both, because both workshops have cloth; the question has to be where it comes from.

> Sample exchange: *A: Are the bowls made by hand? — B: Yes. — A: Mine too. Is the cloth made there? — B: It is. — A: Mine is bought in. One. How many people? — B: Twelve. — A: Four. Two. Is anything thrown away? — B: Nothing. — A: Nothing here either. So two? — B: Two, and the cloth was the big one.*

**Part 4: Describing How Something Is Made**

Marking points: ☐ A asks what it is made of ☐ A asks whether it was made by hand ☐ B names the material ☐ B gives one length of time ☐ B explains the slow part.

Card A must produce: the material question, the by-hand question, a question about time, a question about the slow part.
Card B must produce: a material, a confirmation, a length of time, and the reason the slow part cannot be hurried.

The commonest fault is B answering the time question with the working time rather than the total. *Three days, and most of that is waiting* is the answer the card wants, because the waiting is the thing a learner describing any process forgets to mention and the thing every process has.

***Stretch:*** you made it and it was made badly. Accept *I made this one and it is not good. The foot is thick and it was not dried slowly enough. The next one will be better and I am not going to say sorry about this one twice.* The marker is naming the fault once and moving on.

**Part 4: Mini-Presentation**

Marking points: ☐ about one minute ☐ one thing described ☐ how it was made ☐ at least two passives.

> Sample (50 words): *This is a tin box and it was made in about 1950. The sides were cut from one sheet and folded, so there is no join at the bottom. It was painted by hand and the paint has gone from the corners. Nothing inside it has ever been kept dry.*

**Part 5 · Reading**

**Part 5: Why Nobody Knows Who Made Anything**

0. No. *(given)*
1. **D)** Because it is made in stages, in two countries
2. **C)** The machines
3. **A)** The maker

> Item 2 is the sentence the unit is built on and it is worth a minute. *The grammar followed the machines* is a claim, not a fact, and a strong class should be invited to push back on it: English had the passive long before it had factories. What is true is that the passive became ordinary when the maker became unknown. Say which part is the claim.

**Part 5: Three Things That Are Made Twice**

0. All three, but metal almost nothing. *(given)*
1. Because melting takes nothing out of it.
2. The fibres are shortened, so after six or seven turns it is only good for boxes.
3. About a twentieth of the power needed to make it from ore.

> The last line of the reading is the one to leave them with: the material most worth recycling is the one people throw away without looking. Metal is worth about twenty times what glass is worth to recycle, and it is the one that goes in the wrong bin.

**Part 6 · Writing**

Each task already prints a model. These are the marking points and a second sample, so that the printed model is not the only answer a learner has seen.

**Part 6: How Something Is Made (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ the passive at least twice ☐ the stages in order ☐ one material named ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (55 words): *Paper is made from wood and water and almost nothing else. The wood is broken up until it is a grey soup. Then it is poured flat and most of the water is pulled out underneath. What is left is pressed and dried. A sheet of paper is a thing that was once a puddle.*

**Part 6: Should Things Be Made to Last? (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ a clear opinion ☐ two or three reasons ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (56 words): *They should not all be made to last, and that is not the popular answer. A thing nobody will want in six years should be made cheaply and recycled. The argument is only about the things people do keep, and almost everything sold as long-lasting is sold to people who were going to keep it anyway.*

**Part 6: Something You Have Made or Mended (50–70 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what you made or mended ☐ how it was done ☐ how you feel about it ☐ 50–70 words.

> Sample (50 words): *I have made one shelf and it is up. The wood was cut too short, so a block was put behind it, and the block is the only reason it is level. Nobody has ever noticed. I notice it every single day, and I have stopped meaning to fix it.*

**Part 6: Reflection — A Thing You Threw Away (40–60 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what it was ☐ why it was thrown away ☐ why you wanted it ☐ 40–60 words.

> Sample (49 words): *A radio was thrown out because it only got two stations. Both of them were the ones I listened to. It was replaced by something with four hundred stations and an application, and I have not turned it on since February. The radio was made in 1974 and worked.*

**Part 7 · Real-World File — Talking About How Things Are Made**

**7A: Phrase Bank**

1. b · 2. e · 3. a · 4. d
Not needed: **c)** *how much it will cost*

Item 3 is the one to teach, because it is the one with money in it. *It can be mended* changes what a thing is worth, and the whole of 7B is about who decides whether that sentence is true.

**7B: The Thing That Cannot Be Mended** (Track 17.5)

0. No, it can only be opened once. *(given)*
1. It has been glued shut.
2. Because a thing nobody can open is cheaper to produce.
3. Another one is produced.

**7C: Controlled Practice — Explaining How a Thing Is Made**

0. Name the material — **1** *(given)*
Say what is done last — **4**
Say where it is made — **2**
Say what is done first — **3**
Say which stage goes wrong — **5**

> The wrong stage comes last, after the whole process, and not next to the stage it belongs to. That is the order every good explanation uses: describe the thing working, then say where it fails. A learner who puts the fault in the middle loses the listener, who stops following the process to worry about the fault.

**7D: Role-Play — One Thing, Two Prices**

Marking points: ☐ A asks about the price ☐ A says they look the same ☐ A agrees out loud when shown ☐ B names how it was made ☐ B shows one thing ☐ B gives one length of time ☐ B does not run the other one down.

> Sample: *A: Why is this one three times the price? — B: The handle was put on by a person. — A: They look identical. — B: From the front. Turn it over. — A: There is a line on the other one. — B: That is where the machine closed it. It takes forty seconds. This one takes twenty minutes. — A: All right. I can see it now.*

***Stretch:*** you are not sure it is worth the price. Accept *Honestly, for a cup you will use every day, I would buy the cheap one. This one is worth it if you are going to look at it. Most people are not, and I am still going to sell you one.* The marker is the doubt said out loud and the sale not abandoned.

**7E: Writing — A Note About a Thing Being Mended (50–80 words)**

Marking points: ☐ what is being mended ☐ one date ☐ why it costs what it costs ☐ 50–80 words.

> Sample (61 words): *The oven has been taken away to be mended. It went on Monday and it is promised for Thursday, which probably means Friday. The door spring is no longer produced, so one is being cut by hand, and that is where the money goes. Everything this week is being done in a pan. I am told that is good for us.*

**Part 8 · Global Story — The City That Drinks Its Own Water Twice**

0. Most of its water. *(given)*
1. It is carried to a plant, pushed through membranes, and cleaned again with light.
2. About two-fifths.
3. Because a thing is easier to drink after it has been rained on.

> Item 3 is the human sentence in a technical story and it is the one to discuss. The water that goes into the reservoir is cleaner than the water already in it. Putting it there changes nothing about the water and everything about drinking it. That is worth ten minutes with a strong class, and it is also the best argument in the unit for why the passive exists: nobody wants to be told who made their water.

**Part 8: Discussion**

Open. Each learner should produce at least one *similar* frame and one *different* frame, with a reason.

> Sample: *This is similar to where I live because the water comes from a river that three cities upstream have already used, which nobody says out loud. This is different from where I live because here it is not admitted and there it is named and sold. I think the most important thing is that being told makes people object to water they were already drinking, which is an argument for telling them and not against it.*

**Part 9 · Close to Home — The Bowl That Was Never Finished**

0. Since March. *(given)*
1. The clay was shaped.
2. That it has been finished for five of the six months.
3. The glaze.

> Every answer in this set is a passive except the last, and the last is the only one Yuki says about herself. That is not an accident and a strong class can be asked why: the bowl is described in the passive because nobody is working on it, and the moment Yuki is the subject she is talking about something she can still do.

**Part 9: Decision Task**

No option is wrong. The task is the reasoning, and the frame must be used.

Marking points: ☐ a choice stated in the first sentence ☐ a *because* reason ☐ a second point after *Also* ☐ a comparison with one rejected option ☐ 50–70 words.

The three options, and the strongest case for each:
- **(a) finish it badly** — the only one that produces a finished thing, and a finished bad thing teaches you more than a perfect plan. It also puts a bad object in your house for years, where it will annoy you about once a week.
- **(b) throw it away** — honest, fast, and it ends the small daily failure of walking past it. It also throws away the three days already in it, and the next one will have the same hard part waiting.
- **(c) leave it where it is** — the hard part may become easy with another six months of practice, and the bowl is not costing anything but shelf space. It is also how the last four ended up as four, and a cloth that gets changed is a thing being tended rather than made.

> Sample (59 words): *I think I should finish it badly, because the glaze is the part I cannot do and I will not learn it by not doing it. Also, the bowl has stopped being a bowl and become a reminder. This is better than leaving it there, because another six months under a cloth makes it harder to touch, not easier.*
